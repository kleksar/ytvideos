The Press Conference That Sent Thousands to the Berlin Wall

November 9, 1989. East Berlin.

A government official is answering questions on live television. Near the end, a journalist asks when a new travel rule will take effect.

The official looks through his papers.

“Immediately. Without delay.”

A few hours later, thousands of people are standing at the Berlin Wall, demanding to cross into the West.

But there is a problem.

Nobody has ordered the border guards to open the gates.

And the people at the front have no intention of going home.

In 1961, East Germany sealed the border dividing Berlin. Families found themselves separated by concrete, fences, and armed checkpoints. West Berlin remained an island surrounded by East German territory. For ordinary East Germans, visiting the other side could be impossible.

People had tried to escape over the Wall, through tunnels, and across the border. Some made it. Others were arrested. Some were killed.

For nearly three decades, the government controlled who could leave, and when.

Then, in 1989, that control began to collapse.

East Germans were finding routes to the West through other countries. Hungary opened a path toward Austria. Thousands more sought refuge at the West German embassy in Prague. Even when officials stopped one route, people looked for another.

Meanwhile, demonstrations spread across East Germany. In Leipzig, crowds filled the streets. On November 4, hundreds of thousands gathered in East Berlin, calling for change.

The country's long-serving leader, Erich Honecker, had already been replaced. His successor, Egon Krenz, promised reforms. But the crowds wanted more than promises.

And there was one demand the government could no longer ignore.

People wanted to travel without asking the state for permission.

On November 6, officials proposed new rules. East Germans would still need documents, applications, and approval. To people who had waited years to travel freely, it was not enough.

Three days later, the leadership tried again.

This time, a small group of officials drafted emergency travel regulations. The rules would make it much easier to apply for visits and permanent departure. Crucially, they were supposed to be announced the following morning, when the authorities could put procedures in place.

The government was preparing a controlled change.

It was about to get something very different.

Late that afternoon, Krenz handed the new document to Günter Schabowski, a senior Communist Party official who was about to face the international press.

Schabowski had not taken part in drafting the details. He had notes, a document, and a live television audience.

At six in the evening, the press conference began.

For almost an hour, he spoke about politics, party changes, and the future of East Germany. The most important announcement came near the end.

An Italian journalist, Riccardo Ehrman, challenged him about the government's travel policy. Schabowski searched his papers and read out the new regulation.

East Germans could now apply to travel abroad without having to prove a special reason. Permission would be granted quickly. Permanent departure would be possible through official crossing points.

The words sounded significant, but also bureaucratic.

You could apply. You could receive permission. The system would process your request.

Then another journalist, Peter Brinkmann, asked the question that changed the evening.

When did the rule take effect?

Schabowski checked his papers. He didn't find a clear answer in his notes.

So he answered, in German, “Sofort. Unverzüglich.”

Immediately. Without delay.

That was not how the government had planned to introduce the new rules.

And thousands of people were about to take him at his word.

Within minutes, news agencies began sending out alerts. Television reports condensed a complicated travel regulation into a much simpler message.

East Germany had opened its border.

The problem was that, at the actual border, almost nothing had changed.

The gates were still closed. The guards were still checking documents. Their instructions had not suddenly been replaced by a new system.

But people watching television saw a senior official say they could leave immediately.

And they believed him.

At first, only small groups appeared at Berlin's checkpoints. Guards told them to come back the next day.

Then the eight o'clock news announced that the border was open.

More people came.

At Bornholmer Straße, one of the crossings into West Berlin, an officer named Harald Jäger was trying to work out what he was supposed to do.

He had watched Schabowski's announcement on television. He called his superiors. They had no clear instructions that matched what the public had just heard.

Outside, the crowd continued to grow.

By around nine thirty, hundreds of people were gathered there. They weren't trying to sneak across. They were demanding the journey they believed their own government had just authorized.

Jäger and his colleagues were trapped between two realities.

On television, the border was open.

At their checkpoint, it was not.

Then the authorities tried a solution that made everything worse.

They would let a small number of the most insistent people through, hoping to relieve the pressure. But for some of those people, officials stamped across the photographs on their identity cards.

The mark invalidated the document. Under the plan, they would not be allowed to return to East Germany.

And the people crossing did not know.

Imagine thinking you are making a short visit across town, while the officials behind the counter are treating it as a permanent departure.

This was meant to protect the border.

Instead, it gave the crowd proof that crossing was possible.

People who had been told to wait could see others being let through. They pushed for the same right. The pressure on the checkpoint increased.

By ten o'clock, the problem was no longer how to process applications.

It was how to hold back the people outside.

Television kept spreading the news. At 10:42, a West German news broadcast declared that the gates in the Wall were wide open.

At Bornholmer Straße, that still wasn't true.

Not yet.

The officers faced thousands of people and a barrier that was never designed to withstand a crowd this size. The possibility of panic or a violent confrontation was growing.

Jäger contacted his superiors again. There was still no workable answer that would empty the checkpoint safely.

And something else had changed.

Many of the people outside did not want to abandon East Germany forever. They wanted to visit West Berlin, look around, and come home. The government's attempt to quietly turn a visit into permanent emigration had misunderstood the crowd completely.

Soon, the guards had to confront a choice that no television announcement could make for them.

Keep the gate closed, and risk the situation getting out of control.

Or stop checking documents, and let everyone through.

At about eleven thirty that night, Jäger and the other officers at Bornholmer Straße gave way to the crowd. The checks stopped. The barrier opened.

People poured over the bridge into West Berlin.

Some ran. Some walked. On the other side, strangers greeted them, cheered, and embraced them. People who had lived minutes apart, but behind different political systems for decades, could finally meet without a wall standing between them.

Around twenty thousand people crossed at Bornholmer Straße in roughly the next forty-five minutes.

Other Berlin crossings opened that night, too.

What had begun as an announcement was now an irreversible public event.

For months, East Germans had been leaving, protesting, and demanding change. The country's leadership had changed, and the government was struggling to maintain control over travel.

Schabowski's announcement sent thousands to the border crossings before the new procedures were in place. As the crowds grew, the guards still had no clear instructions for handling them. At Bornholmer Straße, they finally made their own decision to let people cross.

Schabowski had not intended to create that scene. He went home after the press conference, expecting the bureaucracy to carry out the regulation as planned.

Instead, ordinary people had already made his words real.

The concrete Wall did not disappear overnight. But its power to keep Berlin divided had been broken.

Less than a year later, on October 3, 1990, East and West Germany were reunited.

The world remembers the two words spoken at that press conference: “Immediately. Without delay.”

But those words did not lift a single barrier.

Thousands of people went to the gates and refused to leave. And the officers standing opposite them finally let them through.

That evening, a journalist had asked when the border would open.

By midnight, the people of Berlin had answered him.
