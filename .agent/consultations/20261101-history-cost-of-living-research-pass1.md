### 1. Robert C. Allen — “The Great Divergence in European Wages and Prices from the Middle Ages to the First World War”

- **Publication/data:** *Explorations in Economic History* 38(4), 2001, 411–447. DOI [10.1006/exeh.2001.0775](https://doi.org/10.1006/exeh.2001.0775). Published dataset: Harvard Dataverse [10.7910/DVN/TMZGSF](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/TMZGSF).
- **Coverage:** several European cities; London is the relevant case. London wage sources are reported as Rappaport 1457–1609, Boulton 1610–1699 and Schwarz 1700–1860; earlier/later London values are extrapolated from Phelps Brown–Hopkins southern-English urban series. Some prices are interpolated or substituted; bread is direct for 1545–1780, with earlier bread inferred from wheat prices.
- **Categories/method:** wages, bread/food and fuel; fixed Laspeyres-style basket (bread, pulses, meat, dairy, eggs, beer, soap, linen, candles, lamp oil and fuel) compared with daily building-worker/artisan wages. Welfare ratios assume 250 workdays/year. This is a reconstructed research series assembled from published and archival series.
- **Limits/use:** actual rent is absent from CPI and replaced by a constant 5% rent allowance; household composition and 250 workdays are model assumptions. Could support London daily-wage purchasing power for a fixed basket roughly 1457–1860, but cannot establish changing access to housing.

### 2. E. H. Phelps Brown & Sheila V. Hopkins — “Seven Centuries of Building Wages” and “Seven Centuries of the Prices of Consumables, Compared with Builders’ Wage-Rates”

- **Publications:** *Economica* 22(87), 1955, DOI [10.2307/2626898](https://doi.org/10.2307/2626898); and *Economica* 23(92), 1956, 296–314, DOI [10.2307/2551457](https://doi.org/10.2307/2551457).
- **Coverage:** southern England, typically Oxford, 1264–1954. Wage series for building craftsmen and labourers compiled from secondary historical sources.
- **Categories/method:** building wages; consumables with a broad fixed-weight “composite commodity” (food dominates; fuel/light included) compared with builders’ daily rates. Reconstructed from historical accounts and published price/wage collections.
- **Limits/use:** actual rent is largely unknown; daily wage rates do not reveal how many days of work were available annually; some prices are closer to wholesale/institutional prices than household retail. Useful as a long-run benchmark for daily building wages against food/fuel, weaker for annual household survival.

### 3. Gregory Clark — “The Condition of the Working Class in England, 1209–2004” and English Economy datasets

- **Publication/data:** *Journal of Political Economy* 113(6), 2005, 1307–1340, DOI [10.1086/498123](https://doi.org/10.1086/498123). [UC Davis data page](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/data.html).
- **Coverage:** England; relevant usable interval is reported as 1209–1869. Data page has nominal/real wages, prices for 22 farm products (1500–1914), house rents (1550–1869), and farm day wages by four broad regions. National building-wage series averages London, South East, South West, Midlands and North. Original database is reported to contain 46,000 wage quotes, 90,000 price quotes for 49 commodities and 20,000 house-rent quotes.
- **Categories/method:** wages, food/grain, fuel (including firewood/faggots) and rents/housing; reconstructed from historical wage, price and rent observations, regionally aggregated with CPI. Housing method is also discussed in “Shelter from the Storm: Housing and the Industrial Revolution, 1550–1909,” DOI [10.1017/S0022050702000578](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022050702000578), which estimates average rents, constant-quality rents and housing quality.
- **Limits/use:** full combination is England-wide, not a single city; building workers are not “average English people”; hours/day and source geography/quality vary over time. Strongest lead for England 1550–1869 if actual housing costs matter, rather than Allen’s fixed rent allowance.

### 4. Sara Horrell — “Household consumption patterns and the consumer price index, England, 1260–1869”

- **Publication:** *The Economic History Review* 76(4), 2023, 1023–1050, DOI [10.1111/ehr.13236](https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13236).
- **Coverage/categories:** England, 1260–1869; reconstructed working-household budgets, with food, fuel and rent/housing weights. It is not an independent wage series.
- **Method:** chained-Laspeyres CPI with changing household expenditure weights, drawing on long-run price series including Allen and Clark, and varied budget evidence (medieval accounts, peasant housing, probate inventories, institutional diets and later household budgets).
- **Limits/use:** reconstructed CPI, especially for medieval periods; much of the evidence is rural England rather than London; does not itself say how much a person worked. Useful to test how conclusions change when the survival basket changes; later consumption substitution makes a fixed medieval basket less realistic.

### 5. Jane Humphries & Jacob Weisdorf — “Unreal Wages? Real Income and Economic Growth in England, 1260–1850”

- **Publication:** *The Economic Journal* 129(623), 2019, 2867–2887, DOI [10.1093/ej/uez017](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/uez017).
- **Coverage:** England, 1260–1850; approximately 6,800 actual annual payments, with the reported sample split 52% Midlands, 34% South and 14% North; period bins include 1260–1499, 16th/17th centuries and 1700–1850 subperiods.
- **Categories/method:** strength is annual earnings, built from manorial, estate and farm accounts, settlement examinations, diaries, memoirs and other sources; earnings are deflated by a cost-of-living basket. In-kind compensation is monetized through the basket. Reconstructed series, with wage observations drawn from historical payments.
- **Limits/use:** regional/national rather than city-specific; heterogeneous sources and valuation of in-kind compensation; comparison with casual day labour still requires care. Particularly useful to distinguish a high day rate from the actual number of days paid over a year.

### 6. Sara Horrell, Jane Humphries & Jacob Weisdorf — “Beyond the male breadwinner: Life-cycle living standards of intact and disrupted English working families, 1260–1850”

- **Publication:** *The Economic History Review* 75(2), 2022, 530–560; DOI [10.1111/ehr.13105](https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13105).
- **Coverage/categories:** predominantly rural England, 1260–1850; male, female and child earnings, with food/fuel/rent represented through Allen-style respectability costs rather than a new independent price series. More detailed demographic evidence is available from 1560.
- **Method:** life-cycle household model using annual male earnings, women’s/children’s wage and labor inputs, family size, fertility, savings and household structures. Reconstructed model, not primary observations of every household.
- **Limits/use:** findings depend on assumptions about women’s/children’s labor, savings, family structure and available work; not London and not a standalone long-run price database. Useful for showing that household survival cannot be reduced to a single male wage; implied child-labor days are model outputs, not observed time sheets.

## Honest comparison choices

1. **Cleanest city comparison:** London, about 1457–1860, Allen. Compare a building labourer’s daily rate with bread, food, fuel and a fixed basic basket. This supports “purchasing power of a day’s labour,” not annual workdays or actual rent trends.
2. **Most complete material-cost comparison:** England, 1550–1869, Clark + Horrell. Includes wages, food, fuel and a more evidence-based housing/rent series; costs the single-city focus.
3. **Best fit for “how much labor?”:** England, 1260–1850, Humphries–Weisdorf + Horrell. Annual earnings avoid assuming 250 workdays; CPI gives cost of living. This is a national reconstruction, not one city.

Suggested next check: use London/Allen as a visually clear city backbone, compare any annual-work interpretation with Humphries–Weisdorf, and, if housing is central, narrow the main proof to England 1550–1869 using Clark rather than a fixed 5% allowance.

## Independent source check (coordinator, 2026-11-01)

Checked scholarly article copies and publisher/author data pages. These checks validate the source map’s main distinctions, with the corrections below:

- **Allen:** the MIT-hosted article directly identifies London wage inputs (1457–1609 Rappaport, 1610–1699 Boulton, 1700–1860 Schwarz), with pre-1457/post-1860 extrapolated from southern-English towns. It states that rent was unavailable for most cities; the welfare-ratio poverty line applies a minimal flat 5% rent allowance and explicitly calls this an oversimplification. The ratio annualizes day wages at 250 days and assumes a notional family. Treat the 5% as a model allowance, not a London rent observation or an observed rent share. Source: [Allen article PDF](https://web.mit.edu/14.731/papers/greatdivergence.pdf), especially Appendix I and the welfare-ratio discussion.
- **Phelps Brown–Hopkins:** the article itself says the series contains only day rates, does not establish annual days worked, knows little or nothing about rent, and uses prices that are often more wholesale than retail. This is a day-wage purchasing-power comparison, not a full household cost-of-living history. Source: [1956 article text](https://users.pop.umn.edu/~ruggl001/hist5011/phelps-brown%20and%20hopkins.pdf) and [JSTOR journal record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2551457).
- **Clark:** Clark’s [UC Davis data page](https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/data.html) lists England nominal/real wages (1209–1869), farm-product prices (1500–1914), constant-quality house rents/average housing quality (1550–1869), and regional farm day wages. The publisher abstract for “Shelter from the Storm” says Clark estimates average rents, constant-quality rents and housing quality for 1550–1909. Call these estimates/reconstructed series, not direct rent observations throughout. The consultation’s counts of wage/price/rent quotes are not settled: the cited paper versions use different counts, so omit them unless the exact release/version is pinned.
- **Horrell CPI:** the LSE-hosted 2023 article confirms a chained-Laspeyres CPI with changing household expenditure patterns for England 1260–1869. Rent is part of the modeled budgets/weights; for early periods the author says monetary rents are not recorded and accommodation costs are estimated from building-cost evidence. It is not a direct rent series for every year. Source: [Horrell article PDF](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/117518/3/Horrell_household_consumption_published.pdf).
- **Humphries–Weisdorf:** the LSE-hosted accepted article confirms an annual-income series from year-employed worker payments, 6,800 in total, with 34% South / 52% Midlands / 14% North. It was designed to avoid converting daily rates into annual earnings without evidence about working days. It is England-wide/regional, not a London series. Source: [accepted article PDF](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/90328/1/Humpries_Unreal%20wages.pdf); publisher record: [Oxford Academic](https://doi.org/10.1093/ej/uez017).
- **Horrell, Humphries & Weisdorf household model:** the Wiley publisher page identifies the study as predominantly rural English working families, 1260–1850, and describes women’s/children’s contributions, annual male earnings and family life-cycle modeling. This is a model of household resources, not a census of observed workdays. Source: [Wiley article](https://doi.org/10.1111/ehr.13105).
- **Access limitation:** Harvard Dataverse’s Allen dataset page presented a robot-verification screen in this browser check. The article itself is verified; the dataset’s current metadata/files were not independently inspected and should not yet be represented as examined.

## Current evidence verdict

The strongest defensible development is likely an England-wide working-family story, not a seamless London rent-price series. Allen remains a clear visual comparison for daily purchasing power, but the 250-day year and 5% housing allowance must be named as assumptions. If the film centers on how a household met its needs, the 2022 family model and the 2023 changing-basket CPI offer a closer fit, while their reconstructed/model components must be marked honestly. A precise final period and claim are still pending further research and counterexample review.
