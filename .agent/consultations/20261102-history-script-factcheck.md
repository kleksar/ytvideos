## Coordinator's independent source check

Checked the submitted narration against Robert C. Allen, “The Great Divergence in European Wages and Prices from the Middle Ages to the First World War” (2001): [primary article PDF](https://web.mit.edu/14.731/papers/greatdivergence.pdf).

- **C1 — wages:** Table 2, p. 415, supports 3.2 g/day in 1500–1549 and 7.1 g/day in 1600–1649 for London building laborers. The hook was clarified to attribute the series to Allen rather than suggest a wage record for an identified worker.
- **C2 — prices:** Table 4, p. 425, supports London's consumer-price index values 0.45 and 1.25. The table sets Strasbourg, 1745–1754, to 1.00. Added a short line explaining that these are relative index values, not currency prices.
- **C3 — welfare ratios:** Table 6, pp. 427–428, supports London's building-laborer ratios 1.42, 1.16, and 2.15 for the stated periods. A ratio of 1.42 corresponds to a modeled margin of 42% above the basket; 1.16 corresponds to 16%; neither is presented as a real household surplus.
- **C4 — reversal:** The period-to-period factors are 7.1 ÷ 3.2 ≈ 2.22 for the daily silver wage and 1.25 ÷ 0.45 ≈ 2.78 for the consumer-price index. Thus the bounded statement that prices rose proportionally faster is supported. The welfare ratio falls from 1.42 to 1.16.
- **C5 — longer context:** p. 429 supports about 0.75 of the modeled poverty line in pre-Black-Death London and says fifteenth-century laborer earnings were generally high enough to rise above it. The narration keeps this as a model comparison and does not state that every household crossed the line.
- **C6 — method:** pp. 424–426 support the welfare-ratio definition, assumed 250 workdays, notional family of a man, woman and two children, and the 5% minimum rent markup. Allen explicitly says these assumptions could vary. The draft correctly labels the figures as a model, not observed household accounts or schedules.
- **C7 — basket:** Table 3, pp. 420–421, supports the listed categories. Allen notes local substitutions; the narration calls this a reconstructed benchmark and does not stage it as one family's actual meal or pantry.
