## Visible Extra High answer — saved summary

- The core discrepancy concerns the length of the working year, not only the day rate. The authors compare payments to annual employees with day rates and infer how many days a day labourer would need to earn the same yearly remuneration.
- Method sequence: collect documented annual pay (including monetized in-kind pay such as food and lodging); compare with day rates; infer equivalent days per year. This depends on labor-market arbitrage: worker mobility and employer flexibility are assumed to align annual earnings across day and annual contracts. It is not a register of actual days worked.

## Independent primary-source check (coordinator)

Primary source: [LSE-hosted accepted article PDF](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/90328/1/Humpries_Unreal%20wages.pdf); publisher record: [Oxford Academic, DOI 10.1093/ej/uez017](https://academic.oup.com/ej/article/129/623/2867/5490321).

- Abstract and introduction (working paper pp. 2–3; journal pp. 2867–69) state that the 250-day convention overestimates medieval annual incomes and underestimates them during the Industrial Revolution. The new series uses payments to workers employed by the year; the authors infer annual workdays by comparing those annual payments with day rates.
- Methods and data (working paper pp. 5–6): this inference requires workers to be sufficiently mobile between day and annual employment and employers sufficiently flexible for annual earnings to converge across contracts. The paper monetizes in-kind payments, including board and lodging. Those days are an implied equivalent, not a count of each worker’s actual days.
- “The Industrious Revolution,” §4.4 and Figure 4 (working paper pp. 18–19; journal section/page to be cited by section/figure): before the Black Death, roughly four days of casual work a week could match an annual worker’s pay; after c. 1350, sharply rising day rates and more modest growth in annual rates meant only about two or three casual-work days per week were required for the equivalent. The authors give two possible explanations for shorter post-plague labor input: day workers could not find enough work, or their labor supply bent backward (they preferred more leisure after reaching income goals). Neither is established as the sole cause.
- Figure 4 infers a more than twofold rise from under 150 days during the medieval period to well over 300 during the Industrial Revolution; the figure’s caption says the curve is days a day labourer would need to earn annual-worker pay, while triangles are scattered independent estimates. The article says the long work-years around 1760–1830 align with Voth’s time-use study, but the authors do not claim that all plotted days are directly observed.
- In the conclusion (working paper pp. 22–23), the authors explicitly leave the cause of rising labor input unresolved: voluntary exchange of leisure for goods, structural changes in employment, erosion of alternatives to wage labor, more dependents, and bargaining-power shifts remain possible explanations.

## Editorial implication (coordinator inference)

The defensible human turn is: **a day rate did not tell a worker’s whole-year income.** After the Black Death, a stronger day rate could coexist with fewer implied workdays and a smaller annual-income “Golden Age” than the 250-day calculation suggests; by the Industrial Revolution, the inferred work-year is much longer and the fixed convention understates annual income. The reason the year lengthened is not settled in this paper, which can serve as an honest open question rather than a causal claim.

This does not reconstruct one family’s actual hours, food bill, rent or work diary. Any story should label Figure 4 as implied equivalent days under a stated labor-market assumption, and describe the price basket as a separate reconstructed measure. The paper itself identifies the decisive limitation: whether the added work was chosen or imposed remains unresolved.
