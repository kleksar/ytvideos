## Completed Extra High response (verbatim)

### HOOK

Задача: начать с конкретного препятствия — она уже пересекла океан, но ещё не получила возможность учиться.

On June 28, 1883, an eighteen-year-old woman in Roselle, New Jersey, wrote to a medical college in Philadelphia.

She had seventy dollars. Her husband expected to send twenty dollars a month, minus the cost of sending it. She admitted that she might not meet all the college's entrance requirements.

She had already traveled from India to America to study medicine. But she was not yet a medical student.

Her name was Anandibai Joshee.

She wanted to become a doctor for women in India who struggled to receive treatment from male physicians.

Could she get the qualification, return home, and actually begin that work?

### INTRO

Задача: объяснить её личную цель и медицинскую проблему, которую она собиралась решить.

Anandibai had grown up in British-ruled India. She married Gopalrao Joshee when she was nine years old. He encouraged her education, taught her, and later appealed to others for help with her studies.

As a teenager, she gave birth to a son. The baby died after ten days.

Anandibai believed that better medical care might have changed the outcome. The loss became one reason for choosing medicine, but the problem she described reached far beyond her own family.

At the time, some women in India avoided treatment from male doctors because of social customs. Female physicians already existed, including women who had come from overseas. But language, religious practice, and unfamiliar customs could make access difficult.

Anandibai believed India needed women trained in medicine who understood the women they would treat.

### DEVELOPMENT

Задача: провести через публичное решение, поездку, препятствия при поступлении и получение медицинского образования.

On February 24, 1883, she explained that plan at a public meeting in Serampore, in eastern India.

The questions she addressed went far beyond medicine.

Why travel to America when some medical instruction existed in India? Why make the journey without her husband? Would she lose her place in her religious community by crossing the ocean? What if something went wrong?

She did not say India had no medical teaching for women. She argued that the opportunities available to her were not sufficient for the training she sought.

She also made clear that she intended to remain Hindu while studying abroad and after returning home.

The opposition was not merely theoretical.

In her address, Anandibai described how people had stared, mocked her, and sometimes thrown pebbles at her when she went to school in India.

Her journey would carry her far beyond the streets where she had already faced that hostility.

But she had help.

Gopalrao supported her education and had sought assistance for a journey to America. Theodocia Carpenter, a woman in Roselle who had corresponded with Anandibai, offered her a place to stay.

Gopalrao remained in India, with relatives who depended on him. Anandibai traveled without him.

Reaching America solved the problem of distance.

It did not solve the problem of admission.

In her June letter to Alfred Jones, a member of the medical college's executive committee, Anandibai explained what she could afford and what she had studied.

She described her work in English grammar and arithmetic, and the languages she could read and speak. She acknowledged that her preparation might fall short of the regular requirements.

She also explained what the training was for.

She had crossed the ocean despite opposition from friends and her caste, and she wanted to qualify to give medical assistance to women who would not accept it from a man.

This was an application, not an acceptance.

Even if the college agreed to admit her, she still needed a way to afford the course.

By October 1883, however, she was enrolled at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia.

Admission changed the obstacle.

She now had to complete a demanding course of medical study, far from the country where she intended to work.

Over the following two and a half years, she studied medicine in Philadelphia, including anatomy, the study of the structure of the human body.

She continued wearing a sari, the traditional draped clothing she had worn in India, even when it made her stand out among her classmates.

Her health also became a concern during her years in Philadelphia.

Rachel Bodley, the dean of the college, helped by bringing Anandibai into her home, where she could eat food familiar to her.

It was practical support during the education she had crossed an ocean to pursue.

### CLIMAX

Задача: выплатить первую половину обещания — диплом и реальную возможность применить образование в Индии.

On March 11, 1886, the college held its graduation ceremony in Philadelphia.

Anandibai completed her medical degree. Her thesis concerned obstetrics, the branch of medicine dealing with pregnancy and childbirth.

She became the first woman from India to earn a degree in Western medicine in the United States.

Now the second part of her plan began to take shape.

She received and accepted an appointment as Physician-in-Charge of the female ward at the Albert Edward Hospital in Kolhapur, western India.

This was more than recognition of her academic achievement. It was an appointment to lead a hospital ward for women — the kind of work she had spent years preparing to do.

The woman who had asked whether a college might admit her in 1883 was, three years later, a qualified doctor with a job waiting at home.

But receiving a degree and beginning medical practice were two different things.

### RESOLUTION

Задача: завершить путь возвращением и показать, почему назначение не превратилось в работу с пациентками.

In October 1886, Anandibai sailed back toward India.

Her health had already troubled her during her studies. When her ship reached India in November, she was seriously ill.

She had returned with the medical qualification she sought.

She had accepted a hospital appointment.

Yet she was not able to begin working in the women's ward.

On February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Poona, now called Pune.

She was twenty-one years old.

She never began her medical practice in Kolhapur.

### FINAL

Задача: дать окончательный ответ на исходный вопрос, сохранив одновременно достижение и несостоявшийся результат.

Back in Serampore, she had faced questions about whether she could cross the ocean, study medicine, remain Hindu, and return home.

She had done those things.

In Philadelphia, she had earned the qualification she requested.

She had accepted a position at a hospital in Kolhapur, where she was supposed to care for women.

But the purpose that connected those achievements was to treat patients in India.

Anandibai reached the point of being qualified for that work.

She died before she could begin it.
