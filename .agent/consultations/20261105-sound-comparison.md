Recommended 20-second sound design

Use one continuous rhythmic music bed and three carefully placed effects. The music carries tension; effects reinforce specific story beats rather than continuously simulating an underwater environment.

1. Music bed. Use throughout: Tempo 72–82 BPM. Low, muted rhythmic pulse, almost like a distant mechanical rhythm. Dark, sustained atmospheric layer underneath. Gradual harmonic tension building toward the rescue discovery. No aggressive drums, dramatic trailer impacts or obvious heartbeat effects. The rhythm should suggest that something is happening beneath the surface, without competing with narration.

2. Three selective sound effects: descent / approaching the wreck: soft, low-frequency pressure swell to establish depth and tension; diver reaches or examines the wreck: short, subdued metallic creak to give physical weight to the environment; survivor or air pocket is discovered: brief, softened bubbling / airy release to emphasize the revelation. These are illustrative cinematic effects, not claims about sounds actually recorded during the historical event. Do not force all three into every 20-second excerpt. Each effect must correspond to something the viewer sees or hears in the narration.

3. Voice intelligibility: Keep narration clearly in front. Duck music approximately 4–6 dB while speech is present. Reduce competing musical energy around the voice's intelligibility range (roughly 1–4 kHz). Put effects primarily in narration gaps. Avoid loud transient effects underneath important names, numbers or revelations. Never apply heavy underwater filtering to the narrator's voice.

4. How to compare the two versions: Produce two otherwise identical 20-second edits: A — Music only: Narration + continuous rhythmic music bed. B — Music + selective effects: Same narration and music + 2–3 effects. Compare them at matched perceived loudness, preferably on both headphones and ordinary phone speakers. Evaluate three things: Intelligibility: Can every spoken word be understood immediately? Emotional impact: Does version B make the discovery or danger more tangible? Authenticity: Do effects sound like deliberate cinematic design rather than fabricated historical recordings? Selection rule: Version B wins only if the effects noticeably improve the storytelling without drawing attention away from the narration. Otherwise, use version A.

5. Important production rule: Never confuse atmospheric sound design with historical evidence. Avoid invented radio transmissions, recognizable sonar equipment, realistic archival hydrophone recordings or specific diver communications unless they are historically documented. The desired result is not to convince viewers they are hearing the actual underwater rescue. It is to make them feel the tension of the situation while understanding that they are watching a narrated reconstruction.

Decision: identical music and voice for comparison; sparse illustrative effects tied to the chosen footage, if suitable footage is available. BPM and ducking values are suggestions to check by listening, not requirements.
