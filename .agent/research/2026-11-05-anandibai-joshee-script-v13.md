**Central question:** Could Anandibai Joshee qualify as a doctor, return to India, and begin treating the women for whom she chose medicine?  
**Answer:** She earned the degree, returned with a hospital appointment, and died before she could begin that work in Kolhapur.

## 1. The Goal

*Цель героини и главный драматический вопрос.*

Anandibai Joshee wanted to become a doctor for women in India. To make that possible, she sought medical training in the United States.

But reaching an American medical college was only the beginning. She would have to find a way into a program, complete her studies, and return home qualified to do the work she had chosen.

She would eventually earn the degree. She would even receive a medical appointment in India.

But earning a qualification and beginning a medical career were two different things. And in the summer of 1883, even the first step—entering college—was not yet settled.

## 2. Seventy Dollars

*Финансовая неопределённость и открытая возможность.*

In a letter dated June 28, 1883, Joshee described admission conditions that seemed within reach.

She had seventy dollars. She expected her husband to send money each month.

That gave her a possible way forward, but it did not mean she had already been admitted. Nor did the expected payments amount to a complete financial plan.

Yet within a few months, Joshee would be studying medicine in Philadelphia.

The exact process by which the college approved her admission, and how all her expenses were arranged, is not established. What is clear is that she still had an important choice to make: which medical education to pursue.

## 3. Two Paths

*Выбор программы и переход к зачислению.*

That autumn, Joshee considered a scholarship at a homeopathic medical college in New York.

But she chose a different route.

Instead of the homeopathic program, she selected the regular medical course at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania.

This was more than a choice between two cities. She was deciding which kind of medical training would lead toward the career she wanted.

Mrs. Carpenter accompanied Joshee to Philadelphia and introduced her to the college's dean, Rachel Bodley.

That introduction was part of her arrival, but it does not establish who secured her admission.

By October 1883, Joshee was enrolled.

The uncertainty surrounding her entry had given way to a concrete fact: she was now a medical student, with five academic terms ahead of her.

## 4. Support and Illness

*Развитие учёбы через поддержку и проблемы со здоровьем.*

Her years at college were not simply an uninterrupted stretch between enrollment and graduation.

A few months after she entered, Joshee was awarded a scholarship of four hundred dollars.

It was significant financial support. But it does not establish that every expense of her education had been covered.

There were also problems that money alone could not resolve.

In February 1884, Joshee suffered a severe case of diphtheria. Later, she experienced headaches and chest colds.

These illnesses were part of her experience during medical training, although they do not establish the cause of her later death.

Meanwhile, Dean Bodley helped her find lodging and obtain food that was familiar to her.

These were practical forms of support, not dramatic turning points. They made a difference to the circumstances in which Joshee continued her education.

And while her health presented difficulties, her academic progress could be measured.

## 5. The Degree

*Экзаменационный результат, завершение образования и достижение цели.*

In March 1885, Joshee took examinations in anatomy, physiology, and chemistry.

Among forty-two women students, she ranked eighth.

That result provides something more concrete than a general statement that she studied medicine. She had taken examinations in major medical subjects and placed among the stronger students in her class.

Her training continued through the following year.

By March 1886, Joshee had completed five academic terms at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania.

She graduated, becoming the first woman from India to earn a degree in Western medicine in the United States.

This was a specific achievement: an Indian woman had completed a Western medical degree at an American institution.

The qualification she had sought was now hers.

But graduation was not yet the career for which she had prepared. She still needed the opportunity to put her medical education into practice.

## 6. The Unfinished Appointment

*Стажировка, возвращение, трагическая развязка.*

After graduation, Joshee began a six-month internship at New England Hospital.

She did not complete it.

Illness forced her to stop before the training period was over.

Even so, an appointment in Kolhapur followed.

Joshee returned to India with her American medical degree and a position awaiting her.

The journey that had begun with uncertainty over admission and money had brought her remarkably close to her original goal.

She had chosen her college, completed the course, passed examinations, and received the qualification she needed.

Now there was a position in India where she could begin the medical work she had wanted to do for women.

But she never began that work.

On February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Pune, before taking up her appointment.

Her degree remained a landmark achievement. She had become the first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States.

But the medical career she had prepared for remained unfinished.

She returned with the qualification she had sought—and died before she could use it in the position waiting for her.
