**Central question:** Can Anandibai Joshee get the medical education she wants, return to India, and use it to care for women?

## Narration

In 1878, Anandibai Joshee's infant son died after ten days. Joshee believed that better medical care might have saved him. She began to think about becoming a doctor herself.

She wanted to help women in India who would not accept medical care from a male physician. But she had never completed an English school education, and the college she wanted was thousands of miles away in Philadelphia.

Could she get in, earn a medical degree, and return to care for the women she had set out to help?

On June 28, 1883, Joshee wrote to the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. She had seventy dollars. Her husband expected to send twenty dollars a month, minus the cost of sending it. She did not know whether her education would meet the college's requirements. Still, she asked to be admitted on terms she could afford.

She also told the college why she had come to America: she felt bound to help women who needed medical care but would rather die than accept it from a man. She did not ask for a place for private advancement; she asked for the education she needed to do that work.

Her letter made no claim that she already met every requirement. She had learned seven languages, but her English arithmetic had only reached division. She was eighteen and had come despite opposition from friends and her community. With seventy dollars and a request for admission, she was asking the college to take a chance on her preparation—and on her determination.

By October, she was enrolled in Philadelphia.

The first obstacle was behind her. Now Joshee had to make it through five academic terms, while living far from home and managing serious illness.

In February 1884, she suffered diphtheria. During her time in Philadelphia, she also struggled to get food familiar to her. Dean Rachel Bodley helped her find housing and customary food. Joshee lived in the dean's home for the rest of her stay in the city. Her studies continued through five academic terms.

A scholarship of four hundred dollars was awarded during her first year. The next year brought a different test: in the examination room. In March 1885, she took exams in anatomy, physiology, and chemistry. Among forty-two women students, she ranked eighth.

She kept going. By March 1886, she had completed all five terms at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. Joshee graduated, becoming the first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States.

She had done what she crossed the ocean to do. But a degree alone could not bring medical care to the women waiting in India. She still needed to return and begin work.

After graduation, Joshee began a six-month internship at New England Hospital. Illness forced her to leave before it was complete. She accepted a position as physician in charge of the women's ward at Albert Edward Hospital in Kolhapur and sailed for India in October 1886.

The post was waiting. Her journey home was complete. But her medical work was not yet begun.

On February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Pune, before she could take up the appointment.

She had earned the degree and returned to the hospital where she hoped to serve. She never got the chance to treat its patients.

## Internal source and claim notes

- Joshee’s letter to Alfred Jones, dated June 28, 1883, supports the admission request, $70, expected $20 monthly from her husband less transfer costs, incomplete English mathematics, seven languages, opposition, the stated purpose of serving women who would not accept care from male physicians, and her appeal to determination: [SAADA / Drexel](https://www.saada.org/explore/archive/items/20120711-721).
- Caroline Healey Dall’s 1888 biography says Joshee's infant child died after a few days; she believed proper medical advice might have saved him, and from then her thoughts turned to the need for women doctors. Treat the claimed counterfactual as Joshee’s belief, not as established medical causation: [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sketches_of_some_distinguished_Indian_women/Chapter_3).
- Drexel’s Legacy Center guide reports the infant lived ten days; confirms Bodley’s practical help with housing and familiar food; the four-hundred-dollar scholarship; five terms; graduation; appointment as Physician-in-Charge of the female ward at Albert Edward Hospital; return voyage in October 1886; and death before beginning practice: [Drexel Legacy Center](https://drexel.edu/legacy-center/the-collections/research-guides/joshee/).
- The New England Hospital internship and illness forcing her to resign are supported by the hospital’s 1887 annual-report account reproduced in this historical review: [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5234964/).
- The 1885 examination rank among forty-two women is reported in Dall’s biography, quoting the National Association annual report. The narration specifies examination rank; do not imply an overall academic rank.
- The official achievement wording stays narrow: first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States: [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2020/03/anandibai-joshee-the-first-indian-woman-to-earn-a-medical-degree-in-the-united-states/).
- No admission mechanism is supplied. The narration states the documented sequence—petition, then enrollment—without inventing who secured the decision or treating the gap as a mystery. It omits the unexplained homeopathic-versus-regular-course choice from v13.
- The ending does not attribute her death to diphtheria or the later illness that ended her internship.
