**Central question:** Can Anandibai Joshee earn a medical degree and return to care for women in India?

## Narration

In 1878, Anandibai Joshee's infant son died after ten days. She believed better medical care might have saved him. She began to think about becoming a doctor herself.

She wanted to help women in India who would not accept medical care from a male physician. The college she wanted was thousands of miles away, in Philadelphia.

Could she earn a medical degree and return to care for the women she hoped to help?

In June 1883, eighteen-year-old Joshee wrote to the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. She had seventy dollars. Her husband expected to send twenty dollars a month, less the transfer cost. Despite opposition from friends and her community, she asked to be admitted on terms she could afford.

By October, she was enrolled in Philadelphia. Now she had to finish the course while living far from home and coping with illness.

In February 1884, she suffered diphtheria. She also struggled to obtain food familiar to her. Dean Rachel Bodley helped her find housing and customary food; Joshee lived in the dean's home for the rest of her stay in Philadelphia.

In 1885, Joshee ranked eighth among forty-two women students in examinations.

After five terms, Joshee graduated in March 1886, becoming the first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States.

After graduation, she began a six-month internship at New England Hospital. Illness forced her to leave before it was complete. Joshee accepted a position as physician in charge of the women's ward at Albert Edward Hospital in Kolhapur. In October 1886, she sailed for India to take up the post. When she arrived in November, she was seriously ill.

Three months later, on February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Pune before she could take up the appointment.

## Internal source and claim notes

- Joshee’s letter to Alfred Jones, dated June 28, 1883, supports the admission request, $70, expected $20 monthly from her husband less transfer costs, incomplete English mathematics, seven languages, opposition, the stated purpose of serving women who would not accept care from male physicians, and her appeal to determination: [SAADA / Drexel](https://www.saada.org/explore/archive/items/20120711-721).
- Caroline Healey Dall’s 1888 biography says Joshee's infant child died after a few days; she believed proper medical advice might have saved him, and from then her thoughts turned to the need for women doctors. Treat the claimed counterfactual as Joshee’s belief, not as established medical causation: [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sketches_of_some_distinguished_Indian_women/Chapter_3).
- Drexel’s Legacy Center guide reports the infant lived ten days; confirms Bodley’s practical help with housing and familiar food; the four-hundred-dollar scholarship; five terms; graduation; appointment as Physician-in-Charge of the female ward at Albert Edward Hospital; return voyage in October 1886; and death before beginning practice: [Drexel Legacy Center](https://drexel.edu/legacy-center/the-collections/research-guides/joshee/).
- The New England Hospital internship and illness forcing her to resign are supported by the hospital’s 1887 annual-report account reproduced in this historical review: [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5234964/).
- The 1885 examination rank among forty-two women is reported in Dall’s biography, quoting the National Association annual report. The narration specifies examination rank; do not imply an overall academic rank.
- The official achievement wording stays narrow: first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States. The Library of Congress also reports that Joshee arrived in India seriously ill in November 1886: [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2020/03/anandibai-joshee-the-first-indian-woman-to-earn-a-medical-degree-in-the-united-states/).
- No admission mechanism is supplied. The narration states the documented sequence—petition, then enrollment—without inventing who secured the decision or treating the gap as a mystery. It omits the unexplained homeopathic-versus-regular-course choice from v13.
- The ending does not attribute her death to diphtheria or the later illness that ended her internship.
