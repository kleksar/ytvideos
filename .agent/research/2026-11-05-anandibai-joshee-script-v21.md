## Narration

Anandibai Joshee's infant son lived for just ten days.

She believed better medical care might have saved him. His death helped shape a decision that would take her far from home: she would study medicine and become a doctor.

Joshee had a particular purpose. Some women in India would not accept treatment from male physicians. She wanted to be able to treat those women herself. But to prepare for that work, she planned to travel to the United States, thousands of miles from the country where she hoped to practice.

On February 24, 1883, Joshee publicly explained her intention to study medicine in Serampore, India. She addressed concerns about women entering the profession, about traveling overseas, and about the implications of that journey for religion and caste.

The speech marked a public commitment, not the completion of a plan. She still needed to get into a medical college and find a way to afford her time there. The distance between declaring an intention and qualifying as a physician was enormous, and the next stage would be measured in far more than miles.

She did have support. Her husband encouraged her education and expected to send money. In the United States, Theodocia Carpenter, a woman living in Roselle, New Jersey, had corresponded with Joshee and offered lodging. Each offered something she needed as she tried to turn a stated intention into a course of study.

By late June, Joshee was in Roselle. She had reached the United States, but she was not yet enrolled in medical school.

On June 28, 1883, she wrote to the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia. She wanted admission on terms she could afford, and she set out the finances she expected to rely on.

She had seventy dollars. Her husband expected to send twenty dollars a month, less the cost of sending the money. These were the resources she described while seeking a way to study. The letter itself did not give her a place in a classroom. She was still asking for that opportunity.

By October, Joshee was enrolled at the college in Philadelphia.

Now the plan she had defended in Serampore had become a demanding course of study. Admission was the first threshold. Completing the education required her to remain in Philadelphia and finish the work that stood between her and a medical degree.

Her life outside the classroom also mattered. In 1884, Joshee suffered diphtheria. She struggled to obtain food that was familiar to her, a difficulty alongside the illness as she continued her studies away from India.

Rachel Bodley, the dean of her college, helped with housing and familiar food. Joshee lived in Bodley's home for the remainder of her stay in Philadelphia. The assistance was concrete: a place to live and help with something as ordinary, and as necessary, as eating.

She continued her studies and completed five terms. In March 1886, she graduated from the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. Her thesis concerned obstetrics, the branch of medicine dealing with pregnancy and childbirth.

Joshee had become the first woman from India to earn a degree in Western medicine in the United States.

The qualification answered one part of the ambition that had brought her across the ocean. She had prepared to become a physician. But she had wanted to practice in India, and graduation in Philadelphia was not the same as starting that work.

After graduation, she began a six-month internship at New England Hospital. It was a further period of training, beyond the medical college. But she could not complete it. Illness forced her to leave the hospital before the internship was over.

Her education had reached another turning point. She had earned the degree; her next period of training had been interrupted. She then accepted an appointment in India, as physician in charge of the women's ward at Albert Edward Hospital in Kolhapur.

That post gave her a specific responsibility: medical care in a women's ward in the country where she had originally intended to serve. It brought her stated purpose and a real hospital appointment together, even though she had not yet started the job.

In October 1886, Joshee sailed for India. She arrived in November, seriously ill.

She had returned with a medical degree and an appointment, but she would not begin work at the Kolhapur hospital.

On February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Pune.

She had set out to qualify as a physician for women in India. She had earned the degree, accepted the post, and come home. The one step she never reached was beginning practice in that ward.

## Internal source and claim notes

- Joshee’s letter to Alfred Jones, dated June 28, 1883, supports her admission request, $70, expected $20 monthly from her husband less transfer costs, and stated purpose of serving women who would not accept care from male physicians: [SAADA / Drexel](https://www.saada.org/explore/archive/items/20120711-721).
- Her infant’s death after ten days and her belief that medical care could have changed the outcome are reported in Caroline Healey Dall’s 1888 biography and Drexel’s guide; the narration presents the counterfactual as Joshee’s belief, not established medical causation: [Dall](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sketches_of_some_distinguished_Indian_women/Chapter_3), [Drexel Legacy Center](https://drexel.edu/legacy-center/the-collections/research-guides/joshee/).
- The Serampore public address took place on February 24, 1883. Its surviving text is preserved through Dall’s later biography, not an independent transcript: [Speaking While Female](https://speakingwhilefemale.co/health-joshi/). Narration summarizes its subject and does not present invented dialogue as quotation.
- Drexel’s guide supports October 1883 enrollment, five terms, Bodley’s help with housing and familiar food, graduation, the Kolhapur appointment, and return voyage. It does not establish the exact admission/funding mechanism between the June letter and enrollment.
- The New England Hospital internship and illness forcing her to resign are supported by the hospital’s 1887 annual-report account reproduced in this review: [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5234964/).
- The Library of Congress reports her arrival in India seriously ill in November 1886 and identifies her as the first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States: [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2020/03/anandibai-joshee-the-first-indian-woman-to-earn-a-medical-degree-in-the-united-states/).
- No admission mechanism is supplied. The narration states the documented sequence—petition, then enrollment—without inventing who secured the decision or what aid covered the cost.
- The ending does not attribute her death to diphtheria or the later illness that ended her internship.
