**Central question:** Can Anandibai Joshee earn a medical degree, return to India, and begin caring for the women she hopes to help?

## Narration

Anandibai Joshee's infant son lived for just ten days.

She believed better medical care might have saved him. His death helped shape a decision that would take her thousands of miles from India: she wanted to study medicine and become a doctor.

Joshee had a particular purpose. Some women in India would not accept treatment from male physicians. She wanted to be able to treat those women herself. But she was seeking medical training that she did not believe she could obtain at home.

On February 24, 1883, Joshee publicly explained her plan in Serampore, India. She addressed concerns about women studying medicine and about her intention to travel overseas.

She acknowledged that some medical instruction was available in India, but considered it inadequate for the thorough training she wanted. She also hoped to return with medical knowledge and establish instruction for women.

Travel raised another concern. Going overseas could lead to exclusion from her religious and social community. Joshee intended to remain Hindu, keep her customs, and return to India. Her plan was to study abroad, then bring what she had learned home.

Her husband supported her education and expected to send money. In the United States, Theodocia Carpenter, a woman living in Roselle, New Jersey, had corresponded with Joshee and offered lodging.

By late June, Joshee was in Roselle. She had crossed the ocean, but she was not yet enrolled in medical school.

On June 28, 1883, she wrote from Roselle to the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia, seeking admission on terms she could afford.

She had seventy dollars. Her husband expected to send twenty dollars a month, less the cost of transferring the money. Joshee described those resources as she asked the college to admit her.

By October, she was enrolled in Philadelphia.

She had reached the college where she would spend the next several years studying medicine. But life in the United States brought difficulties beyond the demands of the course.

In 1884, Joshee suffered diphtheria. She also struggled to obtain food that was familiar to her.

Rachel Bodley, the dean of the medical college, helped her with housing and familiar food. Joshee lived in Bodley's home for the remainder of her stay in Philadelphia.

She continued her studies through five terms. In March 1886, she graduated from the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. Her thesis concerned obstetrics, the branch of medicine dealing with pregnancy and childbirth.

Joshee became the first woman from India to earn a degree in Western medicine in the United States.

After graduation, she began a six-month internship at New England Hospital. But she was unable to complete it. Illness forced her to leave before the internship was finished.

She then accepted an appointment in India as physician in charge of the women's ward at Albert Edward Hospital in Kolhapur.

The position would allow her to practice medicine in India, where she had always intended to work. She had completed her medical education, and a hospital had appointed her to lead its women's ward.

In October 1886, Joshee sailed for India. She arrived in November, seriously ill.

On February 26, 1887, Anandibai Joshee died in Pune.

She had earned the medical degree she had crossed the ocean to seek. She had accepted an appointment and returned to India. But she died before she could begin practicing medicine in the women's ward she had been appointed to lead.

## Internal source and claim notes

- Joshee’s letter to Alfred Jones, dated June 28, 1883, supports the admission request, $70, expected $20 monthly from her husband less transfer costs, and stated purpose of serving women who would not accept care from male physicians: [SAADA / Drexel](https://www.saada.org/explore/archive/items/20120711-721).
- Her infant’s death after ten days and her belief that medical care could have changed the outcome are reported in Caroline Healey Dall’s 1888 biography and Drexel’s guide; the narration presents the counterfactual as Joshee’s belief, not established medical causation: [Dall](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sketches_of_some_distinguished_Indian_women/Chapter_3), [Drexel Legacy Center](https://drexel.edu/legacy-center/the-collections/research-guides/joshee/).
- The Serampore public address took place on February 24, 1883. The surviving text says some medical instruction existed in India but was insufficient for the training Joshee sought; she intended to return with thorough knowledge and open instruction for women. It also records her plan to remain Hindu and keep her customs. This speech survives through Dall’s 1888 biography, not an independent transcript: [Speaking While Female](https://speakingwhilefemale.co/health-joshi/).
- Drexel’s guide supports October 1883 enrollment, five terms, Bodley’s help with housing and familiar food, graduation, the Kolhapur appointment, and return voyage. It does not establish the exact admission/funding mechanism between the June letter and enrollment.
- The New England Hospital internship and illness forcing her to resign are supported by the hospital’s 1887 annual-report account reproduced in this review: [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5234964/).
- The Library of Congress reports her arrival in India seriously ill in November 1886 and identifies her as the first woman from India to earn a Western medical degree in the United States: [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2020/03/anandibai-joshee-the-first-indian-woman-to-earn-a-medical-degree-in-the-united-states/).
- No admission mechanism is supplied. The narration states the documented sequence—petition, then enrollment—without inventing who secured the decision or what aid covered the cost.
- The ending does not attribute her death to diphtheria or the later illness that ended her internship.
