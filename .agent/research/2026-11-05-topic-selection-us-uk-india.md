## Candidate comparison

### Recommended: Eiffel Tower's reprieve through scientific and military radio use

**Angle / audience question:** why did a tower conceived for an 1889 exhibition survive its limited operating concession? A coherent arc is exhibition construction → limited concession and future uncertainty → experiments and wireless telegraphy → military usefulness → 70-year concession renewal from 1910. This has a familiar landmark and a wider, understandable question about whether a structure can earn a permanent role. That is a cross-country accessibility rationale, not evidence of demand specifically in US, UK or India.

**Initial factual check:** the official Eiffel Tower site says the 20-year concession ran through 31 December 1909; demolition was seriously considered in 1903; Eugène Ducretet demonstrated wireless telegraphy between the Tower and Panthéon on 5 November 1898; military use expanded under Gustave Ferrié from 1903; and the concession was renewed for 70 years on 1 January 1910. Sources: [official history of the Tower's controversy and preservation](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/history-and-culture/when-eiffel-tower-was-subject-controversy), [official Eiffel Tower and science/broadcasting history](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/the-monument/eiffel-tower-and-science), and a documented history from the International Telecommunication Union, [From Semaphore to Satellite](https://search.itu.int/history/HistoryDigitalCollectionDocLibrary/12.25.72.en.100.pdf).

**Important wording control:** avoid saying a demolition order was signed or that radio was the sole cause unless primary records establish that. The official site uses simplified “saved by radio” language; a full story check should inspect the concession, City decisions and other reasons for preservation. A fitting working format is **Paper Detour long-form, about 4–6 minutes**: several dated turns and the distinction between experiments, military use and renewal make a complete arc, rather than a one-beat Short. Runtime is an editorial estimate, not a performance prediction.

### Alternative: Albert Alexander and the shortage during penicillin's first systematic treatment

**Angle:** how could a new treatment seem to work while the experimental supply still ran out? Oxford Health Histories dates systematic treatment to 12 February 1941 and says the 43-year-old policeman improved before the team ran out of penicillin; its archive includes an Alexander death certificate. The University of Oxford's [first penicillin treatment account](https://www.phc.ox.ac.uk/news/blog/on-this-site-penicillin-a-historic-first), [Oxford Health Histories timeline](https://oxfordhealth.web.ox.ac.uk/penicillin), and peer-reviewed review [The Discovery of Penicillin—New Insights After More Than 75 Years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5403050/) provide a starting base.

**Caveat:** do not call Alexander the first person ever to receive penicillin: prior human administrations and different definitions of “first” exist. Distinguish early experimental administration from the systematic treatment episode. Medical detail makes the India connection broadly legible, but there is no topic-specific demand evidence for any of the three countries. Extra High provisionally favored this for a 75–90-second Paper Short; the coordinator does not select it as the best first topic because the cross-market demand and distinctiveness evidence is no stronger, and its complete story is less naturally expansive than Eiffel's multi-stage arc. This alternative is not an active production task.

### Not selected: Operation Mincemeat

The model proposed a 5–7-minute Paper film about the 1943 deception. It is documentable, but the five-year Trends results are sparse and confounded by theatre and screen adaptations. It is also less visually self-evident across the three markets and has a generic wartime-deception neighbor to active dark-web law-enforcement stories (although those are not the same event). No reason to prefer it over the Eiffel Tower candidate at this stage.
