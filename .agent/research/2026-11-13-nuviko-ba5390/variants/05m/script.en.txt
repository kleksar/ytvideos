How did a passenger jet land with its captain outside the cockpit? Who kept him there, and why had the windscreen failed on the aircraft's first flight after a repair?

On June tenth, nineteen ninety, British Airways Flight fifty-three ninety left Birmingham for Málaga. Eighty-one passengers were aboard, along with four cabin crew and two pilots. The first officer, Alastair Atchison, flew the takeoff. As the aircraft climbed, Captain Tim Lancaster took over the controls. Nothing in the departure had suggested what was about to happen.

The departure had seemed routine to the cabin attendants too. At seven thirty-three they were preparing food and drinks. Both pilots had released their shoulder harnesses after takeoff, and Lancaster had loosened his lap belt. That mattered when the window failed: he was not secured by the full restraint of his seat. The report described pressure pushing the windscreen out, not any impact from outside. A cockpit that should have enclosed both pilots now had an opening beside the flight controls.

At about seventeen thousand three hundred feet, the left cockpit windscreen suddenly came out. Cabin pressure forced Lancaster partly through the opening and back over the cockpit roof. The cockpit door flew onto the radio and navigation console. Flight attendant Nigel Ogden, who had been nearby, rushed forward and grabbed the captain around his waist.

That grip kept Lancaster from being carried farther outside. But it created a new problem: the men could see his head and torso through the neighboring window, yet the rushing air prevented them pulling him back in. Atchison had to fly and descend while his colleagues held a person against the wind outside his aircraft.

Atchison initially struggled to regain control after the violent decompression. He began a rapid descent and tried to send a distress call. The roar in the cockpit made the reply difficult to hear. Meanwhile, the senior cabin crew member cleared the fallen door and helped hold the captain. Other crew members told passengers to fasten their belts and take emergency positions. Those were two different jobs happening at once: keeping the aircraft airborne and keeping the captain attached to it.

Ogden's arms were weakening from cold and bruising against the window frame. Another attendant took over the hold. He found the captain's legs trapped around the cockpit structure, secured himself into a folding crew seat and gripped both ankles. Even then, the captain moved farther out before the new grip held. The crew stopped trying to win a tug-of-war with the slipstream. Their immediate task was to keep their hold until the plane could land.

Atchison slowed the aircraft and headed for Southampton, where controllers confirmed a runway was available. He brought it down safely. The passengers left by the steps. Airport rescuers then pulled Lancaster back through the opening where he had remained during the descent and landing.

Lancaster had fractures, bruising and frostbite, but he was alive. The other passengers and crew survived; one attendant had injuries to his arm. Investigators now had another question to answer. How had a windscreen held by ninety bolts detached during an ordinary climb?

It had been replaced twenty-seven hours earlier. Eighty-four of its bolts were too small in diameter; the remaining six were the right diameter but too short. On this aircraft the front windscreen was fitted from the outside. Its bolts had to resist the air pressure pushing outward from the cabin. A panel could appear firmly seated while the aircraft stood in a hangar; that appearance alone did not establish how its fasteners would behave during the climb. The flight to Málaga was the first since the new window had been installed. There had been no independent final inspection to catch the incorrect parts before the aircraft carried passengers.

The fitter had compared replacements with bolts from the old installation rather than checking the correct part numbers. That earlier installation had itself used mostly incorrect bolts. One maintenance worker had also certified the replacement, without an independent final inspection.

The crew had prevented the immediate disaster. The inquiry showed why preventing the next one required more than praising the crew: a critical repair needed correctly identified parts and a check capable of catching an error before passengers took off.
