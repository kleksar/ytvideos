How did a passenger jet land with its captain outside the cockpit? How did the crew keep him from falling, and why did a window fail on the very first flight after it had been replaced?

On June tenth, nineteen ninety, British Airways Flight fifty-three ninety left Birmingham for Málaga. Eighty-one passengers were aboard, along with four cabin crew and two pilots. The first officer, Alastair Atchison, flew the takeoff. As the aircraft climbed, Captain Tim Lancaster took over the controls. Both pilots released their shoulder harnesses after takeoff. Lancaster also loosened his lap belt. In the cabin, the attendants were preparing to serve breakfast and drinks. Thirteen minutes after departure, they heard a bang and the cabin filled with mist.

At about seventeen thousand three hundred feet, the left cockpit windscreen suddenly came out. Cabin pressure forced Lancaster partly through the opening and back over the cockpit roof. The cockpit door flew onto the radio and navigation console. Flight attendant Nigel Ogden, who had been nearby, rushed forward and grabbed the captain around his waist.

That grip kept Lancaster from being carried farther outside. But it created a new problem: the men could see his head and torso through the neighboring window, yet the rushing air prevented them pulling him back in. Atchison had to fly and descend while his colleagues held a person against the wind outside his aircraft.

As Lancaster was forced out, his movement displaced the controls and the autopilot disconnected. Atchison recovered control and put the aircraft into a descent. He sent a distress call, but the airflow drowned out the reply. The controller on the ground did not yet understand what had happened. In the cabin, two attendants got the passengers ready for an emergency. The senior attendant cleared the fallen door, returned to the cockpit and looped an arm through a crew-seat belt so he could help Ogden hold Lancaster.

Ogden's arms were weakening from cold and bruising against the window frame. Another attendant took over the hold. He found the captain's legs trapped around the cockpit structure, secured himself into a folding crew seat and gripped both ankles. Even then, the captain moved farther out before the new grip held. The crew stopped trying to win a tug-of-war with the slipstream. Their immediate task was to keep their hold until the plane could land.

When radio contact was restored, Atchison asked for the nearest airport and was directed to Southampton. He told a controller that he believed Lancaster was dead. The attendant kept holding the captain's ankles. At seven fifty-five, twenty-two minutes after the windscreen came out, Atchison stopped the aircraft on the runway. As passengers filed out by the front and rear steps, the fire crews brought Lancaster inside.

Lancaster had fractures, bruising and frostbite, but he was alive. The other passengers and crew survived; one attendant had injuries to his arm. Investigators now had another question to answer. How had a windscreen held by ninety bolts detached during an ordinary climb?

It had been replaced twenty-seven hours earlier. Eighty-four of its bolts were too small in diameter; the remaining six were the right diameter but too short. On this aircraft the front windscreen was fitted from the outside. Its bolts had to resist the air pressure pushing outward from the cabin. A panel could appear firmly seated while the aircraft stood in a hangar; that appearance alone did not establish how its fasteners would behave during the climb. The flight to Málaga was the first since the new window had been installed. There was still a question: why had anyone fitted the wrong bolts in the first place?

The manager who replaced the window had taken an old bolt to the stores to use as a sample. The store supervisor told him the window normally used a longer bolt, but the manager chose to match the old one. Most of the old bolts were already too short. The new ones he selected at another store were thinner as well. He signed off his own work; the first test under cabin pressure came with eighty-one passengers aboard.

The investigation called for a review of how safety-critical repairs were checked. For Lancaster, there was another outcome. Five months after the flight, he was back at work as a pilot.
