How can a passenger aircraft lose its captain through a cockpit window and still land? Why could the crew hold him outside but not pull him back? And how could ninety bolts appear to secure a window that would fail on the next flight?

Twenty-seven hours before this flight, a maintenance manager had fitted a new left cockpit windscreen during a night shift. Ninety bolts held it around the edge. The aircraft had not flown since. On its next flight, passengers would be aboard when those fasteners faced the load of a climb. No one looking forward through the glass could see whether the bolts were the correct size.

British Airways Flight fifty-three ninety was a scheduled service from Birmingham to Málaga in Spain. On board the BAC One-Eleven were eighty-one passengers, four cabin crew and two pilots. First officer Alastair Atchison conducted the takeoff. Captain Tim Lancaster then took the controls as the aircraft climbed. The two pilots had released their shoulder harnesses, and Lancaster had loosened his lap strap. Meanwhile, cabin attendants were preparing to serve food and drinks.

At seven thirty-three in the morning, while the aircraft climbed through about seventeen thousand three hundred feet, the left windscreen came out of its frame. The loss of pressure filled the cockpit with mist. Lancaster was forced partly through the opening and blown backward across the roof. The cockpit door was thrown onto the radio and navigation console. The man who had been flying was now outside; Atchison had to recover the controls, while the cabin crew had to reach Lancaster before he moved farther into the slipstream.

A cabin crew member, Nigel Ogden, had been working near the door. He rushed forward and caught Lancaster around the waist. The senior cabin crew member removed the fallen door and stowed it in the forward toilet. The other attendants prepared the passengers for an emergency. Ogden's first grip gave the crew a chance, but it did not solve the problem. Through the adjacent window they could see the captain's head and torso outside. The slipstream prevented them from bringing him back in.

Atchison was momentarily disoriented and had to get the aircraft under control. The captain's sudden movement had displaced the control column and disconnected the autopilot. Atchison recovered control, started a rapid descent and re-engaged the autopilot. He also transmitted a distress call. Air rushed through the damaged flight deck so loudly that he could not make out its acknowledgment. The controller did not immediately understand the nature of the emergency.

Meanwhile, a second person was helping Ogden hold Lancaster. The senior attendant hooked an arm through the belt of an unused jump seat. Their effort still failed to move the captain back through the opening. Ogden was developing frostbite and bruising against the window frame, and his arms were giving way. The team needed to change hands without losing the person they were trying to save.

Another attendant relieved Ogden. Lancaster's right leg was caught between the cockpit structure and a control column; his left leg was wedged against a seat cushion. The replacement rescuer secured himself into a left-side jump seat and managed to grasp both legs. Before he could do that, Lancaster moved another six to eight inches outside. The attendant then held him by the ankles throughout the remaining descent and landing. No one could know from that grip alone what injuries Lancaster had suffered or whether he would survive. The practical choice was to keep holding.

Atchison descended to a lower altitude, slowed the aircraft to about one hundred and fifty knots and requested directions to the nearest airport. Controllers guided him toward Southampton. Once he had confirmation that there was enough runway, he made a visual approach to runway zero two. The aircraft touched down at seven fifty-five in the morning, twenty-two minutes after the windscreen failed. For the people holding Lancaster, landing was not the end of the rescue. They had to maintain their grip until the aircraft stopped.

The engines were shut down. While passengers disembarked by the front and rear stairs, airport and local fire crews recovered Lancaster from his position across the windscreen frame. He was taken to hospital with fractures in his right arm and wrist and left thumb, along with bruising, frostbite and shock. Another attendant had cuts and bruising to an arm. There were no fatalities. Atchison had landed an aircraft after losing the commanding pilot through its front window; the cabin crew had prevented the captain from disappearing into the slipstream.

Investigators recovered the missing windscreen near Cholsey, in Oxfordshire. They counted its fasteners. Eighty-four bolts had a smaller diameter than specified. The other six had the correct diameter but were too short. That difference was decisive: this windscreen was fitted from the outside, so cabin pressure pushed outward against the bolts. The glass had stayed in place on the ground. Under pressure during the climb, its attachment failed. How had a recently replaced window ended up with ninety incorrectly sized bolts?

The replacement had taken place between roughly three and five on Saturday morning. The shift maintenance manager decided to fit the windscreen himself. He consulted the maintenance manual briefly but did not check the required fastener numbers in the illustrated parts catalogue. Instead, he removed a bolt from the old windscreen and used it as his sample. That choice carried a hidden problem into the new repair. The old window had itself been held mainly by bolts that were too short.

At the first parts store, a supervisor identified the correct type of bolt. The manager continued trying to match the parts he had removed. Only a few candidates were available there, so he went to another parts carousel beneath the international pier. The lighting was poor. He compared the metal pieces by sight and touch rather than relying on their labels. The bolts he selected seemed similar to the sample, but their diameter was smaller. A shortcut based on an already incorrect installation had produced a second, different mismatch.

Back at the aircraft, eighty-four of those undersized bolts went into the frame. For the other six positions, the manager reused old, short bolts after longer ones did not fit the corner trim as expected. While tightening the fasteners, he worked from a position that restricted his view. The inquiry identified signs that the smaller bolts were not gripping properly. One was visible on the outside: their heads sat too deep in the holes. On the right-hand windscreen, the heads filled the holes. The manager missed the difference where the two windows met. He also needed glasses for small print but had not worn them during the job.

One final safeguard might have exposed the mismatch: an independent inspection. It did not happen. Under the maintenance system, a windscreen replacement was not classified as a job requiring that second check. The same manager fitted the window and certified the work. The Air Accidents Investigation Branch found failings in the fitter's practices and in the airline's supervision and quality monitoring. The report traced a chain of missed checks rather than treating the accident as a single unobserved mistake.

There was one more opportunity before the aircraft departed. The following night, the same manager replaced a right-hand windscreen on another aircraft. This time the parts had been laid out in advance. He noticed that the bolts were the longer, correct type. According to the inquiry, he explained the difference to himself as a variation between aircraft. The earlier repair remained untouched.

After the accident, British Airways checked windscreen fasteners on other One-Eleven aircraft. The investigators also recommended stronger independent checks for critical maintenance and better supervision. These measures addressed a danger the flight crew could not see when they took off. In the cockpit, one attendant had to pass the captain to another without letting go; Atchison had to keep flying until they could recover him. In the hangar, the person who selected the bolts had also been the person who signed off the work. The aircraft landed because the crew managed the emergency. The inquiry showed where that emergency could have been prevented.
