How did a shipwreck become a massacre?

And why did two small boats decide who survived?

September, sixteen twenty-nine. Two boats raced toward the same Dutch rescue ship.

One carried soldiers trying to warn its commander. The other carried mutineers hoping to seize the ship. From the deck, both looked like castaways.

The answer begins months earlier, before anyone had set foot on those islands.

In late 1628, a new Dutch trading ship called Batavia left the Netherlands for the East Indies.

She carried sailors, soldiers, civilian passengers, and chests of silver for the Dutch East India Company.

The destination was a colonial city also named Batavia, in what is now Indonesia.

But the danger was already aboard before the ship ever struck a reef.

The ship had a senior commander, Francisco Pelsaert, and a skipper named Ariaen Jacobsz.

They distrusted each other. Meanwhile, another officer, Jeronimus Cornelisz, became close to the skipper.

Historical accounts describe a plot to take the ship and its valuable cargo.

Before that plot could be carried out, the ocean changed the story.

The Batavia sailed east with no reliable way to measure how far east she had travelled.

Navigators had to judge their position from courses, speed, time, and imperfect observations.

Shortly before dawn on June fourth, sixteen twenty-nine, the hull struck Morning Reef.

The ship stopped. Waves broke around her. Below deck, people struggled to understand what had happened.

The reef lay among the Houtman Abrolhos islands, far off Western Australia's coast.

Those islands offered land. They did not offer a reliable supply of drinking water.

As daylight came, boats carried people away from the wreck to a small island.

That island is now called Beacon Island. It soon became the centre of a much worse disaster.

Others remained aboard the damaged ship, hoping to get more people or supplies ashore.

For the survivors, the first question was simple: where could they find fresh water?

Pelsaert and the skipper searched nearby islands and the coast. They failed to find enough.

Then Pelsaert made a decision: take the longboat all the way to the Dutch settlement on Java.

It was roughly three thousand kilometres away. The journey took thirty-three days.

The commander survived the crossing and arranged another ship, the Sardam, to return for the others.

But the stranded passengers did not know how long rescue would take, or whether it would come.

A man named Jeronimus Cornelisz had stayed behind on the broken Batavia.

Only after the wreck began to break apart did he reach the island with the other survivors.

There, people needed someone to distribute what little food and shelter they had.

Cornelisz placed himself at the head of their council and filled it with his allies.

He took control of weapons, rafts, and the boats that allowed people to move between islands.

To anyone outside his circle, getting away was now much harder.

The surviving soldiers were a particular problem for Cornelisz: they knew how to act together.

He sent twenty-two of them to another island, claiming they should search for water.

Before leaving them there, his men removed their weapons.

The soldiers, led by Wiebbe Hayes, were stranded on West Wallabi Island, separated from the main camp.

And this is where Cornelisz's plan met its first unexpected obstacle.

The soldiers found fresh water. They also found ways to feed themselves.

They were supposed to be out of the way. Instead, they now had a chance to last.

Back on Beacon Island, Cornelisz's followers began killing other survivors.

Some were sent to nearby islands and never came back. Others disappeared from the camp.

People who had escaped a shipwreck were now threatened by the people controlling their food and boats.

The violence continued through July and August. It was not one sudden clash or a single bad night.

Museum accounts put the number murdered at more than one hundred.

A few people managed to escape to West Wallabi and tell Hayes what was happening.

That warning transformed the soldiers' position. They were no longer simply waiting to be rescued.

They knew that if Cornelisz reached a rescue ship first, the killings might be hidden.

So Hayes's men built makeshift defences from coral blocks and pieces washed ashore.

They had no firearms. Their experience as soldiers would have to make up the difference.

Cornelisz tried to bring the island under his control. His first attack failed.

A second attempt failed as well. The men he had abandoned were still refusing to surrender.

By now the balance had changed. The armed group held Beacon Island; the defenders held the high island.

On a later attempt to take West Wallabi, Cornelisz came within the defenders' reach.

Hayes's soldiers captured him. His leadership of the island killings was over.

But capturing the leader did not end the danger. His followers still had weapons and boats.

Wouter Loos took command of the remaining mutineers and prepared another attack.

This time, the attackers brought muskets. The defenders had no equal answer at that distance.

Three of Hayes's men were killed. The island defence was suddenly in serious trouble.

Then, on September seventeenth, a sail appeared beyond the reef.

Pelsaert had finally returned aboard the Sardam. His voyage back had taken many weeks.

From the approaching ship, the islands were just low shapes with scattered signs of life.

The commander hoped to find castaways. He had no way to see who controlled the boats.

Loos's men wanted the newcomers to hear their story first. Hayes's men needed to warn them.

Both sides put small boats into the water and began rowing toward the rescue party.

There was no racecourse, no starting gun, no prize. There was one chance to be believed.

A boat from West Wallabi reached Pelsaert first. Wiebbe Hayes was among its men.

He warned the commander about the murders, the prisoners, and the plan to seize the rescue ship.

That warning changed the reception awaiting the other boat.

The men sent by Loos were arrested. The remaining mutineers on the islands soon surrendered.

Only then could Pelsaert begin to understand the scale of what had happened during his absence.

The shipwreck had been only the beginning. Most deaths came after people had reached land.

The prisoners were questioned and tried. The records also describe torture used to obtain confessions.

Cornelisz and six accomplices were executed on the islands. Others faced punishment after the voyage.

Two young mutineers were left on the Australian mainland. Their later fate is unknown.

Surviving passengers were taken aboard. Hayes was later rewarded for resisting the mutineers.

The number of people murdered is recorded as more than one hundred. It was a catastrophe within a catastrophe.

And the story did not disappear with the last boat. Pelsaert's records preserved its terrible outline.

Centuries later, divers located the wreck. Archaeologists studied the camps and the remains of stone shelters.

The survivors' stone defences left marks that can still be studied on West Wallabi Island.

Four centuries later, we remember the shipwreck. But the turning point was made by people left behind.

The men sent away without weapons survived, held the island, and reached the rescuers first.
