How did a Dutch shipwreck become a massacre? And why would two small boats race to reach the same rescue ship?

In June 1629, the trading ship Batavia struck a reef off Western Australia. Survivors escaped to low islands, but there was no reliable drinking water. Their commander, Francisco Pelsaert, left in a small boat to seek help on Java.

While he was gone, Jeronimus Cornelisz seized control of the stranded camp. His supporters held the weapons and boats. To get the soldiers out of the way, he sent twenty-two of them, led by Wiebbe Hayes, to another island without their weapons.

Cornelisz had made a mistake. Hayes's men found fresh water. When escapees brought news of murders in the main camp, the soldiers built makeshift defences from coral and wreckage. They repelled two attacks and eventually captured Cornelisz.

But his arrest did not stop the mutineers. Wouter Loos led another assault with muskets. Three defenders were killed.

Then a sail appeared: Pelsaert had returned aboard the Sardam.

Loos's men rowed toward the ship, hoping to reach its commander first. Hayes's men launched their own boat with a warning. To the rescuers, both groups looked like survivors.

Hayes reached Pelsaert first. The commander learned about the killings and the plan to seize his ship. The other boat's men were arrested, and the remaining mutineers surrendered.

More than a hundred people had been murdered after surviving the wreck. Seven leading offenders were executed on the islands. Two others were later marooned; their fate is unknown.

The decisive act was not another battle. It was getting the truth to the rescue ship before the killers did.
