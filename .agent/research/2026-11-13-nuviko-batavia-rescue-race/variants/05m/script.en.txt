How could a shipwreck turn into months of murder? How did twenty-two disarmed soldiers resist the men who controlled every boat? And why did the survivors' fate come down to a race across the water?

In June 1629, the Dutch trading ship Batavia struck Morning Reef off the coast of Western Australia. The passengers escaped to small islands in the Houtman Abrolhos. They had reached land, but there was no dependable supply of fresh water.

Commander Francisco Pelsaert searched for water and then took a longboat toward the Dutch settlement on Java. That journey took thirty-three days. He arranged a rescue ship, the Sardam. Nobody left on the islands knew when he would return.

The Batavia had been carrying valuable silver. Historical accounts also describe a plot involving skipper Ariaen Jacobsz and an officer named Jeronimus Cornelisz to seize the ship. The wreck happened before that plan could succeed.

Cornelisz survived the breakup of the wreck and reached the main camp on Beacon Island. He took charge of its council and filled the group with loyal men. His supporters controlled the weapons and the boats between islands.

Now the shipwreck became something else. People could not simply leave the camp. Cornelisz singled out a group of twenty-two soldiers under Wiebbe Hayes. He ordered them to a separate island, supposedly to search for water, and had their weapons taken away.

He expected the soldiers to be stranded. Instead, Hayes's men found fresh water and food on West Wallabi Island. That discovery changed how long they could hold out.

Back on Beacon Island, Cornelisz's followers began killing other survivors. The violence continued for weeks. More than one hundred people were murdered, according to historical accounts. A few escaped across the water to warn Hayes.

The soldiers now knew they faced more than starvation. The men controlling the main camp had weapons, boats, and a powerful reason to silence witnesses.

Hayes's group built rough defences with coral blocks and washed-up debris. They had no firearms. Cornelisz attacked West Wallabi twice and failed both times. On a later attempt he was captured.

For a moment, that might seem to end the story. But the mutineers still controlled their boats. Wouter Loos took over, brought muskets on another attack, and killed three of Hayes's men.

Just as the defenders were under renewed pressure, a ship appeared beyond the reef.

It was the Sardam. Pelsaert had found the islands after weeks of searching. But from his approaching vessel he could not know that the people waiting to be rescued had split into two hostile groups.

Loos's men launched a boat. They wanted to reach the commander before their crimes were reported, and the mutineers had plans to seize the rescue vessel itself.

Hayes's men launched another. They had to make the commander believe their warning before the other side could tell its own story.

The boats rowed toward the same ship. This was not a sporting race: the first account Pelsaert heard would shape his next decision.

Hayes reached him first.

He told Pelsaert about the killings, the remaining prisoners, and the danger to the Sardam. The men arriving from Loos's side were arrested. A party then moved against the mutineers on Beacon Island, who surrendered.

The rescue could finally proceed. The prisoners faced interrogations and punishments, including torture recorded in contemporary accounts. Cornelisz and six accomplices were executed on the islands. Two other mutineers were abandoned on the Australian mainland, where their later fate is unknown.

Hayes's soldiers had been sent away without weapons. They found water, defended an island, and then got the warning through. Their survival made it possible to stop the killers before the rescue ship itself was taken.

Today, archaeological traces of their stone shelters remain on West Wallabi. The story is remembered for a shipwreck, but the decisive moment came when one boat reached the rescuers before the other.
