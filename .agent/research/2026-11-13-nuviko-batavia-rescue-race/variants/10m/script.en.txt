How could more than a hundred people be murdered after surviving the wreck of a trading ship?

How did twenty-two disarmed soldiers stop the killers from taking their island?

And why did it matter which of two boats reached a rescue vessel first?

In late 1628, a new Dutch trading ship called Batavia left the Netherlands for the East Indies.

She carried sailors, soldiers, civilian passengers, and chests of silver for the Dutch East India Company.

The destination was a colonial city also named Batavia, in what is now Indonesia.

But the danger was already aboard before the ship ever struck a reef.

The ship had a senior commander, Francisco Pelsaert, and a skipper named Ariaen Jacobsz.

Their dispute had already become public at the Cape of Good Hope, where Pelsaert reprimanded Jacobsz for leaving his duties and drinking. During the ocean crossing, Pelsaert fell ill. Jacobsz separated the Batavia from the rest of its fleet during a storm.

The ship's undermerchant, Jeronimus Cornelisz, joined Jacobsz in plotting to take the vessel and its silver.

On May fourteenth, men connected to the plot assaulted a woman passenger named Lucretia van der Mijlen. Historical accounts suggest the attackers may have hoped Pelsaert would punish the crew collectively and stir up resentment; the purpose of the assault is not certain. Pelsaert did not order such a collective punishment.

The conspirators had still not seized the ship when another danger appeared ahead.

The faster route across the Indian Ocean used powerful westerly winds. But without a reliable way to measure longitude, the ship's officers could not know exactly when to turn north to avoid the Australian coast.

On the night of June third into June fourth, a pale line appeared on the water. According to one historical account, Jacobsz took the breakers for reflected moonlight. Shortly before dawn on June fourth, sixteen twenty-nine, Batavia struck Morning Reef.

The crew tried and failed to free the hull from the rocks. They began getting people off the wreck instead.

The reef lay among the Houtman Abrolhos islands, far off Western Australia's coast.

Those islands offered land. They did not offer a reliable supply of drinking water.

As daylight came, boats carried people away from the wreck to a small island.

That island is now called Beacon Island. It soon became the centre of a much worse disaster.

Others remained aboard the damaged ship, hoping to get more people or supplies ashore.

For the survivors, the first question was simple: where could they find fresh water?

Pelsaert, Jacobsz, and a boatload of officers searched the islands and the mainland for water. They found no dependable source. Instead of returning to the main camp, the party continued toward the Dutch settlement on Java, roughly three thousand kilometres away.

The crossing took thirty-three days. Pelsaert secured a rescue vessel, the Sardam, but locating the wreck on the return voyage would take many more weeks.

On Beacon Island, people woke to find the senior officers gone. They had no way to know when another ship might come.

A man named Jeronimus Cornelisz had stayed behind on the broken Batavia.

Only after the wreck began to break apart did he reach the island with the other survivors.

Rain and wreckage had supplied some water, food, and canvas for shelter. Cornelisz first took charge of rationing those supplies and chaired the survivors' council.

Then he replaced council members with his own followers. They gathered the weapons and took control of the rafts and boats. The scattered groups of castaways now depended on Cornelisz's men for passage between islands.

The best-organised resistance would have come from twenty-two surviving soldiers under Wiebbe Hayes. Cornelisz sent them eight kilometres across the water to West Wallabi, supposedly to look for fresh water.

His followers confiscated their weapons before leaving them there. But sending the soldiers out of the way also put them beyond his immediate control.

Hayes's men found fresh water and small wallabies they could hunt for food. Their island, chosen as a place to abandon them, gave them a way to survive without the supplies controlled by Cornelisz.

Back on Beacon Island, Cornelisz's followers began killing other survivors.

Cornelisz had also sent groups to other islands. Killings began in secrecy and then spread across the camps, including an attack on people stranded on another island.

The men who controlled food, weapons, and boats could decide who was able to leave. The killing continued for weeks. More than one hundred people were murdered, though historical counts differ.

Some escaped across the water on makeshift rafts and reached Hayes with a warning.

The soldiers now faced two tasks: defend the people who had escaped and stay alive long enough to warn the next rescue ship. Cornelisz's faction intended to seize that ship, too.

So Hayes's men built makeshift defences from coral blocks and pieces washed ashore.

They had no firearms. Their experience as soldiers would have to make up the difference.

Cornelisz tried to bring the island under his control. His first attack failed.

A second attempt failed as well. The men he had abandoned were still refusing to surrender.

By now the balance had changed. The armed group held Beacon Island; the defenders held the high island.

On a later attempt to take West Wallabi, Cornelisz came within the defenders' reach.

Hayes's soldiers captured him. His leadership of the island killings was over.

But capturing the leader did not end the danger. His followers still had weapons and boats.

Wouter Loos took command of the remaining mutineers. He brought two muskets that Cornelisz had kept in reserve for use against a rescue vessel.

Now those weapons were turned against the defenders. Three of Hayes's men were killed. The men behind the coral walls could not match the firepower.

Then, on September seventeenth, a sail appeared beyond the reef. The Sardam had found the islands after a difficult return search.

Pelsaert was coming back to a group he had left without a dependable water source. He did not yet know that the castaways had split into victims, defenders, and killers.

Loos's men launched one boat. Hayes's men launched another. Both headed for the same commander.

One side hoped to get aboard before the murders were reported. The other knew the ship itself might be taken. If Pelsaert heard the wrong account first, he could welcome armed mutineers as ordinary survivors.

A boat from West Wallabi reached Pelsaert first. Wiebbe Hayes was among its men.

He warned the commander about the murders, the prisoners, and the plan to seize the rescue ship.

That warning changed the reception awaiting the other boat.

The men sent by Loos were arrested. The remaining mutineers on the islands soon surrendered.

The Sardam had arrived to rescue people, but Pelsaert also had to investigate the crimes committed while he was away. He convened a court on the islands. Confessions were obtained amid torture. This was not anything like a modern criminal trial.

Cornelisz and six of his followers were executed. Others faced punishment later. Two men, Loos and the cabin boy Pelgrom, were marooned on the Australian mainland. Their later fate is unknown.

During the same expedition, Pelsaert's men salvaged valuable silver from the wreck. The cargo that had made the Batavia a target for mutiny was still being recovered while the surviving passengers prepared for the voyage onward.

Pelsaert's written account preserved the killings and the inquiry. Centuries later, remains found on Beacon Island and stone structures on West Wallabi helped researchers locate where people had died and where the soldiers resisted.

The twenty-two men sent away without firearms found water, sheltered people who escaped, and survived long enough to warn the Sardam. They could not undo the murders. But the killers did not get to tell their story first.
