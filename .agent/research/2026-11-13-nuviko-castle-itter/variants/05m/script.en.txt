Why would a German officer ask an American tank commander for help? Why were former French prime ministers still locked in an Austrian castle when the war in Europe was almost finished? And who was coming for them?

It was early May 1945. Germany's armies were collapsing, but the end of a war does not reach every prisoner at once. Castle Itter held French political detainees: former heads of government, senior officers and other figures whom the Nazi authorities had kept under guard. The people inside feared that SS troops would attack before they could be freed.

In a nearby town, German Major Josef Gangl was working with Austrian resistance fighters rather than fighting them. The prisoners needed help at the gate, not simply news that Americans were approaching.

The roads were not clear even before the rescue column set out. Lee had encountered armed German soldiers who had not yet surrendered. The battalion account says he warned them that a far larger American force was coming, claiming sixty tanks and a thousand infantrymen. Those were his words to the German troops, not the force available to defend Itter. The claim helped persuade men along his route to lay down their weapons.

Gangl went to American troops near Kufstein. Lieutenant Jack Lee listened and then did something unusual: he went with the German officer to inspect the castle for himself.

Lee found that the prisoners really were there. He returned to gather a relief party. It looked as though he might bring several tanks and supporting infantry. But that plan shrank on the road. Bridges, narrow approaches and the need to hold positions elsewhere prevented the full detachment from reaching Itter.

By late evening, Lee had arrived with a small American group, German defenders and just one Sherman tank. Inside the castle, some German guards had already put down their weapons and were preparing to leave. Lee reorganized them. Men who had worn opposing uniforms now had to watch the same walls. He parked the Sherman at the gate, the castle's critical entrance.

That night, an SS patrol tried to climb the wall. The defenders drove it off. During the following morning, one member of the castle guard fled in the direction of the SS positions. The battalion record does not establish that this caused the attack; it records the escape before the attackers appeared in force. The larger assault came soon afterward.

Lee saw guns being positioned beyond the castle and SS infantry moving through the woods. Then the bombardment started. The Sherman fired from the gate, but enemy shells disabled it. The attackers turned their guns toward the castle itself.

The defenders had lost their strongest weapon, while the buildings around them were being hit. French prisoners picked up German and American rifles and joined the defense. No one had planned this mixed force as an official alliance. Each turn had left those inside with fewer ways out.

The ammunition was running low. One prisoner, former French tennis champion Jean Borotra, made his way through the fighting to help bring reinforcements. His journey mattered because the garrison could not hold its position indefinitely.

Before relief arrived, Gangl was killed while trying to protect a prisoner. Other men defending the castle also died. The people Gangl had tried to save would live, but he would not see their rescue.

Then American infantry and tanks reached the castle. The SS assault ended, and the French prisoners came out alive.

The German and American armies had not suddenly become allies. Gangl had chosen to seek help, Lee had chosen to answer, and a handful of soldiers had held the gate long enough for the relief force to arrive. Gangl did not survive to see the people he helped save walk free.
