How could a German major and an American tank officer end up defending the same castle in the last days of World War Two? Why did they arrive with only one tank? And what happened when the men attacking the castle destroyed it?

In May 1945, the German military position was collapsing. American forces were moving through Austria, and surrender was close. But Castle Itter still held prominent French prisoners. They included former prime ministers Édouard Daladier and Paul Reynaud, senior military officers, and other political figures. These people had been arrested by the Nazi regime. They were not safe just because the front line was moving.

The castle was a prison, not a stronghold prepared for a major battle. Some of the detainees feared that SS troops might reach them before Allied soldiers could. For the people inside, the question was immediate: could anyone get through the mountain roads before that happened?

One of the men trying to answer it wore a German uniform. Major Josef Gangl was working with the Austrian resistance around Wörgl. Other German forces were still nearby, and armed SS units had not simply disappeared. Gangl could not protect the French prisoners with the announcement that the war was almost over. He needed soldiers who could reach them.

The American battalion's report gives this rescue a more complicated beginning than the familiar story of an officer asking for an alliance. It says Lee met a German major after American troops entered Kufstein. The major spoke about negotiating the surrender of German forces around Wörgl. Lee obtained permission to investigate, and traveled with him. They were not yet leading a rescue column to the castle.

The road into Wörgl was still dangerous. The American account records explosive charges on two bridges prepared for demolition. Lee had the charges removed as he passed. If those bridges were blown, tanks might never reach the town, much less the prisoners beyond it. He also saw German gun positions along the route. The war was ending, but the roads were not simply open.

In Wörgl, according to that report, a German colonel and general declined to surrender. Then a French civilian told Lee about political prisoners being held several miles away at Castle Itter. A trip that had begun with surrender talks had found a new purpose. Lee and the German major went to check the story themselves.

They found the French detainees and the castle guard. The prisoners were real; so was the danger that the approaching SS might arrive first. Lee promised to come back with help. Later accounts put Gangl's concern for the prisoners earlier in the story. This version follows the sequence recorded by the American battalion, without pretending the accounts agree on every step.

Lee returned to organize a detachment. He gathered several medium tanks with supporting infantry. On paper that could have provided a much stronger defense of the castle. But the routes were the problem. The bridges could not safely carry the entire armored group, narrow roads restricted movement, and soldiers were needed to hold other positions along the way.

There was another complication. German soldiers in the area had not all surrendered. The American battalion's record describes Lee persuading groups to put down their weapons by telling them overwhelming American forces were about to arrive. It records his claim of sixty tanks and a thousand infantrymen. That was the number he told the Germans, not the size of the force he actually took to Itter. The bluff helped get armed men out of his path, but it did not bring sixty tanks to the castle.

As the rescue column moved, much of its strength stayed behind. A single Sherman reached the gate with a small group of Americans and Germans. If the SS attacked in force, there would be no large armored reserve waiting just outside.

Inside Castle Itter, German guards had another plan: leave before the approaching SS troops came. According to the American unit account, some had already laid down their weapons. Lee reorganized them and put them back on watch with his own men. He positioned the Sherman at the main gate.

The arrangement was strange even by the standards of the war's final week. Americans and men in German uniforms were holding the same walls. The French prisoners they were protecting had been detained by the regime those uniforms represented. Individual soldiers were acting against the SS, not representing a new agreement between countries.

Lee's improvised force faced more than the possibility of an attack from outside. The guard was assembled in haste, and its members had different reasons for being there. They had little time to learn how to fight together or settle every uncertainty about the soldiers around them. That uncertainty soon became visible in the battalion's account.

At four in the morning, according to the American unit record, a four-man SS patrol tried to climb the castle wall. Machine-gun fire drove it off. The defenders had stopped a probe, not the attack they feared. Hours later, one member of the castle guard deserted toward the SS positions. The report records the desertion before the assault, but does not say it caused the assault.

Then something almost opposite happened. The battalion says eight more German soldiers surrendered to Lee at the castle that morning and subsequently fought on the defending side. One guard had walked away; other men who had laid down their weapons were now holding the walls. Lee's force was still small, but the line between former enemies and defenders was changing in both directions. These are the report's observations, not evidence that the German army as a whole had switched sides.

From the castle Lee could see three heavy guns being brought into position across the valley and SS infantry moving through the woods. The battalion recorded the guns as 88-millimeter weapons, roughly eight hundred yards away. The castle was becoming surrounded. Soon the guns opened fire.

The Sherman at the gate answered, but it could not move to a safer position without giving up the entrance it was guarding. The American report says the tank took six hits from the heavy guns. Once it was out of action, the attackers redirected their fire toward the castle. A tower was destroyed. The weapon that had protected the only gate was gone, while the building behind it was taking the shells.

That changed everything for the defenders. The tank had covered the entrance; now that entrance had lost its strongest weapon. The men inside still had rifles and machine guns, but enemy artillery was hitting the building. The walls offered cover; they could not stop the bombardment.

Some French prisoners joined the fighting with German and American weapons. These were not soldiers sent to Castle Itter as a unit. They had been held there as political detainees. With the tank disabled and SS troops close, they helped defend the place where they had been imprisoned.

One of them was Jean Borotra, a former French tennis champion. Multiple later historical accounts describe him getting out through the fighting to help direct American reinforcements to the castle. The garrison's ammunition was running low. Getting help to the right entrance mattered more than the number of flags on the walls.

Gangl was killed while trying to protect one of the French detainees. Men from the castle guard also died, as recorded by the American tank battalion. The officer who had gone to the Americans for help would never see the rescue completed.

The battalion's account says the heavy fighting continued into the afternoon. The attackers then appeared to fall back, with snipers still covering the castle. Infantry and medium tanks reached the position afterward. Lee handed the prisoners and the castle over to the arriving infantry commander, then returned to his own unit. The report does not describe a lasting American-German garrison; it describes a rescue completed and responsibility passed on.

The French prisoners were alive. Gangl, the major who had traveled with Lee through the contested roads, had been killed before they could leave. The battalion also recorded two castle guards dead. The familiar picture of a lone Sherman at a medieval gate is only the middle of the story. It began with an attempt to negotiate surrender, changed when news of the prisoners reached Lee, and ended with another American unit taking custody of people whose guards had nearly abandoned them.

The unusual uniforms matter because of the decisions behind them: men laid down weapons, some picked them up to defend the prisoners, one deserted, and others joined the defense. The prisoners survived that sequence. The German major who helped make it possible did not.
