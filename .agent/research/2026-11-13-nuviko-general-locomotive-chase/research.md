## Three competing briefs after fact/outcome check

### A — The chase, chosen
- **Audience:** English-speaking curious history/railway/strange-true-story viewers; no Civil War expertise required.
- **Exact opening:** “A train conductor stepped out from breakfast and saw his steam engine leaving without him. He ran after it.”
- **Stop the swipe:** a man physically chasing a departed train, clear absurd visual but real stakes.
- **Stay:** he changes from foot to handcar to steam engines; the raiders' attempts to destroy track and ignite a bridge fail; the pursuer deprives the escape engine of time to refuel.
- **Payoff:** the General stops out of steam, raiders captured, eight executed, mission fails; surviving locomotive now displayed yards from theft site. Important: the executed men are not comic relief.
- **Best objection:** the Civil War context and all names can slow cold viewers; must minimize exposition, distinguish raiders from conductor, and not hide the grave result.
- **One-sentence compression:** A train stolen during breakfast triggered an escalating pursuit by handcar and backward-running locomotive, ending when it ran out of steam, with grave consequences for the raiders.

### B — Palomares H-bomb, nearest challenger
- **Audience:** English-speaking disaster history / Cold War viewers.
- **Exact proposed opening:** “A hydrogen bomb fell into the Mediterranean, and for weeks nobody could find it.”
- **Stop:** an actual missing nuclear weapon, direct risk.
- **Stay:** three weapons recovered on land, a fisherman reports a descending parachute, Navy narrows the sea search and uses a deep-diving submersible.
- **Payoff:** the missing bomb recovered from the sea; search mechanism and fallout must be explained accurately.
- **Best objection:** visual promise of a threatened nuclear detonation is not justified; technical search could stall. WHOI eyewitness account and Brookings retrospect opened; independently interpreting risk and cleanup needs deeper work.
- **One sentence:** After a U.S. bomber collision scattered four nuclear bombs near Spain, a fisherman's sighting of a parachute helped locate the fourth in the Mediterranean.

### C — Slotin's screwdriver, other finalist
- **Audience:** English-speaking science/engineering hazard history viewers.
- **Exact proposed opening:** “A screwdriver slipped, and a physicist received a lethal dose of radiation.”
- **Stop:** everyday tool causing nuclear-lab emergency.
- **Stay:** explain plutonium assembly, hemispheres, how the reaction was halted and what followed.
- **Payoff:** Slotin died nine days later, strengthening the case against hands-on criticality demonstrations.
- **Best objection:** the essential event is already revealed immediately; aftermath is primarily explanation, not another material development; cannot stretch it to 60–90s.
- **One sentence:** A slipped screwdriver brought a plutonium experiment to criticality; Slotin separated the parts but died from the exposure.

**Decision:** A over B because it has observable successive choices and setbacks with an honest ending; not because of image ease, sources alone, or viral-score guesses. B has a striking object but would need a much longer risk/search explanation to earn the payoff. C is strong as a very short, factual explainer, less suited to this narrative Short. Remaining risk in A: the museum epilogue may feel like a reset into the loop; independent reader must assess it.
