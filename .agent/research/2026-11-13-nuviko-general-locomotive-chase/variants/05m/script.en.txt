How could a conductor catch a train stolen while he was eating breakfast? And why did the men driving it run out of time before they ran out of track?

In April 1862, during the American Civil War, a railway from Atlanta toward Chattanooga carried supplies through Confederate territory. Union forces wanted to interrupt that connection. A civilian scout named James Andrews proposed an audacious way to do it: steal a locomotive, head north, cut the telegraph wires, and burn bridges before Confederate troops could respond.

Andrews and his companions traveled south disguised as civilians. They boarded a regular train, then waited for a breakfast stop at Big Shanty, Georgia. The station had no telegraph office. That mattered: if they could take the engine here, the crew could not immediately send an electrical warning up the line.

On April 12, while the passengers and crew went to eat, the raiders uncoupled the coaches. They steamed away with the locomotive General, its tender, and three freight cars.

The conductor, William Fuller, was suddenly standing beside a railway without his train. He started running along the tracks with other railroad men.

For a little while, the thieves had exactly the advantage they wanted. They could tear up a section of rail, cut the wires, and leave the pursuers unable to ask the next station for help. But doing those things required them to stop. Every minute spent damaging the line gave Fuller another minute to catch up.

Fuller found a railway handcar and pressed on. Then he obtained a small locomotive called the Yonah. At Kingston he switched to another engine, the William R. Smith. When damaged track stopped that engine, the chase did not end. The railroad men went forward on foot again.

The raiders, meanwhile, had their own obstacle. They were using a railway that still had ordinary traffic. They had to wait for opposing trains to clear the single track, and they needed stops for fuel and water. The chase was no longer simply about which engine was faster.

Fuller eventually reached a southbound locomotive called the Texas. There was no time to turn it around. The crew detached its cars and drove it north in reverse, with the tender in front.

Andrews's men tried dropping freight cars behind the General. Near the Oostanaula River they set their remaining car alight, hoping it would ignite a wooden bridge. The bridge was damp from rain. It would not burn.

Now the Texas was close enough that the raiders could barely pause to gather wood and water. Their plan depended on stopping to destroy the line; escape depended on not stopping at all.

After a pursuit lasting about seven hours and eighty-seven miles, the General lost steam north of Ringgold, still short of Chattanooga. The raiders scattered into the countryside. They were captured.

The mission failed to cut the railway for long; the damaged track and telegraph lines were repaired. Its human cost was much greater. Eight raiders, including Andrews, were executed. Others survived imprisonment, escape, or prisoner exchanges. Six of the released soldiers later received the first Medals of Honor.

The original General still stands in a museum in Kennesaw, near the place where it was taken. The remarkable part of the chase is not that a man outran a steam train. It is that every delay the raiders needed for sabotage helped the man pursuing them find another way forward.
