How do you steal a train behind enemy lines without letting anyone warn the next station? And how can a conductor catch it after the thieves have torn up the track behind them? The answer turns a railway chase into a struggle over minutes, messages, and a few pieces of wood.

The story begins in Georgia in the spring of 1862. The American Civil War was underway, and the rail link between Atlanta and Chattanooga mattered to Confederate movement and supply. Union Brigadier General Ormsby Mitchel hoped to advance toward Chattanooga. Cutting the railway south of that city might make his task easier. But an army could not simply appear behind the Confederate lines and destroy every bridge.

James Andrews, a civilian working with Union forces, proposed a smaller operation. Men dressed as ordinary travelers would get deep into Confederate territory, board a northbound train, take its locomotive, and tear up the railway as they drove it back toward Union forces. They would cut telegraph wires and burn wooden bridges to keep repairs and reinforcements from catching up.

It was a plan that relied on speed. It also required frequent stops. That contradiction would become the story's central problem.

The volunteers traveled separately in civilian clothes. Some failed to reach the train. Others boarded with ordinary passengers at Marietta. They had to move through hostile territory while trying to look like people with an innocent reason to travel. The leader was not driving his own engine toward a friendly depot; he was riding on an enemy-operated railway and waiting for a particular moment to take control.

That moment came on April 12 at Big Shanty, now part of Kennesaw, Georgia. The northbound train stopped for breakfast. The locomotive was named the General. Its crew and the passengers headed for food near the station.

Andrews had chosen the location partly because Big Shanty had no telegraph office. In a world where the next station could pass a warning along the wires, this was a crucial gap. If someone stole the engine here, the railroad workers would first have to find a physical way to pursue it.

Andrews's men disconnected the passenger coaches, leaving them at the platform. The General pulled away with its tender and three empty freight cars. The raiders had an engine, a route north, and a head start. For the people eating breakfast, it looked like the train had simply departed without its passengers.

The conductor, William Fuller, did not have another locomotive standing ready. He and fellow railroad men set off on foot after the General. It sounds absurd: men running down the tracks behind a steam engine. Yet the thieves were not trying only to escape. At intervals they needed to stop, damage track, and tear down telegraph wires. Their mission itself meant that their lead could shrink.

Fuller's first useful discovery was a handcar. Railway workers could push this small vehicle along the rails, much faster than they could run for the whole distance. They took it north. When the opportunity came, Fuller obtained a small locomotive named the Yonah and continued the pursuit with steam power.

Meanwhile Andrews was learning that the railroad would not obey the neat timing of his plan. The line carried ordinary Confederate traffic. On a single track, a northbound engine could not ignore southbound trains. At Kingston, the raiders had to wait for other trains to pass. They could not explain that their real purpose was to destroy the very route they were traveling on. Time spent waiting was time that Fuller could use.

The chase passed through a chain of improvisations rather than one continuous race between two engines. Fuller changed from the Yonah to another locomotive, the William R. Smith. Later he reached rail that the raiders had damaged. The engine could go no further on that stretch. Once again, the pursuers were reduced to using their legs.

The obstacle was supposed to be a victory for Andrews. A missing piece of track could stop an engine. But it could not prevent a determined crew from going around the gap and looking for another locomotive beyond it. Fuller kept moving north.

The next engine he took over was the Texas. It had been heading south, toward the stolen General. There was no time to turn a whole locomotive around or arrange a leisurely train movement. Its cars were detached, and it raced north backward, with its tender leading. Now the chase had a visual absurdity of its own: a locomotive pursuing another locomotive in reverse.

The raiders tried to interrupt that pursuit. They damaged rails and let freight cars go behind them to delay anyone following. These were real obstacles, but none became the permanent barrier their mission required. The men on the Texas continued to find ways around them.

Andrews's most important target was not another train. It was a bridge. A burned bridge could stop the railway for much longer than a short broken section of rail, and it would affect Confederate transportation after the raiders had disappeared. Near Resaca, where the tracks crossed the Oostanaula River, the raiders tried setting their last freight car alight to ignite the wooden structure.

The bridge was wet from rain. The fire did not take. Instead of a severed connection, Andrews had a failed attempt and still more time lost. The railway remained open enough for the pursuit to go on.

The telegraph was another contest. The raiders had cut wires behind them to delay the spread of news, but the railroad network extended ahead of them too. Before the raiders severed a line near Dalton, Fuller managed to send warning toward Chattanooga. The men stealing the General could obstruct communication, but they could not make the entire region forget that a locomotive was coming.

And a steam engine needed more than an open track. It needed fuel for its fire and water for its boiler. Under normal circumstances, a crew could pause to replenish both. Andrews's party had to decide between stopping for those supplies and keeping ahead of the Texas. Every stop also meant another opportunity for Fuller to close the gap.

This is why the supposed advantage of being first on the tracks was so fragile. Andrews was trying to destroy the railway, run an engine, outrun pursuers, and reach his destination. Fuller had one immediate objective: find the stolen train and keep it from getting away. The raiders had to succeed at several things. Their pursuers only had to keep interfering with one of them.

The journey continued past Dalton and Tunnel Hill. But the General was losing the practical resource that had made the whole plan possible: steam. After roughly seven hours and eighty-seven miles, north of Ringgold and still short of Chattanooga, the engine could continue no farther.

Andrews's men abandoned the General and scattered into the countryside. The train chase ended there, but their ordeal did not. Confederate forces searched for the fugitives and captured the raiders, including men who had missed the train at the start. They had worn civilian clothing on a mission to sabotage transport in enemy territory; the consequences would be severe.

James Andrews was tried as a spy and executed in June 1862. Seven more members of the expedition were executed. Others were imprisoned. Some escaped, while others survived through prisoner exchanges. Those are separate fates, not one simple ending in which every man either won or lost together.

And the military objective? The cut wires and damaged rails were repaired quickly. The raid did not permanently disable the line or deliver the decisive advantage its planners had hoped for. The Union advance toward Chattanooga did not arrive in time to turn the railway seizure into a successful campaign.

There is another consequence that sounds almost contradictory. Six raiders who were later released in a prisoner exchange received the very first U.S. Army Medals of Honor in March 1863. Other participants received medals afterward. A failed mission could still be remembered as an extraordinary act of military daring, even while the people caught in it paid terrible costs. Andrews, a civilian, was not among the soldiers decorated.

The original General did not disappear with the war. It survives at the Southern Museum in Kennesaw, only a short distance from the station where the raiders took it. And another part of this story survives in a primary account written by participant William Pittenger, as well as contemporary reports describing Fuller's pursuit. These sources do not agree on every headcount or recollected detail, so there is no need to invent a precise conversation or a perfect second-by-second timetable.

Stand beside that preserved locomotive and the first question comes back into focus: how does a man on foot catch a stolen train? He doesn't outrun it. He finds a handcar, then another engine, and then another, while the men ahead lose time doing exactly what they came to do. The chase was won in the delays between bursts of speed.
