Rows of American tanks stood near the Rhine. Up close, they were made of rubber.

March 1945. The U.S. Ninth Army was preparing to cross the river into Germany.

Two real infantry divisions moved toward the crossing point after dark. Their old positions had to look occupied.

The secret unit had just eleven hundred men. They were meant to stand in for more than thirty thousand soldiers.

Artists inflated tanks and guns, then arranged them where distant observers could spot them.

Trucks played recordings of troops moving and bridges being built. Radio operators sent fake traffic reports.

Real engineers even drove bridge-building equipment around the decoy area, so it looked busy.

Meanwhile, the actual troops gathered at the crossing point, hiding their vehicles and guns.

On March 23, the two divisions crossed the Rhine and met little resistance.

Afterward, American troops captured a German military map. It placed one division roughly where the decoys stood. The other was missing entirely.

The decoys were only part of a much bigger operation, so they can't take all the credit. But the map showed the Germans were wrong about where the divisions were.

And if you'd looked where that map pointed, what would you have seen?
