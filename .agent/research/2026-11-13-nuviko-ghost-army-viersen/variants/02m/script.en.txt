In March 1945, an American truck driver stopped at what looked like his own division's headquarters. He used to serve in its military police platoon, but he didn't recognize any of the men there.

According to the unit history, he came away with a grim explanation: his old comrades must have suffered terrible losses. He had actually walked into a performance.

The American Ninth Army was preparing to cross the Rhine. Two infantry divisions, the 30th and 79th, had to move without giving away the real crossing. A secret unit of roughly eleven hundred men was assigned to impersonate them.

Their tanks were inflatable. Their radio messages described movements that weren't happening. Recorded vehicle sounds made the preparations seem much larger, and real engineers helped complete the picture with bridging equipment.

The men at the headquarters wore the insignia of the 30th Division. That was why the truck driver had stopped to ask for directions and look for his old friends. He had the right division and the wrong men.

American troops crossed the Rhine during the night of March 23 into 24. The unit reports describe comparatively light resistance, but that alone couldn't tell the impersonators what German intelligence had believed.

A captured German military map supplied a different kind of evidence. The 79th was marked near its false position. The 30th was missing entirely.

The map can't tell us how many lives the operation saved. It does show that the Germans had lost track of two real divisions. And the Germans weren't the only people who mistook the imitation for the real thing.
