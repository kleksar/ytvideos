A whole American division seemed to be waiting beside the Rhine. But one of its tanks could be lifted without an engine.

March 1945. The U.S. Ninth Army was preparing to cross the river into Germany. Two infantry divisions, the 30th and 79th, slipped away from their old positions after dark. If German observers noticed they had gone, the real crossing might lose its surprise.

So roughly eleven hundred men from a secret American unit took their place. They put inflatable tanks and guns in fields. Radio operators pretended the missing divisions were still sending messages. Loudspeakers played the sounds of trucks and bridge construction.

Even real engineers brought bridge-building equipment to the false assembly area. From the opposite bank, the preparations could look genuine. Close up, a rubber tank was just a prop.

While that noisy camp drew attention, the real divisions gathered at a different crossing sector, with their vehicles hidden. During the night of March 23 into 24, American forces crossed the Rhine. The two divisions met comparatively light resistance.

Then came a test nobody on the fake-tank crew could arrange. American soldiers recovered a German military map of the Allied positions.

The map placed the 79th Division roughly where the impersonators had set up. The 30th was missing altogether. German intelligence had tracked the army that was being performed, while losing sight of one that was real.

The deception was only one part of the Ninth Army's wider plan; that map cannot tell us how many lives it saved. It does show what the enemy thought was waiting beside the Rhine: an American division, complete with tanks. And one of those tanks could be lifted without an engine.
