How do you hide two infantry divisions in front of an enemy that is watching the river? And how could you tell afterward whether the disguise worked?

In March 1945, the American Ninth Army was preparing to cross the Rhine into Germany. The river was a major obstacle, and a crossing needed troops, artillery, engineers, boats and bridge equipment. Any visible concentration could warn the German defenders where the attack was coming.

The army chose one crossing sector, but two infantry divisions needed for it were still stationed elsewhere. The 30th and the 79th were ordered into the actual assembly area under cover of darkness. Their artillery, vehicles and engineer facilities had to disappear from view. The problem was what to leave behind.

That job went to the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, a secret formation of about eleven hundred Americans. It did not have the manpower of two divisions. It had artists, camouflage specialists, radio operators and sound crews who could make a small group appear much larger.

On March 18 the real divisions began moving toward the chosen sector. At the same time the impostors prepared a different one. A field that had been quiet acquired rows of tanks and artillery pieces. But the tanks had no engines, and the guns could be inflated. The crew arranged them where distant observation would make them look like real military equipment.

A tank in an empty field would not be enough. An army also needs traffic. Radio operators built fake communication networks and reported movements that were not happening. Sound trucks played recorded vehicles and the noises of bridging work. These were different kinds of evidence, aimed at someone listening, watching or intercepting signals from the other side.

The bigger Ninth Army joined the performance. In the decoy sector, genuine engineers moved bridging equipment around. Real infantry helped fill out the illusion, and other units increased activity. In the actual crossing sector, the newly arrived divisions stayed concealed. The aim was to give German intelligence a plausible answer to a very specific question: where are the Americans preparing to cross?

But a rubber tank convincing from the air could give the game away to someone walking past it. According to the unit's operational chronicle, men wearing the 30th Division's insignia directed traffic and guarded an area around a small lake. In the other decoy division's sector, military police ordered civilians out and posted signs marking a restricted zone. Near the lake, families were also displaced from homes beside the staged bridge works. Keeping people away from the dummies was a real security duty for the soldiers playing impostors, and a real disruption for those who lived there. One close inspection could undo days of carefully planted clues.

The crucial moment came on the night of March 23 into 24. American troops began crossing the Rhine in the real sector. Accounts of the 30th and 79th describe comparatively light and confused resistance. The deception team had finished its performance, but that alone did not prove what the enemy believed.

Then American soldiers captured a German order-of-battle map, a document showing where German intelligence thought Allied units stood. The 79th Infantry Division appeared roughly in the area where the 23rd had impersonated it. The real 30th had disappeared from the picture.

That is the turn in this story. The fake guns were visible, but the map was evidence of the mistake: at least part of the German picture tracked the false army while missing the real movement. The 23rd was one element of a much larger plan, so the map does not prove how many casualties it prevented, or that inflatable tanks alone caused the weak resistance.

What it does prove is unsettling enough. For several critical days, one army moved toward the Rhine while another army existed in the enemy's reports. Only one of them could actually cross the river.
