How could a secret American unit make two infantry divisions appear to be somewhere they were not? Why did even soldiers from one of those divisions believe the imitation? And what did a captured German map reveal about the result?

In March 1945, the American Ninth Army had reached the Rhine, the river standing between its troops and the next advance into Germany. Crossing it meant bringing infantry, artillery, engineers and bridge equipment together. You could hide a patrol. Hiding the preparations of multiple divisions was a different problem. A buildup might be photographed, heard or picked up through radio traffic before the first boat touched the water.

The Ninth Army planned a crossing in the sector assigned to its XVI Corps. Two infantry divisions, the 30th and 79th, would reinforce that attack. But they had been stationed in other sectors. Moving them was only half the job. If their old areas suddenly went quiet, German intelligence could follow the missing pieces toward the real crossing.

The deception plan therefore had two sides. In one area, the real divisions would arrive under strict concealment. Elsewhere, a substitute force would make it seem as though those divisions were still preparing to cross at a different point.

The substitute was the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops, a secret American unit of about eleven hundred people. The men included camouflage artists, engineers, signal specialists and sound crews. Instead of matching a real division soldier for soldier, they made the things an enemy might notice: vehicles, headquarters, radio traffic and the sounds of movement.

The operation was called Viersen, after a town in the lower Rhine region. From March 18, the 30th and 79th began moving to the real assembly area at night. The official unit history describes a strict blackout. Vehicles, guns and engineer parks had to be camouflaged. Artillery carried on with fire patterns intended to avoid revealing the new concentration. On paper, the divisions were making a quiet journey that German observers were not supposed to detect.

Meanwhile, the fake divisions took shape. The 23rd displayed the 30th and 79th in another army sector. Each imagined division gained close to a hundred extra rubber vehicles in the official history. There were inflatable tanks and guns, and even dummy light aircraft. Aerial photographs of the display could resemble the organization of a real military camp.

Yet an overhead photograph was only one test. Anyone who walked right up to a rubber tank would see an empty shell. The deception therefore depended on controlling the setting as carefully as the props. Military police and restricted areas made the fake positions look like serious installations. Roads, headquarters and the behavior of the people around the equipment became part of the scene.

A real army also makes noise. Sound crews brought recordings of vehicles moving and bridges being assembled. Radio operators used false networks and traffic reports to suggest heavy movement near the false crossing. They were not merely telling the enemy that a division was present; they were presenting sounds and signals that an enemy observer might recognize as a division at work.

And some of the busiest people in the deception were not impostors at all. The larger Ninth Army assigned real infantry to help fill out the picture. In the false sector, genuine engineers established parks and paraded bridge-building equipment. Artillery and patrol activity increased. Reconnaissance over the decoy sector could be made to look like attention paid to an important operation.

Why risk using real resources in a false plan? Because a few rubber tanks beside a quiet river would be a weak clue. Engineers carrying bridging equipment, radio networks indicating movement and artillery activity in the same area could reinforce one another. It was an entire set of misleading observations, not just a row of inflatable vehicles.

The men staging the deception had real duties as well. The 406th unit history describes soldiers assigned to police the simulated positions. They even investigated a report of German soldiers in a house nearby. The search turned up no soldiers, and the account did not establish that they had actually been there. The fake camp was still in a real war.

Those duties also had consequences outside the staged combat picture. The detailed unit history records civilians being ordered out of restricted areas so that no one could inspect the dummy equipment at close range. The operation was clever, but it was still a wartime military deception carried out amid people who lived there. That fact belongs beside the familiar photographs of smiling men holding up lightweight tanks.

Then the unit discovered that its work could confuse its own side. The 406th chronicle describes infantrymen assigned to guard the area who found a field full of unfamiliar equipment where there had been none. They soon learned that many of the vehicles were dummies, but the men kept debating whether more distant ones were genuine. Even nearby soldiers couldn't reliably tell them apart.

A truck driver from the real 30th Division stopped at the imitation headquarters to ask directions and visit his former military police comrades. He asked the man on duty about people he knew. The policeman didn't know them, and the driver recognized none of the other men outside. According to the account, he told his companion that the old platoon must have suffered terrible losses. The apparent headquarters had given him a plausible but completely wrong explanation for the absence of his friends.

Another visitor, a medical major from the actual 30th, had just been to its real headquarters when he came across signs for another one. He stopped to investigate. The chronicle records that he explained the contradiction to his driver by assuming the division had split its headquarters functions between the two buildings. He left accepting the imitation as genuine. Neither anecdote establishes what German intelligence knew, but both show how convincingly the soldiers had assumed a familiar identity.

The Rhine crossing began during the night of March 23 into 24. The real American forces attacked in the sector for which they had been preparing. Accounts from the 30th and 79th describe the defenders there as surprised and resistance as comparatively weak or disorganized. That is an outcome worth noting, but it is not a controlled experiment. No one can replay the battle without the fake tanks and count a difference in casualties.

The Ninth Army's after-action history also makes clear that the Ghost Army was only one contributor to the operation. The reports from the crossing were encouraging, but they were written by the army that had just carried out the plan. The strongest evidence would have to come from the other side.

The intelligence section of the 79th Division captured a German military map showing the positions the enemy attributed to Allied units before the attack. Here was a record of the German picture of the battlefield rather than another American impression of the deception.

The 79th Division was shown approximately where the 23rd had portrayed it in the decoy area. The 30th Division was not shown at all. In reality, both had been transferred to the genuine crossing sector. The enemy map had followed the counterfeit presence of one unit and had lost track of the other.

The false headquarters had persuaded an American driver that his friends were gone. The map revealed a related mistake on a much larger scale: the German intelligence picture had lost the actual locations of both divisions. It cannot establish which part of the deception caused that mistake or how many lives the operation saved.

The Ninth Army commander, William Simpson, commended the unit afterward. Viersen had been its final deception mission of the war. Recognition beyond the army came much later. On March 21, 2024, Congress presented a Congressional Gold Medal honoring the 23rd and another wartime deception unit. Three surviving veterans, Bernie Bluestein, John Christman and Seymour Nussenbaum, attended the ceremony at the Capitol.

The photographs of the inflatable tanks made the unit famous. But the small human encounter at the headquarters and the mistake recorded on the captured map tell us something the photographs cannot: the illusion extended beyond the props. It could change what people believed they were seeing.
