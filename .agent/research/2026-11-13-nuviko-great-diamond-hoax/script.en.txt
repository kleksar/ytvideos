Two prospectors carried a bag of real diamonds into a San Francisco office. The mine they were selling didn't exist.

Philip Arnold and John Slack claimed they'd found the stones somewhere out West. They kept the location secret.

Investors were interested anyway. They sent samples to New York, where Charles Tiffany valued them at $150,000.

But the investors wanted to see the mine with their own eyes.

Arnold bought more uncut gemstones in London and planted them across a barren patch of Wyoming.

Then he led mining engineer Henry Janin on a long trip to the site.

Janin and the others found diamonds almost immediately. There were rubies and sapphires too.

The engineer endorsed the field, and the prospectors collected their money.

Government geologist Clarence King had already surveyed that part of the West. So he decided to investigate.

King found diamonds too. But there were about twelve rubies for every diamond.

Some stones lay in disturbed anthills. Untouched anthills had none.

King's team dug a trench ten feet deep. There were no diamonds underneath.

Someone had planted real gemstones on the surface.

King warned the investors. The mining company was dissolved.

Arnold and Slack had already left with the money.

And they hadn't even needed to fake the very first piece of evidence.
