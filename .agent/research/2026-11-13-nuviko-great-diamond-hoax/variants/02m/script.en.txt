Two prospectors showed a San Francisco businessman a bag of genuine diamonds. They claimed they knew where he could find more, but they wouldn't tell him where.

He told a banker. Soon a group of investors was trying to buy a share of the discovery.

The men brought them more gems. A sample went to New York, where the jeweler Charles Tiffany valued it at $150,000. The investors still wanted someone to inspect the land.

In June 1872, a mining engineer named Henry Janin went out to Wyoming with the prospectors. His party began finding diamonds within minutes. Janin reported favorably on the field. But the gems had been bought and planted there.

Months later, a government survey team heard about the discovery. Geologist Clarence King wanted to know how his men could have missed it.

His team found precious stones too. The next day, they noticed that the rubies in anthills appeared only where the ground had been disturbed.

They dug into the earth to test whether the gems belonged there. A trench ten feet deep yielded no diamonds.

King returned to San Francisco with his findings. The company stopped a planned share sale, sent another party to inspect the ground and then voted to dissolve.

The prospectors had already been paid. The buyers had verified the stones without verifying where they came from.

What had persuaded the investors in the first place?
