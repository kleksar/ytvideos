How could two men sell a diamond mine that did not exist? Why did a jeweler and a mining engineer help convince the buyers? And what did a few anthills reveal that both experts had missed?

In the American West of the 1870s, people had already made fortunes from gold and silver. Diamonds had also been discovered in South Africa. A new diamond field in America sounded unlikely, but not impossible.

Then Philip Arnold and John Slack walked into businessman George Roberts's San Francisco office with a pouch of real rough gemstones. They would say only that the stones came from a secret location somewhere in the West.

Roberts told others. Banker William Ralston and several investors became interested. Arnold and Slack kept the location to themselves, which meant the investors could neither inspect the supposed mine nor easily walk away from what might be a fortune.

The businessmen tried checking the evidence they did have. They sent a sample to New York. Charles Tiffany, the jeweler, valued that sample at $150,000. His appraisal was much too high, but the stones themselves were real.

This was the first trap. Testing the diamonds could establish that they were diamonds. It could not establish where anyone had found them.

The investors still insisted on seeing the ground. So Arnold and Slack went to London and bought more rough gems. Instead of mining the new stones, the men scattered purchased diamonds and colored gemstones over a barren patch near the Wyoming-Colorado border.

Now a second test was ready to fail.

In June 1872, the investors sent mining engineer Henry Janin to inspect the site. The trip took the party far from San Francisco. Once they reached the remote ground, they began finding stones almost immediately.

Janin saw diamonds, rubies and sapphires lying where miners might expect a rich discovery. He became enthusiastic enough to mark claims on thousands of acres, even though the planted area was tiny by comparison.

The field had passed an inspection. The investors began paying the prospectors and building a company around the claim.

Months later, Janin happened to meet men from a federal geological survey on a train. Their leader was Clarence King. His team had already mapped much of this region. If there really was a rich diamond field there, why had their survey missed it?

King set out to find the site. After a long, cold journey, his men spotted the claim notices and started searching the ground. They too found real gems. For a moment even the geologists were drawn in.

Then the pattern stopped making sense.

Where King found a diamond, he often found a dozen rubies as well. The stones seemed to turn up in ground that had been disturbed. In some anthills there were rubies beside breaks in the crust. Nearby untouched anthills held none.

The team dug a trench about ten feet deep. If the gems belonged to a deposit beneath the surface, digging should help show it. But the soil below contained no diamonds.

One jeweler had examined the stones. One engineer had examined the field. Neither test had established that the stones came from the earth they were lying on.

King hurried back to San Francisco and presented the results. The directors halted a planned sale of shares. After a second inspection, they agreed to dissolve the company. Newspapers exposed the fraud in November 1872.

Arnold and Slack were already gone. Arnold later faced legal action and settled with one investor; the money trail was not simply erased by the exposure.

The decisive evidence had not been hidden in a jewel. It was in the ordinary dirt around the jewels. The diamonds were genuine. The mine was not.
