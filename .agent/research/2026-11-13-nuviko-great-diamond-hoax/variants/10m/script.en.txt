What would convince you to invest in a diamond mine you had never seen? A bag of real stones? A valuation from a famous jeweler? Or a mining engineer who went to the site and picked up diamonds himself?

In 1872, a group of wealthy investors had all three. There was just one problem: the mine did not exist. To see how that happened, follow what each man actually checked. The final test would concern the dirt, not the jewels.

The American West was full of claims about sudden wealth. Gold and silver had already brought prospectors and bankers into remote territory. News of diamonds in South Africa made another discovery sound possible. A diamond field in the United States could be an enormous business.

Philip Arnold knew the mining trade. He had worked for a company that supplied diamond-tipped drills. With John Slack, his cousin and partner, he visited businessman George Roberts in San Francisco. They carried a small bag containing real rough diamonds and other gemstones.

The two men said the stones came from a rich deposit somewhere in the West. They would not give Roberts the location. He couldn't inspect the ground, but he could see the diamonds. Soon he was telling other businessmen, including banker William Ralston.

The investors had a difficult choice. Refuse a deal they could not verify, or risk losing it to somebody else. Arnold and Slack guarded the supposed secret, while the buyers pressed for a share.

There was a sensible first step: examine the stones. The prospectors supplied a larger collection, and a sample went to New York. Charles Tiffany assessed it at $150,000. His valuation turned out to be far too generous. But the stones themselves were genuine.

That distinction mattered. An appraisal of diamonds is an appraisal of diamonds. It cannot show whether they came from a Wyoming hillside, a shop in London, or someone's pocket.

Arnold and Slack had already been shopping abroad. In London, using other names, they bought rough diamonds and colored gems from a merchant. Some could be displayed to the investors. Others were destined for a patch of ground that had never produced a diamond.

The buyers still wanted a survey of the supposed mine. They chose Henry Janin, an experienced mining engineer, to travel there. This was a stronger test. A pouch could be assembled anywhere. Surely finding stones on the ground itself would establish the claim.

Arnold and Slack prepared a barren site near the Wyoming–Colorado border. They scattered purchased gemstones over a small area. Miners called this trick salting a claim: putting valuable material where a visitor expects to find it naturally.

In June 1872, Janin and several investors traveled to Wyoming. Arnold led them on a long, winding journey by horse. The site remained hard for the visitors to locate on their own. When they finally reached the ground on June 4, the group started looking immediately.

Stones appeared within minutes. First one visitor found something glittering; then more diamonds, rubies and sapphires turned up. To the men who had paid for the expedition, it looked as though the earth itself had confirmed the story.

Janin was impressed. He marked out claims covering thousands of acres, although the area actually salted with gems was only a small fraction of that land. He earned a fee for his inspection and received an opportunity to buy company shares.

That financial interest deserves noting, but it is not proof that Janin knowingly helped the fraud. The engineer had visited a real place and examined real stones. What he had not established was that the surrounding earth naturally held them.

The businessmen paid Arnold and Slack and started building a company around the field. They prepared to sell shares to a wider group of investors. A story that began with a mysterious pouch was becoming a public investment scheme.

Months later, on a train in October, Janin encountered members of a federal geological survey. They worked for Clarence King. His team had spent years mapping a vast strip of the West, including the region where the rumored diamond field was thought to lie.

This raised a question the investors had not asked. If such a valuable deposit existed in the area the surveyors had covered, how had those surveyors missed it?

King did not declare the mine fraudulent from a railway carriage. He sent colleagues ahead and followed them to Wyoming. After gathering supplies, five men made a cold, difficult journey of roughly 150 miles toward the suspected site.

They found notices marking Janin's claims. Nearby was a bare patch of iron-stained rock. The men dismounted, knelt on the ground and searched. Geologist Samuel Emmons found a small ruby. Other gems followed.

For one evening, even King's group gathered stones. Their hands were cold, the little finds were real, and the promise of an extraordinary mineral discovery still seemed possible. These were not men who had arrived to prove a conclusion at any cost.

Then they started comparing where the stones lay.

A diamond was often accompanied by roughly a dozen rubies. The pattern was suspicious, but the more revealing evidence was smaller. Some anthills had damaged crusts and marks nearby. Rubies lay beside breaks in the mounds. Intact neighboring anthills had none.

Those were two kinds of ground only a few steps apart. If gemstones belonged naturally to the deposit, why did they cluster around places that had been disturbed? The team considered whether somebody had pushed stones into the mounds.

They also tested what lay below the surface. In a place where diamonds should have appeared in deeper material, they dug a trench about ten feet down. They found none.

Now the three inspections told different stories. Tiffany had confirmed valuable-looking material. Janin had seen gems on the surface. King's group looked at the pattern of the ground and under it. The first two tests could be passed by planting genuine stones. The third exposed the planting.

But the men still had to get that finding back to San Francisco. While they were at the camp, a visiting diamond dealer appeared, having followed their journey. One surveyor disclosed news of the fraud before the group could make a formal warning.

The planned share sale meant a delay could matter. King decided to return quickly. He and a colleague rode to the railway and reached San Francisco on November 10. He went to Janin first and explained what the geologists had found.

The following morning, King and Janin met the company directors. King's report described a worthless field and a deception. The directors stopped the planned sale of shares. Yet they did not simply take one man's word as the final answer. They sent another party, with company representatives, back to the site.

That last trip changed the outcome. The visitors examined the bare rock, the distribution of gems and the ground where the supposed mine should have been. They could find scattered stones, but no convincing natural deposit.

On November 25 the directors voted to dissolve the company. The newspapers published the exposure the following day. Arnold and Slack had already gone. Arnold later settled a legal claim with one investor; uncovering the trick did not automatically return everyone's money.

King later became the first director of the United States Geological Survey. For the investors, the more immediate lesson lay in what had happened before he arrived.

They had checked the stones. They had paid an engineer to check the site. Both inspections produced true observations. Neither answered the question on which the purchase depended: had these diamonds actually come from that ground?

The geologists answered it by kneeling beside damaged anthills and digging into empty soil. The diamonds were real. The mine was not.
