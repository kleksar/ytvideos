An oil rig began sinking into a lake that was barely ten feet deep.

Then, in front of the crew, the lake itself began to disappear.

This was Louisiana, 1980. Oil drilling was underway above a huge underground salt mine.

When the platform tilted, the drillers thought its supports had failed. They escaped before the rig vanished.

Deep below, a mine electrician heard fuel barrels crashing together. Muddy water was racing through the tunnels.

He flashed the power off and on three times: the signal to evacuate.

But four miners were working beyond those warning lights. A foreman drove through the tunnels to find them.

They made it to the lift. Everyone underground reached the surface in four elevator trips.

Fresh water was rushing into the salt mine, dissolving salt and enlarging the opening.

Above, a giant whirlpool dragged in barges and a tugboat.

Then sections of shoreline collapsed. Within hours, the lake had drained underground.

And now the canal that normally carried water out of the lake began flowing backward.

Water from the Gulf rushed in over a towering temporary waterfall to refill the basin.

Everyone survived. But the salt mine was lost, and the refilled lake was deeper and saltier.
