What happens when a lake starts draining into a mine beneath it? How do miners escape a flood they cannot see coming? And why would the canal carrying water out of that lake suddenly run the wrong way?

In Louisiana, on November 20, 1980, oil crews were drilling near Jefferson Island. Lake Peigneur looked unremarkable: a shallow expanse of water above a salt dome. Far below its surface, people were working in a network of salt-mine tunnels.

Then one drilling platform began to tilt. The crew initially suspected that something had happened to its supports. They got away. The rig sank out of sight into a lake too shallow to hide it.

But the more urgent danger was underground.

Junius Gaddison, an electrician in the mine, heard something crashing. Fuel drums were knocking against each other as muddy water swept through the workings. This was no small leak that workers could patch. Water was entering a mine whose rooms lay beneath a lake.

Gaddison switched the electrical power off and on three times. In that mine, flashing the lights meant evacuate.

There was a problem. Four miners had been working beyond the reach of the warning lights. They could not see the signal everyone else was following.

Maintenance foreman Randy La Salle drove through the underground passages and picked them up. The men made their way to the evacuation point, where supervisors checked who was present. From there they headed to the lift.

Between 8:40 and 9:00 that morning, everyone who had been below ground reached the surface in four elevator trips. No one was injured.

Now the story shifts back above the lake. The rescue was over, but the water had not stopped moving.

The salt mine gave the lake somewhere to go. As fresh water entered the underground workings, it could dissolve salt and enlarge the routes through which more water flowed. This explains how the disaster could grow so quickly. It does not establish what first opened the way in.

On the surface a whirlpool grew. It swallowed the drilling equipment, barges and a tugboat. Sections of shoreline collapsed into the expanding depression. The water level dropped until an entire lake had effectively vanished into the ground.

The canal connected to Lake Peigneur normally carried its water toward Vermilion Bay and the Gulf of Mexico. With the lake suddenly empty, the difference in water levels reversed that flow. Water now came in from the direction of the Gulf.

For a time, it poured over the edge of the new basin like a huge waterfall. The channel that had once drained the lake was refilling it.

When the water finally settled, Lake Peigneur was deeper and saltier. The mine was lost. Its workers had escaped, but their workplace had not.

An official federal investigation examined both the salt mine and the drilling operation. It could not determine the precise initiating cause. The visible sequence was clear; the first failure was not.

That distinction matters. A story often told as one proven drilling mistake is also the story of an evacuation signal, a foreman who reached four workers beyond it, and a shallow lake that briefly turned its own outlet into an inlet.
