A pig wandered into a potato patch on an island that Britain and America both claimed. In 1859, the farmer who owned that patch shot it. Within weeks, British warships were facing American soldiers.

The island was San Juan, between Vancouver Island and the North American mainland. Thirteen years earlier, a treaty had drawn the border through “the channel.” There were two channels. Put the border through one, and the island was American. Choose the other, and it was British.

The pig belonged to the British Hudson’s Bay Company. The farmer, Lyman Cutlar, offered to pay for it. But British officials threatened to arrest him, and American settlers asked for military protection.

Sixty-four American soldiers arrived. The British governor sent a warship. Two more followed. As American reinforcements dug in with cannon, British sailors fired practice rounds at rocks along the bay.

Neither country wanted to surrender its claim. Yet no one fired at the opposing troops. President James Buchanan sent General Winfield Scott to negotiate a way out. The sides reduced their forces and eventually kept separate camps on the same island.

That uneasy arrangement lasted twelve years. In 1872, the German emperor’s arbitration awarded the disputed islands to the United States, using the Haro Strait as the boundary.
