How could one dead pig put American soldiers opposite British warships? Why didn't anyone fire at the other side? And why did the answer finally come from a German emperor, thirteen years later?

Start with a map of the Pacific Northwest. In 1846, Britain and the United States agreed to a border that ran down a channel between Vancouver Island and the mainland. There was a problem: two waterways fitted the treaty's wording. Haro Strait lay to the west of the San Juan Islands; Rosario Strait lay to the east. The treaty did not settle which route the border should follow.

So both governments claimed the islands. The British Hudson's Bay Company ran a sheep farm there. American settlers moved onto land the company considered its own. For years, people lived with a disagreement that no one had resolved.

Then, on June 15, 1859, an American settler named Lyman Cutlar shot a company pig that had been rooting around his garden. Cutlar offered compensation. British officials demanded payment and threatened arrest. The pig was dead, but now the argument was about who had the power to arrest someone on the island.

The American settlers appealed to Brigadier General William Harney. He ordered Captain George Pickett to land with sixty-four soldiers. Pickett set up camp near the company's wharf. British governor James Douglas saw an armed American presence on land Britain claimed.

Douglas dispatched the frigate HMS Tribune. He wanted the American troops removed, but its commander, Geoffrey Hornby, refused to risk a landing that could start a fight. Two more British warships joined him. Pickett refused to leave and asked for reinforcements.

Hornby waited for his superior, Rear Admiral Lambert Baynes, rather than carry out the governor's demand. When Baynes arrived in August, he cancelled the plan to land British marines. Douglas still claimed the island, and the Royal Navy still had enough firepower to challenge the Americans. But the commanders had stopped an order from turning into a battle.

Here was the extraordinary turn: the soldiers and sailors were preparing for a confrontation that neither capital had authorized as a war. More American troops arrived. By the end of August, hundreds of soldiers and fourteen field guns occupied the American position. British crews practiced firing at rocks along the bay. The guns made plenty of noise, but they did not fire at one another.

Some officers even attended church together and met socially while their forces faced each other. Those encounters could not settle a border. More important was the choice to keep marines aboard the ships: it left time for the governments to find a way out.

When President James Buchanan learned how far things had gone, he sent General Winfield Scott. Scott had handled earlier disputes with Britain. Rather than decide the border himself, he arranged for the reinforcements to withdraw. One American company would remain; a British warship would stay nearby.

The arrangement changed again in 1860, when Royal Marines established a separate British camp at the other end of the island. Two countries had placed their soldiers on the same disputed land, without agreeing whose land it was.

They lived that way through the American Civil War and beyond. The provisional occupation continued for about twelve years. The next decisive step wasn't another gunboat. It was the Treaty of Washington in 1871, which sent the boundary question to arbitration by Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany.

A three-member commission considered the competing routes. On October 21, 1872, the emperor's award selected Haro Strait. That placed the San Juan Islands on the American side of the line. British marines departed later that year; American troops remained for a time after the verdict.

The legal answer arrived thirteen years after Cutlar shot the pig. During the armed standoff of 1859, neither side suffered a human combat death. The famous single casualty was the animal that started the quarrel. The deeper cause was a border treaty that had left two possible channels on the map.
