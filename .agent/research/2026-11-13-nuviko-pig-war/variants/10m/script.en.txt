How did a pig killed in a vegetable garden almost bring Britain and the United States into a military clash? Why did armed men stand on the same island for years without fighting? And what exactly did a German emperor decide that neither country had been willing to settle?

To answer that, look at the waterways between today's Washington State and Vancouver Island. The broad border was negotiated in 1846. The Oregon Treaty followed the forty-ninth parallel westward, then ran through “the channel” separating Vancouver Island from the mainland. On a map, however, there wasn't just one obvious passage.

Haro Strait passed west of the San Juan Islands. Rosario Strait passed east. A boundary down Haro put the archipelago on the American side; a boundary down Rosario put it on the British side. Both governments claimed the islands, and the treaty's wording gave neither a quick way to end the argument. The dispute existed well before anybody shot a farm animal.

The Hudson's Bay Company had a commercial reason to stay. It established Belle Vue Sheep Farm on San Juan Island in the 1850s. According to the National Park Service, its flock grew from roughly fourteen hundred animals to more than forty-five hundred in six years. This wasn't an empty dot on a map. There were farm buildings, employees, grazing animals, and a British business using the ground every day.

American settlers also came. By the spring of 1859, eighteen had staked claims on land the British company used for grazing. To the newcomers, they were settling territory they expected the United States to recognize. To company officials, these were unauthorized occupants. Each side could point to a map, but neither map could make the other people disappear.

One of those settlers was Lyman Cutlar. On June 15, a Hudson's Bay Company pig was rooting in his garden. Cutlar shot it. That was the immediate act everyone would later remember. But he did not walk straight from the potato patch to a warship. He went to the company to offer compensation for the animal.

A dispute over payment followed. The National Park Service records British officials demanding one hundred dollars and threatening Cutlar with arrest. British authorities also challenged the Americans' right to remain on the island. A dead pig had exposed the unresolved question beneath every fence and every claim: which country's officials had legal power there?

The American settlers asked for troops. Brigadier General William Harney, who commanded the US Army's Department of Oregon and opposed the British position, ordered Captain George Pickett and Company D of the Ninth Infantry to the island. In late July, sixty-four American soldiers landed near the Hudson's Bay Company wharf.

They set up camp on the land the British claimed. For the settlers, soldiers meant protection against a threatened arrest. To Governor James Douglas on Vancouver Island, the troops were a new assertion of American authority. A personal property dispute had become a confrontation between governments.

Douglas sent HMS Tribune, a British steam frigate with thirty-one guns. He ordered its commander, Geoffrey Hornby, to dislodge the Americans if possible, while avoiding an armed clash. That second part mattered. Hornby faced an order to challenge another country's soldiers without starting the war that might follow.

Pickett refused to leave. Hornby now faced a decision. Governor Douglas wanted the American soldiers removed, but Pickett had warned that he would resist a British landing. Sending marines ashore could have turned a territorial argument into a firefight.

On August 3, Hornby kept his marines aboard. He waited for his superior, Rear Admiral Lambert Baynes, instead of forcing a landing. Two more British warships, Satellite and Plumper, joined the Tribune. Pickett could see the naval guns, but the marines did not come ashore.

Baynes arrived on August 5. He backed Hornby's refusal and cancelled the governor's landing order. Neither officer had decided who owned San Juan Island. They had, however, stopped the most immediate path to battle: British troops landing against armed Americans.

Across the water, Pickett reacted as though a confrontation might still come. He appealed to Harney, who sent Lieutenant Colonel Silas Casey with troops, guns, and supplies. By the end of August, the National Park Service counts 461 Americans and fourteen field cannons. Eight heavy naval guns were also brought ashore for a defensive earthwork. The British landing order was gone, but the American position had become much harder to challenge.

British crews kept practicing with their own guns, firing at rocky bluffs along Griffin Bay, not at American troops. Excursion boats brought people to watch. Some officers from the opposing forces even attended church aboard a British vessel. These scenes were striking precisely because the standoff was still real. Social visits could not cancel the troops' orders, and none of these people could resolve the wording of the treaty.

The island was full of weapons because local commanders had acted before their governments could settle the question. Restraint aboard the British ships had prevented one possible battle, but it had not yet brought the Americans home.

When the news reached Washington, President James Buchanan sent General Winfield Scott. Scott was an experienced senior officer who had dealt with earlier border tensions between the two nations. His purpose was to contain the danger, not settle geography by ordering one side to surrender.

Scott arrived in October and negotiated with Douglas. Most American reinforcements would leave; one company would remain, with a British warship nearby. Scott also replaced Pickett with Captain Lewis Cass Hunt. He had reduced the danger without asking either side to abandon its claim.

In November, both governments approved the principle of a joint occupation until the boundary could be settled. That became more than a ship waiting offshore: on March 21, 1860, British Royal Marines landed at Garrison Bay and began building English Camp. American soldiers stayed at American Camp. This time the landing was part of an agreed arrangement, not the opposed landing that Hornby and Baynes had refused.

The compromise still had an opponent inside the American command. Harney, who had sent Pickett to the island in the first place, put him back in charge the following spring, undoing Scott's personnel decision. Scott complained, as did British officials. In June 1860, Harney was replaced as commander of the Department of Oregon. The dispute had now cost an American general his command, even though the British and American troops had never fought.

The two military camps were about fifteen miles apart. Their soldiers sometimes visited for Independence Day and Queen Victoria's birthday. That sounds like the end of the crisis, but the uniforms stayed. Each government wanted a presence on the island while neither would concede the boundary.

Then the American Civil War began in 1861, diverting Washington's attention. The Hudson's Bay Company gradually withdrew from the island, but the competing national claims remained. There was no new battle to settle them, and no official line on the map that both sides accepted.

The dangerous summer of 1859 had ended in a negotiated drawdown. The shared occupation lasted about twelve years. Preventing a firefight and deciding sovereignty turned out to be two very different achievements.

The next answer came through diplomacy in 1871. Britain and the United States signed the Treaty of Washington, referring the San Juan boundary question to Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany. The choice was no longer about the price of the pig or whether one captain would obey another governor. It was a legal decision between possible boundary lines.

Wilhelm referred the matter to a commission of three experts, who considered the case in Geneva. On October 21, 1872, the award went to the United States. The border would run through Haro Strait, west of the islands. That made the disputed San Juan group American territory.

The result was followed by withdrawals, but not all at once. British Royal Marines left English Camp in November 1872. The final American troops departed in 1874. The difference between those dates matters: the 1872 award resolved sovereignty; it did not instantly erase every military post.

Go back to the garden. Cutlar had killed a pig owned by a British company. That act triggered demands for payment and threats of arrest, but the danger came from something older: a treaty whose vague geographical wording had left two countries claiming the same land.

In the 1859 armed standoff, the opposing forces never exchanged fire and suffered no human combat deaths. The pig was the famous lone casualty of the incident. The ships, guns, camps, and thirteen years of argument were real. So was the peaceful ending.
