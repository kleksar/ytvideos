## Three competing finalists in comparable form

### A — USS S-5 (winner)

- **Audience:** broad English-speaking viewers interested in real survival, early 20th-century history and ingenious decisions. No submarine terminology needed to understand the opening.
- **Opening line and picture:** `A white T-shirt was waving above the Atlantic waves.` A lone cloth flag above a sub's protruding stern.
- **Why stop:** a normal piece of clothing emerges from an otherwise empty sea; dozens of people are imprisoned underneath.
- **Why stay:** accidental flood -> inability to pump -> rising toxic gas -> blow rear ballast -> only stern breaks surface -> no exit -> manually drill -> ships pass -> makeshift flag -> ship turns -> holes enlarged -> rescue -> cable snaps, sub sinks again.
- **Payoff:** deliberate engineering and a last-minute signal rescue all 38, and the vessel alone is lost on a second sinking.
- **Strongest objection:** a naval disaster is broad genre overlap with USS Porter #17, and too many procedural steps risk a 90-second monotone. Keep physical actions simple, make one clear choice per beat, omit crew names besides commander, and measure TTS before making scenes. Independent viewer preference is unknown.
- **Evidence:** Navy official chronology + US Naval Undersea Museum; tactical white-shirt/previous passing ships/quote detailed in Naval Institute December 2020, corroborated broadly by Navy H-Gram. Sources below.

### B — BA flight 5390 (strongest rival)

- **Audience:** general aviation, disaster, suspense, human courage.
- **Opening:** `The pilot was hanging outside his own plane—and the flight still landed safely.`
- **Why stay:** windscreen fails -> flight attendant holds captain -> copilot descends/diverts -> pilot survives -> investigators discover 84 of 90 windscreen bolts are wrong size.
- **Payoff:** crew save a man despite decompression and engineering quality control failure.
- **Objection:** extremely recognizable air-disaster replay, likely competing with extensive existing shorts and dramatic reenactments; one main accident followed by explanation, fewer distinct in-world decisions than S-5. This is not a negative assessment of aviation as a niche.
- **Evidence:** official UK AAIB final report 1/92 and ABC reporting citing its findings. Direct PDF text was opened for orientation; visual screenshot access failed; no specific figure or quote beyond indexed official synopsis is used as a script claim.

### C — Canadian Six (Argo)

- **Audience:** spy-thriller and historical deception fans.
- **Opening:** `Six Americans escaped a hostage crisis by pretending to be a Canadian science-fiction film crew.`
- **Why stay:** embassy seizure -> hiding -> CIA builds fake movie business and adverts -> Canadian passports -> rehearsed identities -> airport exit.
- **Payoff:** successful January 28 1980 flight to Zurich.
- **Objection:** the 2012 film makes the core reveal familiar, and a Short must compress the Canada/CIA division of labor without misleading.
- **Evidence:** CIA Museum exhibit and US National Museum of American Diplomacy.

**Why A instead of B:** BA 5390 has the more instantly visceral image. S-5 has the stronger multi-step progression that can hold attention after revealing the premise: the crew first changes the submarine's position, then attempts drilling, then loses two possible rescues, then invents a signal, then needs another vessel's tools. The second sinking adds a final true reversal rather than an unverified manufactured climax. This is an **editorial judgment**, not an empirically measured likelihood of more views.

**Why A instead of C:** Both have clever improvisation and survival, but S-5 has simpler geographical and institutional context, a stronger physical first frame, and fewer familiar spoilers.
