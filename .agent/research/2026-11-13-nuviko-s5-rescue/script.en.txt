A white T-shirt fluttered above the Atlantic. Directly below it, thirty-eight sailors were trapped in a sunken submarine.

September 1920. The American submarine S-5 began a practice dive with an air-intake valve still open.

Seawater poured in. The bow hit the seabed, almost sixty metres down. The pumps couldn't stop the flood.

Water reached the batteries. Chlorine gas spread through the compartments. The men were running out of breathable air.

The captain, Charles Cooke, tried another way. He sent compressed air into the ballast tanks at the back.

The bow stayed on the bottom. But the stern rose until part of the hull stuck out above the waves.

One problem: there was no escape hatch up there. So they started drilling through the steel.

Hours of drilling, and the hole was still too small for a person. Two ships went past without stopping.

Then came a third. The sailors pushed a copper pipe through the hole and tied a clean white T-shirt to it.

They waved it from inside. The ship kept sailing away. And then it turned back.

The captain asked where the submarine was headed. The reply, tapped through the hull: “To hell, by compass!”

A second steamer arrived with tools. Rescuers widened the hole, and all thirty-eight men climbed out.

The Navy tried to tow S-5 away. The cable snapped, and she sank again. Nobody was aboard this time.
