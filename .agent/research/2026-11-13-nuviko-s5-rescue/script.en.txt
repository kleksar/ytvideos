A white T-shirt was waving above the Atlantic waves. Just beneath it, thirty-eight sailors were trapped inside a submarine.

In September 1920, the American submarine S-5 began a practice dive. One of its air-intake valves had been left open.

Seawater poured in. The submarine's bow struck the seabed almost sixty metres below the surface. The pumps couldn't keep up.

Water was reaching the batteries, threatening poisonous gas. And the air inside was getting worse.

Commander Charles Cooke made a desperate choice: pump compressed air into the ballast tanks at the stern.

The bow stayed on the bottom. But the stern rose, leaving several metres of the hull above the waves.

There was no escape hatch there. The men started drilling through the steel hull by hand.

Hours passed. The opening was still too narrow. Two ships sailed past without stopping.

When a third appeared, they pushed a copper pipe through the hole and tied a white T-shirt to it.

That ship kept sailing away. But then it turned around.

Its captain asked where the submarine was headed. Tapped through the steel came the answer: “To hell, by compass!”

Another steamship arrived with tools. The rescuers enlarged the opening, and all thirty-eight sailors climbed out.

Later, the Navy tried to tow the empty submarine. The cable snapped. S-5 sank for a second time.

This time, nobody was inside. The rescue had begun when a passing captain noticed something white above the water.
