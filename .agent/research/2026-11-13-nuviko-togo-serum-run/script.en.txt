An experienced sled dog crossed dangerous sea ice to help deliver medicine to sick children.

His name was Togo. In 1925, diphtheria threatened the remote Alaskan town of Nome.

Ships couldn't reach its frozen port, and planes were too risky. The town urgently needed antitoxin.

Twenty dog-sled teams would relay the medicine from a railway hundreds of miles away.

But Togo and his driver, Leonhard Seppala, left Nome, racing toward the incoming relay.

They cut across the ice of Norton Sound, hoping to intercept the medicine.

Without Seppala knowing, the plan changed. Extra teams had joined the route.

He nearly passed the team carrying the antitoxin. At the last moment, the drivers met.

Seppala took the medicine, turned back, and crossed the same dangerous ice.

An old dog by racing standards, Togo covered more than four hundred kilometres with Seppala on this mission.

Finally, they handed off the medicine. Other teams carried it through the Alaskan winter.

Less than six days after leaving the railway, the antitoxin reached Nome.

The final team arrived with Balto among its dogs. He deserved credit, and New York gave him a statue.

Balto's team travelled about eighty kilometres. Togo had led the relay's longest and most dangerous section.
