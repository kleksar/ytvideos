The sled leaving Nome was empty. The dog at its front, Togo, was going away from the children who needed medicine.

In January 1925, diphtheria was spreading through the Alaskan town. Ships could not reach its frozen harbour, and flying was considered too dangerous. A train brought antitoxin to Nenana, still hundreds of miles away. Twenty dog-sled teams would pass it west.

But Togo and his musher, Leonhard Seppala, travelled east to intercept that relay. They crossed the exposed sea ice of Norton Sound before the medicine was even aboard.

Then organisers added teams and moved the handoff. No message reached Seppala. Near Shaktoolik he almost passed Henry Ivanoff, who was carrying the antitoxin toward him. The drivers spotted each other. Seppala took the cylinder, turned west, and crossed the dangerous ice again, now protecting the medicine.

In Golovin he handed it onward. Togo's team had covered more than four hundred kilometres going out and back.

Then a different message failed. Nome's doctor ordered a pause because the storm threatened the serum. The warning reached Rohn, who was waiting for the last leg, but not Gunnar Kaasen, who took the package from another driver.

A gust overturned Kaasen's sled. The cylinder flew into the snow. He found it and kept going. At Port Safety, Rohn was asleep, expecting the relay to stop. Kaasen continued with Balto among his dogs.

The vials arrived in Nome unbroken, about five and a half days after the antitoxin left the railway. Balto became famous for that finish. Togo, Seppala, and many other teams had made it possible.

Twice, an important message missed its driver. The public saw the medicine arrive. The longest journey that brought it there had begun in the opposite direction.
