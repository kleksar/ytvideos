The sled leaving Nome was empty. The dog at its front was about to run the longest stretch of a race to deliver medicine.

His name was Togo. In 1925, diphtheria was spreading through the isolated Alaskan town. Children had already died, and its doctor needed fresh antitoxin.

Ships couldn't enter the frozen port. Flying was considered too dangerous. The medicine came by train as far as Nenana, still hundreds of miles away.

So twenty dog-sled teams formed a relay. They would carry the precious package west, from one driver to the next.

But Togo and his musher, Leonhard Seppala, set off east from Nome to meet it. They crossed the dangerous ice of Norton Sound on the way out.

Then the plan changed. Extra relay teams were added, but Seppala was already beyond reach of the message.

Near Shaktoolik, he nearly passed Henry Ivanoff, who was bringing the serum toward him. The drivers found each other just in time.

Now the sled carried antitoxin. And the team had to turn around and face Norton Sound again, with the medicine aboard.

Seppala and Togo eventually passed the package to another driver. More teams carried it onward. In about five and a half days from the railway, it reached Nome.

The final team included a dog called Balto. He helped complete the rescue and became famous enough to receive a statue in New York.

But Seppala and Togo had covered more than four hundred kilometres on their outward and return journey. The longest, most hazardous part of the relay wasn't the part the cameras remembered.

The medicine reached Nome because many teams did their jobs. Togo's job started before anyone placed the medicine on his sled.
