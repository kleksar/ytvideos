Why did a team trying to save a town race away from it with an empty sled? How could a last-minute change make a life-saving delivery harder? And why did a dog called Balto become famous for a journey led for much longer by Togo?

In January 1925, diphtheria was spreading through Nome, on Alaska's western coast. The town's doctor, Curtis Welch, had discovered that its stock of antitoxin was outdated. Children were dying. Fresh medicine existed, but getting it to Nome was the problem.

Winter ice closed the harbour to ships. Officials considered an aircraft, then decided the weather and conditions made the flight too dangerous. A train could bring the medicine only to Nenana. From there, more than a thousand kilometres of frozen country separated the package from Nome.

The solution was a dog-sled relay. Twenty drivers and their teams would hand over the serum, one stretch at a time. These weren't ornamental racing dogs. Sled teams already moved mail and supplies in an environment where other transport failed.

Leonhard Seppala was given a particularly difficult assignment. Instead of waiting in Nome for the package, he would leave with his own team and intercept the incoming relay far to the east. His lead dog was Togo, an experienced Siberian husky. They had worked together for years.

That meant something easy to miss when you hear the phrase 'serum run': Seppala's first miles carried no serum at all. Every mile east would have to be matched by a return journey west once he collected it.

He took a shortcut over frozen Norton Sound. The exposed sea ice saved distance, but wind and unstable ice made it dangerous. Still, the team needed to meet the medicine before conditions closed in.

While Seppala was on the trail, officials changed the relay. More teams were added to move the package faster. The change made sense for the patients waiting in Nome. But the organisers had no reliable way to tell Seppala, who was already travelling toward the original meeting point.

Now the two plans were running at once: an updated relay carrying medicine west, and a driver heading east toward a meeting that had moved.

Near Shaktoolik, the mistake almost became a disaster. Seppala was close to passing Henry Ivanoff's team without realising Ivanoff had the serum. The drivers finally recognised the encounter, and the package changed hands.

That was the main turn in the story, but not the end of the danger. Seppala immediately reversed course. His team had already travelled an enormous distance. Now the medicine was aboard, and the hazardous shortcut lay in front of them again.

On the return crossing of Norton Sound, the storm and the ice posed a fresh choice: take the shorter but exposed route or lose more time going around. Seppala trusted the lead dog that knew how to negotiate the ice. Togo guided his team through conditions in which a wrong move could take them away from land.

After the crossing and further travel, Seppala passed the antitoxin on at Golovin. His work covered more than four hundred kilometres, counting the outbound trip to find the medicine and the return with it. The medicine then continued toward Nome in other hands.

One of those later drivers was Gunnar Kaasen, waiting at Bluff with a team led by Balto. But he wasn't supposed to be the one who reached Nome. Another musher, Rohn, was stationed at Port Safety for the final stretch.

The storm had become so fierce that doctor Welch feared the last teams might lose the medicine. Officials ordered a pause. Rohn received word; Kaasen, already waiting farther down the trail, did not. It was the second time an important change in the relay failed to reach the person it affected.

Charlie Olson brought the package to Kaasen through the storm. His fingers were so badly frostbitten that untethering it from his sled was difficult. Kaasen waited for the weather to ease, then set out with Balto at the front.

The wind flipped his sled. The cylinder holding the serum fell into the snow. Kaasen searched until he found it. He could not know whether the glass vials inside had broken, but he still had to carry the package onward.

At Port Safety, the roadhouse was dark. Rohn was asleep, expecting the relay to remain paused. Kaasen chose not to wake him and prepare another team. He pressed on toward Nome with the dogs already harnessed.

Early on February second, they arrived. The serum had frozen, but the vials were intact and the medicine could be thawed for treatment. The arrival could not undo the deaths that had already happened.

Balto earned his place in the story: his team completed the storm-bound final miles. But Togo and Seppala had crossed the dangerous sea ice and carried the longest assignment, while many other teams had moved the medicine across Alaska. The statue of Balto in New York captures the finish, not the whole relay.

The first missed message almost cost the rendezvous with the serum. The second changed which driver reached Nome. The rescue worked because, despite those broken links and the lost cylinder, the medicine kept passing from one team to the next.
