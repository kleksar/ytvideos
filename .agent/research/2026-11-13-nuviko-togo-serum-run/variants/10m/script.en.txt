What did it take to move one box of medicine across Alaska when the sea was frozen and flying was too dangerous? Why did the man entrusted with the most difficult section leave the sick town travelling in the opposite direction? And how could a change meant to save time nearly make him miss the medicine entirely?

The most familiar picture of the 1925 Nome serum run is a dog called Balto arriving with the last sled. That picture shows an important ending. It doesn't explain the journey that made the ending possible.

In January, the town's only doctor, Curtis Welch, faced a growing diphtheria outbreak. The disease could obstruct a child's breathing. A supply of antitoxin was available locally, but it was out of date. Some children had already died. The doctor needed a fresh supply before the outbreak spread further.

Nome lay on Alaska's remote western coast. Winter ice kept steamships out of its harbour. An aircraft was considered, but the risk of flying through the conditions of the time was judged too great. The medicine could come by railway to Nenana. The final distance to Nome was about 674 miles, more than a thousand kilometres, over land and ice.

Officials turned to a transport system they knew could work in Alaska: sled dogs and experienced drivers. Rather than ask one person to cross the whole route, they arranged a relay. Twenty mushers, supported by more than 150 dogs, would pass the package forward. Each team could move it closer to the patients without requiring one exhausted driver to finish the whole road.

Yet the relay had an unusual piece in its design. A skilled musher named Leonhard Seppala was to depart from Nome, head east, and meet the advancing serum. He would then bring it back through the most hazardous western section. His lead dog was Togo.

Seppala wasn't taking an untested puppy on his first expedition. Togo had once been a troublesome young dog. In accounts of his early years, he was given away and returned by escaping through a window. Once harnessed to a team, he proved he could work and lead over long distances. Seppala and Togo went on to race and travel together for years.

That history mattered here. A driver can see a trail on a calm day. In darkness, snow, and moving sea ice, knowing the habits and judgment of the dog at the front may be just as important as knowing the map.

The man and dog were asked to work while the town waited for antitoxin. Seppala's own daughter was among the children at risk. But this was not a rescue in which he simply took a parcel from a railway platform and raced home.

At the start he had no parcel. Seppala and Togo left Nome going east. The antitoxin was travelling west from Nenana through other teams. Somewhere far from town, the two sides were supposed to meet.

To shorten the outbound trip, Seppala crossed frozen Norton Sound. It was a shortcut across sea ice, not a road guaranteed to remain in place. Winds could shift the ice and open water between the team and shore. The advantage was time. The price was exposure to a hazard that could change during the journey.

Meanwhile, the organisers had their own problem. The storm was worsening, the town could not wait, and the incoming relay was moving. Officials added more teams, changing where the handoff would take place. More drivers could shorten the time that any one team held the medicine.

But there was no dependable way to contact Seppala on the trail. He continued toward the original rendezvous, unaware that the medicine was already coming closer in a different pattern.

That decision created two separate routes in a single rescue operation. The package wasn't lost. The dogs weren't necessarily moving too slowly. The danger was that the best driver might pass the medicine because he did not know it had reached him.

Near Shaktoolik, on January 31, Seppala almost passed another musher, Henry Ivanoff. Ivanoff had the serum, transferred from the incoming relay. According to later accounts, the drivers caught each other's attention just in time. Seppala's planned long journey farther east was suddenly unnecessary. The package was already there.

It is tempting to imagine that this was the moment the mission was safe. In reality, it was the moment the difficult return could finally begin.

Seppala turned his team west, toward Nome. The outbound distance was already behind the dogs, but none of that effort had moved the medicine even one mile closer to town on their own sled. Now there was a fresh cargo to protect, and still a difficult coast to cross.

Norton Sound offered a cruel calculation. Following the longer coastal route cost time, while crossing exposed ice could save many miles. Conditions were becoming worse. Wind was pushing parts of the ice pack away from land, and darkness made open leads difficult to distinguish.

The team took the risk. On stretches of ice that were hard for a person to judge, Togo guided the dogs onward. Some retellings describe a particularly dramatic incident involving a detached ice floe and a rope. The precise timing and mechanics of that incident are not secure enough to reconstruct here as an unquestionable scene. What is well supported is the dangerous crossing itself, the extreme weather, and the team's successful return to land.

After their crossing, the team still had ground to cover. There were more miles to travel before they could pass the medicine onward. A relay is only as fast as its handoffs; keeping a box with one exhausted team longer than necessary would defeat its purpose.

At Golovin, Seppala finally gave the antitoxin to the next driver. By then he and Togo had travelled more than 400 kilometres on the mission, including the outbound journey to find the serum. Published accounts differ a little on the exact mileage, often giving 261 or 264 miles. That difference does not change the essential fact: their part was substantially longer than those of the other teams.

The package still wasn't in Nome. Other mushers now carried it through their own storms and difficult trails. One important driver in the final stages was Gunnar Kaasen, working with a team that included Balto, another dog from Seppala's kennel.

Kaasen was expected to hand the serum onward, but he continued through the storm rather than stopping at the planned final transfer. His team reached Nome early on February 2, roughly five and a half days after the medicine had left the railway. The serum could finally be used by Welch.

It was a success, with a hard limit to what success means. The package arrived and helped bring the outbreak under control. It could not reverse deaths that had already occurred, and some deaths among Alaska Native people were never fully counted.

That same omission of people from the familiar story appears in how the relay was remembered. Native Alaskan mushers covered a large share of the route, yet popular accounts centred on a handful of names. Every handoff mattered, even when the names of the people and dogs behind it were not printed in the famous photograph.

The public, however, had a simple picture to celebrate: Kaasen and Balto, the team at the finish. Balto was photographed, praised, and given a bronze statue in New York's Central Park later in 1925. He truly was part of the rescue. But stories sometimes credited him with achievements belonging to earlier teams.

Seppala objected to the imbalance. He had spent years working with Togo and knew what the older dog had done. The disagreement wasn't proof that Balto contributed nothing; it was a reminder that the last face seen in a relay is not the whole relay.

Over time Togo's story became more widely told. He gained his own memorials, and historians and museums revisited the part he played. But this delayed recognition is only the afterword. The decisive event happened on a snowy trail when the original plan failed to reach the man it needed.

The workers in Nome needed a parcel. Officials made a new plan to get it there faster. One of their most experienced drivers nearly missed the parcel because he could not hear about that new plan. Togo then led his team back across the dangerous ice, so others could finish the route.

The city was not saved by a statue, a single finishing photograph, or one dog running the entire distance. It was saved by a chain of teams in which one almost-missed exchange could have broken the link.
