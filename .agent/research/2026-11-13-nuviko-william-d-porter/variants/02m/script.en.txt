A destroyer sent to protect President Roosevelt accidentally fired a live torpedo at his battleship.

It was November 14, 1943. Roosevelt was aboard USS Iowa, crossing the Atlantic on his way to meet Allied leaders. Three American destroyers were escorting him.

The president was watching a naval exercise. Iowa's guns were firing at target balloons, while the escorts practiced torpedo attacks. The crews weren't supposed to fire actual weapons at their own battleship.

On USS William D. Porter, though, one torpedo left its tube and began running toward Iowa.

The destroyer's crew had to warn the ship carrying the president. But the formation was under wartime radio silence. So they tried a signal lamp.

The first message described the danger incorrectly. The next message gave an entirely different, wrong instruction. The weapon was still moving.

Porter's commander finally broke radio silence. Iowa received the warning, increased speed and turned sharply away.

The torpedo missed. It exploded in the battleship's wake. Roosevelt and everyone aboard Iowa survived the incident.

The investigation traced the accidental firing to a primer charge that should have been removed before the exercise. A training button had triggered a real launch.

Later stories made the accident even wilder. Some claimed the entire destroyer crew was arrested. The ship's logs don't support that story. They do record the torpedo leaving Porter and Iowa receiving the warning.

The danger had come from a ship assigned to keep Roosevelt safe. And that leaves one question: what could make a presidential escort break radio silence in the middle of the Atlantic?
