How could a ship assigned to protect the American president nearly hit him with a torpedo? Why did the first two warnings fail? And what does the record say actually happened?

In November 1943, Franklin Roosevelt was crossing the Atlantic aboard the battleship USS Iowa. He was traveling toward meetings with other Allied leaders while the war continued. His journey was secret enough that the ships were under radio silence.

Iowa was not sailing alone. Three destroyers traveled with her, including the new USS William D. Porter. Their job was to help protect the battleship. That matters because the weapon that soon threatened Roosevelt did not come from an enemy submarine.

On November 14, Roosevelt watched an exercise. Iowa's anti-aircraft crews fired at target balloons. The destroyers also practiced attacks, using Iowa as the imaginary target for torpedo drills. A torpedo officer could line up a shot and operate the firing controls without sending a real torpedo into the sea.

That was the plan. Then Porter launched one.

The destroyer's log records an accidental firing at 2:36 in the afternoon. The weapon had left the tube during an exercise, and Iowa was in its path. There was no time for a polite explanation. Porter needed to tell the other ship to maneuver.

But the whole formation was supposed to keep quiet on the radio. A warning could reveal the position of ships carrying the president across the Atlantic. Porter's signalmen tried the lamp instead, sending flashes that could be read from the battleship.

The first warning gave the wrong side for the approaching torpedo. A second message went wrong in another way: it indicated that Porter was moving astern. Neither told Iowa clearly what the destroyer had actually done.

The torpedo was not waiting for the correction. Porter's captain, Wilfred Walter, broke radio silence. Iowa's operators initially had to establish who was calling. The warning finally got through, and Iowa's own log places its receipt at 2:38.

Now the battleship could act. At high speed, Iowa made an emergency turn. The torpedo passed without striking her and exploded in her wake. The president's voyage had not ended with an American weapon hitting his own ship.

Why had a practice drill fired a live torpedo at all?

The investigation found that a firing primer had remained in the torpedo apparatus. During a simulated attack, that charge should have been removed. Because it had not been, the training sequence became an actual launch. The men on Porter were not carrying out an assassination attempt. They had made a dangerous error while practicing a routine maneuver.

The ship's records provide a surprisingly calm detail after all that confusion: at 3:03, Iowa resumed anti-aircraft practice. It was just twenty-seven minutes after Porter logged the accidental launch.

The incident did not vanish. Porter was sent to Bermuda for an inquiry, and the chief torpedoman faced punishment. Roosevelt later commuted that sentence. The destroyer's commander, contrary to a persistent story, was not removed from command because of the accident.

That distinction matters. The real sequence already contains a loaded weapon, confused signals, an urgent radio call and a ship carrying the president changing course. It does not need the invented claim that an entire American crew was arrested.

It also answers the three questions. A training charge left in place made the launch real. Incorrect lamp messages failed to deliver the warning. The commander broke radio silence when that method was no longer enough.

For a few minutes, the ship meant to defend Roosevelt had become the reason Iowa needed to defend herself.
