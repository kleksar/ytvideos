How could a destroyer assigned to protect President Roosevelt send a live torpedo toward his battleship? Why did its first warnings fail? And what did the Navy uncover after Iowa escaped?

In November 1943, Franklin Roosevelt was crossing the Atlantic aboard USS Iowa, heading toward meetings with Allied leaders. The voyage was secret. The ships were under wartime radio silence, and three American destroyers were assigned to protect the battleship. One was USS William D. Porter.

On November 14, Roosevelt watched Iowa's gunners fire at target balloons. The escorts were also training. They practiced torpedo attacks using Iowa as the imaginary target. The crews were supposed to operate the controls without launching a weapon. For that to work, the firing primer had to be removed from the torpedo apparatus.

At 2:36 in the afternoon, Porter fired a real torpedo.

That time comes from the destroyer's log. The weapon was now moving through the water toward the battleship carrying the president. Porter had to warn Iowa immediately, but the radio-silence order was still in force.

The signalmen tried a lamp first. Its flashes could pass a message between ships without a radio transmission. Their first warning identified the wrong side of the approaching torpedo. The next signal had a different mistake: it indicated that Porter herself was moving astern. Neither message clearly explained that an American escort had just launched a live weapon toward Iowa.

The torpedo kept going. Porter's commander, Wilfred Walter, abandoned radio silence and called the battleship. The operators on Iowa first had to establish who was calling. Then the warning got through. Iowa's log records receiving it at 2:38, about two minutes after the launch.

Iowa increased speed and made an emergency turn. The torpedo passed without striking her and exploded in the water behind the battleship. Roosevelt survived, and his voyage continued.

Twenty-seven minutes after Porter's recorded launch, Iowa resumed anti-aircraft practice. But the crew of the destroyer still had to answer a different question: how had a simulated shot become a real one?

The investigation traced the problem to torpedo number three on Porter's second mount. Its firing primer had not been removed before the drill. The Navy's later historical account adds another action: after the launch, the chief torpedoman threw evidence of his mistake overboard, trying to conceal what had happened. The evidence disposal belongs to the subsequent investigation; it was not a warning sent to Iowa during the emergency.

Porter went to Bermuda for an inquiry. Chief Torpedoman's Mate Lawton Dawson was reduced in rank. A Navy historical account also describes a hard-labor sentence later commuted by Roosevelt, though the exact length repeated in popular stories is not securely documented.

And what happened to the captain who broke radio silence to warn the president? Walter remained in command. The often-repeated story that Marines arrested the entire crew is not supported by the ships' logs.

The ship assigned to guard Roosevelt had put him in danger with one missing primer. Two confused lamp signals failed to deliver a usable warning; a radio call finally did. Iowa could resume her voyage. Porter had to explain both the accidental launch and the attempt to hide its cause.
