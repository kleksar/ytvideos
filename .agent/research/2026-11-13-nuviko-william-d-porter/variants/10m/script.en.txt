How did an American destroyer nearly torpedo the president it was supposed to protect? Why did the warnings sent from that destroyer initially make so little sense? And how did a four-minute accident become a story about a supposedly cursed warship?

To answer those questions, start with a crossing that the public was not meant to know about.

November 1943. President Franklin Roosevelt was aboard USS Iowa, a powerful new American battleship traveling across the Atlantic. He was on the way to meetings that would bring Allied leaders together during the Second World War. Protecting that journey mattered, and so did concealing the ships' position.

Iowa had three American destroyers as escorts: Cogswell, Young and William D. Porter. A destroyer was much smaller than the battleship, but it could help screen her from threats. These were not ships that were supposed to fire on their own formation. Yet before the journey was over, the most immediate danger to Iowa would come from one of them.

There was already an unglamorous problem aboard Porter. A boiler tube failure had forced the destroyer out of position until another boiler could be brought into service. Unlike many stories told later about the ship, that technical failure appears in the records. It matters here for a less dramatic reason: it shows the difference between an ordinary documented malfunction and the legends that eventually surrounded this voyage.

On November 14, the ships conducted battle exercises. Roosevelt wanted to watch Iowa demonstrate anti-aircraft fire. Balloons were released as targets, and the battleship's gunners fired at them. Some balloons moved toward the escorts, and Porter joined the shooting.

The destroyers also practiced torpedo attacks. In the exercise, Iowa was the designated target. The crews could line up their torpedo mounts, work through the sequence and rehearse the procedure without releasing live weapons. This was not an enemy vessel on the other end of the sights. It was the battleship carrying their commander-in-chief.

A torpedo tube contains more than a weapon sitting ready to roll out. A firing charge initiates the launch. For a safe simulated firing, that charge had to be removed. The procedure only works as a harmless rehearsal if the people carrying it out make the weapon safe first.

At 2:36 in the afternoon, that distinction stopped being theoretical.

Porter's deck log records a torpedo accidentally fired from her second mount during the drill. A real weapon had entered the water and was heading toward Iowa. The planned exercise had become an emergency inside the very formation that was meant to protect Roosevelt.

The problem was not just that the torpedo had launched. Porter had to tell Iowa what was coming, and the formation was observing radio silence. In a wartime ocean crossing, broadcasting needlessly could expose a ship's presence or position. The first response was therefore not to call across the radio. The destroyer tried to communicate by flashes from a signal lamp.

That choice was understandable. The messages were not.

The initial lamp warning identified the wrong side from which the torpedo was approaching. A subsequent message indicated that Porter herself was going astern. Imagine receiving that information on a battleship: it does not clearly tell you that an escort has accidentally launched a real weapon toward you.

The crew could correct a message, but it could not stop the weapon by explaining itself. The torpedo continued its run. The captain of Porter, Lieutenant Commander Wilfred Walter, had to decide whether the order to keep silent could still serve its purpose when the president's ship might be struck.

Walter broke radio silence.

Even the radio warning was not instantly simple. The first exchange required identification of the caller. Then the danger finally became clear. Iowa's log places receipt of the warning at 2:38, about two minutes after Porter's recorded launch.

Two minutes can sound like a comfortable interval on paper. In the water, it was a moving torpedo. The men on Iowa now had a warning they could act on, not merely a garbled lamp message.

The battleship accelerated and made an emergency turn. The torpedo did not hit Iowa. It detonated in the water behind her, in the wake. Accounts differ on its precise distance from the ship; the essential result does not. No one aboard Iowa was killed by that torpedo, and Roosevelt continued his journey.

One detail later attached to this scene is unusually vivid. An account says Roosevelt wanted his wheelchair moved so that he could watch the approaching weapon. The episode appears in the Naval Institute's reconstruction, but it is not necessary to imagine the president's thoughts or to give him dramatic dialogue. The documented action that saved the ship was Iowa's maneuver after the warning.

Now consider another timestamp.

Porter's log gives the firing at 2:36. Iowa records the warning at 2:38. Porter's log then says Iowa resumed anti-aircraft practice at 3:03. The same afternoon that a live torpedo came toward the battleship, the gunnery exercise went on.

That calm entry does not make the error harmless. It tells us what the record can support and where the storytelling should stop. The ship was not sunk, the president was not hit, and the drill's aftermath was an investigation rather than a naval battle.

Investigators traced the launch to a primer charge that had not been removed from the torpedo tube. A chief torpedoman had failed to take it out. The Navy's historical account also says that, after recognizing the mistake, he threw evidence of the failure overboard. That last detail belongs to the investigation, not an invented scene of someone confessing while the torpedo was still moving.

Porter was directed to Bermuda, where an inquiry was held. The chief torpedoman was sentenced to hard labor. Roosevelt subsequently commuted that sentence. The commander, Walter, was not stripped of his command because he had supposedly tried to attack the president.

This is where the story acquired another life.

Popular retellings say that Marines surrounded Porter, arrested everyone aboard and suspected the entire crew of an assassination plot. Those claims are much bigger than the surviving ship logs justify. Naval historians have pointed out that the dramatic harbor mishaps and exploding depth charge sometimes placed before the torpedo incident also lack support in the relevant logs. The documented boiler failure is different: it really was entered.

If the genuine event already includes an accidental launch, failed warning signals and an emergency turn by a president's battleship, why add more? Because a series of misfortunes is easier to remember as a legend about one unlucky ship. But the legend makes the real decisions harder to see.

There is a final chapter to Porter's own wartime service. The destroyer went on to serve in the Pacific. In June 1945, during the fighting near Okinawa, an attack involving a Japanese kamikaze led to an underwater explosion that fatally damaged the ship. Porter sank, but all 273 aboard survived.

That ending was not another near-hit on Roosevelt. It was a different event, with different people in danger. It does help explain why the destroyer's reputation lasted. The escort that once accidentally launched a torpedo at an American battleship was later lost to a weapon exploding beneath her.

The three opening questions now have answers. A primer left in place turned a rehearsal into a real firing. Radio silence and two mistaken lamp messages made the warning harder to deliver. The captain finally used the radio, Iowa turned and the torpedo missed.

And the cursed-ship story? Some of it grew from records, and some from details that were repeated because they were irresistible.

The most striking part of this incident needs no embellishment: for a few minutes in 1943, the ship assigned to protect the president was the reason his battleship had to get out of the way.
