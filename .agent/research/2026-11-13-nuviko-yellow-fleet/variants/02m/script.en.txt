A cargo ship in the Suez Canal had become a football ground. The sailors put up nets so they could play without losing the ball overboard.

In June 1967, war broke out while merchant vessels were passing through the canal. Egypt blocked the waterway, and fourteen freighters ended up at anchor in the Great Bitter Lake.

The war lasted six days, but the ships weren't going anywhere. Sand accumulated on the decks, earning them the nickname Yellow Fleet.

Most of the original sailors went home. Their employers sent replacement crews to look after ships that still needed maintenance, even when they weren't sailing.

In October, the crews formed an association aboard the Melampus. Sailors from opposite sides of the Cold War could now help each other with everyday supplies.

They even held their own games in 1968. A sailor named Uwe Carstens kept the silver medal he'd won in a sailing race for decades.

Meanwhile, shipping companies began grouping ships together so fewer men could maintain them. And in 1973, renewed fighting claimed one of the freighters, the African Glen.

When the passage became usable again in 1975, only two ships from the lake could sail away on their own. Münsterland and Nordwind made it back to Hamburg. The other survivors had to be towed.

Eight years had changed what these ships were worth. They had also changed what the men could do with an ordinary cargo deck. Just look at what happened to one of those ships.
