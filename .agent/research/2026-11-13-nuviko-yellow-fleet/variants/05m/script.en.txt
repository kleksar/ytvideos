How can a six-day war trap a ship for eight years? And what would the sailors do when no one could tell them when they might leave? On one ship in the Suez Canal, the answer eventually included a football pitch. But the strangest part of the story came when the canal finally reopened.

In June 1967, fifteen merchant ships were traveling north through the Suez Canal. They were ordinary cargo vessels, carrying goods between ports. Then war broke out between Israel and Egypt, along with other countries in the region.

Egypt blocked the canal with sunken vessels and mines. One freighter was stranded farther north, in Lake Timsah. Fourteen others stopped in the Great Bitter Lake, a wide stretch of water within the canal. Their journey had turned into an anchorage with no exit.

The fighting lasted six days. The crews did not know that the canal would remain closed for years. At first, they could still imagine a delay measured in days or weeks. Then weeks became months. Sand blew across the decks, giving the ships a name: the Yellow Fleet.

A freighter cannot simply be switched off and forgotten. Machinery needs attention, lines need checking, and someone must keep the vessel safe. As the delay grew longer, many of the original sailors went home. Shipping companies sent replacement crews to maintain the ships.

Now imagine arriving to work on a vessel that could not go anywhere. The next port was not a destination. It was a promise nobody could date.

In October 1967, officers and crews met aboard the British ship Melampus. They established the Great Bitter Lake Association. It gave fourteen separate ships a way to share supplies and help one another. Their home countries stood on different sides of the Cold War; on this lake, an available tool or a spare ingredient mattered more than those divisions.

Then the improvised community grew beyond maintenance. The crews made sailing rigs for lifeboats and raced them. On the cargo ship Port Invercargill, they played football. Sailors had turned the deck of a stranded freighter into a place for a match.

In 1968, as the Summer Olympics took place in Mexico City, the crews staged their own Bitter Lake Games. They competed in events such as sailing and football and handed out medals they had made themselves. Uwe Carstens, a sailor from Nordwind, later recalled keeping the silver medal he won in a sailing event. A competition invented to fill the waiting had left a real keepsake.

They devised stamps for mail as well. The Egyptian postal authority recognized the ships' handmade postal markings, so letters could travel beyond the lake. To the families on shore, the little fleet was not a rumor. It could send things home.

But organizing games could not keep fourteen ships in working order forever. By 1969, the owners had reduced crews further. Several vessels were moored together so fewer sailors could watch over them. The association had become a practical means of looking after a fleet that still had no departure date.

And the danger had not vanished with the first ceasefire. During another Arab-Israeli war in 1973, the African Glen was sunk while being used by Egyptian forces as an observation post. Even years into the wait, the ships were still caught in the consequences of a military frontier.

After the 1973 war, work began to clear mines and wrecks from the waterway. In 1975, the canal reopened. For the first time in nearly eight years, the crews could plan a departure.

There was one last problem. Most of the ships had deteriorated or become impractical to return to service. Only two, the German Münsterland and Nordwind, sailed away under their own power and returned to Hamburg. The other ships that survived the blockade had to be towed out. During their long captivity, modern container shipping had also changed the business they would be returning to.

So the football game was never the whole story. These sailors built a functioning community while their ships slowly lost their future. When the waiting finally ended, most of the vessels could not make the journey their crews had spent eight years imagining.
