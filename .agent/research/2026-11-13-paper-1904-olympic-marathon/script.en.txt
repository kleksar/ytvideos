At the 1904 Olympic marathon, Fred Lorz crossed the finish line first. Trouble was, he'd covered part of the race in a car.

It was a blazing afternoon in St. Louis. The road was dusty, and there was only one official water stop.

Lorz had started on foot, then dropped out, exhausted. He accepted a ride back toward the stadium.

Partway back, the car broke down. He got out and ran the remaining stretch.

He reached the stadium ahead of the other runners. The crowd cheered.

Then officials learned about the ride. Lorz was disqualified, and the race had a different winner.

Thomas Hicks was still out on the road, struggling to keep going.

His handlers gave him egg whites, brandy and small doses of strychnine, a poison used as a stimulant at the time.

By the final stretch, they were practically holding him up. Hicks made it into the stadium.

He was awarded the gold medal. The man who rode in a car got a lifetime ban.

Yet the drugs weren't against the Olympic marathon rules in 1904. A ban arrived for the 1908 race.

Hicks never ran another marathon. Lorz's lifetime ban, meanwhile, was lifted.

In 1905, Lorz won the Boston Marathon. This time, he ran the whole way.

The first time a crowd had celebrated him, though, it saw only the finish, not how he'd reached it.
