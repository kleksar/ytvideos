The man's head moved. The horse stopped after eight taps. How could Hans, a horse in 1904 Berlin, know that three plus five was eight?

At first, experts looked for his owner signaling him. They watched closely and found no deliberate trick.

His owner even stepped away. Hans could still answer questions from strangers.

Then psychologist Oskar Pfungst changed one detail. The people asking the questions no longer knew the answers. Hans started getting them wrong.

Next, the people knew the answers, but large blinders stopped Hans from seeing their faces. His accuracy dropped again.

Pfungst stopped staring at the hoof and watched the questioners.

As Hans tapped, they leaned forward, waiting. At the tap they expected, their posture changed. Sometimes a head lifted by scarcely a millimetre.

Hans noticed. Nobody was cheating. They didn't know they were moving.

Pfungst even tried it himself. He tapped with his own hand as someone silently chose a number, and stopped when he read their movement.

Hans wasn't doing the sum. He was watching the person who already knew the result.

And the clue was there from the start. Watch the questioner's head.
