What's three plus five? In 1904 Berlin, a horse seemed able to answer questions like that by tapping his hoof.

His name was Hans. A panel of experts watched him and found no obvious trick.

Even when his owner stepped away, Hans could answer other people's questions.

Then psychologist Oskar Pfungst tried questions whose answers the people asking did not know.

Hans started making mistakes. So Pfungst changed one more thing: could the horse see the questioner?

With large blinders blocking his view, correct answers almost vanished. Let him see again, and they returned.

So Pfungst stopped watching the hoof. He watched the humans instead.

As Hans tapped, the questioner leaned forward, waiting for the number they expected.

At that number, their head flicked upward—sometimes by barely a millimetre.

That tiny movement told Hans to stop. The people weren't cheating. They didn't even know they were moving.

Pfungst then played the horse himself. He tapped with his hand while others silently chose numbers.

And he read their movements too. Hans wasn't doing arithmetic. He was remarkably good at reading people.
