How could a horse solve sums even when his owner wasn't asking the questions? And why would he suddenly fail when the person standing in front of him changed almost nothing?

In Berlin in 1904, people came to see Hans. Someone would pose a calculation. Hans would lower a hoof and tap out a number. Often, it was the number the person expected. His owner, Wilhelm von Osten, a former mathematics teacher, believed the horse really understood what he was being asked.

An expert commission investigated. It found no obvious deliberate trick. That sounds like a triumph for Hans—until you notice what the commission had not established. A performance can be genuine, in the sense that no one is secretly giving commands on purpose, without proving the performer understands arithmetic.

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst took up the harder question: what, exactly, made a correct answer possible?

First, he tried removing the owner from the equation. Other people asked Hans questions and got answers too. So a simple story about a master whispering a secret instruction did not explain the results. Pfungst had to change something less obvious.

He arranged tests using number cards. In one condition, the questioner knew which number Hans could see. In another, the card was hidden from everyone except the horse. If Hans read the card, the human's knowledge should not matter.

But it mattered enormously. In this series, when the person knew the number, Hans was correct in 98 percent of the trials. When the person did not, he was correct in only 8 percent. The horse's eyes had not changed. The information in the human head had.

That gave Pfungst a clue, not yet a mechanism. Was Hans hearing an almost inaudible sound? Von Osten thought that might explain the feat. Pfungst found he could obtain answers without speaking the question—even silently to himself. The signal did not need words.

So he tested sight. He fitted large blinders so Hans knew someone was nearby but could not see that person. Correct answers again collapsed. In a separate series, the result was 89 percent correct when the questioner was visible and only 6 percent when not visible. These are results from specific tests, not Hans's lifetime batting average.

Now the mystery had narrowed. The person had to know the answer, and Hans had to be able to watch them. What was there to see?

Pfungst looked away from the hoof and studied the questioner's body. As the tapping began, the person leaned forward to watch. When the hoof reached the number they were waiting for, the person relaxed. Their head made a tiny upward movement, sometimes barely perceptible. Hans noticed the change and stopped.

The most unsettling part was that the people usually had no idea they were doing it. They were not playing a trick. They were giving away an answer merely by expecting to hear it.

Pfungst checked that explanation by changing the signal deliberately. He leaned forward without needing to ask anything, and Hans began tapping. On one occasion, he asked for thirteen but stayed bent until twenty. Hans kept going to twenty. That test exposed who was controlling the stopping point.

Then Pfungst tried something that made the result harder to dismiss as a peculiar horse trick. He took Hans's role. People silently held numbers in mind while he tapped with his hand. He watched their small movements and used them to decide when to stop. A person could reproduce the same effect without knowing the hidden number.

Hans did not solve the arithmetic. Nor does that make him stupid. He had learned to detect signals his human partners could not detect in themselves.

The lasting lesson is about the experiment, not just the horse. If the examiner knows the answer and can unknowingly communicate it, a test may measure the examiner's expectations instead of the subject's ability. The next time a result looks impossible, ask who else in the room already knows what it is supposed to be.
