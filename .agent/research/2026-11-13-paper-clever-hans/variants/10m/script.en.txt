What does it take to prove that a horse can do mathematics? Is a correct answer enough? And if no one is deliberately cheating, what could still be wrong with the test?

In Berlin at the beginning of the twentieth century, a horse called Hans seemed to have an astonishing range of talents. He would tap out answers with his hoof. He appeared to handle sums, numbers shown on cards, and even questions involving words. Spectators did not have to be specialists to understand the attraction. Someone asked. A horse tapped. The count often stopped at the expected number.

Hans belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a former mathematics teacher. He had spent years training the animal and believed his lessons had developed genuine reasoning. This matters, because the investigation was not simply a hunt for an assistant hidden behind a curtain. Von Osten himself could sincerely expect a correct answer without knowing how his own actions might affect it.

In September 1904, a commission examined the performances and found no evidence of an ordinary deliberate trick. But the conclusion was narrower than the public miracle. The experts had not proved that Hans was solving the problems in his head. That distinction opened a new question: if he was getting answers from somewhere else, where?

Psychologist Oskar Pfungst took on the question through experiments that changed one condition at a time. He first established that the owner's presence was not essential. With other questioners, Hans could still produce correct answers. Any explanation restricted to a private signal invented by von Osten was therefore incomplete.

The crucial difference turned out not to be which human stood there, but what that person knew.

Pfungst showed numeral cards to Hans. In some trials the questioner also knew the number. In others, the horse alone could see the card. The test was sharp: if Hans was reading the numeral, it ought not to matter whether a human could read it too.

The results were nothing like an independent reading skill. In 42 trials with knowledge, 98 percent of answers were correct. In 49 trials without knowledge, only 8 percent were. An apparent ability had almost vanished when information was removed from the person, not the horse.

Then Pfungst devised a more revealing test of arithmetic itself. Von Osten whispered one number into the horse's ear. Pfungst whispered another. Neither man knew the other's number, so no human standing there knew the sum. If Hans could add, here was his chance to prove it.

In thirty-one attempts like that, Hans got just three sums right. When the same sort of problem was repeated with the sum known to the experimenters, he answered correctly twenty-nine times out of thirty-one. The horse heard numbers in both conditions. What disappeared was a person who could anticipate when the hoof should stop. This was not merely a test of recognizing a printed card. It challenged the very skill the audience had come to see.

That still left several possible explanations. Could the person be breathing differently? Whispering too softly for observers to notice? Von Osten even entertained the notion that Hans might somehow pick up words the human formed internally. Pfungst tested claims about very faint or silent speech. Answers could be obtained without any spoken question, or even a question mentally rehearsed as words. He was finding that the act of asking was not what carried the answer.

The decisive information seemed to arrive while Hans was tapping. So Pfungst looked at a sense the horse could easily use: sight.

He put large blinders on Hans. Sometimes the questioner was in view, sometimes not. The horse was aware of a nearby person in both conditions. Yet in this separate set of experiments, he gave correct answers in 89 percent of the clear-view cases and 6 percent when the questioner was hidden. Not every trial was easy to classify, and different people were not equally skilled at eliciting answers. The reported percentages belong to these particular experimental conditions.

Now two necessary ingredients were visible. Somebody needed to know the expected result, and Hans needed access to that person's body. That makes a good detective question, but a bad final explanation until the exact signal is found.

Pfungst watched the humans instead of the hoof. When a questioner expected an answer, they leaned their head and torso a little forward to see the tapping. Hans would begin. As the hoof approached the number in the person's mind, the watcher grew more intent. At the expected count, a slight upward jerk of the head coincided with Hans bringing his hoof back.

To the crowd, the horse had finished counting. To the investigator, he had reacted to a change in posture. The movement was so small that many people giving the signal never noticed it themselves. Some remained unconvinced even after the mechanism was explained.

The claim demanded another test. If posture really controlled the horse, could the investigator cause an answer without a mathematical question?

Pfungst leaned forward as though to watch or write something down. Hans began tapping. In one set of thirty such trials, twenty-nine worked. Then Pfungst deliberately held his posture past the expected number. On one occasion he asked for thirteen but stayed bent until the hoof had tapped twenty. Hans went on to twenty. The moment the human released his posture mattered more than the number spoken aloud.

This was not just a clever after-the-fact interpretation. Pfungst could use the discovered cue to produce a different result. He had moved from watching a correlation to deliberately testing a mechanism.

One last complication put that new explanation under pressure. Pfungst and psychologist Carl Stumpf stood beside Hans and silently chose different numbers. In ten such trials, Hans always followed Pfungst. When Stumpf thought of five and Pfungst of eight, the horse tapped eight. When Stumpf chose seven and Pfungst four, Hans stopped at four. So he was not simply choosing the bigger number.

Then Pfungst walked away. Stumpf could get the horse to follow his own number again. When Pfungst returned, that influence vanished. The identity of the person Hans watched could change the answer. Removing the owner had not removed the problem; it had only changed which person's movements could supply the stopping signal.

And how small was this movement? Pfungst later used laboratory recording equipment to trace tiny motions of the head on smoked paper. Movements could be around a millimetre, sometimes smaller. There was nothing supernatural in the evidence. There was a physical signal almost too slight for an untrained observer to recognize, but still visible to a watchful animal at close range.

Pfungst then removed the horse from the demonstration entirely. In a series of human experiments, people silently thought of numbers. He took Hans's role and tapped with his hand, watching for the telltale motion that would let him stop. Most participants gave an involuntary sign. They did not know that their bodies were supplying an answer. Pfungst, without knowing the number in advance, could sometimes reproduce the apparent feat.

This final step matters more than merely saying Hans 'read body language'. If a human can do the same thing in the same kind of setup, the mechanism does not require mathematical knowledge in the horse. It depends on what the observer expects, how that expectation changes the observer's movements, and what the animal has learned to notice.

There is a fair conclusion for von Osten, too. The historical investigators did not need to pronounce him a fraud to explain what happened. The signals could be unintentional. His confidence and the horse's skill could be real at the same time, while the explanation of that skill was wrong.

Hans did learn something remarkable. He became exceptionally attentive to minute human movements. But answering an examiner's cue is different from reading a number that nobody else can see.

The test itself was the source of the illusion. Only by hiding the answer from the person, blocking the person's visibility, and then reproducing the signal did Pfungst discover what was being measured. The lesson is not that animals lack intelligence. It is that a correct answer proves less than we think when the person conducting the test already knows what answer to expect.

So before calling any performance impossible, ask two smaller questions. Who knew the result? And could the performer see them?
