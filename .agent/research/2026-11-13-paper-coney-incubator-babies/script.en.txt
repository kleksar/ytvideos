At Coney Island, visitors paid to see premature babies in glass incubators.
The exhibit stood inside an amusement park. Nurses worked inside, while rides were nearby.
In the early 1900s, many American hospitals couldn't offer specialized care for babies born too soon.
Martin Couney ran the exhibit. Admission money paid for the nurses and equipment, so parents weren't charged.
In 1920, one of those babies was Lucille Horn.
Her twin sister died at birth. The hospital told Lucille's father that it couldn't help her.
He and his wife had already seen the incubators on their honeymoon.
He wrapped Lucille in a towel, hailed a taxi, and took her there.
She spent about six months in Couney's care, while paying visitors came to look.
Lucille survived. Years later, she returned to the exhibit as a young woman.
Couney happened to be there, and she introduced herself.
Nearby, another father was watching his own baby in an incubator.
Couney tapped the man on the shoulder and told him that Lucille had been one of his babies.
Once, Lucille had been behind that glass. Now she stood beside the visitors looking in.
