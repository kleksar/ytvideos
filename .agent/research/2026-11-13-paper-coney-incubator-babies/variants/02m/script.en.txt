Visitors paid to see premature babies in glass incubators.

The exhibit stood at Coney Island, among amusement rides. But inside, nurses fed the infants and kept them warm. The adults watching paid for that care.

In the early 1900s, many American hospitals lacked specialist care for babies born too soon. Martin Couney ran one of these exhibits. Parents weren't charged. The ticket window financed equipment and staff.

In 1920, that strange arrangement became a lifeline for one family.

Lucille Horn was born early, alongside a twin who died. The hospital told her father there was little it could do for the surviving baby.

Her parents had visited Couney's incubators on their honeymoon. Her father remembered where they were. He wrapped Lucille in a towel, hailed a taxi, and took her to the amusement park.

Behind the glass, nurses cared for Lucille while strangers came to look. She stayed for months. Her own recollection, recorded much later, put it at about six months.

Lucille survived. Years later, she returned to the same exhibit, this time as a young woman.

Couney happened to be there, and she introduced herself. A different father was looking at his own baby inside an incubator.

Couney tapped the man's shoulder and showed him Lucille. She had once been one of those babies. No one could promise what would happen to his child. But standing in front of him was a person who had grown up.
