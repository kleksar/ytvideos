In 1970, chunks of whale rained from the sky on an Oregon beach.

A dead, forty-five-foot sperm whale had washed ashore near Florence. It weighed about eight tons, and the smell was getting worse.

The highway department had to clear the beach. They ruled out burial because the waves might uncover the carcass again.

So the crew treated the whale like a giant boulder: they decided to blow it apart.

They used half a ton of dynamite, hoping tiny pieces would fly toward the sea and be eaten by scavengers.

About seventy-five spectators and reporters watched from a quarter of a mile away. That seemed far enough.

Then came the blast. A towering cloud shot up, but the whale didn't disappear into harmless little pieces.

Huge chunks fell back toward the crowd. People ran for cover as whale blubber splattered the sand.

One chunk smashed the roof of a parked car. Thankfully, nobody was reported hurt.

Even worse for the cleanup crew, enormous pieces of the whale were still on the beach. The seagulls weren't helping.

By evening, the workers were burying the remains anyway. Later, burying beached dead whales became the standard approach.
