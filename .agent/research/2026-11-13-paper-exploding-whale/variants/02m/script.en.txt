A quarter of a mile. That's how far back spectators stood when an Oregon highway crew blew up a dead whale in 1970. Some still had to run for cover.

A forty-five-foot sperm whale had washed ashore near Florence. It weighed roughly eight tons, and the smell was getting worse. The state highway division had to remove it.

They worried the surf would uncover it if they buried it. So the crew chose dynamite: break the carcass into small pieces and let the sea and scavengers finish the cleanup.

They used about half a ton. Roughly seventy-five people watched from the dunes, including reporters. The plan was to throw small fragments toward the water.

The blast sent a huge column into the air. But not everything came down in small pieces. Heavy chunks flew toward the spectators.

People scattered. One chunk crushed the roof of a parked car. No injuries were reported.

And the beach? It wasn't clean. Large pieces of whale still lay in the sand. The gulls didn't finish the job. By evening, the crew was burying the remains anyway.

Nine years later, forty-one more sperm whales stranded and died nearby. Officials skipped the explosives and buried them.

But before that first blast, the crew had taken one precaution for the people watching. They moved everyone back.
