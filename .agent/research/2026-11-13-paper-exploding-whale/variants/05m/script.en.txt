How could a dead whale crush a car parked a quarter of a mile away? Why did Oregon's highway department use dynamite for a beach cleanup? And after the explosion, who actually got rid of the whale?

November 1970. Near Florence, Oregon, a forty-five-foot sperm whale washed onto the sand. It was already dead. It weighed roughly eight tons, and after several days, the smell was becoming impossible to ignore.

The beach was the responsibility of the state highway division. Officials were also concerned about curious visitors getting too close to the decomposing body. Moving something that heavy was not simple. They worried that if they buried it near the surf, the sea could uncover it again. Cutting it up was another unpleasant option.

Then someone proposed treating the whale like a huge rock blocking a road. Break it into pieces with dynamite, let the sea and scavengers do the rest.

The department consulted people with explosives experience. The engineer overseeing the operation was George Thornton. On camera, before the blast, he admitted that the crew was not sure exactly how much explosive would break the body into pieces small enough for gulls and crabs. If large parts remained, there might be more work to do.

That uncertainty was the part nobody on the dunes could see.

On November twelfth, television reporter Paul Linnman and cameraman Doug Brazil came from Portland to film the operation. Newspaper reporter Larry Bacon was there too. About seventy-five spectators and journalists stood on dunes roughly a quarter of a mile from the whale. The distance seemed generous. Gulls had been gathering around the carcass, and the plan depended on scavengers being willing to finish the job.

The workers used about half a ton of dynamite. Their intended result was straightforward: pieces small enough to be carried off or eaten, with as much of the material as possible sent toward the water.

The blast threw an enormous column of sand and whale into the air. For a moment, the sight was so spectacular that spectators reacted as if the job had succeeded.

Then the larger pieces came down.

The cameras stopped. People on the dunes started running. Heavy chunks landed around the very people who had been moved back to safety. One piece, described as about the size of a coffee table, smashed onto a parked Oldsmobile and flattened its roof. Bacon's car had been parked nearby. No injuries from the explosion were reported.

The whale had made it to the cars. Most of the whale, however, had not made it off the beach.

Large portions of the carcass were still lying in the sand. The gulls the crew had been counting on were nowhere useful. The explosion had spread foul-smelling material across a much larger area, and the work was not finished.

As darkness approached, highway workers were burying the remaining pieces anyway. In a later interview, Bacon recalled Thornton believing the blast had mostly gone as expected, apart from the force traveling upward. That was Bacon's recollection of the engineer's assessment, not evidence that the beach was cleared. The workers still faced a substantial job that the scavengers were supposed to have done. The method they had originally rejected was now the method used to finish the cleanup.

There was a real consequence beyond a crushed car and an unforgettable news report. In 1979, forty-one sperm whales stranded and died on a beach south of Florence. Authorities considered how to remove them, but blasting them apart was quickly ruled out. The bodies were buried.

That is how this story ends: not with a second explosion, and not with anyone hurt, but with the discovery that making a whale disappear and making a problem disappear are very different jobs.
