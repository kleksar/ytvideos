How could a dead whale crush a car parked a quarter of a mile away? Why was a highway engineer handling a beach cleanup? And after the explosion, who actually removed the whale?

In November 1970, a forty-five-foot sperm whale washed ashore near Florence, Oregon. It was already dead. The eight-ton carcass lay on the sand for days, decomposing. People came to look, and officials worried that visitors might climb onto it or fall through its weakening surface.

Oregon's State Highway Division was responsible for the beach. Its workers needed to remove the whale, but none of the options looked good. Burying it near the tide line might only let the sea uncover it again. Cutting it apart would be a difficult job. Burning it was ruled out.

The department consulted people with explosives experience. A whale was not a rock, but the proposed solution borrowed from roadwork: break the obstruction into smaller pieces and let the sea, gulls, and crabs dispose of what remained.

That second part was essential. The explosion was only supposed to begin the cleanup.

Engineer George Thornton oversaw the operation. In an interview before the blast, he expressed confidence in the plan, then acknowledged that the crew did not know exactly how much dynamite would produce pieces small enough for scavengers. He knew large chunks might require more work.

So the crowd was about to watch a solution whose most important result nobody could predict.

On November twelfth, television reporter Paul Linnman and cameraman Doug Brazil arrived to film it. Newspaper reporter Larry Bacon, new to his job, came too. Roughly seventy-five spectators and journalists stood on dunes about a quarter of a mile back. That seemed a comfortable distance from the carcass. The crew hoped to send the fragments toward the water, away from those watching.

Workers used about half a ton of dynamite. The blast threw a towering column of sand and whale into the sky. At first the spectators reacted to the spectacle.

Then they saw where the larger pieces were going.

Linnman and Brazil stopped filming and ran. The people who had been moved back for safety scattered as heavy fragments fell around them. One piece, later compared to a coffee table, crushed the roof of a parked car. Bacon's own car was beside it. He later remembered smaller particles falling like a blubber snowstorm, with the smell clinging to people's clothes.

No explosion-related injuries were reported. But a piece of the whale had reached the cars, while much of the whale still had not left the beach.

Large sections of the carcass remained on the sand. Other pieces had been spread across a wider area. The gulls did not come in to finish the job. Instead of clearing one large obstacle, workers now had to collect its scattered remains.

By evening, they were burying the whale after all.

Bacon later recalled that Thornton described the operation as going largely to plan, except that the force had gone upward. That was the reporter's account of Thornton's assessment, not an established explanation of the blast or proof of successful cleanup. The physical result was plain: the planned sea-bound fragments had landed among spectators, and the burial the crew had hoped to avoid was still needed.

Nine years later, forty-one more sperm whales stranded and died south of Florence. Authorities again faced the question of disposal. This time they quickly set aside dynamite and buried the bodies.

The 1970 whale had been broken apart. The beach hadn't been cleared by the blast. The lasting result was a smashed car, a news film, and a very different decision when whales next washed ashore.
