How can a beach keep finding the same telephone for more than thirty years? And if someone discovers where the phones came from, does that finally stop them washing ashore?

On the coast of Brittany in western France, volunteers kept collecting pieces of a very particular object. Bright orange plastic. A cat's face. Sometimes two eyes. Sometimes a keypad with a curly cord still attached.

These were Garfield telephones, real household landlines shaped like the cartoon character. The eyes opened when you lifted the receiver. It was a novelty product from the nineteen eighties, long after which its broken parts continued appearing in the Atlantic surf.

Claire Simonin-Le Meur led a local beach-cleaning group called Ar Viltansoù. They normally collected fishing gear and other washed-up rubbish. But Garfield kept turning up alongside it. In 2018 alone, the group counted more than two hundred phone fragments. They could put the pieces in a bag. They couldn't stop more arriving.

Where were the phones coming from? A lost cargo container seemed plausible. If someone could find it, perhaps they could remove the remaining stock before it scattered any farther. The volunteers had no location to search. They had even asked people familiar with diving in the area, without finding the supposed container.

Then, in 2019, after the story attracted publicity, a local farmer named René Morvan approached the group's leader. He wasn't offering another theory. He remembered actually seeing the phones, decades earlier, inside a container.

Morvan said a storm in the nineteen eighties had scattered the orange objects along the shore. He and his brother went exploring and found a damaged container wedged into a cave in the cliffs. He could show the volunteers the place.

That was a startling lead, but not yet proof. The group had expected to search underwater. Morvan was pointing to a rock opening that sometimes disappeared beneath the tide. Getting there required knowing the coast and waiting for the sea to retreat. The cave was hazardous; they couldn't simply walk in whenever they wanted.

Bad weather delayed the expedition for weeks. On March twenty-second, 2019, Simonin-Le Meur and several volunteers finally went with the low tide. They crossed slippery, seaweed-covered rocks. Near the entrance they spotted orange phone parts.

Deeper in the fissure were rusted pieces of metal. Under the boulders lay more telephone components, including twenty-three complete handsets with their wires and electronics.

Morvan's memory had led them to a real find. The container had indeed held Garfield phones. There was no longer any need to guess why those particular objects kept reaching the beach.

But the discovery created a worse answer to the volunteers' original problem.

They had hoped that locating the container would let them stop the supply. Instead, what remained of the container was mostly empty, and the inaccessible portion was still lodged among rocks. The sea had already spread much of the cargo over decades. Recovering twenty-three handsets couldn't recover the countless smaller pieces.

The investigators also did not establish which ship had carried the container, precisely when it had been lost, or whether one container could account for every phone found along the coastline. Those questions remained open.

And finding the source did not clean the water. After the expedition, Simonin-Le Meur described more beach cleanups that still brought in Garfield fragments.

The funny face had served another purpose by then. The volunteers used it to help children notice something they might overlook if it were an ordinary shard of plastic. A recognizable cat could make people stop, ask where it came from, and understand that a product thrown into the sea does not stay in one place.

So the people who solved the mystery kept doing the same work they had done before. They could now point to the cave and explain the orange pieces. They still had to pick them up.

A telephone mystery had an answer. The cleanup did not have an ending.
