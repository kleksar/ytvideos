Why did pieces of the same orange telephone keep washing onto French beaches for more than thirty years? And if someone finally found the source, could they stop the phones from coming back?

On the Brittany coast, the pieces were hard to mistake. A pair of cartoon eyes. A striped orange back. Sometimes a keypad and a curly cord. They belonged to real household telephones shaped like Garfield the cat. Lift the receiver of an intact one and its eyes would open.

For beach cleaner Claire Simonin-Le Meur and the volunteers of Ar Viltansoù, Garfield was no longer just a curiosity. They collected fishing waste and plastic along the coast. In 2018 alone, they counted more than two hundred Garfield fragments. Taking those pieces away did not prevent more arriving with the tides.

The volunteers had a theory: a shipping container full of the phones had been lost at sea. They asked people familiar with diving nearby to help locate it. But nobody could give them a position. Until they found a container, they could not know whether it still held phones that might escape.

Then, in 2019, Claire met a local farmer named René Morvan while people were dealing with dolphins washed ashore. The strange phone story had recently received publicity. Morvan said he knew where the orange phones had come from. He could show her the place.

That was the first real change in the search. The volunteers had imagined a container on the seabed. Morvan was talking about a cave in the cliffs.

He told Claire that after a storm in the nineteen eighties, he and his brother had gone looking for the phones scattered across the beach. According to his recollection, they found an open, damaged container caught among the rocks, with Garfield phones inside. Decades later, he still knew the location. But a memory was not yet proof that the wreckage remained there.

There was another problem. The cave could be reached only when the sea retreated far enough. At high tide, water covered the entrance. The rocks were steep and slippery. Even with Morvan's directions, the volunteers could not simply walk in.

Bad weather delayed them for weeks. On March twenty-second, 2019, Claire and a small group reached the cave during a low tide. Near the opening they spotted orange fragments. Then came rusting pieces of metal, caught among the rocks.

Under boulders they found telephone parts and twenty-three complete handsets, some with wiring and electronics still attached. The objects matched Morvan's account. There really had been Garfield phones in a container inside that cave.

It was the answer to the mystery. But it wasn't the result the beach cleaners had hoped for.

They had wanted to find a remaining stock of phones before the sea carried it away. Instead, the damaged container was largely empty. Parts of the wreckage were still trapped in inaccessible rock. The recovered handsets were physical proof of the source, not a way to retrieve the countless fragments already scattered along the coast.

Even the discovery had limits. Investigators did not establish which ship had carried the container or the exact date of its loss. They could not rule out another container. Finding this one cave did not prove that it explained every fragment on every beach.

After the expedition, Claire still described finding Garfield pieces during cleanups. The volunteers could finally explain one source of the strange objects, but they still had to remove what the sea had already spread.

They also used the recognizable orange face to show children what happens to products lost in the ocean. A broken phone eye caught attention in a way that an anonymous shard of plastic might not. That made the long search useful beyond solving a riddle, though it did not clean a single beach by itself.

Morvan had shown them where to look. The volunteers had found the container and twenty-three handsets. And on the next reported cleanups, they were still collecting Garfield fragments.
