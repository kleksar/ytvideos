Neil Armstrong's spacecraft was spinning so fast he could barely read the controls.

This was 1966, three years before the Moon landing. It was his first trip into space.

He and David Scott had just docked their Gemini capsule with another spacecraft. Nobody had done that before.

Then the two vehicles began to roll. Armstrong steadied them, but the roll came back.

They couldn't reach Mission Control. They thought the trouble might be in the unmanned craft attached to them.

So they pulled away from it. The spin got faster.

Without the mass of the other spacecraft, the small capsule was turning almost once a second.

The two men were close to blacking out.

Armstrong switched off the normal control thrusters.

He fired the thrusters meant to keep them steady during reentry. The capsule stopped spinning.

The fault had been in Gemini all along: one thruster was stuck on. They only worked that out afterward.

Stopping the spin used almost three-quarters of the fuel in the reentry system. Under the flight rules, they had to come home.

Scott's spacewalk was cancelled. They splashed down early in the Pacific, both alive.

How close had they come to not making it home?
