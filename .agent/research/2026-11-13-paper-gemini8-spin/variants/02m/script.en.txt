Neil Armstrong's spacecraft began spinning so fast that Earth swept past the window almost once every second.

This was his first spaceflight, in 1966. He and David Scott had just completed the first docking of two spacecraft in orbit.

Their Gemini capsule was connected to an unmanned target called Agena. Scott was supposed to go on a spacewalk later in the mission. First, the crew had more docking tests to do.

But after the docking, the joined vehicles began rolling without permission.

Scott shut down the target's steering jets. Armstrong used Gemini's controls. For a moment, that helped. Then the motion returned.

They were out of radio contact with Mission Control. At first they suspected Agena. But Gemini's fuel reading was falling. The crew detached.

That was when the problem became terrifying.

Without the heavy target attached, Gemini spun faster, approaching one full rotation every second. The astronauts could barely see their instruments. Passing out was a real danger.

Armstrong switched off the normal steering system and used a separate set of jets reserved for reentry. The capsule finally steadied.

But the rescue had a price. The reentry system had used much of its fuel, and flight rules required an early landing. Scott's spacewalk was cancelled.

Both men splashed down safely in the Pacific. Engineers later traced the trouble to a Gemini steering thruster that had stuck on.
