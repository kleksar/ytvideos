How do you stop a spacecraft that is turning almost once every second? Why did leaving another spacecraft make the danger worse? And how could saving the crew end a mission that had just made history?

In March 1966, Neil Armstrong was not yet the man who had walked on the Moon. He was making his first spaceflight, alongside David Scott. Their spacecraft, Gemini VIII, was built for two people. They had a three-day plan: catch an unmanned target in orbit, dock with it, and later send Scott outside for a spacewalk.

Docking mattered because future crews would need to connect vehicles in space. NASA had practiced the maneuver on Earth. Now Armstrong was approaching a real target, Agena, with nothing underneath either vehicle but Earth.

He drew close enough for Scott to inspect the target. Then he lined up the docking port and eased forward. The latches connected. For the first time, two spacecraft had docked in orbit.

That success did not last half an hour before the mission changed.

The joined vehicles began to roll unexpectedly. At first, the astronauts suspected the Agena. Scott shut down its steering jets while Armstrong tried to keep the combined craft steady using Gemini's. The roll eased, then came back, stronger and on more than one axis.

They could not simply call Houston and ask what to do. Their orbit had carried them out of the ground stations' radio range.

Armstrong checked the fuel indicators. Gemini's steering fuel was disappearing. That was a warning that the problem might be on their own side of the docking port.

He separated Gemini from Agena. The heavy target drifted away. Instead of settling down, the capsule accelerated into a much faster spin.

Here was the trap: attached to Agena, the faulty steering force had been turning a large joined vehicle. Alone, Gemini was much smaller. The same unwanted thrust could swing it around faster.

The rate approached a full revolution each second. Armstrong and Scott had trouble reading their instruments. If they lost consciousness, nobody on the ground could reach in and take the controls.

Armstrong had one remaining way to fight the rotation. Gemini carried ordinary steering jets for maneuvers in orbit and a separate control system intended for reentry. He shut down the ordinary system and fired the reentry jets against the spin.

The movement slowed. Finally, they had a stable capsule again.

Only now did the second problem become clear. Using the reentry system to stop the spin had consumed most of the propellant they would normally reserve for getting home. Under the flight rules, using that emergency system meant the mission had to end at the next suitable landing opportunity.

Scott would not make his planned spacewalk. There would be no further docking tests. The achievement had lasted, but the planned three days in orbit were gone.

NASA shifted the landing from the Atlantic to a contingency area in the western Pacific, east of Okinawa. The capsule reentered the atmosphere and splashed down after a flight of just ten hours and forty-one minutes. Rescue personnel reached the astronauts, and a Navy destroyer took them aboard.

The investigation found the culprit inside Gemini: a steering thruster had remained on when it should have shut off, probably because of an electrical short. The crew's initial suspicion of Agena had been understandable, but wrong.

Armstrong and Scott had completed the first docking in space, then survived a failure that could have ended both lives. Their rescue was also a demonstration of why a second control system mattered: the system that ended their mission was the one that allowed them to return.
