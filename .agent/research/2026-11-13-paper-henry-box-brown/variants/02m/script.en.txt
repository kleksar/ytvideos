“THIS SIDE UP WITH CARE.” That was the warning on a wooden crate in Virginia in 1849.
Inside was Henry Brown, an enslaved man trying to escape.
But the crate was turned upside down before his journey had even begun.
Months earlier, Brown's wife and three children had been sold away from him.
He devised an escape in which he would travel as cargo.
Friends helped seal him in a box with three small air holes.
He carried some water, but had barely enough space to curl up.
The box passed from a wagon to a train, then a steamboat.
On the boat it was tipped onto his head again.
Brown later wrote that his eyes seemed to swell and his temples throbbed.
He endured the pain in silence for roughly two hours.
Then two men turned the box upright, finally relieving the pressure.
One even asked what the box contained. The other guessed: mail.
After twenty-seven hours, the crate reached Philadelphia.
Abolitionists opened it, and Brown came out a free man.
He later became a stage performer, reenacting the escape for audiences.
The crate was no longer something he had to survive: it was a story he could tell.
That first box, though, carried a warning its handlers repeatedly ignored.
