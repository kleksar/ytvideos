“THIS SIDE UP WITH CARE.” The warning was painted on a crate leaving Richmond in 1849. Workers turned it upside down anyway. Inside was Henry Brown, a man escaping slavery.

His wife Nancy and their three children had been sold away the year before. Brown could not stop it. Two helpers arranged to ship him to abolitionists in Philadelphia.

They gave him water, drilled three air holes and nailed the lid shut. Handlers saw ordinary freight. That secrecy kept him hidden but left him helpless.

At the express office, workers stood the box on its end, leaving Brown head-down. On the train it lay properly. Then aboard a steamboat, he was turned head-down again. Brown later wrote that he felt his eyes swelling and pressure building in his temples. He stayed silent rather than expose the escape.

After what he estimated to be roughly two hours, two men looking for a seat rolled the crate onto its side. They sat on it, unaware they had relieved him. One guessed the box contained mail.

After about twenty-seven hours, the crate reached Philadelphia. Abolitionists opened it. Brown stepped out alive and free. But his wife and children were still gone; the surviving records do not establish whether they reunited.

The escape became famous. After the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act, Brown went to Britain and reenacted his journey before audiences.

On stage, he decided when to leave the box. During the real journey, he could not even decide which way it stood. His friends couldn't tell the handlers who was inside. They could leave only one instruction.
