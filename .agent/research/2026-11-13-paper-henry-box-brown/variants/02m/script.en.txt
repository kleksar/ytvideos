“THIS SIDE UP WITH CARE.” Those words were painted on a wooden crate in Richmond in 1849. The handlers stood it on its end anyway. There was a living man inside.

Henry Brown worked in a tobacco factory, but he was enslaved. The previous year, his wife Nancy and their three children had been sold away to North Carolina. He could not stop the sale. To escape, Brown chose a route on which freight could travel without being questioned as a passenger.

Two helpers arranged for antislavery supporters in Philadelphia to receive the shipment. They gave Brown water, made three small holes for air and nailed him into a box so tight he had to curl up. The handlers would see a package. They would not know why its position mattered.

At the express office, the box was stood with Brown's head down. On the train it was righted. Then, aboard a steamboat, it was placed head-down again. Brown later wrote that pressure built around his eyes and temples. He stayed silent because calling out might expose the escape. He estimated that he endured the position for about two hours.

Then two men needed somewhere to sit. They laid the box on its side and sat on it. Without knowing, they relieved his suffering. One of them guessed the crate contained mail.

After roughly twenty-seven hours, abolitionists in Philadelphia opened the box. Brown was free. But Nancy and the children were still gone; the surviving record does not establish that he ever saw them again.

His very public escape made the disguise harder to repeat. One helper was later imprisoned after a different escape effort. After the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act, Brown went to Britain, where he reenacted his escape for audiences.

The first time, strangers decided when his box moved. On stage, Brown chose when to step out. He no longer had to trust his life to the warning painted on the wood.
