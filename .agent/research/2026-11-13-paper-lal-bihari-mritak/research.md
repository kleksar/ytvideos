## Audience and promise
English-speaking viewers drawn to everyday absurdity, a determined ordinary person and a concrete reversal of a system. **One-sentence story:** After relatives allegedly had Lal Bihari marked dead in Indian land records, he spent eighteen years trying to prove he was alive and founded an association for others trapped by the same fraud.

**Stop:** a living man is told he's dead while seeking a loan. **Continue:** real attempted legal and bureaucratic tactics—complaints, widow pension, election campaigns, police files—fail to shift the land register, until collective action broadens the fight. **Payoff:** 1994 correction after eighteen years, continuing help for others, later official 2003 Ig Nobel Peace Prize. Tone: dry absurdity grounded in land dispossession and actual livelihood costs, never a joke at the victim's expense.

## Three competing short pitches (same decision bar)
**A — Lal Bihari (chosen).** Audience: English-speaking viewers who understand bank paperwork and unfair administrative traps. Exact opening: “Lal Bihari went to a bank in India for a loan. A clerk told him he was dead.” Stop: an impossible live/dead contradiction. Stay: widow's pension application, elections, attempted arrest, Association of the Dead. Payoff: registered alive in 1994 after 18 years; prize recognized later activism. Objection: the relatives' alleged bribery must be attributed, and there is a risk of piling up similar attempts rather than escalation. The script moves from one individual's records to collective action and correction.

**B — Great Stork Derby (strongest alternative).** Audience: history-of-absurd-wills and unusual legal disputes. Exact opening: “A Toronto lawyer died and left his money to whichever mother had the most babies over the next ten years.” Stop: an actual bizarre will. Stay: decade of competition and the question whether the contested clause survives court. Payoff: the Supreme Court upheld the will and four women tied with nine children each. Objection: treating the births and Depression-era financial desperation as an amusing game would be exploitative; numerous legal conditions would consume a short's running time.

**C — Boston molasses flood.** Audience: unusual disasters and engineering accountability. Exact opening: “A huge tank burst in Boston, and a wave of molasses swept through the streets.” Stop: the unlikely fluid as a deadly hazard. Stay: prior leaks, destruction and official negligence inquiry. Payoff: 1925 finding of negligence and changes to safety accountability, with 21 fatalities. Objection: grim human loss and engineering inquiry can clash with a light oddity opening; closer to existing disaster shorts.

**Winner vs nearest alternative B:** B has stronger one-line premise but much of the middle would repeat births/counting or legal exposition; A's attempts visibly change process (pension → ballots → police → collective advocacy), and its final official correction is about an identifiable person. A remains vulnerable to an independent first listener finding those three failed approaches repetitive; if that happens, reject or rewrite. The difference is narrative progression and human consequence, not generation ease or a view score.
