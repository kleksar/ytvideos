Lal Bihari went to a bank in India for a loan. A clerk told him he was dead.

Bihari was standing right there. But the land register said his inherited plot now belonged to relatives.

It was 1976. Bihari said those relatives had bribed officials to enter his death in the records.

He filed complaints and went to court. The entry didn't change.

If he was officially dead, why wasn't his wife entitled to a widow's pension? He tried to claim one for her.

That went nowhere. He began searching for another official record that could confirm he existed.

He ran in elections, including against former prime ministers. At least his name might appear on a ballot.

He even tried getting arrested. A police file would have to identify the person being arrested.

But his death entry still stayed in the land register.

He started signing his name Lal Bihari 'Mritak'. In Hindi, that last word means 'dead'.

As he argued with officials, he met other people whose land records also called them dead.

They'd lost their land on paper, just as he had.

In 1980 he founded the Association of the Dead, to fight for their records as well as his own.

In 1994, officials finally reversed his death entry. It had taken eighteen years.

He kept the name Mritak and carried on helping other people.

In 2003, the campaign earned him an Ig Nobel Peace Prize.

The association kept helping people who walked into an office alive, only to find a different story in the records.
