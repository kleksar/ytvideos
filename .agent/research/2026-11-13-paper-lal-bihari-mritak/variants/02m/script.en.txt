A loan for his weaving business. That was what Lal Bihari was trying to arrange when a land official in northern India told him he was dead.

According to a land-register entry dated 1976, Bihari's inherited plot now belonged to relatives. Bihari later accused them of bribing officials to record his death.

He hired lawyers and filed complaints. Years passed. He said the fight cost him his business.

If the government considered him dead, perhaps his wife could get a widow's pension. He applied for one on her behalf. It didn't get his record corrected.

In 1980 he adopted the name Mritak, meaning dead in Hindi, and founded the Association of the Dead. He began meeting other people who had lost land through similar entries.

He even stood in elections. A man whose name could appear on a ballot still couldn't get his death entry removed.

By May 1994, according to Bihari, local land officials had recommended a correction. Then the file disappeared before the decision was completed.

He protested again. In June, the land administration finally restored his living status. The death entry had stood for eighteen years.

Bihari kept his new name and continued fighting for other people. His campaign earned him the Ig Nobel Peace Prize in 2003.

He had spent much of his working life getting one piece of paper corrected. What had brought him to the land office in the first place?
