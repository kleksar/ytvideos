Lal Bihari went to collect documents for a bank loan. A land official told him he was dead.

He was standing in front of the official. Yet a land register in northern India recorded his death, and his inherited plot had been transferred to relatives.

The death entry was dated 1976. Bihari alleged that members of his family had bribed officials. Now the entry that took his land would not be corrected.

He complained, hired lawyers and visited offices. Nothing changed. The problem wasn't whether people could see him breathing. It was getting his name restored to the record.

He even applied for a widow's pension for his wife. If officials considered him dead, surely his wife could claim it? That attempt failed too.

Then he stood in elections. A ballot could carry the name of a man whom the land office insisted was dead. Still no correction.

He tried to provoke a police case against himself. Even that failed to produce the proof he needed.

So he added a word to his name: Mritak. In Hindi, it means dead.

In 1980 he started an Association of the Dead. He had found other living people whose land had been taken after their names were marked deceased.

The fight became bigger than his own plot.

In June 1994, eighteen years after his recorded death, officials finally restored his living status. He continued helping other people in the same trap.

In 2003 he received an Ig Nobel Peace Prize for the campaign.

He was alive again on paper. But the association still had other people to fight for. And his fight had begun the day
