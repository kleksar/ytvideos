How do you prove you're alive when the office holding your land records says you're dead? Why didn't lawyers, an election or even a police investigation settle it? And how did one man's case become an association for other living people?

Lal Bihari was a weaver in northern India. He wanted a loan for his business. The bank required documents, so he went to his family's village to obtain them.

There, a land official confronted him with a record of his own death.

Bihari was standing in the office. The land record said that his inherited property had passed to relatives. According to Bihari, those relatives had paid officials to declare him dead.

The entry was dated 1976. A stroke of a pen in a land register could transfer a field to somebody else. Undoing that stroke was much harder.

Bihari wanted his name back. He went to lawyers, submitted complaints and visited government offices. But the same system responsible for the land records was now expected to correct them.

The stakes went far beyond a strange piece of paper. Bihari later said the struggle cost him his weaving business and his livelihood. His family had to live with the consequences while he travelled from one office to another.

He tried a different route. If he was officially dead, his wife ought to qualify for a widow's pension. He applied on her behalf. It didn't get his record corrected.

In 1980 Bihari began calling himself Lal Bihari Mritak — Lal Bihari, the dead man. He founded an Association of the Dead, initially a way to make his case visible.

Then the name stopped being merely a protest. He met other people who said their relatives had used land records to take property from them in the same way.

His search for an official record also led to a serious act of misconduct.

He later recounted taking a young relative away in the hope that the family would file a police complaint. A police report would have to name him. The complaint he wanted didn't materialize. He returned the boy. It was a dangerous and wrong attempt, and it still didn't solve the land case.

He took his protest to the state legislature instead. In the mid-1980s he scattered leaflets during a session and was detained. His case became public, but his land record stayed unchanged.

In 1988 he stood against V. P. Singh, who would later become prime minister. In 1989 he stood against Rajiv Gandhi. His name could appear on election papers while he remained dead in the land register.

By May 1994 even local land officials had recommended correcting the record. Bihari recalled that the file disappeared before the order could be completed.

He protested again. In June 1994, the correction finally went through.

Eighteen years after being listed as dead, Lal Bihari was recorded as living. The land dispute that began with one entry had taken much of his working life to untangle.

But the organization he had created outlived the case. Other people were still fighting to recover a name and a plot of land. In 2003 Bihari received an Ig Nobel Peace Prize for that campaign.

A single document had taken away a man's land. Eighteen years of actions, many of them extraordinary, were needed to get that document changed.
