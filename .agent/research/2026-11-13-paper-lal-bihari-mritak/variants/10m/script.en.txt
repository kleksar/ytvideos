How can a living man be recorded as dead by the office holding his land records? Why did elections and public protests fail to get his name restored? And why did other people still need his help even after he won his own case?

In a village in northern India, land could be a family's most valuable asset. The register didn't merely describe who lived on a plot. Its entries helped determine who could claim it.

Lal Bihari worked as a weaver. He wanted a bank loan for his business. The bank needed documents, and he went back to his family's village to collect them.

He expected paperwork about his identity and property. He discovered that the local land office had recorded his death.

Bihari was alive. He had travelled there himself. But an entry said his inherited land had passed to relatives. He later accused family members of bribing officials to put his death on paper.

The disputed record dated to 1976.

Think about the position that put him in. He could stand in a room and speak to an official. He could bring people who knew him. But the institution responsible for the land record was treating a written entry as the problem's starting point, not as an error that could simply be erased.

The land was gone from his name. So he went to a lawyer. He filed complaints. He visited offices and asked the officials to correct it.

In his later account, some officials laughed at him. He described how the people reviewing a complaint were connected to the very system that had made the entry.

Nothing moved.

And the delay had a cost. Bihari said he lost his weaving business and spent money on lawyers. His wife and children lived through years when he was pursuing documents instead of earning as before. The injustice was absurd, but its effect on their lives was real.

Around 1980 he received advice that changed the way he protested. If the authorities insisted he was dead, he could make that claim public.

He added Mritak to his name. It means dead in Hindi. Instead of hiding the label, he put it on his correspondence. A living man was signing papers as a dead one.

He founded the Association of the Dead. It was initially just an extraordinary title attached to his struggle. But the title drew attention, and he began meeting others with similar land disputes.

A reporter in 2009 described two cases that show why the association mattered. One man, Deepchand, had left his village to work. When he came back, he discovered that his name had disappeared from the land records and that a brother had taken his share. Another man, Ram Lallak, told the reporter he had been restored to life on paper, then lost that status again through appeals.

A register can be altered. But if an inheritance, a court process and another person's property claim are built on the alteration, reversing it can take years.

Bihari was still fighting his own case in the 1980s. He kept looking for another official record that could contradict the first.

Consider a widow's pension. A pension is meant for the surviving spouse of someone who died. Bihari applied for one on behalf of his wife. If the state thought he was dead, perhaps a decision on the claim would force officials to acknowledge it.

The attempt did not restore his land entry. In his account, the refusal did not even mention him in a useful way.

He tried the police route too. He later said he offered money to a policeman to register an offence involving him. Once the policeman learned the purpose, the report did not emerge.

Then he did something far more serious.

In 1985, according to his own later account, he took a young relative from school and kept him with him for several days. He wanted the child's family to file a complaint, which would require the police to name a living suspect.

The complaint did not come, and Bihari returned the boy. This was a serious act of misconduct that put a child at risk. It did not resolve the land dispute.

A journalist then helped publicize his case. A politician raised questions about it in the state legislature.

The issue was finally reaching people outside the land office. Yet coverage and official correction turned out to be different things.

In 1986 Bihari went to the state assembly with leaflets. During a sitting, he scattered them into the chamber and shouted for recognition. He was detained.

That was another government process identifying him as a person physically present. Still the crucial property record did not change.

Next came elections.

In 1988 he stood in a parliamentary contest against V. P. Singh, who later became prime minister. In 1989 he filed papers against Rajiv Gandhi. Bihari was no political front-runner. What he wanted from these campaigns was an official trail: a name on a candidate list, a vote count, the public absurdity of a 'dead' man standing for office.

He even argued that an election should be challenged because it involved a supposedly dead candidate. The election went ahead.

So here was the strange result. He could become visible to newspapers, voters, police and legislators. Yet a correction to the original land record required action from the land administration.

By the early 1990s Bihari's campaign had become known well beyond his village. He continued seeking a formal decision.

In May 1994, by his account, local land officials recommended correcting his status. At last, the people whose records mattered were moving.

Then, he said, the file vanished before the decision was finished.

After so many years, a missing file was another way for a living man to stay dead on paper.

Bihari threatened a new protest at the office and prepared notices about it. In June 1994, the land administration finally issued an order restoring his living status.

The exact dates in later reports are not always consistent about when he first learned of his supposed death. But the central span is well documented: his record dated from 1976, and he regained official recognition in 1994.

Eighteen years.

Bihari had wanted papers for a loan. Instead he spent a large part of his adult life struggling with papers about his existence.

Some accounts say that after his victory Bihari allowed the relatives involved in the dispute to keep or use the land. His campaigning continued.

Bihari used the Association of the Dead to press those cases. His son and other literate helpers assisted him with piles of documents. The work was less spectacular than the demonstrations, but the papers mattered to each person whose land was at stake.

In 2003 the Ig Nobel Peace Prize recognized Bihari's campaign. He kept the surname Mritak even after officials accepted he was living.

In 2018, reporting showed Bihari pursuing compensation for the years he had lost. That report described an ongoing court process; it did not establish a final award. The fight over the consequences continued beyond the restoration of his name.

Bihari kept the name Mritak. The word he had once used to draw attention to his own case now belonged to a campaign for people whose names had still not been restored.
