**One-sentence story:** In 1982 a man lifted an ordinary patio chair into airliner-used airspace with 42 helium balloons, had to improvise his descent after dropping his gun, survived snagging power lines, and his chair ultimately entered the Smithsonian.

**Start:** familiar furniture in an impossible place. **Stay:** ordinary lift becomes extreme altitude; his planned descent tool works then falls; a second correction affects the landing; powerline snag. **Satisfaction:** he survives, is fined, and a child given the chair eventually donates it to an authentic museum. Tone: comic curiosity under real danger, without a fabricated villain or tragic outcome.

## Three comparable, complete pitches

### 1. A — Larry Walters (winner)
- Audience: English general curiosity and inventive-human-story Shorts.
- Stop: A garden chair is at airliner's altitude. First narration: “Sixteen thousand feet above California, a man was floating through the sky in a lawn chair.”
- Continue: 42 helium balloons → unintended altitude → sighting by pilots → shooting balloons → losing gun → dumping water → power-line landing.
- Receive/payoff: Alive, fined, gave chair to a child; child donated it to the Smithsonian.
- Main objection: Well-known folklore retold elsewhere; early balloon reveal could exhaust novelty unless later concrete decisions hold interest.

### 2. B — Pepsi jet (strongest alternative)
- Audience: English advertising trivia and legal-scheme followers.
- Stop: Fighter jet shown for seven million redeemable points. First narration: “A soda company put a fighter jet in its prize ad, and someone tried to order it for seven million points.”
- Continue: Commercial → purchasable extra points → raises $700k → order attempt → Pepsi rejection → contract-law battle.
- Receive/payoff: 1999 court says reasonable viewer would not take commercial as actual serious offer.
- Main objection: Ending predictable to casual audience; existing repository research and 2022 streaming documentary reduce novelty.

### 3. C — fake ship surgeon Ferdinand Demara
- Audience: English impostor true-story audience.
- Stop: A successful ship's surgeon lacked qualifications and used another man's documents. First narration: “A ship's doctor helped save injured soldiers, and the Navy was preparing to honor him — until it learned his name wasn't even real.”
- Continue: False enlistment → textbook/treatments → serious wounded men → commendation publicity → real doctor's family recognizes identity.
- Receive/payoff: 1951 official discharge for fraud; Canadian House of Commons confirms case.
- Main objection: Must not glamorize untrained surgery; adjacent naval subjects saturate current Draft queue.


**Larry versus strongest alternative Pepsi:** Pepsi has broader recognizable corporate identity and a primary court decision, but its conclusion (no jet) is already strongly anticipated and the private repo already contains November 6 Pepsi research/consultation. Larry instead has two genuinely different intervention attempts and a verified surviving physical artifact, plus is tonally unlike pending naval/space/crime rescue Draft PRs. This is editorial inference, **not** a view/CTR forecast.
