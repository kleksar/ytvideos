How can thousands of barrels of maple syrup disappear from a warehouse without anyone noticing? And why would thieves put water back into barrels they had already emptied?

In Quebec, maple syrup is more than something poured over pancakes. Producers make it during a short season, but customers buy it all year. Their organization stored part of the harvest in a reserve, releasing syrup when supplies were tight. One rented warehouse in Saint-Louis-de-Blandford held thousands of heavy metal barrels.

On July 30, 2012, accountant Michel Gauvreau came to check the inventory. The barrels were stacked six high. A full one weighed around six hundred and twenty pounds. As he climbed, a barrel shifted under his weight, and he almost fell.

That should have been impossible for a full barrel. He shook it. It was empty. He checked others and found more.

He alerted the producer organization. Inspectors returned and opened a barrel that looked full. Instead of thick brown syrup, they found clear water.

Now the problem changed. This was not an inventory mistake. Somebody had made a warehouse full of valuable syrup look untouched while its contents disappeared.

The reserve existed because the harvest was unpredictable. In years with plenty of syrup, producers could store some rather than flood the market. During leaner years they could release it. A single barrel represented hundreds of pounds of processed product. And this particular warehouse was a rented storage site, not a government vault.

Investigators reconstructed an operation that had run for roughly a year. Syrup left the warehouse in barrels. Some containers were emptied elsewhere and brought back with water. The product passed through middlemen and was resold outside Quebec. By moving something ordinary in industrial quantities, the thieves made a huge loss difficult to notice at a glance.

But the fake weight was not a perfect disguise. Empty containers rocked when someone expected them to be solid. Other containers held water, which looked nothing like syrup once opened. A routine inventory finally forced someone to inspect what was inside.

The eventual count was 9,571 stolen barrels. Their market value exceeded eighteen million Canadian dollars. The missing stock was not a pile of cash that police could simply recover intact. Much of the syrup had already entered commercial supply chains.

Quebec police launched a major investigation and arrested sixteen people. One important figure was Richard Vallières, who helped sell the stolen syrup. At his trial, he said the syrup had been sold for about ten million dollars and that his own profit was roughly one million.

The criminal sentence was eight years in prison. But the money question went on for years. The trial judge imposed a fine exceeding nine million dollars because property he had sold could not be returned. An appeals court reduced that amount to around his personal profit.

So in 2022 Canada's Supreme Court had to decide something unusual: when stolen goods are gone, does a thief owe what he personally kept, or the value of what he sold?

The court restored the larger fine. The judgment did not mean he had already paid it. It meant that the law could not let the cost of accomplices and transport turn stolen property into a smaller financial obligation.

The theft had started with trucks, middlemen and a warehouse that appeared full. It unraveled when one accountant discovered a barrel that did not weigh what it should.
