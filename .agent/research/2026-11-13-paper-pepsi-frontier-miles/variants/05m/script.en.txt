How did a Pepsi commercial turn into a seven-hundred-thousand-dollar order for a fighter jet? Why did the company refuse it? And why did a different airline bring the dispute back thirty years later?

In the mid-1990s, Pepsi offered customers something simple: collect points printed on drinks, then exchange them for prizes. Shirts, sunglasses, jackets. The television ads showed those items with their point prices.

Then a Harrier fighter jet landed outside a school. On screen: seven million Pepsi Points.

John Leonard, a young man living in Seattle, saw that number and opened the actual rewards catalog. He wanted to find out whether he could claim the aircraft.

At first, seven million points sounded impossible. But the order instructions contained a way around collecting seven million packages. Customers could purchase missing points for ten cents apiece, provided they included at least fifteen original points from Pepsi packaging.

That turned an outrageous number into an expensive calculation. Fifteen real points, nearly seven million bought points, and roughly seven hundred thousand dollars. A Harrier cost many millions more.

Leonard approached acquaintances for backing. On about March 27, 1996, he sent an order form, fifteen original points, and a check for exactly $700,008.50. He had written “1 Harrier Jet” into the item column himself.

There was a problem, though. The television ad displayed the jet's price, but the catalog's list of redeemable prizes did not include any aircraft.

Pepsi's fulfillment company returned the check in May. Its letter said only catalog merchandise could be ordered and that the jet in the commercial was meant as humor. It even enclosed coupons for free Pepsi products.

Leonard's lawyers answered with a demand for the aircraft. The ad agency also replied that the scene was a joke. Neither side accepted the other's interpretation, and the argument became a legal fight.

One detail makes this more interesting than a customer complaining about a misleading price. Pepsi went to court in New York seeking a declaration that it did not have to deliver a jet. Leonard then brought his own action in Florida. The cases eventually came before a federal judge in New York.

The question wasn't whether Leonard had truly mailed the money. He had. The question was whether the commercial had legally offered a plane in the first place.

In 1999, Judge Kimba Wood ruled against him. The ad directed viewers to a separate catalog. That catalog contained no jet. And an ordinary viewer, she reasoned, would recognize the spectacle of a military aircraft parking at a school as a joke, not a binding deal.

Leonard appealed. In April 2000, the appeals court affirmed the decision. The check had been returned, and he never received a Harrier.

So what happened to the seven million points?

In February 2026, Frontier Airlines revived the story for its own advertising campaign. It announced that Leonard would receive seven million Frontier airline miles, a reward for civilian flights rather than ownership of a fighter jet. In an interview, Leonard described how much more useful those miles would be for traveling with his family.

Frontier wasn't reversing the court ruling. And Pepsi hadn't finally delivered an aircraft. The number from the old commercial had simply been turned into a different kind of flight reward, thirty years after the original order.

The precise price tag that started the whole fight had outlived the fight itself.
