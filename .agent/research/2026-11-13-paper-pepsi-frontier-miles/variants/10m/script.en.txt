How did an ordinary soft-drink promotion produce a real seven-hundred-thousand-dollar order for a fighter jet? What did the small print actually allow? And how could the same number become a new airline prize thirty years after a judge rejected the claim?

The story began with a rewards catalog, not a courtroom.

In 1995, Pepsi was testing a campaign in the American Pacific Northwest. Customers collected Pepsi Points from specially marked drinks and exchanged them for merchandise. The television commercial followed a student getting ready for school. His shirt had a price in points. So did his leather jacket and sunglasses.

Then the setting changed. Outside the school, a military Harrier appeared, blowing papers around the classroom and landing beside a bicycle rack. The student opened the cockpit, holding a Pepsi. The final price appeared: seven million Pepsi Points.

A shirt was one thing. A fighter plane was something else entirely. But the advertisement gave both a number, and John Leonard, living in Seattle, decided to examine the rules behind it.

The printed catalog listed fifty-three kinds of merchandise. It went from tiny accessories to a mountain bike. The Harrier did not appear anywhere on the order form. That absence would become one of the most important details of the case.

Leonard initially considered collecting the points by buying drinks. Then he examined the redemption instructions. Customers who were short of points could buy extra ones at ten cents each. They had to send at least fifteen original points from packaging with the order.

This rule changed the problem. Seven million points did not require seven million drinks. Take fifteen points from the packages, buy the rest, and the points cost just under seven hundred thousand dollars. Add the stated handling amount, and the check would be for $700,008.50.

For an ordinary promotion, that is absurdly expensive. Compared with a Harrier, which the court later described as costing about twenty-three million dollars, it looked like a remarkable discount. That difference helps explain the size of the gamble. It does not mean the airplane was actually for sale.

Leonard raised around seven hundred thousand dollars through acquaintances. On about March 27, 1996, he submitted the official order form with fifteen original points and the check. On the form, he wrote in “1 Harrier Jet” himself and entered the seven-million-point total.

The check was drawn on his lawyers' account. His accompanying letter made clear that he wanted the points specifically for the aircraft. The company now had an actual order to answer.

On about May 7, Pepsi's fulfillment company returned the check. It explained that the jet was absent from the catalog and that only catalog items could be redeemed. It also said the aircraft had been included in the commercial for humor. The letter came with some free-product coupons.

Leonard's lawyers did not accept that answer. In May they demanded that Pepsi arrange delivery of a new Harrier. The advertising agency responded that the whole jet scene was clearly intended as a joke. A further demand followed in June.

This was the turning point. The money had not purchased an aircraft, yet the paperwork had created a real disagreement: was the commercial merely inviting viewers to order items from a catalog, or had its giant number already promised a plane to anyone who met the condition?

And a second surprise arrived before the judgment. Pepsi itself filed an action in New York in July 1996 asking the court to declare that it had no duty to supply the jet. Leonard sued in Florida the following month. The Florida case was eventually transferred to New York.

By August 1999, federal judge Kimba Wood had to decide whether a viewer could accept an offer simply by mailing the form.

Her opinion discussed a case from 1957. A Minneapolis store had advertised fur coats for one dollar each, available to the first customers. Morris Lefkowitz arrived with his dollar, but the shop turned him away under a rule about who could buy that the advertisement hadn't mentioned. The court held the store to its stated terms.

Could Leonard do the same? In his case the television commercial told viewers to consult a separate catalog. The catalog set out the ordering process, but never listed a jet. The judge treated Leonard's mailed form as his own offer, which Pepsi had not accepted. The check had been returned.

That is one reason Leonard lost. But it wasn't the only one.

The judge described the school scene in detail. The jet arrived in a place where a student could not reasonably park a military aircraft. Wind from the landing scattered papers and humiliated a teacher. The student treated the whole spectacle like a normal commute. A reasonable viewer, the court concluded, would understand the absurdity rather than treat the scene as a serious business proposal.

The cost also mattered. The advertised points could be bought for roughly seven hundred thousand dollars, while the real jet cost vastly more. The court treated that gap as another sign the imagined deal was too good to be real.

There was a further legal obstacle involving the requirement for a sufficient written contract. But for a viewer, the central distinction is easier to see: printing a number beside an object in an advertisement does not, by itself, establish a legally enforceable promise to deliver that object.

Leonard argued that the commercial presented the jet in the same style as the real prizes. He also pointed out that Pepsi had changed the commercial. According to the court record, later versions increased the jet's point price to seven hundred million and then added a visible joke disclaimer.

Leonard pointed to those changes as evidence for his case. The judge declined to treat them as proof that the first version made an offer. What mattered was the commercial Leonard had actually seen.

The judge granted Pepsi's motion in 1999. Leonard appealed, but on April 17, 2000, the federal appeals court affirmed the decision. The Harrier remained unavailable. The check had been returned years earlier.

For a long time, the final image was that returned piece of paper and an aircraft that existed only in a commercial.

In 2022, the dispute became the subject of a Netflix documentary. The case reached a new audience, but the legal result did not change.

Then, in February 2026, another company's advertising team approached the story from a different direction. Frontier Airlines, which operates civilian passenger flights, announced a campaign built around Leonard's old seven-million-point claim. Instead of a military Harrier, it proposed seven million Frontier miles.

The airline presented the miles as enough to support a great deal of travel. The exact booking conditions still depend on the loyalty program; miles are not ownership of a jet and should not be confused with an unlimited legal entitlement to flights.

Leonard told People magazine that his first reaction to the approach had been reluctance, remembering the earlier fight. After discussions, he agreed to participate. He now had children and described how useful airline travel rewards could be for his family. He also observed that he could not have flown or maintained a fighter jet himself.

The 2026 promotion featured him with a passenger jet, continuing the visual joke from the 1990s. Frontier used the occasion to advertise a separate, limited-time points-exchange offer to other travelers. That was marketing, not a judicial correction of the Pepsi case.

And this is the final reversal. The same man who had once tried to exchange seven million soda points for a machine he couldn't operate was now being offered seven million miles he could actually use. The prize came from another company, under another program, thirty years later.

Pepsi never had to hand over a Harrier. The courts never changed their minds. But the exact number that Leonard wrote on his order form found its way into a new deal.

One advertisement created a dispute about what a number beside a jet meant. Another advertisement offered a different answer.
