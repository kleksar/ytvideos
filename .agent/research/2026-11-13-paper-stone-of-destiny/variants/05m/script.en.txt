How do four students remove a royal coronation stone from Westminster Abbey without being caught? And why, months later, do the police use a fake motorcade to bring it back?
The answer begins with a block of sandstone. It weighed 152 kilograms, sat beneath Britain's coronation chair, and had been taken from Scotland by King Edward I in 1296. For many Scots, it was a symbol of a lost political voice, not a valuable gem.
In December 1950, law student Ian Hamilton and three friends decided to take it north. They were linked to a movement campaigning for a Scottish assembly. Their plan was meant to draw attention to the stone's place in Scottish history.
Three students entered Westminster Abbey on Christmas Eve and concealed themselves until the building closed. Kay Matheson waited outside in a borrowed Ford Anglia.
Inside, the men reached the chair and worked at the grille holding the stone. When they tried to lift it, the stone broke. Accounts disagree about the size of the damaged piece, but the immediate problem is clear: the object they had come to recover was now in pieces, and they still had to carry it out.
They moved the stone to the car. Then Hamilton noticed a mistake. They had left the Abbey door open.
He and Alan Stuart went back. As they reached the entrance, a policeman appeared. According to the Glasgow Police Museum's account, the two men hid behind the door while Matheson and Gavin Vernon pretended to be a couple sitting together in the car. The officer checked the door and went on.
The car escaped. But could they get a broken coronation stone across Britain when the police knew it had vanished?
The search stretched north. One part was reportedly hidden briefly in a field in Kent before the stone reached Scotland. In Glasgow, stonemason Robert Gray helped repair it.
There was almost another discovery. A night watchman in Gray's yard spotted a strange stone with rings at its ends. He recognised it from a newspaper and told police. By the time officers arrived, it had disappeared again.
Detective William Kerr followed a different clue. He checked who had recently studied the stone at Glasgow's Mitchell Library. One reader stood out: Ian Hamilton, who had also been absent around Christmas. The trail was suggestive, but it did not put the stone in police hands.
In April 1951, the stone appeared at Arbroath Abbey, a place associated with Scotland's historic declaration of independence. Police recovered it and took it to Glasgow, where an official identified it as the genuine stone. None of the four students was prosecuted.
At the police office, the problem changed. Detectives had been trying to find a missing object. Now they had to get that same object back to Westminster without losing it a second time. The reporters waiting by the gate had radio-equipped cars, and police were worried that others might try to stop the journey.
That sounds like the end. Yet Glasgow police faced a new problem: the press was waiting outside their headquarters, ready to follow any car carrying the stone back to London. Police also feared an attempt to intercept it.
The obvious vehicle was a large limousine. Instead, officers first sent a Jaguar out through the gates. Reporters suspected a decoy and stayed put. When the escorted limousine left a few minutes later, they chased it.
The joke was on the watchers. The stone was in the Jaguar all along.
It returned to Westminster. Decades later, in 1996, the government formally sent it back to Scotland, with specialists and a police escort. It came south for Charles III's coronation in 2023 and now rests at Perth Museum.
In 1950, a borrowed car took it away. In 1951, a police Jaguar brought it back. The stone ended up in Scotland by official decision—but the first journey had started with four students and a door they nearly forgot to close.
