What does it take to steal a 152-kilogram stone from beneath Britain's coronation chair? Why did the thieves have to hide two pieces separately? And why did the police later trick a crowd of reporters with a car that never carried the stone?
The object at the centre of this story was a plain sandstone block with two iron rings. It was known as the Stone of Destiny, or Stone of Scone. By 1950, it had sat beneath the Coronation Chair at Westminster Abbey for centuries. It was not valuable for its material. It mattered because Scottish rulers had used it as a symbol of kingship, and Edward I of England had taken it from Scotland in 1296.
That history made it a political object as well as a ceremonial one. In the middle of the twentieth century, campaigners were seeking a devolved Scottish assembly. One of them was a young Glasgow law student named Ian Hamilton. With Gavin Vernon, Alan Stuart and Kay Matheson, he planned an act that would put the stone in newspaper headlines.
The four travelled south just before Christmas 1950. They had two cars. In their later recollections, an initial attempt failed; they returned to the Abbey over Christmas and this time reached the Coronation Chair.
The stone lay beneath the seat. As they were removing it, it broke. They now had two pieces to recover, and getting one out did not guarantee the other would follow.
Hamilton carried the smaller piece out to Matheson's car. According to the students' later account, a policeman approached them. They passed themselves off as a couple looking for accommodation and were allowed to go. The remaining part of the stone was still inside the Abbey.
There is a different account from the Glasgow Police Museum. It describes Matheson and Vernon in the car, while Hamilton and Stuart returned to close the Abbey door. The two accounts disagree over who was where when the policeman appeared. They agree on the larger outcome: nobody was stopped there, and the stone was taken away broken.
The students eventually recovered both pieces, but did not keep them together. One was concealed in Kent. The other was hidden separately. They had to retrieve and reunite the parts before the stone could be repaired.
Meanwhile the theft was reported. London detectives knew the object's Scottish connection. Police in Glasgow were alerted, and the search began to move towards the students' home city.
The pieces reached Scotland. A Glasgow stonemason named Robert Gray, who supported the Scottish cause, helped repair the stone. What he did with the fragments left from the break would become another part of the story decades later.
The repair yard nearly gave everything away. A night watchman came across a peculiar stone with a metal ring at each end. The next day, after seeing its photograph in a newspaper, he reported what he had found. Police came to the yard. The stone had already gone.
Now follow the investigation from the other side. Detective Inspector William Kerr in Glasgow had to identify people capable of knowing where the stone was and how to remove it. He visited the Mitchell Library, which held material about the Abbey and the stone. Records pointed to a recent reader of those books: Ian Hamilton.
Kerr also learned that Hamilton had been away from his usual surroundings around Christmas. He could begin linking names and movements, but, as the police museum itself notes, the evidence remained circumstantial. A suspect was not the same thing as recovering the stone.
Meanwhile the theft had become a public argument. The students were connected to a movement seeking Scottish self-government, but their stunt did not speak for every Scot. King George VI was seriously ill, and newspapers raised the possibility that the stone might be missing when a future coronation took place. The police museum describes support for the thieves as weakening amid that concern. It is a retrospective account of public mood, not a survey of Scottish opinion.
Then, in April 1951, the stone appeared in Arbroath Abbey. That place had a separate national significance: the Declaration of Arbroath was issued there in 1320. The stone was left for recovery, and police brought it to Glasgow. An official identified it as the genuine coronation stone.
The immediate question was settled. The famous missing object was back in police custody. On 19 April the Attorney General told Parliament that three suspects had admitted their own involvement without implicating others. He had decided that criminal proceedings would not be in the public interest. The decision did not settle the political argument over where the stone belonged. But the story had one more act, and this time the people planning the deception wore police uniforms.
The recovered stone was kept at Glasgow's Central Police Office. Outside, reporters waited with cameras and radio-equipped cars. If the police moved it, the press planned to follow. The authorities were also concerned that someone might try to prevent its journey south.
The original plan was straightforward: put the stone in a large limousine and send it toward London with a Jaguar as escort. Then officers reconsidered. The press already expected a trick. What if the stone travelled in the less conspicuous escort vehicle?
They checked the Jaguar's boot. The sandstone block fitted. That made the car itself useful as a hiding place in plain sight.
At 5:17 on the morning of 13 April, police opened the gates. The Jaguar drove out carrying Detective Inspector Kerr. Reporters recognised him and suspected that the early departure was an obvious decoy. They stayed where they were.
Three minutes later, a large limousine emerged between police escort cars. That looked like the real departure. The press vehicles started after it and followed the convoy through Glasgow. The limousine even stopped to collect two Scotland Yard detectives at Whitburn before continuing.
But the stone was never in that limousine. It had left first, in the Jaguar the reporters chose not to follow. The vehicles met again farther south at Newcastle and continued to London without the stone being intercepted.
Back at Westminster, the stone was kept in secure storage before being placed beneath the Coronation Chair again. It was used at Elizabeth II's coronation in 1953.
But the repair in Glasgow had left a second trail. Research by the archaeologist Sally Foster, published in 2025, reconstructed the history of small fragments kept by Robert Gray. He had numbered 34 of them; other pieces were also preserved. Some went to people connected to Scotland's nationalist movement, and one reached a museum in Australia.
One recipient was especially unexpected. In a statement made in 1973, Detective Inspector William Kerr recorded that Gray had given him a fragment. Foster's research suggests the gift may have been made in 1956, although that date is not certain.
The same detective who had investigated Hamilton and accompanied the recovered stone south had ended up owning a small piece himself. The gift does not establish that Kerr helped the students or that the stone returned to Westminster was a replica. It is evidence of the object's complicated life after the repair.
Historic Environment Scotland has maintained that the recovered stone is authentic. A repaired historical object can be genuine without being unchanged.
The official return to Scotland would take another four decades. In 1996, Prime Minister John Major announced that the stone would be housed in Scotland while still travelling south for future coronations. This time there would be no borrowed Ford and no hurried removal.
Conservation specialists rehearsed how to lift 152 kilograms out of the chair. They built scaffolding and a pulley system, raised the stone gradually, and lowered it onto a specially designed carrying barrow. Police guarded the operation. The object that students had once dragged through the Abbey now moved under a carefully planned conservation procedure.
Its arrival in Scotland did not end the questions surrounding the stone. During examination after the return, conservators found a small sealed tube with a scrap of an authentication document placed inside in the 1970s, apparently intended to help establish the object's identity if it were taken again. The concern over another theft had left a physical mark on the artifact.
The stone was used at Charles III's coronation in 2023. Since the opening of Perth Museum in 2024, it has been displayed there. Visitors can look at the same plain block of sandstone that once occupied the centre of a national manhunt.
Visitors can see the repaired fracture, but they cannot see the pieces that travelled away from it. One was given to the detective who investigated its disappearance. The stone finally returned to Scotland by agreement; the separate fragments tell a different story of how far one broken object had already travelled.
