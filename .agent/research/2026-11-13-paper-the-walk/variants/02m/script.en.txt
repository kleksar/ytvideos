The police had to wait for a performance to finish before they could arrest the performer.

The man was Philippe Petit. On August seventh, 1974, he was standing on a steel cable stretched between New York's Twin Towers, more than four hundred metres above the streets.

There was no safety net. And he had not asked permission.

The previous night, Petit and his friends had entered the towers with a balancing pole, ropes and a heavy steel cable. To pass the first thin line across the gap, they used a bow and arrow. They pulled thicker lines over after it, until the cable could be secured between the roofs.

Just after seven in the morning, Petit stepped out.

He reached the opposite building, turned, and came back. People stopped in the street to look up.

Then police officers appeared on a roof.

Petit saw them. Instead of coming in, he smiled, knelt on the cable, and continued his performance. At one point he lay down on the wire above the city.

The officers stayed on the roof. After about forty-five minutes, he finally returned to solid ground, where they took him into custody.

He faced trespass and disorderly-conduct charges. But those charges were dropped on one condition: he would give a free high-wire show for children in Central Park.

He had crossed between two skyscrapers without permission and left with an invitation to perform again.

So what could the police do while he was still out there?
