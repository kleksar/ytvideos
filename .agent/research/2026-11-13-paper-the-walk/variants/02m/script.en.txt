The police had to wait until a high-wire performer finished his show.

On August seventh, 1974, Philippe Petit was walking between New York's Twin Towers, more than four hundred metres above the streets. He had no safety net and no permission to be there.

Getting the wire up there had taken him and his friends most of the night. They had to work on both buildings, and the first connection across the gap was a thin line sent over with an arrow.

Just after seven in the morning, Petit stepped out. He reached the other tower, turned around and walked back. People down in the street stopped to look up.

Then the police arrived. When they called him back to the roof, he turned away and went out on the wire again. One of the officers later remembered seeing him bounce on it. Petit even lay down above the city.

After about forty-five minutes he came back onto a roof, and the officers arrested him.

People cheered when he reached the street. He was charged with trespassing and disorderly conduct, but the prosecutor agreed to drop the charges if he gave a free show for children in Central Park.

Three weeks later, there was a wire above a lake in the park. Under searchlights, Petit performed again, this time in front of thousands.

The arrest had led to another show. But what could the officers do about the first one while the performer was still in the air?
