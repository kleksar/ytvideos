How do you stretch a steel cable between two skyscrapers without anyone stopping you? What can the police do when the person they need to arrest is already walking on it? And why did this illegal act end with a show for children?

New York, August 1974. The Twin Towers were still relatively new, and their roofs stood more than four hundred metres above the streets. French wire-walker Philippe Petit wanted to perform between them. There was no ticket, no audience invitation and no permission from the owners.

He had already walked high above Notre Dame in Paris and on Sydney Harbour Bridge. But here he faced a different problem. Before he could take a single step, his friends would have to get a cable from one roof to the other.

They divided into teams and entered the buildings carrying equipment that would look distinctly out of place at a normal office. Among it were a dismantled balancing pole, ropes and a heavy steel cable. Some of the men wore work clothes to help them get through the site.

The towers were occupied. Security guards still patrolled. So the group hid and waited for the right moment rather than hauling everything openly onto the roof.

Now came the part that could have ended the plan before the walk began. There was a gap of roughly forty metres between the buildings. Nobody could simply throw a steel cable across it.

The team used a bow to fire a thin line to the opposite roof. That line pulled over a heavier one; the heavier line helped bring across the cable. Then they had to secure it and prepare it for a person's weight, working through the night.

The cable was finally ready around dawn.

Shortly after seven on August seventh, Petit stepped onto it carrying his balancing pole. Below him, Manhattan was starting its working day. There was no net or harness to catch him.

He reached the far side. For most people, that alone would have been the ending.

Petit turned around and walked back.

People in the streets began to gather. A tiny figure was moving between buildings high above them. The crowd could look up, but no one down there could bring him back.

Police officers were sent onto the roof. One of them, Sergeant Charles Daniels, later described the sight: Petit smiled at the officers, and when he got close to the building, he did not surrender. He went back out on the wire.

For the officers, that changed everything. Moving closer to him was not the same as reaching him. The safest practical moment to take him into custody was when he chose to return to a roof.

Meanwhile, the performance continued. Petit bent his knees. He lay down on the wire. He turned the arrest that awaited him into part of an audience's extraordinary morning.

After about forty-five minutes, he finally stepped back onto the roof. The officers took him into custody. He was booked for trespass and disorderly conduct, and he underwent an examination at a hospital.

That sounds like the ordinary ending to an unauthorized stunt. But the public reaction changed what happened next. Photographs of the walk reached newspapers. The prosecutor dropped the charges on the condition that Petit give a free performance for children in Central Park.

He did. The man who had just been arrested for putting on an uninvited show was asked to put on another one.

And there is a final difference between the legend and the event. You may hear that Petit crossed the wire exactly eight times. He later said that number had been exaggerated. The crucial fact needs no inflated count: police arrived, watched him continue, and could arrest him only after he came back.

The most unexpected part of the story wasn't that he reached the other roof. It was that reaching the roof did not end the show.
