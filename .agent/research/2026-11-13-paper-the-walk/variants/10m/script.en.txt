Why did a young Frenchman smuggle a steel cable to the top of New York's Twin Towers? How did he manage to walk between them while the police watched from a roof? And why did the city respond to his arrest by asking him to perform again?

In August 1974, the towers were still new landmarks. Their enormous size had made them famous, but it had not made everybody love them. Many offices were still empty. Then, one morning, people on the streets of Manhattan looked up and saw a person moving through the sky between the two buildings.

He wasn't a construction worker. He was twenty-four-year-old Philippe Petit, a high-wire performer who had entered the buildings without permission.

The sight looked impossible. But there was another, less visible achievement behind it. Petit could balance on a cable. First he and his friends had to put that cable in a place where no cable belonged.

He had previous experience of unauthorized high walks. He had performed at Notre Dame in Paris, and later on Sydney Harbour Bridge. Those were audacious locations. The Twin Towers created a much harder equipment problem. Each roof was an enormous distance above street level, and the gap between the buildings was about forty metres.

Petit needed more than his own balance. He needed a group of people in two different buildings, a way to communicate across the gap, enough time without interference, and a steel cable that could carry his weight. The walk itself would last less than an hour. Installing its stage would take all night.

Before the event, the group studied the buildings and their security. Petit had posed as a journalist while learning about the towers. They also had help from someone who worked inside the complex, Barry Greenhouse. This was a carefully prepared intrusion, even if the performance it made possible was going to look effortless.

On August sixth, they moved their equipment into the towers. Some were disguised as workers. Others entered looking as if they belonged in offices. A disassembled balancing pole and heavy cable were among the things that had to reach the upper floors. No one could carry them openly across a busy lobby without attracting attention.

Then the plan slowed down. Guards were patrolling, and the teams had to hide. Reaching the roof early did not help if a guard caught them with the equipment before they had attached anything.

When the group finally worked above the city, the problem was physical. There was open air between the roofs. The cable was far too heavy to shoot or throw across. So the team began with something light: an arrow carrying a thin line. The line could draw a thicker rope across. That rope could be used to pull over another, and eventually the steel cable.

It sounds straightforward when told in a sentence. On the roofs, each part depended on the last. A line lost across the gap meant another attempt; a cable not properly secured meant there could be no walk. They worked in darkness, connecting and tightening the equipment until the first morning light reached the buildings.

The steel cable also needed to be stabilized. This was not an ordinary circus stage with a ready-made platform and safety net. It was a temporary rig high above a working city, assembled by friends who had no permission to be there.

Around seven on the morning of August seventh, they were ready. Petit carried his balancing pole to the edge of the South Tower.

He stepped onto the wire.

Below him, the city was waking up. From the pavement, the gap between the buildings looked small. From the wire, there was no pavement below, no net, and no second chance supplied by the rig.

Petit moved toward the opposite tower and reached it. The first crossing was complete.

Had he stopped there, he might still have made world news. Instead, he turned around.

He went out again, and people below began stopping to watch. More spectators arrived. Some had only looked up because others were staring at the roofs. They found a man apparently walking through the air.

The police soon arrived on a roof to bring him down.

Sergeant Charles Daniels, one of the officers, later gave an extraordinary account of what happened. Petit saw them and smiled. When he reached the building, the officers asked him to leave the wire. He turned and went back out toward its middle.

It was a peculiar confrontation. The officers were standing on solid ground. Petit was visible in front of them, but beyond their reach. Chasing him across the wire would have meant joining the act, not ending it. They needed him to decide to come back.

That is when the walk became a performance with an unexpected audience. Petit bent his knees and bounced on the wire. He lowered himself and lay down. On one account, the police moved back and kept quiet as he continued.

The people in the street could not know what the officers were saying on the roof. They could see the tiny figure moving, pausing and moving again. There was no music or ticketed show, yet a crowd had gathered in the middle of a working morning.

The historical record contains different accounts of exactly how many times he crossed. Eight became the famous figure. But much later, Petit said that was an exaggeration; he suggested the number was smaller. There is no need to settle a disputed count to understand what the officers saw. One crossing had turned into another, and then the performance continued.

After roughly forty-five minutes in the air, Petit stepped back onto the roof. This time the officers could reach him. They took him into custody.

The authorities booked him for criminal trespass and disorderly conduct. He was taken to a hospital for an examination before his release. For a performance with no permission, an arrest was not surprising.

What followed was.

Pictures of the tiny figure between the giant towers spread through newspapers. People cheered him as he came back down to street level. An event that could have been remembered merely as a security breach had also given the towers an unforgettable public image.

The Manhattan district attorney dropped the charges, but attached a condition. Petit would perform a free aerial show for children in Central Park.

Three weeks later, he did. That second performance took place above a park lake, with the audience on the ground and the event arranged rather than smuggled into a building. What began with fake access and an overnight cable had ended with a city-sponsored invitation.

The Port Authority later even gave Petit a lifetime pass to the towers' observation deck. None of that changed the fact that he had entered without permission. It did show how completely the public response altered the way this particular trespass was treated.

For years, stories about the walk made the preparation sound longer and the number of crossings more exact than Petit himself would later accept. In a fiftieth-anniversary interview, he said the familiar tale of discovering the towers in a dentist's waiting room was invented, and that his real planning had taken eight months rather than six and a half years.

That correction matters, because the true story doesn't require the larger legend. The men on those roofs really had to move a heavy cable through a guarded building. The first thin line really had to cross an empty gap. Petit really stepped onto the finished wire, and the officers really had to wait for him to come off it.

The central mystery of that morning was never whether the police had found their man. They could see him clearly. It was how they could bring him down without making the situation worse.

In the end, Petit chose when the performance finished. And once the city had arrested him, it asked him to take the show to the park.
