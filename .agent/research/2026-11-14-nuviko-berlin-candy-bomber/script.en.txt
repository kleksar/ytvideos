Chocolate floated down over West Berlin on tiny parachutes made from handkerchiefs.

It was 1948. The Soviet blockade had cut West Berlin's land routes.

American and British aircraft were bringing food and fuel into the city.

American pilot Gail Halvorsen met children watching planes outside Tempelhof airport's fence.

He had two sticks of gum. The children shared tiny pieces, and some even sniffed the wrappers.

Halvorsen promised more. But how would they recognize his plane among so many?

He told them to look for a plane rocking its wings on approach.

Back at base, he pooled candy with his crew and made little handkerchief parachutes.

On his next approach, he rocked his C-54's wings and dropped the packets.

He kept doing it. More children came, and a Berlin newspaper printed a photo of the drops.

That's how his commander found out. Halvorsen hadn't asked permission.

His commander was angry. But the general running the airlift approved the idea and let the drops continue.

Other crews joined. Americans sent candy and cloth, and schoolchildren helped make more parachutes.

By the end of the airlift, about twenty-three tons of sweets and gum had been dropped.

Halvorsen went home before the airlift ended. The candy drops carried on without him.
