Two sticks of gum were all an American pilot had for the children of blockaded Berlin.

In 1948, planes were bringing food and fuel into West Berlin after the Soviet authorities closed the land routes.

At Tempelhof airport, Gail Halvorsen met about thirty children behind a fence. He broke his gum into four pieces. Children who got none shared strips of the wrappers and smelled them.

He promised to bring more. They asked how they could recognize his aircraft. He told them to watch for its wings rocking.

Back at base, Halvorsen and his crew combined their candy rations and attached three small parcels to cloth parachutes.

On the next flight, the children recognized the signal. After unloading his transport, Halvorsen saw them waving the parachutes.

The crew repeated the drops. Soon a photograph appeared in a newspaper. Their commander found out, and the crew had never asked permission.

The surprise was that General William Tunner supported the idea. The drops could continue with official backing.

Other crews joined. In America, children helped assemble more parachutes. As crowds grew, deliveries spread to parks and playgrounds.

By the end, military accounts estimated some twenty-three tons of candy and gum had been dropped. Halvorsen had already gone home. Other pilots kept the deliveries going.

And what had the man who started all those drops brought to the airport fence in the first place?
