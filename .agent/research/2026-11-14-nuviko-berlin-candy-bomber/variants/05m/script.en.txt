Why did a pilot delivering food into blockaded Berlin start dropping chocolate? How could children tell which plane was his? And what happened to the promise when he went home?

In the summer of 1948, Soviet authorities blocked the land routes used to supply West Berlin. British and American crews began bringing in essentials by air. Their planes carried food and fuel, and they flew into a city still scarred by war.

One of those pilots was Gail Halvorsen. During a stop at Tempelhof airport, he met children watching the aircraft from behind a fence. He noticed something unusual: they did not ask him for candy.

Halvorsen had only two sticks of chewing gum. He gave them to the children, who divided the gum into tiny pieces. Some kept even the wrappers, just to smell them.

He promised to bring more. The children had a practical question: with so many aircraft coming in, how would they know which one was his?

He said he would rock his wings before landing.

That promise created a problem. Halvorsen flew a C-54 transport carrying serious cargo. He could not simply throw heavy bundles towards children standing below.

Back at base, he and his crew gathered their candy rations. They tied small packets to handkerchiefs, making parachutes to slow the fall. On a subsequent supply flight, Halvorsen rocked the wings and released the tiny parcels.

The children could recognize the aircraft. Now they could also see candy descending under white cloth.

He made more drops. Word spread, and the crowds grew. A newspaper reported the flights. Soon his commander learned what had been happening.

Halvorsen had never asked permission.

The commander was displeased: a pilot had been conducting unauthorized drops during a major supply operation. But the airlift's senior commander, General William Tunner, supported the idea. Instead of ending it, the military allowed it to grow.

The little parachutes now needed a supply chain of their own. Other crews joined the flights. Candy companies and families in America donated sweets and cloth. In Chicopee, Massachusetts, schoolchildren helped tie strings to handkerchiefs and prepare packages for children they had never met.

That is the part a single photograph of falling chocolate cannot show. For every little parachute over Berlin, someone had to supply the candy, prepare the fabric, attach the lines and send the parcel across the Atlantic.

The effort became known as Operation Little Vittles. By the end, American military accounts estimated roughly twenty-three tons of candy and gum had been dropped, spread over many flights, not one spectacular delivery.

Halvorsen returned to the United States in January 1949. The Soviet blockade ended that May, and the larger airlift kept operating for a time afterwards.

But Halvorsen's departure had not ended the candy drops. Other pilots carried on what he had started.

Two sticks of gum had become an operation that no longer depended on the man who first reached into his pocket. The cargo planes still brought necessities into Berlin. On some approaches, the children watched for another kind of delivery — a small white parachute from the sky.
