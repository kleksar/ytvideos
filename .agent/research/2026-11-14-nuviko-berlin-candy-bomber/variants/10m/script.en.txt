How did two sticks of chewing gum turn into an organized air operation over Berlin? Why did its pilot need permission only after the drops had begun? And why did one little girl end up receiving her chocolate through the mail instead of from the sky?

In 1948, a new crisis struck a city still rebuilding from the Second World War. The Soviet authorities blocked land access to West Berlin. The Western Allies had to decide how to keep the population supplied without sending their cargo through the blocked roads and railways.

They chose the air. British and American aircraft flew food and fuel into Berlin, again and again. An arriving transport plane was not a novelty. It was how flour, coal and other essentials reached people who could not simply drive out to buy supplies.

American pilot Gail Halvorsen was one of the men flying those routes. He had been on the other side of the war only a few years earlier. Now he was carrying supplies to German civilians.

During a stop at Tempelhof airport, he went to the fence where children stood watching aircraft land. They talked to him, but the thing he remembered was what they did not do. They did not demand candy.

He checked his pocket. He had two sticks of gum.

The children shared them in tiny pieces. Some even kept the wrappers for their smell. It was a small encounter, yet it gave Halvorsen a promise to keep: he would bring them more.

The children immediately spotted a difficulty. Transport planes were arriving all the time. How could they know which one belonged to this particular pilot?

Halvorsen suggested a signal they could see from the ground. As his plane approached, he would rock its wings.

That solved the recognition problem. It did not solve the delivery problem.

Halvorsen's aircraft was a C-54 carrying supplies for the airlift. The normal cargo was too important for an improvised candy mission to interrupt. And dropping unprotected candy packets from a transport plane could hurt a child below.

At base, Halvorsen and two crew members collected candy from their own rations. They used handkerchiefs and strings to make little parachutes, each carrying a small parcel.

On a later approach to Tempelhof, Halvorsen rocked the wings. Then the parcels went out. Below, children saw the signal and the small white pieces of cloth floating down.

The crew had kept its promise. But the next problem was no longer how to make one drop. It was what would happen if more children came.

Halvorsen repeated the deliveries. The crowd at the edge of the airport grew, and the story appeared in the press. Eventually his commander discovered that one of his pilots had been releasing packages without authorization.

This was an airlift with strict operational demands, not a private flight over a playground. His commander was unhappy. Halvorsen had made an extra promise on behalf of an aircraft he did not own.

Yet the idea survived the confrontation. General William Tunner, who directed the airlift, approved it. What began as a crew's improvised gesture could now operate with official support.

The operation was called Little Vittles, a smaller companion to the huge airlift known as Operation Vittles.

The name was playful. The logistics were real.

Candy rations from a few airmen were not enough. Other crews joined. More volunteers needed fabric, string, sweets, boxes and ways to transport everything from the United States to the airlift bases. These parcels were small, but there could be thousands of them.

One of the places that helped was Chicopee, Massachusetts. The town was linked to Westover Air Force Base, a key American hub for supporting the airlift. Schoolchildren and other volunteers prepared the little parachutes. Local businesses and confectionery companies supplied materials and candy.

Children in America were tying strings for children in Berlin. An operation that depended on planes and pilots also depended on hands working at tables, far from the runway.

There were limits in Berlin as well. The crowds near the airfield became a safety concern, and accounts from the RAF Museum describe deliveries being spread to places such as playgrounds and parks. The operation could grow only if the drops did not turn the waiting children into another hazard.

Letters began to reach Halvorsen. For some children, a candy parachute was also proof that someone on an airplane knew they existed.

One letter came from a young girl named Mercedes. She was worried that aircraft frightened her chickens. She nevertheless told the pilot to look for her white chickens and drop candy near them.

That was a request no transport pilot could reliably fulfill from the cockpit. Halvorsen could rock his wings above an airfield, but he could not pick out one flock of white chickens on the ground.

So he found another route: he sent Mercedes chocolate and gum through the post.

There was no parachute for her. The promise was still kept, in a way that actually reached her.

Meanwhile the operation went beyond a single pilot. The U.S. Air Force later reported that by the end, approximately twenty-three tons of candy and gum had been dropped using some quarter of a million small parachutes. These were cumulative estimates across the program, not the load of one C-54 or the accomplishment of one person.

The Soviet blockade was lifted in May 1949. The larger airlift continued for several more months to rebuild reserves. Little Vittles had run through the period when Berlin depended on those flights.

Halvorsen himself had already returned to the United States in January. Other airmen continued the candy drops after he left. His one promise had become work that did not require him to be present.

That might have been the end of the story: children, parachutes and an operation that succeeded. But the letter about the chickens was not finished with Halvorsen.

More than twenty years later he returned to Berlin as commander of Tempelhof. He received repeated invitations to dinner. Eventually, in 1972, he visited a family's home.

The woman who greeted him showed him a letter dated November 1948. It was the answer Halvorsen had mailed to the girl whose chickens he could not find.

His host was Mercedes.

She had kept his letter through all those years. The child who had asked for a delivery beside her white chickens was now the adult standing in front of the pilot.

In the first days, Halvorsen could not identify a single child's house from the sky. The mail had carried that one packet where the parachutes could not.

The airlift had delivered necessities. Little Vittles added small packages that required a remarkable amount of ordinary work on both sides of the Atlantic. And one of those packages left behind something no transport manifest could measure: a letter that Mercedes still had when the pilot came back.
