A museum curator spotted a blue fin sticking out of a trawler's catch in South Africa.

December 1938. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer often checked fishing boats for unusual specimens.

She pushed aside the other fish. This one had heavy scales and fleshy fins that looked almost like limbs.

She took it to the museum and sent a rough sketch to her friend, fish expert J. L. B. Smith.

Smith was away from town. Meanwhile, the fish was spoiling in the summer heat.

The town mortuary refused to keep it. So did a cold-storage facility.

A taxidermist saved the skin for a museum mount. The internal organs were discarded.

Eleven days after her letter, Smith finally saw the sketch and asked her to preserve the organs.

It was too late. But the sketch and preserved skin were enough to bring him to East London.

When Smith examined the fish, he recognized a coelacanth.

Scientists knew the group from fossils. They had thought it disappeared about 65 million years earlier.

Smith named the living species after Marjorie and the river near the catch.

But to study its insides, he needed a second specimen.

He and his wife sent out reward posters along the East African coast, hoping fishermen would spot another.

In 1952, a fisherman in the Comoros caught one. This time, Smith got an intact fish to study.
