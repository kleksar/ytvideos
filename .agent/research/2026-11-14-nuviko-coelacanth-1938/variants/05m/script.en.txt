How can a fish thought to have vanished millions of years ago appear in an ordinary fishing catch? Why would the scientist who identified it spend another fourteen years searching? And what had been lost before he even saw it?

In December 1938, Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was running a natural-history museum in East London, South Africa. She regularly checked local trawlers for unusual specimens. She wasn't expecting a scientific sensation. She wanted something her museum could collect and study.

On the trawler Nerine, a blue fin caught her eye. Beneath the other fish lay a large blue-gray body, covered in heavy scales. Its fleshy paired fins almost looked like limbs. Marjorie had never seen one. A fisherman with decades of experience reportedly hadn't either.

She got the fish to a taxidermist and sent a rough drawing to J. L. B. Smith, a scientist who helped coastal museums identify fish. But Smith was away for Christmas. In South Africa it was summer, and this big animal was already spoiling.

Marjorie tried to find cold storage. The town mortuary refused to keep it. So did a food refrigeration facility. The taxidermist managed to preserve the skin and mount the body. The internal organs were lost.

That changed what scientists could learn. A mount could preserve the visible shape and scales. It couldn't show how the organs inside were arranged, and no one yet realized how important that loss might be.

On January 3, 1939, Smith finally read her letter. He wanted those missing parts preserved immediately. It was too late. He was left with a sketch, some scales, and a skin mount—evidence that seemed to point to something almost impossible.

Smith didn't give up on the lost parts immediately. Their surviving correspondence shows that he even asked where the town dumped its rubbish, hoping the discarded organs might still be found. But the rubbish went into the sea. He could no longer retrieve the evidence he wanted.

Coelacanths had been known from fossils, but the fossil record appeared to end tens of millions of years ago. Smith thought this could be a living member of that ancient group. Before announcing it, he needed to rule out a mistake.

The scales increased his confidence. In February he travelled to East London and examined the specimen himself. It really was a coelacanth. The living species was named Latimeria chalumnae, after Marjorie and the river near the original catch.

The distinction mattered: the fish wasn't an unchanged survivor of an ancient species. It belonged to an old evolutionary lineage whose fossil traces seemed to stop millions of years before. A living specimen overturned the assumption that the entire group had disappeared.

So the first mystery had an answer: the lineage had survived. But a harder question remained. What was the living fish actually like inside? To study its full anatomy, Smith needed another specimen. He couldn't recover the organs from the first.

He and his wife Margaret helped distribute thousands of reward notices along the East African coast. The notices showed the distinctive fish and promised money to anyone who could supply another. Fishermen might recognize what distant researchers couldn't find. The posters offered a reward of one hundred pounds, with descriptions in several languages. The image of the mounted fish could reach people who were at sea every day, far from the museums. Still, year after year, no suitable specimen reached Smith.

Then, in 1952, a fisherman named Ahamadi Abdallah caught a coelacanth in the Comoros. Smith arranged to collect it by aircraft. Fourteen years after the blue fin appeared on a trawler, he finally had a second animal to study.

Marjorie's first fish proved that a group known from ancient fossils still existed. The next one gave researchers the chance to look beyond its skin. The discovery was made in a fishing catch. Understanding it took fourteen more years.
