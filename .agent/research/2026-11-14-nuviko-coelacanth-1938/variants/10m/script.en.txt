What if the most important fish in a museum was also the one nobody could keep cold? How could an animal apparently missing from the fossil record for millions of years turn up alive? And why would the answer require not one discovery, but a succession of specimens over decades?

The first discovery was made on a trawler, by someone who didn't even know the fish's name.

In December 1938, Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was curator of the East London Museum in South Africa. She was a self-taught naturalist who often looked over local catches for interesting specimens. A manager of the local trawler fleet called about fish that might interest the museum, so Marjorie went to the harbor with her assistant.

On the trawler Nerine, a blue fin stood out from a mound of fish. Marjorie pushed aside the catch and uncovered a large blue-gray animal with heavy scales and remarkably thick paired fins. The fins almost resembled limbs. A nearby fisherman with decades of experience said he had never seen one like it.

Marjorie knew that finding something strange wasn't enough. A large fish had to be preserved quickly if a scientist was going to examine it. She moved the specimen to taxidermist Robert Center and tried to contact J. L. B. Smith, a scientist who helped identify fish for local museums.

Smith was away for the Christmas holiday. On December 23, Marjorie sent him a letter describing the fish, together with a rough sketch. She mentioned scales like armor and fins unlike those on an ordinary market fish. That drawing was the beginning of an extraordinary paper trail.

But there was a problem the drawing could not solve. December is summer in South Africa. The fish was decomposing. Marjorie looked for refrigerated storage and was turned away by both the town mortuary and a food refrigeration facility. The taxidermist preserved the skin and some external structure, but the internal organs were lost.

An ordinary observer might have thought the museum had saved enough: the strange fish still had its shape. To someone studying its anatomy, the missing soft tissue was a different matter. A skin mount can show scales and fins; it cannot reveal everything inside.

On January 3, 1939, Smith finally read her description. He urgently asked that the gills and internal parts be kept. Marjorie told him they had already been discarded. Their surviving correspondence shows just how far he was willing to go: he asked where the town dumped its rubbish, wondering whether the organs could still be recovered. The rubbish went into the sea. That search was over.

Why did he want them so badly? The description sounded to Smith like a coelacanth. Scientists had studied coelacanths in fossils going back hundreds of millions of years. But their appearances in the known fossil record stopped around the end of the Cretaceous. That doesn't mean the modern species lived unchanged for sixty-five million years. It means nobody had recognized a living example of the ancient group.

If Smith was mistaken, he would be announcing an extraordinary discovery based on an unfamiliar ordinary fish. He needed evidence stronger than a sketch. Marjorie sent several scales, and their structure helped remove his doubts. In February 1939 he travelled to East London to examine the preserved fish.

The answer was startling. It really was a living coelacanth. The species received the name Latimeria chalumnae, after Courtenay-Latimer and the area of the Chalumna River where the fish was caught. The curator who had followed up an unfamiliar blue fin had brought a supposedly vanished lineage to scientific attention.

But the announcement couldn't reverse what happened during those summer days. Smith had identified the animal without the organs he wanted to study. He still couldn't properly investigate many of its internal features. The discovery had solved one question and exposed another.

The next challenge was geographical. The first coelacanth had arrived in a fishing net off South Africa. That didn't tell researchers where to find another. Smith and his wife Margaret spent years distributing reward notices along the East African coast. The leaflets showed the unusual scales, paired fins and tail, and offered money for an intact specimen.

The scheme depended on fishermen recognizing something important in an everyday catch. Researchers couldn't reliably search a vast coast for an animal whose living habitat they barely understood. The notices kept the possibility alive, even while the years passed without a second fish reaching Smith.

In 1952, fisherman Ahamadi Abdallah caught a coelacanth in the Comoro Islands. The reward was finally claimed. Smith persuaded the South African government to send an aircraft to retrieve the specimen. Fourteen years after Marjorie's find, he had another animal to examine.

The first fish had answered whether the ancient group still existed. The second made fuller anatomical study possible. Yet a single new specimen didn't reveal everything about an entire species. Scientists needed to compare animals and choose carefully which kinds of evidence to preserve.

That choice led to another unexpected discovery, decades after the original blue fin. In 1962, a different coelacanth specimen reached the American Museum of Natural History in New York. For years it remained in the museum collection. In 1975, researchers opened that female specimen and found five developing young inside.

The fish was carrying embryos. That helped settle a major question: coelacanths give birth to live young. The finding didn't come from Marjorie's mounted fish, or from Smith's 1952 specimen. It came from a later animal kept in another museum until researchers could study it.

And it showed why the first preservation failure mattered. The skin of a rare animal can identify it, but only other preserved structures can answer certain questions about its body. A specimen can hold evidence that nobody knows to ask about when it first enters a collection.

Some assumptions even required seeing coelacanths alive, rather than preserved in jars or on mounts. Underwater observations in 1987 revealed how the fleshy paired fins move. They can look almost like legs in a picture, but the fish does not use them to walk on the seafloor. A living animal can correct an idea built from anatomy alone.

In 1997, a second modern coelacanth species was found in Indonesia. That did not turn the 1938 fish into a creature unchanged from the age of dinosaurs. The living animals belong to an ancient lineage, but lineages evolve. Gaps in the fossil record are also shaped by the environments in which fossils are likely to form.

There's a final preservation problem. Modern coelacanths are rare and endangered. Researchers cannot simply demand that fishermen provide an endless supply of them. Museums now work to keep different kinds of tissue and other evidence, so future studies may answer questions that scientists today haven't yet imagined.

Marjorie saved what she could with the storage facilities available. Smith identified the fish from its surviving features and looked for more evidence. Abdallah's catch opened another stage of study. Later museum specimens and observations revealed facts that none of them could have guessed from a blue fin on a trawler.

The discovery wasn't only that a fish from an ancient group was still alive. It was that recognizing an animal and understanding how it lives are two very different achievements. The first happened in a harbor in 1938. The second took fishermen, letters, museum collections and decades of work.
