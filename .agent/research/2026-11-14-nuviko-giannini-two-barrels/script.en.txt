A banker in San Francisco took deposits over a plank balanced on two barrels. His building was gone.

On April eighteenth, nineteen oh six, an earthquake struck the city. Fires spread through whole neighborhoods for three days.

Amadeo Giannini had opened a small bank called Bank of Italy for people larger banks often overlooked.

When the fire threatened his office, Giannini loaded the bank's cash and records into produce wagons.

He hid the valuables under crates of oranges, then took them out to his home in San Mateo.

By the time he returned, his bank building was in ruins. But he still had money available to lend.

Some other banks had to wait before they could reach the contents of their overheated vaults.

Giannini set up a temporary counter on a waterfront wharf. There was no proper desk, just that plank and two barrels.

People needed money to rebuild shops and homes. He started making loans on a handshake.

He also lent money to ship captains so they could buy lumber in Oregon and Washington and sail it back.

He invited neighbors to deposit their savings, too. Those deposits helped keep his little bank working.

Over the years, Giannini opened branches far beyond San Francisco.

In nineteen thirty, Bank of Italy became Bank of America. By nineteen forty-nine, it had more than five hundred branches.
