On a San Francisco wharf, a banker reopened for business behind a plank laid across two barrels. His old office had burned down.

Amadeo Giannini ran the Bank of Italy, serving working families and immigrants overlooked by larger banks. On April eighteenth, nineteen oh six, an earthquake struck the city. The fires that followed burned for three days.

As flames approached, Giannini loaded the bank's cash and records into produce wagons. He hid them beneath crates of oranges and carried them to his home in San Mateo.

He returned to a ruined office. Some other banks couldn't yet open their overheated vaults. Giannini, at least, could reach the cash he'd saved.

But money sitting safely in a wagon wasn't enough. Shopkeepers needed loans to reopen. Families needed access to savings. Giannini set up temporary banking counters, including one on the wharf, and made rebuilding loans, some agreed with a handshake.

Then another obstacle appeared: the city needed lumber, not just credit. Giannini financed ship captains who bought timber in Oregon and Washington and brought it to San Francisco.

He also invited people to deposit savings. Their money helped the bank keep lending. The two barrels mattered because real banking continued there.

Bank of Italy later grew into Bank of America, taking that name in nineteen thirty. Its growth took decades; the earthquake alone didn't create it.

Right after the fire, Giannini's problem was simpler: he had saved the money, but still needed a place where customers could put it back to work.
