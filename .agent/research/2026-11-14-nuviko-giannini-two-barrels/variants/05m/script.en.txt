How could a bank keep working when its building had burned down? And why would anyone trust a banker lending money from a plank balanced on two barrels?

In San Francisco in 1906, Amadeo Giannini was about to face both questions. His Bank of Italy served many working people and immigrants overlooked by larger institutions. Before becoming a banker, he had worked in the fruit business, buying produce and learning how small merchants operated.

He had opened his bank in 1904 and was already attracting deposits by speaking directly with neighbors.

Then the earthquake struck on April eighteenth, 1906. Buildings collapsed, but the fires that followed continued for three days. Streets were blocked and entire neighborhoods burned.

The flames were approaching Giannini's office. The cash and records inside could become inaccessible, just when customers would need them most.

He secured produce wagons, loaded the valuables, and concealed them beneath crates of oranges. He moved the money and records out to his home in San Mateo. Historical accounts disagree on the exact amount rescued, but the practical result was clear: he could still reach his bank's funds.

When Giannini came back, the office was ruined. Some other banks had money in vaults that were too hot to open safely. Their account books might survive, but that did not mean they could immediately hand out cash or make loans.

Giannini's problem was different. He had preserved money, but he had nowhere familiar to serve customers.

He began operating from temporary premises at the waterfront. One counter was a wooden plank resting on two barrels. The image is famous because it looked nothing like the imposing hall people expected from a bank.

But a counter alone was not enough. Families needed savings they could access. Shopkeepers needed money to reopen. And anyone wanting to rebuild a home needed credit before a damaged property could begin earning anything back.

Giannini made rebuilding loans, some described as being agreed with a handshake. That does not prove he ignored borrowers' circumstances or that every loan was risk-free. It does show that he was willing to do business in conditions many lenders struggled to navigate.

Then another obstacle emerged. Even with money, a builder needed lumber. San Francisco's reconstruction demanded materials that had to arrive from outside the city.

Giannini lent money to ship captains who traveled north to purchase timber in Oregon and Washington and bring it back. His bank was helping finance a supply chain, not just lending against damaged buildings.

Meanwhile, the bank had to receive funds as well as distribute them. Giannini invited residents to deposit their savings. Those deposits helped support further lending, and the decision to entrust money to a makeshift bank required confidence that it would continue operating.

This was the real achievement behind the two barrels. People could still bring money in, take needed money out and finance the work of rebuilding. A bank was functioning even without its old walls.

The Bank of Italy was growing before the disaster, and the earthquake alone did not create its future success. After banking rules changed, Giannini expanded the branch network across California. The bank became Bank of America in 1930, and by 1949 it had more than five hundred branches.

The barrels did not become a permanent headquarters. They mattered because the transactions behind them continued when a city badly needed them.
