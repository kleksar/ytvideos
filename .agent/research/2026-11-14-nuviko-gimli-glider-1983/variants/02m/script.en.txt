A passenger jet was gliding toward an old airfield with a drag strip at the far end. Both engines had stopped.

July 1983. An Air Canada Boeing 767 was carrying sixty-one passengers and eight crew to Edmonton.

Its fuel gauges weren't working. Before takeoff, ground crews measured the tanks with metal sticks.

They converted litres of fuel using a figure for pounds. But this Boeing needed kilograms.

The crew believed it had roughly twice as much fuel as was actually aboard.

The same wrong conversion was used before departures from Montreal and Ottawa.

A low-fuel-pressure warning appeared in flight. Captain Robert Pearson turned toward Winnipeg.

The left engine stopped. Then the right. Most of the main cockpit instruments went dark.

They no longer had enough height to reach Winnipeg. Pearson turned toward an old military airfield at Gimli.

Without engine power, they could glide toward the runway. There would be no second attempt.

Pearson also flew gliders. On approach, he slipped the big jet sideways to lose height.

First Officer Maurice Quintal tried to lock the nose landing gear. It wouldn't lock.

The main wheels touched the runway. Then the nose gear folded underneath the plane.

The nose scraped along the concrete, helping to slow the aircraft.

At the far end, the runway was a drag strip. Drivers and their families were camping just beyond it.

The jet stopped before it reached them. The passengers and crew were evacuated.

All sixty-nine people aboard survived. The campers were safe too.
