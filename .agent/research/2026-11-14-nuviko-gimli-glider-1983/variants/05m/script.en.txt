Why would two fuel checks agree with each other and still be dangerously wrong? How do you land a large passenger jet when both engines have stopped? And what if the nearest runway is no longer simply an airport?

On July 23, 1983, an Air Canada Boeing 767 set off on a journey across Canada. Captain Robert Pearson and first officer Maurice Quintal had a modern aircraft, but one vital set of instruments wasn't working: the fuel quantity gauges. The crew believed the amount aboard had been established by measuring it manually.

That decision would put sixty-nine people into an extraordinary emergency.

The 767 was new to the airline. Older aircraft used pounds for fuel weight; this one used kilograms. The ground staff still delivered fuel in litres, which measure volume rather than weight. On a normal flight, the aircraft's electronic gauges would help keep the numbers straight.

Here, those gauges were blank. Ground crews lowered measuring sticks into the wing tanks and converted the readings into litres. Then they multiplied by a number supplied for fuel weight: roughly 1.77.

The problem wasn't the arithmetic. The number converted litres to pounds, not kilograms. The correct kilograms-per-litre factor was closer to 0.8. By labelling the pound result as kilograms, the calculation made an inadequate fuel load look sufficient.

At Montreal, they added far too little fuel. At Ottawa, they checked again with the same method. The answer seemed to confirm the earlier answer, because the same mistake had been repeated.

Above central Canada that evening, a low fuel-pressure warning interrupted the flight. Pearson chose to divert toward Winnipeg. More warnings followed. The left engine shut down. Then the right.

The cockpit changed with them. Many electronic displays went blank as their normal power disappeared. The crew still had essential standby instruments, but no engine thrust. Winnipeg was now too far away.

Quintal helped establish the remaining options, and the aircraft turned toward Gimli, a former military airfield he knew. It was closer, but there was no room for a second attempt. A powered aircraft can add thrust and go around. This aircraft could only spend the height it still had.

Pearson was a glider pilot in his spare time. He understood how to trade altitude for distance. But the Boeing was approaching Gimli too high. Landing long could carry it off the far end of the runway.

So Pearson used a sideslip. He put the aircraft at an angle to its direction of travel, exposing more of it to the airflow. That increased drag and let the jet lose height faster, before he straightened it to land.

The main wheels touched down. The nose wheel, which Quintal had tried unsuccessfully to lock, collapsed. The front of the aircraft scraped along the concrete and slowed it.

That was more than damage to the plane. The old airfield had been partly converted into a drag-racing strip. Families and racers were camping near the far end. The aircraft stopped before reaching them.

All passengers and crew got out alive. There were no significant injuries, and the tanks were found dry.

The inquiry did not reduce the accident to one employee confusing pounds and kilograms. It found an aircraft dispatched with blank gauges contrary to its operating restrictions, inadequate training, unclear procedures, and failures of communication between people who each thought the numbers had been checked.

The same calculation was repeated at two airports. It never became an independent safeguard.

The landing earned the Boeing its nickname, the Gimli Glider. But the most useful part of the story is the contrast: one chain of unchecked assumptions nearly lost the plane; a crew adapting to a completely new situation brought everyone home.
