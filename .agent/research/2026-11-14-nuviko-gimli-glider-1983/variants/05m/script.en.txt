Why did a second fuel check make a passenger jet seem safe when it was actually running out? How could two pilots reach a runway after both engines stopped? And why was escaping the aircraft still difficult once it had landed?

On July 23, 1983, Air Canada Flight 143 was carrying sixty-one passengers and eight crew from Montreal toward Edmonton, with a stop in Ottawa. Captain Robert Pearson and First Officer Maurice Quintal were flying a Boeing 767, a relatively new aircraft for the airline. Its fuel quantity gauges were blank before departure.

The equipment restrictions should have kept it on the ground in that condition. Instead, the flight crew and ground staff tried to establish the fuel load with measuring sticks beneath the wings.

The sticks showed fuel depth. Tables converted that depth into litres, a measure of volume. The flight needed fuel weight in kilograms. But the figure supplied for the next calculation was 1.77: pounds per litre. The correct kilograms-per-litre factor was roughly 0.8.

The people doing the calculation used the pound figure and treated the result as kilograms. At Montreal, the aircraft received far too little fuel. At Ottawa, they checked again, using the same mistaken conversion. The numbers seemed to agree. The second check had repeated the first error.

High above central Canada, a low-fuel-pressure warning appeared. Pearson turned toward Winnipeg. More warnings followed. The left engine stopped, then the right. The electronic displays went dark, leaving essential standby instruments but no engine thrust.

Quintal now had to work out how far the aircraft could glide. The flight computer could no longer provide its normal descent profile, and the crew had no working vertical-speed indication. He repeatedly asked air traffic control for their distance and compared it with their remaining altitude. Winnipeg was beyond reach.

Quintal knew of another possible runway: Gimli, a former military airfield. Pearson turned toward it. Without engine power, there would be no go-around. Every mile toward Gimli cost the aircraft height it could not regain.

But Gimli was not simply an airport anymore. Part of its runway had been adapted for drag racing. Drivers and their families were camping beyond that section in tents and caravans. The pilots needed to land early enough to stop before the far end.

They also had to keep the landing gear raised until late in the descent. Lowering it too soon would increase drag and shorten the glide. When Quintal finally selected the gear lever, nothing happened. He searched the emergency instructions, struggling to locate the alternate procedure. Eventually he released the gear so it could fall under its own weight. The main wheels locked. The nose wheel did not.

Pearson had flown gliders in his spare time, and now that experience mattered. The Boeing was approaching too high. He turned it sideways into a sideslip, adding air resistance to lose height, then straightened it just before touchdown.

The main wheels struck the runway. The unlocked nose gear folded, and the front of the jet scraped along the concrete. The friction helped slow the aircraft. It stopped before reaching the campers.

But the landing was not quite the end of the emergency. Smoke started to collect near the lowered nose. Most passengers were toward the back, where the escape slides had become unusually steep because the tail was high. One slide did not reach the ground. People hesitated and crowded the rear rows.

There was confusion at the exits over the wings, too. Crew members could not see the ramps where their training had led them to expect them, so some passengers were redirected to other exits. Despite those difficulties, everyone was evacuated. There were no deaths or serious injuries.

The official inquiry traced more than a mistake with pounds and kilograms. The plane had departed despite the restriction on blank fuel gauges. Responsibilities and communication around the fuel calculation were unclear. And during the descent, the emergency landing-gear instructions were not easy to find.

All sixty-nine people aboard survived. The crew had managed one powerless landing, and a collapsing wheel helped stop the aircraft before the campsite. Yet after the jet was safely on the ground, its evacuation equipment presented another problem nobody had expected.
