What could turn a routine passenger flight into a silent glide? Why did a second fuel check fail to catch the first mistake? And how could a broken landing-gear leg help save people who weren't even aboard the aircraft?

To answer those questions, we have to follow two separate stories. One begins on the ground, inside a system that should prevent an aircraft from leaving without enough fuel. The other begins in the air, after those safeguards have already failed.

On July 23, 1983, Air Canada Flight 143 carried sixty-one passengers and eight crew members. Captain Robert Pearson and first officer Maurice Quintal were flying a Boeing 767, a relatively new type in the airline's fleet. It had sophisticated electronic displays and was designed to be flown by two pilots rather than the three found on many older large jets.

The aircraft was modern. The airline's procedures had not entirely caught up with it.

The trouble started before Pearson boarded the flight. The 767's fuel gauges had previously gone blank. A fuel-gauge processor had two channels, designed so that one could take over if the other failed. A maintenance technician had managed to restore the indications on an earlier leg by isolating a faulty channel. The problem was recorded in the aircraft log.

But by the time the jet reached Montreal, the indications were blank again. There was a brief conversation between the arriving captain and Pearson about the problem. Their later recollections differed. Pearson understood that the aircraft had been flying with unavailable fuel gauges and that manual measurement could establish a safe fuel load.

That assumption mattered because the airline's Minimum Equipment List did not permit this particular departure with the gauges blank. The list specifies which failures an aircraft can safely fly with and what checks are required. It is not a general invitation to substitute a manual measurement whenever a cockpit instrument fails.

Nevertheless, the crew and maintenance personnel attempted to work out the fuel amount from the tanks themselves.

They used measuring sticks beneath the wings. A reading in centimetres was converted with tables into the volume of fuel in litres. So far, the method was straightforward. But fuel planning needs weight, not just volume, and the new Boeing used kilograms for that weight.

This was a transitional fleet. Air Canada's older aircraft used pounds, while the 767 had been ordered with metric fuel indications. The fuel supplier provided a factor of about 1.77, reflecting pounds per litre. The number needed for kilograms per litre was around 0.8.

The people making the calculation multiplied the litres by 1.77 and read the result as kilograms. The arithmetic looked clean; the unit attached to the answer was wrong.

The final inquiry reconstructed what had happened in Montreal. Before the added fuel, manual readings indicated about 7,682 litres already in the tanks. Multiplying by 1.77 produced a figure near 13,600. That was pounds, but it was treated as kilograms. Against a flight-plan requirement of roughly 22,300 kilograms, the calculated shortfall appeared modest.

Only about 5,000 litres were added. A correct calculation would have called for roughly 20,000 litres to be added on that basis. A jet can depart with an apparently reassuring number on a sheet of paper and still be carrying dangerously little fuel.

The flight stopped at Ottawa. There was another manual check, but it used the same flawed approach. Its apparent agreement with the earlier figures made the situation look more secure. In reality the second check simply repeated the unit mistake.

Notice how different this is from a dramatic single malfunction. The gauges failed, a dispatch restriction was bypassed, responsibility for calculating fuel weight was unclear, and the alternative calculation used the wrong unit. The barriers that should have caught one another's failures had begun to fail together.

The aircraft continued toward Edmonton.

That evening, shortly after eight o'clock local time, a low fuel-pressure warning appeared in the cockpit. The first warning concerned the left fuel pump. Pearson turned toward Winnipeg, about 120 miles away, and started descending from 41,000 feet.

Another pressure warning appeared. Then the left engine stopped. The right followed soon afterward.

With both engines gone, the normal electrical supply disappeared and many electronic displays turned blank. The crew still had standby instruments: an airspeed indicator, an altimeter, an artificial horizon and a compass. Those were enough to tell them how the aircraft was flying. They did not give the Boeing any thrust.

A passenger jet without thrust doesn't simply drop vertically. Its wings still produce lift while it moves through the air. But every mile flown spends some of its remaining height. The crew could not climb, and it could not wait for a better airport to appear.

At around 35,000 feet, the aircraft had been about sixty-five miles from Winnipeg and forty-five from Gimli. It became clear that Winnipeg was out of reach.

Quintal knew of the former military airfield at Gimli. After discussing options with air traffic control, Pearson turned toward it. The crews in the cabin prepared passengers for an emergency landing.

The choice solved one problem and introduced another.

Gimli had a long paved runway, but the airfield was no longer being used as an ordinary military base. Part of the runway had been adapted for drag racing. Racers and their families had brought tents and caravans for the weekend. The people at the far end of the strip were not waiting for a Boeing 767.

Pearson had an unusual advantage for an airline captain: he flew gliders in his spare time. He knew that controlling a powerless aircraft meant managing altitude and speed as carefully as distance.

But glider experience didn't make this approach easy. The Boeing had no normal engine-powered way to go around. Without its usual power, it also lacked the flaps and slats the pilots would use to manage a normal landing. The runway was about 7,200 feet long. They had to arrive neither too high nor too fast, and there would be only one attempt.

As they neared the field, the Boeing was still too high. A long touchdown could carry it toward the racers.

Pearson and Quintal discussed a manoeuvre called a sideslip. Pearson deliberately put the jet at an angle to its direction of travel, using the airflow to create greater drag. The aircraft lost height more quickly. Before touchdown he straightened it again.

The Boeing reached the beginning of the runway and landed within about 800 feet of the threshold. The main landing gear took the first impact.

The nose gear was another story. Quintal had tried to lower and lock it manually, without success. The front gear folded under the aircraft. The nose scraped along the runway, creating friction.

Usually a collapsed landing-gear leg is just another dangerous failure. Here, the inquiry found that the resulting friction helped stop the jet before it reached the people beyond the drag-racing section.

The aircraft came to rest. The passengers and crew evacuated. There were no deaths and no significant physical injuries. Investigators found both fuel tanks dry.

The landing explains why Flight 143 became famous. It was given a nickname that sounds almost playful: the Gimli Glider. But the investigation that followed is the other half of the story.

A neat retelling says the disaster happened because somebody mixed up pounds and kilograms. That is true as far as it goes, but the official board explicitly warned that this explanation was too simple.

The inquiry examined the electronic fuel-gauge processor, the maintenance log, conversations between crews, the airline's operating restrictions and the way its new metric aircraft had been introduced into a fleet accustomed to imperial units.

The processor had not been working reliably. Yet that alone should not have led to an aircraft taking off with an uncertain fuel load. The dispatch rules were supposed to provide a barrier. The manual calculation was supposed to provide another. Neither barrier functioned as intended.

Even after the wrong factor had been supplied, a clear assignment of responsibility and adequate training could have created a genuine cross-check. Instead, staff in both Montreal and Ottawa worked with the same misunderstanding.

The board also criticized communication within Air Canada, not just among the people beside the aircraft but across the departments responsible for flight operations and engineering. It recommended improvements to fuel procedures, training, manuals and oversight. It did not present the entire event as a simple case of one careless employee.

This makes the comparison between the ground and the air especially striking.

On the ground, people had enough time to check the fuel, but the checks shared an error that nobody recognized. In the air, Pearson and Quintal had very little time and only one landing attempt, but they recognized each new problem and adapted: Winnipeg was too far; Gimli might work; the aircraft was too high; a sideslip could shed height; the nose gear wasn't secure.

The successful landing did not make the earlier decisions safe. It prevented their consequences from becoming fatal.

When the inquiry traced the path from blank gauges to empty tanks, the lesson was not that one number can never be trusted. It was that two matching numbers are not two independent checks when both come from the same mistake.

And at Gimli, an aircraft that had lost every engine landed at a runway with people beyond its far end. A failed nose wheel helped bring the aircraft to a stop.

That is how a chain of preventable mistakes ended with sixty-nine survivors.
