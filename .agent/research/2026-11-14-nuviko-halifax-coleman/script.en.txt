“Hold up the train.” Those words were sent minutes before an explosion killed the dispatcher who typed them.

In Halifax on December 6, 1917, two ships collided. Mont-Blanc, one of them, was loaded with explosives.

It caught fire. The crew escaped, but the ship drifted toward Pier 6 beside the railway yards.

People watched from the shore, unaware of the cargo.

A sailor rushed into the railway office and warned that the ship could explode.

Dispatcher Vincent Coleman headed out with his boss. Then he returned to his telegraph key.

A passenger train was due to pass the burning ship. Coleman told stations up the line to stop incoming trains.

His message warned of the explosives and ended with a goodbye to his colleagues.

At 9:05, Mont-Blanc exploded, killing Coleman and around two thousand others. His office vanished.

Railway stations received the warning. Other inbound trains were stopped.

But whether it stopped that particular passenger train is disputed. It may already have passed the last station.

The message had another effect: it alerted the railway to the disaster. A fuller request followed an hour later.

That day, six relief trains left with doctors, nurses, firefighters and medical supplies.
