Could one telegraph warning stop trains heading into an explosion? And if the dispatcher died sending it, how could anyone know what his message changed?

It was December 6, 1917. Halifax was a busy Canadian port serving the First World War. Railway tracks ran beside ships and crowded docks. Dispatcher Vincent Coleman worked in a small wooden office among the freight cars, close to Pier 6.

That morning, the French ship Mont-Blanc collided with the Norwegian Imo. Mont-Blanc carried explosives. A fire broke out and its crew abandoned ship. The burning vessel drifted toward the waterfront. People gathered to watch, unaware of the cargo.

A sailor ran into the railway office and warned that the ship could explode. Coleman started to leave with his supervisor, William Lovett. Then he turned back to the telegraph key.

There was a passenger train approaching Halifax. The overnight service from Saint John, carrying about three hundred people, was due at 8:55. Its route passed near the burning ship.

Coleman sent a warning down the railway line. Its opening is commonly recorded as “Hold up the train.” Accounts differ slightly on the remaining words, but they agree that he warned of the explosives and Pier 6. He ended with a goodbye to the other operators.

And the warning did not stay in that little office. It was carried along the railway network to stations as far as Truro, where staff could hold incoming trains before they entered Halifax.

The people receiving the warning were not standing beside the ship. They had no view of the flames. What they had was an instruction from a railway colleague who could see the danger. The message allowed them to act locally, setting a stop rather than relying on trains continuing into a suddenly silent station.

At 9:05, Mont-Blanc exploded. Pier 6 and the wooden dispatch office vanished. Coleman died, along with roughly two thousand other people. Roads, buildings and communication lines were destroyed.

But operators beyond the ruins had received the warning. Other inbound passenger and freight trains were stopped.

Did Coleman stop the particular passenger train with roughly three hundred people aboard? That famous claim is disputed.

One account collected from passengers and crew says Train No. 10 had already passed Rockingham, the last station before the danger area, and was running late. A newspaper report published the following day says the train was held at Rockingham for fifteen minutes. The two accounts cannot establish one certain sequence.

The message had a second effect, and this one is much clearer. The railway already knew Halifax had suffered a catastrophe, rather than simply losing contact with the city. About an hour later a more detailed request for help came through.

That distinction matters because the blast wrecked ordinary communications. Operators outside Halifax did not have to start by investigating a mysterious breakdown. They had already been told the reason. The later call requested specific help, and the railway could organize a response around information already moving along its lines.

Six relief trains were dispatched that day from towns in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. They carried doctors, nurses, firefighters and medical supplies. A snowstorm slowed travel the next day, making the early response especially valuable. The trains were part of a much wider effort by local residents, soldiers and sailors.

Years later, Coleman's family preserved the telegraph key and his damaged watch. They are now museum objects.

We may never resolve exactly where Train No. 10 was stopped. But other trains were held, the railway received the first alarm, and help moved toward the devastated city. Coleman's message reached people who still had time to act.
