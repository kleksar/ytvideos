How did a railway warning sent from a building that was about to disappear lead to trains being stopped and help being sent to Halifax? And what happened to the dispatcher's own family, who lived close to the harbour?

On the morning of December 6, 1917, Halifax, Canada, was a working wartime port. Ships carried supplies for Europe. Freight trains linked its crowded waterfront with the rest of the country. Dispatcher Vincent Coleman worked in a modest wooden railway office among the Richmond rail yards, about 750 feet from Pier 6.

That morning he had left his wife Frances and their two-year-old daughter Eileen at home on Russell Street. Eileen was wearing a blue dress her mother had made for her. The house was only about six hundred metres from the place where the ship would explode.

At the railway yard, Coleman took over from the night dispatcher. His ordinary job was to direct the traffic serving the docks, including trains carrying soldiers and medical supplies. The railway was part of the daily life of the harbour.

In the narrow harbour channel, two ships were moving in opposite directions. The Norwegian Imo was bound for New York to load supplies. The French Mont-Blanc was bringing war material toward Halifax. They collided.

Mont-Blanc was loaded with explosives. Fire broke out after the collision. The crew abandoned ship, but the burning vessel drifted toward the piers. People onshore gathered to watch the smoke. Most had no idea what was in the ship's holds.

A naval officer who knew the danger sent a sailor ashore with a warning. At Coleman's station, the dispatcher could already see smoke beyond the parked freight cars. Then the sailor came through the door: the burning ship carried ammunition and might explode.

Coleman and his supervisor, William Lovett, started to leave. Then Coleman went back to the telegraph. He was particularly concerned about the overnight passenger train from Saint John, New Brunswick, which was due to arrive in Halifax at 8:55. Its approach would bring it past the burning ship. There were about three hundred people aboard, according to contemporary accounts.

The first words of his message are usually recorded as “Hold up the train.” He warned that an ammunition ship was burning and approaching Pier 6, and that an explosion was expected. The message finished with a goodbye to his fellow operators.

Newspapers recorded slightly different versions of the telegram, so the exact words beyond the familiar opening are not certain. But the instruction was clear. The stations farther along the railway had to stop traffic heading into Halifax.

It was not a note sent to one driver. Railway telegraph lines connected stations. The message from Halifax travelled towards Truro, beyond the threatened office. Operators along that line could set their stop signals and hold trains before they reached the waterfront.

At 9:05 a.m., Mont-Blanc exploded.

The ship and Pier 6 disappeared in the blast. Railway cars were destroyed or thrown about. Coleman's wooden station, only a short distance away, was crushed and buried beneath debris. He died there. Roughly two thousand people across the disaster area were killed.

The damage spread into the residential streets near the harbour. At the Coleman house, a falling kitchen sink injured Eileen's neck. Frances suffered a serious back injury. The house was wrecked and later burned. Both mother and child needed medical care.

Two of their older children, Gerald and Eleanor, came home from school. They helped their mother and sister reach Gottingen Street, where soldiers took them to Camp Hill Hospital. The family was in the hands of local rescuers. No account establishes that the railway warning itself saved them.

The damage reached far beyond the dock. Roads and rail lines were torn apart. Telephone and telegraph communications were disrupted. People outside the city needed to understand what had happened before organized help could travel in.

Farther along the railway there was a different problem: where was Passenger Train No. 10 when the explosion happened? A warning had been sent, but the passengers' fate depended on where the train was when it reached the stations that could stop it.

One was assembled from interviews with passengers and railway workers in 1917. In that account, the train had already passed Rockingham, the last station where it could have been held, and was running late. It was far enough from the epicentre that the blast caused broken windows and minor injuries.

A report in the Moncton Transcript on December 7 gave a different account. Conductor Gillespie said the train had been on schedule until it was held at Rockingham for fifteen minutes. He reported that the blast broke the windows there and that the crew survived.

The two accounts place the train on different sides of the same station. Both report broken windows; neither lets us establish beyond doubt that Coleman's warning saved those particular passengers.

Something else is much more certain. Stations outside Halifax received his warning, and other inbound passenger and freight trains were stopped.

Now the railway faced a new task. After the explosion, the waterfront office could no longer send instructions, but stations outside the city had already received its warning. The alarm had reached them before communications failed.

About an hour later, a fuller request for help was sent from Halifax. The initial warning and this subsequent request helped railway staff organize a response. It would be misleading to credit Coleman's message with every later rescue on its own.

That day, six relief trains were dispatched from places including Truro, Kentville, Amherst, New Glasgow and Moncton. They carried doctors, nurses, firefighters and medical supplies. Equipment and crews could reach a city where many roads and buildings had become impassable.

The next day a snowstorm made transport harder. American relief trains reached Halifax two days after the blast. Those early railway departures mattered precisely because the city's local services were overwhelmed and the weather soon made movement more difficult.

The railway was one part of the wider rescue effort. Local soldiers, sailors and neighbours were already helping people trapped or injured in the city. More medical staff and supplies from outside Halifax could support those overwhelmed services. For the Colemans, the immediate rescue had come from their children and soldiers in the street.

Frances and all four children survived and recovered. Vincent did not return. Searchers found his body in the railway ruins a few days later. His widow received the key, pen and damaged watch recovered after his death.

By the end of the first week, trains were bringing passengers to the repaired North End station. Equipment and work crews had reached the damaged port by rail. A network that had first been told to stop trains was now bringing people and supplies back into the city.

Frances eventually gave Vincent's belongings to the provincial archives. They reached the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic in 2005; his family also donated Eileen's dress that year. The dress and the key survive as evidence of what happened at two different places in Halifax on the same morning.

The fate of Train No. 10 remains uncertain. Yet the message did reach the other stations, other incoming trains were stopped, and after a further call the railway sent six trains carrying help. The little office was gone. The people who had received its last warning were still able to act.
