Running water. That was the sound that led seventeen-year-old Juliane Koepcke out of the Amazon after she fell from an airplane.

On Christmas Eve, 1971, her plane broke apart in a storm over Peru. Her mother was aboard.

Juliane fell about three thousand metres, still attached to a row of seats. By the next morning, she was alone under the trees with a broken collarbone.

Her glasses were gone, and her eyes were swollen. She searched for her mother but couldn't find her.

Her parents studied the rainforest. Her father had taught her that a stream could lead to a river, and a river to people.

Juliane found water trickling out of the ground and followed it downstream.

Search planes flew overhead. The dense canopy kept her hidden.

She had one shoe left, so she tested the ground with that foot before setting down her bare one.

She found a bag of sweets from the wreck and avoided plants she didn't recognise.

The stream grew into a river. As her strength ran out, she sometimes let the current carry her.

Then she spotted a boat near a shelter. At last, she had evidence that people came here.

But the shelter was empty.

When local men finally arrived, Juliane told them in Spanish that she'd been on the missing flight. They took her to safety.

Eleven days after the crash, she was safe. She was the only eventual survivor of the ninety-two people aboard. Her mother had died.

Years later, Juliane became a biologist herself and helped protect part of the rainforest her parents had studied.

The planes could never see her through the trees. What had she heard down on the forest floor that gave her a way out?
