How did a seventeen-year-old survive a fall from an airliner into the Amazon? Why couldn't the planes searching overhead find her? And how could following a sound bring her back to people?

Juliane Koepcke wasn't a stranger to the rainforest. Her parents were scientists who studied it, and she had spent part of her childhood at their research station in Peru. But on Christmas Eve, 1971, she was travelling there by air with her mother.

The flight was approaching its destination when it entered a thunderstorm. Juliane and her mother had delayed the journey so she could attend her school graduation. They were flying to meet her father for Christmas, with another long river journey still ahead. A routine family trip was about to leave Juliane alone in the same forest she had once explored with her parents. The aircraft broke apart. Juliane fell roughly three thousand metres, still attached to a row of seats.

She woke under the trees the next day. Her collarbone was broken, one eye was swollen and her glasses were gone. The row of seats had not delivered her to safety. It had delivered her to a forest so dense that rescuers flying above could not see the ground.

Her first concern was her mother. She searched but found no trace of her. Then she had to decide where to go.

Juliane remembered advice from her father: find running water, follow it downstream, and eventually a stream may lead to a larger river and human settlement. She discovered a tiny flow of water emerging from the earth and began following it.

That gave her a direction, not an easy route.

She had only one shoe. Her dress, bought for a school celebration, was almost no protection against the rainforest. She put that foot down first to check the ground before risking her bare one among thorns and insects. Rain soaked her thin dress. She had found a small bag of sweets among the wreckage, but she knew better than to try unknown plants or mushrooms.

Sometimes she could hear aircraft passing above. The people in them were looking for survivors, yet the canopy hid her. She could not simply wait for someone to spot her.

As she followed the growing stream, she encountered another row of aircraft seats with three bodies still in them. One was a woman. Juliane checked and realised it wasn't her mother. She continued along the water.

The sweets dwindled. Rain had ruined a Christmas cake found near the wreck, and food growing high in the canopy was out of reach. The river grew wider, and she sometimes let the current carry her when walking became too difficult. There were animals along the banks, but she did not interpret every movement as a path toward a village. For days, the river was her only useful guide.

Then she saw a boat beside a shelter. No one was inside. It was the first unmistakable sign that people used this stretch of river. She stayed near it.

Local men eventually arrived. Juliane spoke Spanish and explained that she had been aboard the missing aircraft. They brought her out of the forest.

She had survived eleven days after the crash. Her father, waiting for a Christmas reunion, eventually saw his daughter alive again. Of the ninety-two people aboard, she was the only person to survive. Her mother had died.

The extraordinary fall made headlines, but the journey afterward had depended on something much less spectacular: knowledge her parents had taught her before the flight. She went on to become a biologist herself and later helped protect the rainforest around their research station.

The planes couldn't see Juliane through the canopy. The water, which she could hear before she could see it, gave her a way through.
