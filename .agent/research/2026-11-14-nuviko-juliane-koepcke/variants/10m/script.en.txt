How could a teenage passenger fall thousands of metres from a breaking airplane and still walk away? Why did the aircraft searching for survivors fail to find her? And what allowed her to navigate a rainforest that concealed even the ground from the sky?

In December 1971, Juliane Koepcke was seventeen. Her mother Maria studied birds; her father Hans-Wilhelm studied the animals of the Peruvian rainforest. The family had established a field station called Panguana, where Juliane had already spent long periods among the rivers and trees.

She knew the forest's sounds before she ever had to depend on them. She could recognise some of its birds and frogs. Her parents had also taught her a practical rule: when lost, find running water. A small stream can join a larger one, and a larger river gives you a better chance of reaching people.

Juliane had just finished school in Lima. She and her mother planned to meet her father for Christmas at Panguana. They boarded a commercial flight to eastern Peru on Christmas Eve. The station itself was still a long journey away by river.

It seemed like the ordinary first leg of a trip home. Then dark storm clouds gathered.

The aircraft entered violent weather on its approach. According to Juliane's later account, there was a flash, the plane broke apart and she was suddenly outside it, still held to a row of seats. She fell about three thousand metres toward the trees.

How the fall failed to kill her remains uncertain. It is tempting to claim that the seats acted like a parachute, or that the storm lifted her gently. Those are explanations people have proposed, not facts established by Juliane's memories. She lost consciousness. The next morning she was on the forest floor, alive.

Her collarbone was broken. She was bruised and cut, her glasses were gone, and one eye was swollen enough to make seeing difficult. All around her were trees. No road, no village, no rescue party.

Her mother had been sitting near her on the plane. Juliane looked for her, but found nothing. She had been separated not only from the rest of the passengers, but from anyone who could tell her where she was.

She needed to move. Yet choosing the wrong direction in this forest could take her deeper into it. That was when her father's lesson became more than something she remembered from childhood.

She found a tiny stream emerging from the ground and followed it downstream. At first, following water sounds simple. But the riverbank was not a path. Roots and tangled vegetation slowed her. Juliane had lost one shoe, so she used the remaining shod foot to test uncertain ground before bringing down the bare one. This helped her avoid thorns and insects as she moved.

Her clothes offered little protection from the rain. Near the scattered wreckage, she had found a bag of hard sweets. They were a small supply, not enough for an indefinite walk. She recognised that the rainforest was not a supermarket: unfamiliar fruit and mushrooms might harm her. She left them alone.

There was one especially cruel feature of her predicament. Search aircraft flew above the forest. She could hear them, but beneath the dense canopy there was little chance their crews would see her. To the pilots the forest was an unbroken roof of green. To Juliane it was a maze with a narrow line of moving water.

As she moved downstream, the sounds she knew helped her orient herself. Birds and frogs were familiar from Panguana, but this was not a cheerful return to the field station. The plane crash had left her alone and injured. She still had to find other people.

On the way, she came upon another row of aircraft seats. Three passengers were still in it. One was a woman, and Juliane checked whether she might be her mother. She wasn't. The discovery answered one terrible question, but not the one that mattered most: where was Maria?

Juliane returned to following the stream.

The small channel joined larger water. The days passed. Her bag of sweets emptied. Much of what was growing around her could not simply be eaten, and the rain made shelter and warmth difficult. Sometimes she waded; sometimes, increasingly weak, she allowed the current to carry her.

The water was helping her travel, yet it also exposed a problem. She passed wildlife along the banks, including animals she associated with remote forest. A river did not guarantee that a village was just around the next bend.

She kept the direction. At least water would flow somewhere, while choosing arbitrary turns among the trees gave her nothing she could rely on.

Eventually Juliane found a boat. At first, exhausted, she doubted what she was seeing. She reached it and discovered it was real. A nearby shelter suggested that people worked on this part of the river.

But no one was there.

She had reached a human place without finding a human being. For a teenager who had spent days moving alone, this was an enormous change, but it was not yet a rescue. She stayed close to the shelter. A wound had become infested with larvae. Accounts of her ordeal describe her using fuel found there to remove them; that is a description of what she did, not medical advice.

The following day, local men came to the shelter. Juliane could speak Spanish. Instead of being unable to explain the strange sight of an injured girl emerging from the trees, she could tell them that she had been on the missing flight.

They helped her out along the river and toward medical care. Eleven days after the crash, she was safe.

Ninety-two people had been aboard the plane. Juliane was the sole eventual survivor. Her mother had died. The world learned about a girl who had survived an impossible-looking fall, but the public story did not erase the loss of her mother or the other passengers.

She was reunited with her father. Her later life did not end at the moment of rescue. She studied biology, returned to work in the rainforest and helped sustain the Panguana research and conservation project. The place her family had studied before the disaster became part of her own life's work.

The details that mattered on her eleven-day journey were not the dramatic ones the headlines could show. A single shoe helped her judge the ground. A small bag of sweets bought a little time. Knowing which forest foods to avoid kept her from making a desperate mistake. Speaking Spanish made the last conversation possible.

Above all, the running water changed what she could do. The search planes could not see through the canopy. Her parents had taught her to follow a stream until it grew into a river, and in the end that river brought her to the people who could help her.

The forest had hidden Juliane from the search. Her knowledge of that same forest gave her a way out.
