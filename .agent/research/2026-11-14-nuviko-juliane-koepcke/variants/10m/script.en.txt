Why could seventeen-year-old Juliane Koepcke hear the planes searching for her, while they couldn't find her? How did she reach people after falling into the Amazon from an airliner? And why would she later return to the forest where she had lost her mother?

In 1968, two biologists from the natural history museum in Lima moved into a stretch of forest in eastern Peru with their daughter. Maria Koepcke studied birds; her husband Hans-Wilhelm studied animals and their surroundings. They wanted to understand how a whole patch of rainforest worked, so they began recording the life they found in it. Their station was called Panguana, after a local forest bird, the undulated tinamou.

Juliane spent part of her childhood there. She grew familiar with the calls of animals that she could not necessarily see through the vegetation. Her parents also taught her that flowing water could lead from an isolated patch of forest to a river used by people. Later she went back to Lima to finish school.

In December 1971, Juliane was seventeen. She and Maria postponed their journey so Juliane could attend her graduation celebration. On Christmas Eve they boarded a flight to Pucallpa, the first part of a journey to meet her father. From there, reaching the station would still require a long boat ride. Maria and her daughter were sitting next to each other.

The two of them had made the trip to the rainforest before. This time, the journey would end far from any landing strip.

The aircraft entered violent weather on its approach. According to Juliane's later account, there was a flash, the plane broke apart and she was suddenly outside it, still held to a row of seats. She fell about three thousand metres toward the trees.

How the fall failed to kill her remains uncertain. The seats and the canopy may have slowed or softened her fall, but neither the exact descent nor the impact was observed in enough detail to establish a definite explanation. Juliane lost consciousness. By the following morning she was on the forest floor, alive.

Her collarbone was broken. She was bruised and cut, her glasses were gone, and one eye was swollen enough to make seeing difficult. All around her were trees. No road, no village, no rescue party.

When she tried to get up, she blacked out again. Her hearing, however, still worked. She later described recognising the calls of frogs and birds from Panguana. Among them was the bird after which her parents had named their station. She could tell she was in a kind of forest she knew, although the calls gave her no precise location or route to the station.

Her mother had been sitting near her on the plane. Juliane looked for her, but found nothing. She had been separated not only from the rest of the passengers, but from anyone who could tell her where she was.

The search for Maria gave her no direction. With the canopy hiding the sky and her eyesight impaired, it was easy to lose track even of the way she had just come. Her father's advice offered a route that did not depend on recognising a particular tree or knowing where the station lay.

She found a tiny stream emerging from the ground and followed it downstream. At first, following water sounds simple. But the riverbank was not a path. Roots and tangled vegetation slowed her. Juliane had lost one shoe, so she used the remaining shod foot to test uncertain ground before bringing down the bare one. This helped her avoid thorns and insects as she moved.

Her clothes offered little protection from the rain. Near the scattered wreckage, she had found a bag of hard sweets. There was also Christmas cake in a box, but the rain had soaked it until she could not eat it. She rationed the sweets as the days passed, knowing no one was delivering another meal. They were a small supply, not enough for an indefinite walk. She recognised that the rainforest was not a supermarket: unfamiliar fruit and mushrooms might harm her. She left them alone.

There was one especially cruel feature of her predicament. Search aircraft flew above the forest. She could hear them, but beneath the dense canopy there was little chance their crews would see her. To the pilots the forest was an unbroken roof of green. To Juliane it was a maze with a narrow line of moving water.

Later, she recognised a different sound: the calls of hoatzins, birds that live near substantial waterways. Before she could make out the larger river clearly, she had an auditory sign that the tiny stream was leading somewhere different. There was still no sign of a settlement.

Another kind of bird call brought her to a very different discovery. She recalled hearing vultures and coming across a row of aircraft seats with three dead passengers. One was a woman. With her poor eyesight, Juliane looked at the woman's toenails. They were painted; Maria did not paint hers. It was a detail she could use to rule out one possibility. She still had no idea where her mother was.

Juliane returned to following the stream.

As the water widened, Juliane could spend more time in it. Walking was becoming harder and her only food, the sweets, eventually ran out. She sometimes floated with the current. The river bends kept the next stretch of bank hidden until she reached it.

The water was helping her travel, yet it also exposed a problem. She passed wildlife along the banks, including animals she associated with remote forest. A river did not guarantee that a village was just around the next bend.

The river remained her best clue. The wildlife suggested the forest was still remote, but leaving the water would also mean giving up the one continuous route she could follow with her damaged eyesight.

Eventually Juliane found a boat. At first, exhausted, she doubted what she was seeing. She reached it and discovered it was real. A nearby shelter suggested that people worked on this part of the river.

But no one was there.

The shelter was evidence of human activity, not a promise that anyone would turn up. Juliane's arm wound had become infested with larvae. She later described a painful and risky attempt to deal with it using supplies she found in the shelter. She remained there instead of passing the first clear trace of people she had found.

When men eventually appeared, the sight of her surprised them. Juliane later recalled that they initially mistook her for a figure from a local river legend. Then she addressed them in Spanish and explained that she was a passenger from the missing flight. At last someone could understand who she was and what had happened.

The men took her out along the river towards medical care. Her journey had lasted eleven days. Once she could speak to people, she also learned that the wreckage had not yet been located. She had escaped, but the search for her mother was not over.

Maria's remains were found later. Of the ninety-two people who had been aboard, only Juliane ultimately survived. She was reunited with her father; the Christmas trip that was supposed to bring three people together had ended with only two.

Reporters and strangers sought her out in Peru. At her father's urging she went to Germany, where she could live more quietly. She studied biology and for years avoided interviews about the crash. She did eventually travel to Peru again, and the work her family had begun in the forest remained part of her life.

Her own scientific specialty became bats. In 2000 she took over the leadership of Panguana. The research station had been set up to study how the animals and plants of one forest lived together; the project now also had the task of keeping that habitat intact.

In 2014 Juliane and her husband established a foundation to support the project. It protects forest, supports the research station and works with neighbouring Indigenous communities. The small scientific camp her parents had started became the basis of a much longer effort to preserve the rainforest.

There is a connection between those years of fieldwork and the eleven days after the crash. Juliane could not see the search planes through the trees; she could hear them. She recognised the birds of her childhood, then the calls associated with a wider river. She followed the water until she found a boat and could speak to its owners.

The forest bird after which her parents named their station still gives Panguana its name. Juliane returned to help keep the forest around it standing.
