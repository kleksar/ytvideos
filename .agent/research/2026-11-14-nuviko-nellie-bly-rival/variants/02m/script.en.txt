A clerk at a Hong Kong shipping office told Nellie Bly another woman had sailed from there three days earlier—to race her around the world.

Nellie hadn't known anyone was racing her. She was a New York reporter trying to beat the eighty days in Jules Verne's novel.

Almost six weeks earlier, her editor had given her two days to prepare. She took one small handbag.

She crossed the Atlantic by steamship, then traveled through Europe by train.

In France she met Jules Verne himself. She couldn't stay long: missing her next train meant losing a week.

Meanwhile, a rival magazine sent journalist Elizabeth Bisland west, around the globe in the opposite direction.

In Hong Kong, Nellie's next steamer wouldn't leave for days. She used part of the wait to visit Canton.

She finally sailed to Japan and across the Pacific. Storms battered the ship before it reached California.

From the harbor she learned that snow had blocked most rail passes through the mountains.

Her newspaper chartered a special train. Just one sleeping car and an engine, taking a southern route around the snow.

People came out to see it pass. In Chicago, Nellie switched to an ordinary train.

On January twenty-fifth, 1890, she stepped onto a platform in Jersey City. She had made it around the world in seventy-two days, six hours and eleven minutes.

Elizabeth arrived a few days later. Both women had beaten the fictional eighty-day journey, but Nellie came home first.
