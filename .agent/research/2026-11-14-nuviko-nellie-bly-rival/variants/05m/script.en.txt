How could a reporter race another woman around the world without even knowing she had a rival? Why did a three-day lead in Hong Kong fail to decide the winner? And what got Nellie Bly home when snow blocked her railway?

In 1889, Bly was reporting for the New York World. Her plan was to beat the eighty days in Jules Verne's famous novel. She hoped to finish in seventy-five. Her editor had originally resisted the idea of a woman traveling around the globe on her own.

Then he told her to depart in two days. Bly packed a small handbag and set out on scheduled ships and trains. She could buy tickets, but not control when those services left.

On November fourteenth, her ship sailed east across the Atlantic. That same day, the owner of Cosmopolitan magazine recruited another journalist, Elizabeth Bisland. Bisland initially refused. Within hours she was traveling west by train.

The two women had different routes, but the American press now had a race to follow. Bly had not been told about the other participant.

She crossed the Atlantic, rushed through Europe and managed a brief meeting in France with Jules Verne himself. A missed connection could cost a week, so she continued toward the Suez Canal and across Asia by sea.

Then Bly reached a shipping office in Hong Kong. A clerk told her another woman had departed from that port three days earlier. Her name was Elizabeth Bisland.

That was the moment Bly discovered the second race. Yet the three days proved surprisingly little. Bisland was traveling in the opposite direction; the two still had different ships, weather and rail connections ahead.

Bly's own next steamer would not leave for several days. She could do nothing to move its departure forward. She used the wait to visit Canton, then traveled to Japan and boarded the Oceanic for the Pacific crossing.

The crossing was battered by storms. When Bly reached the San Francisco area in January, the ocean was behind her, but the race was not. Heavy snow had blocked much of the usual railway route through the mountains.

Her newspaper chartered a special train. It had one sleeping car and an engine, and took a southern route across California and the Southwest rather than the blocked direct crossing.

Railroad workers helped make the transfers work. Crowds gathered along the line to see the train pass. The special service could avoid one obstruction, but it still needed working tracks and people to keep it moving.

Bly changed to an ordinary train in Chicago. On January twenty-fifth, 1890, she arrived at Jersey City to a cheering reception. Her total time was seventy-two days, six hours and eleven minutes.

Bisland arrived several days later. She too beat the fictional eighty-day mark. But Bly, who had not even known she was racing Bisland when she left New York, finished first.

The result was decided by more than speed. Both travelers depended on scheduled ships and railways. A rival could leave one port earlier and still lose; a reporter whose railway was blocked could finish by taking a completely different route.
