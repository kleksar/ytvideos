How did two American reporters circle the world in opposite directions while only one of them knew it was a race? Why did a rival seem to be three days ahead in Hong Kong? And how did an unexpected train change decide the finish thousands of miles later?

To understand the race, begin with a novel. Jules Verne had imagined a man traveling around the globe in eighty days. In 1889, real steamships, railways and the Suez Canal made such a trip possible. But one could not simply buy a ticket for the whole world and expect every connection to work.

Nellie Bly had an idea: test the fictional journey for the New York World and try to finish in seventy-five days. She had already become known as a reporter willing to take difficult assignments. Yet her editor initially questioned whether she could travel alone. He worried about language barriers, luggage and the lack of a male escort. The proposal sat for roughly a year.

On November twelfth, 1889, he suddenly told her to leave in two days. Bly chose a small handbag over trunks. Everything she needed for the coming months had to fit around a schedule of steamship departures and railway connections.

On November fourteenth, Bly left the New York area aboard the Augusta Victoria, bound for Europe. Elsewhere in New York, publisher John Brisben Walker learned that the World had sent its reporter off around the globe.

Walker ran Cosmopolitan. He saw that the story would be even more exciting if a second newspaper had a traveler going the other way. He summoned his literary editor, Elizabeth Bisland, and proposed that she go west around the world to beat Bly.

Bisland was not waiting with a suitcase by the door. She originally refused. She had appointments and little interest in the kind of publicity that a newspaper contest would bring. But Walker pressed the idea. That evening she boarded a westbound train.

Bly, meanwhile, was crossing the Atlantic. She was not informed that the other reporter had set out on the same day. Readers at home could compare the two women, but Bly could only follow her own itinerary.

The Atlantic crossing was not effortless. Bly became seasick, then recovered and reached England. From there she traveled through Europe by train. In France she managed a short visit with Jules Verne himself, the author whose imaginary timetable she was trying to defeat.

That meeting was not an excuse to stay. Missing a train in Europe could mean missing the next ship. And an ocean liner might not depart again for many days. Bly hurried onward toward the Mediterranean and through the Suez Canal.

The canal reduced the distance between Europe and Asia, but it did not remove the gap between one ship's arrival and another ship's departure. Her journey moved from the Indian Ocean to ports in South and Southeast Asia, depending on several separate services.

Bisland took a different chain of connections. She first crossed America to the Pacific, then traveled through Asia toward Europe. The women were never moving side by side. A lead at one port might disappear at the next sea crossing.

By December, Bly had spent weeks at sea. Some connections went smoothly; others offered little room to recover lost time. Her journey was unusual enough to attract attention, but in many ports the local people did not know how closely American readers were following her.

Then she reached Hong Kong ahead of the ship's expected arrival date. At a shipping office, she asked about the onward crossing. The clerk told her that another woman had already sailed from Hong Kong three days earlier.

The name was Elizabeth Bisland. This was the first time Bly understood that the press had turned her trip into a contest with another traveler.

A three-day lead sounds decisive. But the women were moving in opposite directions, using different ships and facing different oceans. The clerk knew a departure date from Hong Kong. He could not tell which woman would arrive in New York first.

Bly also learned that her own next steamer would not leave for several days. She could not persuade it to depart early. In the meantime she took a side trip to Canton over Christmas and returned to continue the journey.

She crossed onward to Japan. While she was there, Japanese newspapers took notice of her visit; the world was becoming connected by the very transport and communication routes that made the trip possible. Yet public interest could not accelerate the ship across the Pacific.

From Yokohama she boarded the Oceanic. It was an older, slower steamer than the Atlantic ship that had started her journey. Ahead lay a long Pacific crossing, with no useful rail alternative if the sea became rough.

And it did. Strong storms battered the vessel. Approaching San Francisco, there was also a scare involving the ship's health documents and rumors of disease. The rumor proved false, so Bly did not have to endure the threatened quarantine. But for a moment a port rule, not distance, appeared capable of ending the attempt.

On January twenty-first, Bly reached the American coast. She had crossed the Pacific. Her remaining task looked simple on a map: take a train across the United States.

The weather made it anything but simple. Snow had blocked much of the usual direct rail route across the mountains. Two newspaper colleagues sent to meet her were themselves delayed by the conditions. After crossing oceans on fixed schedules, Bly now needed an entirely different railway route.

The World chartered a train consisting of one sleeping car and an engine. It traveled south through California and then east through the Southwest instead of attempting the blocked direct crossing.

That special train was not free from railway rules. It still needed track, crews and dispatching at every stage. Workers helped keep its journey moving, and one stretch went over a bridge still under repair. The paper had arranged an alternative, but the railway staff had to make it function in the real world.

Word of the race had spread across the country. People gathered at stations to watch Bly pass. Some came to cheer, others simply wanted to see the woman whose journey had become national news.

Bly could not stay to meet everyone. The special train carried her east, and she eventually changed to a regular service in Chicago. She traveled through the eastern states, greeted by crowds and by members of her family along the way.

At 3:51 in the afternoon on January twenty-fifth, 1890, she arrived in Jersey City. The recorded duration was seventy-two days, six hours and eleven minutes. She had beaten both the fictional eighty days and her personal target of seventy-five.

But the other reporter was still on her way.

Near the end of her own trip, Bisland had faced a frantic series of train and ferry journeys through western Europe to reach an Atlantic departure. The ocean crossing then met severe weather. Her obstacles were not Bly's snowy western railroad, but they had the same consequence: an itinerary that looked close to completion could still lose days.

Bisland reached New York several days later, after approximately seventy-six days of travel. She too had circled the world faster than Verne's fictional traveler. In the race created by the newspapers, however, she finished second.

The contrast continued after the journey. Bly's return became a public celebration. Bisland had been reluctant to become famous in the first place. She wrote about her travels, but did not turn the race into a lecture campaign.

Neither woman simply ran a faster version of the same route. They had opposite directions, different ships and different last obstacles. The newspaper scoreboard made them competitors, but they were each dependent on a global network neither controlled.

This is why the news at Hong Kong was such a misleading clue. Bisland had left that port three days before Bly. Bly was delayed at sea and later blocked by snow. Yet by taking the southern railway detour she still reached home first.

Both beat the imaginary eighty-day journey. Only one won the unexpected race.
