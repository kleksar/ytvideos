## Three competing qualified finalist pitches

### 1. Operation Coldfeet — chosen
- **Audience:** English historical-curiosity Short viewers. Familiar words (bomber, ice, balloon) before technical specifics; no need to know NP8/Fulton.
- **Exact first line:** “A bomber lifted a man straight off Arctic ice without landing.”
- **Stop:** visible contradiction to normal pickup/landing, a real physical trick.
- **After hook:** two parachutists explore an empty Soviet station; collect documents, wait through fog; balloon-and-line demonstrated first with cargo; then one officer is dragged, rights himself; second is dragged, catches an ice crack; both recovered.
- **Complete payoff:** evidence about Soviet under-ice submarine detection brought out with both officers. The plane never landed; no answer hidden until replay.
- **Strongest objection:** Another US intelligence/Cold War adventure in an already military-heavy queue, and mechanism may take too many words to explain. The new angle is precise **aerial physical rescue** with two distinct interruptions, not combat. If the first independent listener thinks it is just a technical demo, reject before official gate.

### 2. Project Azorian — nearest alternative
- **Audience:** general English history/engineering and espionage viewers.
- **Exact pitch first line:** “The CIA built a mining ship to lift a Soviet submarine from the bottom of the ocean.”
- **Stop:** civilian deep-sea mining cover conceals massive secret salvage.
- **After hook:** Soviet K-129 wreck discovered; fictional mining cover; custom lifting equipment from great depth; wreck splits during 1974 lift; a partial section recovered; planned second attempt compromised after a burglary and publicity.
- **Payoff:** an important technical but incomplete recovery; six Soviet sailors' remains recovered and buried at sea.
- **Strongest objection:** Too many mechanics, people, multi-year transitions and sombre aftermath to deliver a fair concise Short without reducing story to “the secret claw broke.” Could make better Long.
- **Source read:** CIA Museum, `Project AZORIAN`, https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/ (accessed 2026-11-14), especially paragraphs on 1968 K-129, 1974 recovery, split wreck, burial and abandoned follow-up. Agency retrospective, not independent audit.

### 3. Fall of Berlin Wall, 1989
- **Audience:** broad English public/history; real people finally crossing a political border.
- **Exact pitch first line:** “An East German official announced the border was opening. The guards were still at their posts.”
- **Stop:** immediate discrepancy between public announcement and guarded crossing.
- **After hook:** pre-existing peaceful protests and travel reform, confusing Schabowski press announcement on Nov 9, crowd collects at checkpoints, Bornholmer Straße opens about 23:30, other gates follow.
- **Payoff:** crossings begin that night, a human result with great stakes.
- **Strongest objection:** Commonly retold and the “one accidental sentence opened the wall” angle erases earlier demonstrations/concessions; substantial caveats crowd a Short. Stronger human consequences than Coldfeet, less novelty at current candidate mix.
- **Source read:** German Federal Government, `9 November - a historically significant date`, published 2016-11-08, https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-en/service/archive/archive/9-november-a-historically-signficant-date-411370 (accessed 2026-11-14).

**Selection vs strongest alternative:** Coldfeet is better for this **specific Short**, not necessarily a more important historical story. Azorian's strongest engineering payoff requires several expensive explanations and ends in partial recovery; Coldfeet naturally moves through insertion → material pickup → two separate human pickups → evidence value. Azorian remains viable for long-form research in future, not a duplicate issue/PR created now. Even Coldfeet loses if a first listener finds the middle uninteresting; independent review has not happened.

## Evidence ledger: exact claim, supporting material, uncertainties

All accesses on **2026-11-14**; sources are historical institutional retrospectives unless specified. Exact production scripts intentionally do not announce these sources in the voiceover.

1. **Site and chronology** — CIA museum article `Seven Days in the Arctic`: https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/artifact/seven-days-in-the-arctic/ , paragraph beginning “During the Cold War” and next paragraph “On 28 May 1962”. A Soviet abandoned polar station was spotted in 1961; another abandoned site NP8 was subsequently found. On **May 28 1962**, USAF Maj James F. Smith and USNR Lt (jg) Leonard A. LeSchack parachuted to NP8. EN says “In 1962, American intelligence learned…” without implying 1961 original site was the same NP8. **Limit:** CIA Museum's own retrospective for commissioned art, not contemporary flight orders.
2. **Materials** — same article, May 28 paragraph: photographed site and gathered **150 pounds** of documents and equipment samples. RU `около 68 килограммов` is 150lb ×0.453592≈68.0kg, numerical conversion, not a second measurement. **Limit:** no independent itemized inventory read.
3. **Duration and weather** — same CIA article: planned **72-hour** stay, dense fog delayed pickup by one day; strong winds up to 30kt. EN “three days … another day” and RU “три дня … сутки” identical. **Limit:** no invented cause of fog or danger/timing in scripts. Skyhook was planned beforehand, not improvised due to weather.
4. **Mechanism** — CIA Museum `Skyhook Extraction Mechanism Instructions` https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/artifact/skyhook-extraction-mechanism-instructions/ ; historical artifact/online explanation says helium balloon lifts about **500-foot** line, ground harness attached to other end, low B-17's nose-mounted capture arms intercept line, onboard winch recovers cargo/people without landing. Original instruction card is described as historical artifact; **we read text, not physical card reverse and not actual image reference bytes**. No false claim that a source image was inspected.
5. **Order and cargo extraction** — CIA `Seven Days in the Arctic` pickup paragraph: canvas bag first, then LeSchack, then Smith. Aircraft flew low and snagged line; scripts refrain from inventing exact speed or 425ft altitude. **Limit:** narrative is agency reconstruction of mission, not frame-by-frame timeline.
6. **LeSchack** — same paragraph: high winds dragged him on stomach roughly 300 feet; he corrected body orientation before hoist. Scripts omit distance and fabricated injuries/emotions; “turned into position” matches account.
7. **Smith** — same paragraph: wind dragged him until he caught an ice crack with his heels; he was hoisted aboard. **Limit:** no invented boot count, rescuer dialogue, exact duration or near-death assertion.
8. **Result** — same CIA article paragraph after pickups: intelligence on Soviet acoustic detection of under-ice submarines and anti-submarine methods. Script “papers and samples gave the Americans information” matches CIA assessment without claiming technologies copied, Soviet deployment altered or war prevented. **Limit:** interested institution describes value; independent document verification UNVERIFIED.
9. **Return** — same CIA pickup narrative says both were pulled aboard and team completed operation. EN “got home,” RU “вернулись” mean safely recovered; specific later destination is not independently verified. A reviewer may recommend “were safely aboard” as narrower; must rehash/review if changed.

**No invented quotations, feelings, secret Soviet intentions or visual archive footage.** The CIA's Museum page includes a 2007 painted depiction and an embedded **modern explanatory Skyhook video/transcript** on the instruction-card page. These are not literal 1962 footage. Staged storyboard camera angles and military clothing need later reference checks. Claim “No runway” is visual context, NOT an unsupported detail about a failed landing attempt; actual script says landing was never planned.
