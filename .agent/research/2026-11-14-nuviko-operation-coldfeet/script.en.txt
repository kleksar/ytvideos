A bomber lifted a man straight off Arctic ice without landing.

In 1962, American intelligence learned that a Soviet research station had been abandoned on a drifting ice floe.

Two American officers, James Smith and Leonard LeSchack, parachuted down to see what had been left behind.

They photographed the station and packed 150 pounds of documents and equipment samples.

They planned to stay three days. Thick fog delayed their pickup by another day.

When the B-17 finally came, the wind was still sweeping across the ice. Landing had never been the plan.

The men raised a long cable with a helium balloon. The other end could be attached to a bag or a person's harness.

The bomber flew low, caught the cable on a fork at its nose, and winched the bag of research aboard.

Then LeSchack attached his harness. The wind dragged him on his stomach across the ice.

He managed to turn into position, and the crew hauled him up into the aircraft.

Smith was next. The same wind started dragging him across the ice.

He caught an ice crack with his heels, held his position, and was pulled aboard too.

The papers and samples gave the Americans information about Soviet methods of detecting submarines under Arctic ice.
