A drifting Arctic ice station doesn't stay where an aircraft last saw it. In 1962, two American officers were waiting on one, and a bomber had to pick them up without landing.

James Smith and Leonard LeSchack had parachuted onto an abandoned Soviet station. The Americans wanted to know how the Soviets were studying submarines beneath the ice.

The men photographed the buildings and filled a bag with about 150 pounds of documents and equipment samples.

When the pilots came back, fog hid the station. Another search failed too. A Navy aircraft finally found it and guided the bomber to the ice.

The crew collected the bag first, using a balloon and a line that the plane could catch in flight. Both officers still had to get aboard.

Smith tried to hold LeSchack steady, but the wind dragged him across the ice until a block of ice stopped him. The crew picked him up, and he managed to turn himself into the right position before they hauled him inside.

Smith had nobody left to hold him steady. He clung to a tractor, then braced his heels in a crack in the ice as the wind pulled at him.

The bomber caught his line and brought him aboard too. American investigators reported that the recovered material shed light on Soviet submarine-detection research.

Both men and the bag were safely aboard. So why couldn't the pilots simply fly back to the spot where they had dropped the men?
