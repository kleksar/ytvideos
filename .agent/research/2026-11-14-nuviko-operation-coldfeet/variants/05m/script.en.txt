How do you get two men off drifting Arctic ice when no plane can land? Why would anyone send them there in the first place? And what could they bring home that was worth such an unusual pickup?

In 1961 an American reconnaissance flight spotted an abandoned Soviet research station. Its ice runway had broken up. American naval researchers wanted to see what the Soviets had been studying, especially whether their instruments could help detect submarines beneath the ice.

Getting in was possible by parachute. Getting out was the problem. The station was beyond the reach of an icebreaker or a helicopter.

The first mission stalled. The station kept drifting, equipment tests caused delays, and eventually the Americans switched to another abandoned camp, called NP-8. An aircraft searched for it in April 1962, but found only ice. The mission was called off.

Then a reconnaissance plane located NP-8 farther from its predicted position. The Navy had run out of money, but the CIA brought in a B-17 bomber modified for a system called Skyhook.

Here was the remarkable part: the plane would collect people without ever touching the ground.

On May 28, Air Force Major James Smith and Navy officer Leonard LeSchack parachuted onto the ice. Smith knew Russian and had worked at American polar stations. LeSchack understood scientific instruments used to study the polar environment. For three days they photographed the empty camp and sorted through the equipment and papers left behind.

They assembled about 150 pounds of material. But the aircraft couldn't simply circle back and land. It had to find the drifting station again, then recover the cargo and both men in flight.

Fog hid the site when the pickup was due. A further search failed. Another naval aircraft finally helped guide the B-17 to the station.

The men now faced strong surface winds. Their escape equipment looked almost absurdly simple: a helium balloon, a long nylon line and a harness. The balloon lifted the line into the air. Two metal arms on the B-17's nose caught it, and a winch drew the attached load toward the aircraft.

The crew tested the idea on the documents first. The bomber approached low, intercepted the cable and hauled up the canvas bag. The research was safe, but the two men were still standing on the ice.

LeSchack went next. As the balloon rose, the wind tore him from Smith's grip and dragged him stomach-first across the ice for about 300 feet. An ice block stopped the slide. The aircraft caught his line, but he rose facing the wrong direction. He managed to turn before the crew winched him aboard.

Smith was alone now. He held on to a tractor as he inflated his balloon, yet the wind started pulling him away too. He caught a crack in the ice with his heels and held himself in position.

The B-17 made its final pass. Its nose caught the cable. For a moment the line hung on the outer part of the metal arm, then slid into the catch. The crew secured it and winched Smith aboard.

Both men were safe. According to the American mission account, the recovered material offered valuable information about Soviet research into underwater acoustics and submarine detection beneath Arctic ice.

The larger result was practical as well: the team had demonstrated that an aircraft could insert investigators by parachute and retrieve them from a place without a usable runway. One abandoned ice station had tested not just what the Soviets knew, but whether this unlikely way home would work at all.
