What did Soviet scientists leave behind on an ice station that they could no longer reach? How could two Americans get inside without a runway? And once they were there, how could a bomber pull them back into the sky?

The story begins beneath the Arctic ice. In the Cold War, submarines could move under the frozen ocean, far from ordinary observation. The United States had put listening equipment on one of its own drifting research stations. Naval researchers suspected that the Soviets were investigating similar methods, but they had no direct evidence.

In 1961 an American survey aircraft spotted an abandoned Soviet camp. Its runway had been damaged by shifting ice. The station, called NP-9, was still moving with the pack, and there was no practical way to send an icebreaker or a helicopter to it.

The Americans saw an opportunity to examine Soviet equipment firsthand. They chose two men for the job. Air Force Major James Smith was an experienced parachutist, spoke Russian and had served on American drift stations. Navy officer Leonard LeSchack was a geophysicist who had worked on an Arctic listening network. He trained as a parachutist for the mission.

They could jump in. Leaving was another matter.

An inventor named Robert Fulton had spent years working on just that problem. A plane could pick up a mailbag from the ground using a cable, but lifting a person required a different system. Fulton experimented with balloons, stronger nylon line and a device that would lock the line onto a moving aircraft. In 1958 a Marine became the first person recovered by the completed Skyhook system.

The principle was simple enough to explain. A package on the ground held a harness, a helium bottle, a balloon and roughly 500 feet of line. The balloon carried the line upright. A low-flying aircraft had two long metal arms pointing from its nose. It flew between the line and the sky, caught the line, and transferred the load to a winch. The passenger would rise from the ground and be brought aboard.

But a system that worked in tests was about to face a much harder job on drifting ice.

The Navy delayed the mission while senior officers debated its risks. Cold-weather trials exposed equipment problems, and the original target kept moving farther from the planned air base. By March 1962 the team had a new target: another deserted Soviet station, NP-8, newer and more accessible than NP-9. Its ice runway had also been damaged.

They tried to find NP-8 in April. A transport plane searched the predicted area, then narrowed its search pattern. It found an abandoned American station instead. After repeated flights and no Soviet camp, the mission was called off.

A reconnaissance aircraft finally located NP-8 on May 4, well away from its predicted position. That should have solved the problem. Instead, Navy research funding had run out.

The CIA stepped in. Its contractor, Intermountain Aviation, already had a World War II-era B-17 fitted for Skyhook retrieval. Pilots Connie Seigrist and Douglas Price had practiced catching lines from the aircraft. A support plane joined the operation, and the team moved its staging point to Alaska.

On May 27 the B-17 searched the frozen ocean and came back without finding the station. The next day a Navy patrol aircraft helped locate NP-8. The bomber circled while the two officers selected a place to jump.

Smith went through the aircraft's floor opening first. LeSchack followed. Supplies came down after them. A radio report confirmed they were on the ice. Then the bomber left.

They had planned seventy-two hours at the deserted camp. Smith and LeSchack photographed the buildings and collected exposed film, papers and examples of scientific equipment. The recovered material eventually filled a canvas bag weighing about 150 pounds, roughly 68 kilograms.

The mission had one essential dependency: the men could not be picked up until an aircraft found their drifting patch of ice again.

The pilots returned when the three days were nearly over, but fog concealed the station. A second attempt failed. The men had to remain at NP-8 while the planes searched for a place that was still drifting.

On June 2 a Navy P2V patrol aircraft set off earlier than the bomber. Its better navigation equipment located NP-8 and guided the B-17 toward it. At last the pickup could begin.

Now the weather presented a different difficulty. Surface winds were around thirty knots. From the cockpit, the gray ice and sky offered little horizon. The bomber had to fly low enough to catch a thin suspended line without landing or hitting the ground.

First came the research. Smith and LeSchack attached the loaded bag to the line and inflated a balloon. They had to hold the bag down against the wind while the plane approached. The pilot used the bright marker on the line to help maintain his course. The nose arms caught it; the crew secured the cable and winched the bag aboard.

The papers were out. The people were not.

LeSchack clipped into the harness for the next pickup. Smith tried to hold him steady as the balloon rose. The wind pulled LeSchack out of his grasp and dragged him stomach-first over the ice. He travelled about 300 feet before an ice block stopped him. He was still on the line when the aircraft came in.

The plane caught the cable. LeSchack lifted off, but he was facing the wrong way for the ride. While suspended behind the moving aircraft, he managed to turn into position. The winch brought him inside.

Only Smith remained on the floe. He held tightly to a tractor while preparing his own balloon. Once the line caught the wind, he began sliding too. He braced his heels in a crack in the ice, enough to stop the movement and hold his position for the final approach.

The bomber came back. This time the line caught the outer section of one metal arm and seemed to remain there. Then it moved down into the locking mechanism. Crew members caught the line beneath the aircraft and passed it to the winch.

Smith was lifted off the ice and hauled aboard. The researchers, their photographs and the 150-pound bag were finally together in the plane. All three recoveries had worked: cargo, LeSchack, then Smith.

The Americans later reported that the station's equipment and layout provided evidence of Soviet research into underwater acoustics, submarine detection and the ability to work quietly for extended periods. The materials also revealed information about Soviet polar science. Those conclusions came from the mission's American assessment; the story does not tell us what later decisions the intelligence changed.

The mission had demonstrated something else very concrete. Two men could parachute into an otherwise inaccessible place, work there, and leave in an aircraft that never landed. The extreme wind had made that demonstration harder than the tests.

Decades later, in 2008, the CIA unveiled a painting of the pickup. Members of the team met again after forty-six years. The photograph and documents had been the mission's intended prize. But to get them home, every part of the flight crew's strange retrieval system first had to work, three times in a row.
