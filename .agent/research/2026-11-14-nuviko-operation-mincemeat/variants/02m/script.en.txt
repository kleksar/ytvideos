How could a dead man carry invasion plans into enemy hands? And how could Britain tell whether the Germans believed those plans?

In 1943, the Allies were preparing to invade Sicily. German commanders could see why: the island sat across the Mediterranean from Allied bases in North Africa. Britain needed them to expect an attack somewhere else.

So intelligence officers Charles Cholmondeley and Ewen Montagu built a courier who had never lived. They gave a dead man a Royal Marines uniform, a photograph of a fictional fiancée named Pam, and ordinary receipts. His papers called him Major William Martin.

But the important papers were in his briefcase. One forged letter suggested that Greece was the real Allied target, while Sicily would be a diversion. Another hinted at Sardinia. The idea was to let Germany discover the letters as if by accident.

On April 30, officers from the submarine HMS Seraph released the body near the Spanish port of Huelva. A fisherman found him. Spain was neutral, but German intelligence had contacts there.

Then came a problem Britain could not control: would anyone open the briefcase? British officials urgently demanded its return, making it look valuable. German agents obtained copies. When the originals came back, the British found signs they had been opened.

That proved access, not belief. Stronger evidence came from intercepted German messages: commanders were treating Greece and the Balkans as serious invasion targets and directing defensive attention there.

On July 10, Allied troops landed in Sicily. They faced real resistance; this one trick did not win the campaign on its own.

The courier's life was fictitious, but the body was real. Most accounts identify him as Glyndwr Michael, although that identification has been disputed. His grave in Spain eventually carried both names.

Britain had written the lie. Germany trusted it because its officers believed they had stolen the truth.
