Why would Britain put forged invasion plans beside a dead man and leave him in the sea? How could German spies be persuaded to read them? And how would Britain know whether the enemy had believed the lie?

In spring 1943, the Allies were preparing to attack Sicily after fighting in North Africa. The island had ports and airfields that would help them move towards Italy. German officers knew the same geography. Even if they could not know the date of the attack, Sicily was an obvious place to watch.

British planners wanted German troops preparing for an invasion somewhere else. A public announcement about Greece would convince nobody. But a private letter that appeared to have escaped British control could reach a different audience.

On April 30, a fisherman found a body off Huelva, Spain. The man's papers described him as Major William Martin of the Royal Marines. A briefcase of official correspondence had come ashore with him. The body was real. The officer named in the papers had never existed.

RAF officer Charles Cholmondeley and naval intelligence officer Ewen Montagu had built Martin's identity around a dead man. Most historical accounts identify the body as Glyndwr Michael, who had died in London, although that identification has been challenged. Neither a uniform nor a name was enough: the planners had to make an accidental discovery look ordinary.

So Martin acquired unfinished business. There was a bank overdraft notice, theatre tickets and a receipt for an engagement ring. Letters and a photograph suggested a fiancée called Pam. She was fictitious too. These objects gave the imaginary officer a private life that a person examining the body could investigate.

The military letters were the trap. One, written in the name of General Archibald Nye to General Harold Alexander, pointed towards Greece and Crete as real Allied targets. It mentioned Sicily, but as a diversion. Another letter, associated with Lord Mountbatten, hinted at Sardinia. The papers did not simply deny the obvious invasion; they gave a German reader a reason to think his correct suspicion about Sicily had been planted by Britain.

Before the fisherman found Martin, the submarine HMS Seraph had carried his body towards Spain in a steel container cooled with dry ice. Officers released him into the water with the briefcase attached. The current, the Spanish authorities and German intelligence would have to complete the route. None of them was under British control.

Spain was officially neutral, but German agents had contacts there. The Spanish authorities kept the briefcase. The British could ask for it back, yet an open invitation to inspect its contents would have ruined the disguise.

Instead, British officials sent urgent requests for the papers' return. Some diplomatic messages used a code the Germans had already broken. German listeners could therefore encounter what sounded like British anxiety about a genuine leak. The requests made the letters more tempting without Britain handing them directly to Berlin.

German intelligence eventually obtained copies. According to accounts of the examination, the letters were worked out of their envelopes with thin rods so that the seals could remain intact. The originals were then returned through Spanish hands.

Back in London, the British examined the paper. Damage to its fibres indicated that the sheets had been rolled and handled. That solved one part of the puzzle: the enemy had gained access. It did not show that the enemy had accepted the contents.

A different kind of evidence arrived through intercepted German communications. On May 14, at a meeting with Admiral Karl Dönitz, Hitler treated Greece and Sardinia as major threatened areas rather than Sicily as the next invasion point. German defensive decisions were being made with those alternatives in mind. British intelligence could compare that reaction with the forged claims.

The operation was part of a wider Allied deception effort, so no honest account can assign every German movement to this briefcase alone. But the British now had something stronger than a returned envelope: evidence that the false destinations were influencing decisions.

On July 10, Allied troops landed on Sicily, the target the German officers had first had reason to suspect. German and Italian forces resisted, and the campaign lasted into August. Operation Mincemeat had not made the fighting disappear.

Afterwards, the invented Major Martin became famous. The person whose body had been used was largely absent from the early public telling. His grave in Huelva later also bore the name Glyndwr Michael; historians still debate that identification.

Britain's forged letters worked because the enemy thought it had acquired them against Britain's wishes. The most revealing evidence was not the paper coming home. It was what German commanders began preparing to defend.
