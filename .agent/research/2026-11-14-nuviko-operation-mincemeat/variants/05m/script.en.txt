Why send a dead man into the sea with secret invasion plans? How could such a delivery reach German intelligence? And what would prove that Germany acted on the message?

In spring 1943, the Allies were preparing their next attack after the campaign in North Africa. Sicily was a useful stepping stone into Axis-held Europe. Its ports and airfields mattered. Unfortunately for the Allies, its position also made it an obvious target.

Britain could not make an entire invasion force disappear. It needed German commanders watching other coasts when the landings began. Greece and Sardinia looked plausible enough to be useful alternatives.

An announcement naming Greece would sound like propaganda. A letter discovered where Britain did not want it found might have a different effect.

On April 30, a fisherman near Huelva, Spain, found a body in the water. The dead man appeared to be a Royal Marines officer carrying military correspondence. His identity papers said William Martin. But the officer's life was an invention.

Two planners, RAF officer Charles Cholmondeley and naval intelligence officer Ewen Montagu, had worked backwards from the discovery they wanted. They needed a body, a believable person and documents that would look accidental rather than planted.

Most historical accounts identify the body as Glyndwr Michael, a Welshman who had died in London; some historians dispute that identification. The fictional officer, however, is well documented. His rank, photograph and personal history were made to survive scrutiny.

Martin had a bank overdraft notice. There were theatre tickets and an engagement-ring receipt. He had letters and a photograph from a woman called Pam, fictional as his fiancée. These details made the deception believable: an officer with errands and debts was easier to accept than a body carrying only a classified envelope.

Inside an attached briefcase were the letters that really mattered. One, in the name of General Archibald Nye to General Harold Alexander, described an attack against Greece and Crete. Sicily appeared as the diversion. Another, associated with Louis Mountbatten, hinted at Sardinia in a casual way.

That was the reversal. Anyone already expecting an attack on Sicily could now interpret their correct suspicion as the false plan described in a supposedly private letter.

But the forged correspondence was useless unless it crossed into German hands. Britain chose officially neutral Spain, where German agents maintained contacts. The body was packed in a steel container cooled with dry ice and carried aboard the submarine HMS Seraph. Before dawn on April 30, officers released it off the Spanish coast with the briefcase attached.

They could not choose where the current would carry the body. They could not force a Spanish official to open the letters, or a German officer to trust them. The fisherman's discovery solved only the first uncertainty.

Spanish authorities retained the briefcase. British officials demanded its return, emphasizing that the letters were sensitive. Those demands were part of the performance: if Britain seemed anxious about losing them, they might seem worth stealing.

German intelligence obtained copies. Examination of the returned originals showed signs that the letters had been removed and put back. That answered the question of access. It did not answer the more important question: did Berlin believe them?

British codebreakers were also reading some German communications. Those intercepts showed German attention turning towards Greece and the Balkans, with defensive priorities directed towards threatened areas. This was evidence of influence, not proof that one briefcase alone redirected the entire war. Mincemeat worked alongside a broader Allied deception campaign.

On July 10, the landings began where the Allies had intended all along: Sicily. German and Italian forces resisted, and the campaign continued into August. The briefcase did not make victory automatic.

After the war, the invented officer became famous. The real man whose body carried the letters remained largely outside the public story for years, and the debate over his identity has not entirely disappeared.

The operation exploited the difference between a secret Britain announced and a secret Germany believed it had stolen. German commanders trusted their discovery enough to plan around it.
