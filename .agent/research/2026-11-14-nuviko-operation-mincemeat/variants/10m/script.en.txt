Why would British officers send a dead man into the sea with invasion papers? How could they make German intelligence trust what it found? And how would they know whether the plan had worked?

After the campaign in North Africa, British and American forces needed a route towards Axis-held Europe. Their real target was Sicily, the large Italian island between North Africa and the Italian mainland. Its ports and airfields could support further operations. But geography made Sicily an obvious place to expect an invasion.

An announcement about an attack on Greece would hardly persuade Germany to abandon its assessment of Sicily. The British wanted to give German intelligence something it would have a reason to trust: private correspondence that was never meant to reach Berlin. The correspondence would have to be discovered under circumstances that made it seem genuine.

On the morning of April thirtieth, a fisherman found a dead man near Huelva, on Spain's Atlantic coast. He appeared to be a British officer, with an identity card, personal belongings, and a briefcase of military correspondence. The officer's name was William Martin. His life, his job, and the secrets he carried had all been invented.

But the operation had nearly ended before anyone was sent to Spain. Charles Cholmondeley of the Royal Air Force had proposed using a dead courier. The British deception committee initially rejected his scheme. Its chairman, John Masterman, nevertheless assigned naval intelligence officer Ewen Montagu to work on it with him. The rejected idea became a real operation.

Years earlier, an intelligence memorandum associated with Ian Fleming had listed the idea of misleading the enemy with a corpse. They needed the Germans to believe they had found an officer who had died during an aircraft accident at sea.

A dead courier solved one problem: he could not be interrogated. His body created another. If a doctor found evidence inconsistent with the account of an air crash at sea, the documents might suddenly look suspicious too. The planners consulted the pathologist Bernard Spilsbury about this medical obstacle.

Most historical accounts identify the body as Glyndwr Michael, a Welshman who had died in London after ingesting rat poison. The body was obtained with help from a coroner. Its use raised questions that wartime accounts did not openly explain. There is still disagreement about the body's true identity. But the false identity British intelligence built is well documented.

They called him Captain William Martin, an acting major in the Royal Marines. That choice helped explain his uniform. A Marine officer could plausibly travel in a looser service battledress. A forged identity card gave Martin a face and a rank. But a card by itself would not make him real.

The fictional officer needed a plausible reason to carry confidential papers. At the same time, nothing in his appearance was supposed to announce that he had been prepared especially for an intelligence operation. The choice of a Royal Marines officer helped with the clothing problem, but the papers had to provide a personal history as well as military credentials.

The planners filled his pockets with ordinary traces of a life: keys, receipts, bills, and theatre ticket stubs. A bank demanded repayment of an overdraft. A jeweller had issued a receipt for an engagement ring. There was also a photograph of a young woman called Pam, and letters from her to the man she expected to marry. Pam was not Martin's real fiancée. Both the relationship and the officer existed only for this deception.

These were belongings with a function. A receipt for a ring supported the story in the love letters, while a notice from the bank put an ordinary financial problem beside the official military identity. Someone inspecting the personal papers could discover several details that seemed to belong to the same life.

The planners attached a briefcase to Martin so the papers would be recovered with him. But what secrets did the letters claim to contain? One letter carried the name of General Archibald Nye. It was addressed to General Harold Alexander in North Africa. It discussed an Allied assault against Greece and Crete as if that were the genuine plan. Sicily appeared in the same letter — but as a feint designed to distract the Germans. That was the clever reversal. A German officer expecting Sicily could interpret that correct guess as exactly what Britain wanted him to think.

A second letter, associated with Lord Louis Mountbatten, introduced Martin to a senior naval commander.

Mountbatten's correspondence also referred to the failed raid at Dieppe. Montagu regarded such a candid admission as a useful touch. A casual reference to sardines pointed towards Sardinia. The two letters offered different pieces of information within the same misleading picture of Allied intentions.

Real senior officers were involved in producing the forged message. The deception relied on believable signatures and believable paperwork. They chose Spain, officially neutral yet home to German intelligence officers and officials willing to share information with Berlin. The port of Huelva offered currents that might wash a body ashore and contacts through which the papers might pass.

There were several ways this could fail. The body might be lost. The briefcase might remain sealed. Or a Spanish doctor could notice something that made the story of an accidental drowning impossible.

The team placed the body in a steel container cooled with dry ice and sent it north to Scotland. A British submarine, HMS Seraph, took the container aboard and sailed towards the Spanish coast. Most of the submarine's crew did not know what the container truly held. Its officers handled the final task.

Before dawn on April thirtieth, Seraph surfaced offshore. The officers placed Martin's body into the sea. A life jacket supported it. The briefcase and its documents went into the water with him. No one aboard could steer a corpse through the current. After the release, all they could do was leave.

When the body was discovered, the operation passed into the hands of people in Spain. The local British vice-consul, Francis Haselden, now had to deal with the authorities and the medical examination of an officer who did not really exist.

During the examination, Haselden urged the Spanish doctor to finish, referring to the heat and the condition of the body. The examination ended without exposing the deception. Martin was buried with military honours in Huelva. The medical problem that had troubled the planners had become a real obstacle in Spain, and the cover had survived it.

The funeral did not mean the mission was complete. The Spanish navy still held the briefcase. London had to ask for the papers back while leaving German intelligence enough reason to obtain them. British diplomatic messages stressed their urgency, and some of that traffic used a code the Germans could read.

For days, the British could not tell whether the letters had been read. Would the briefcase come back with its contents unopened?

German intelligence eventually acquired copies of the letters. When the originals reached London, they were examined for evidence of handling. The paper bore traces that indicated the documents had been removed and replaced. That gave British intelligence an answer to one of its questions: the material had been accessed. It still said nothing certain about the German military response.

The answer came from another kind of intelligence. British codebreakers were reading some German communications. The messages showed German commands treating Greece and the Balkans as important prospective Allied targets.

On May fourteenth, Hitler discussed the anticipated Allied offensive with Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz. Greece and Sardinia figured in German defensive thinking. The British were now seeing a response to the kind of threat described in the planted letters, not merely a sign that a briefcase had been opened. That was the more important evidence.

German forces were moved or allocated towards those threatened areas. The British had evidence that their story had influenced decisions. The deception was not acting alone. Other Allied operations were also feeding Germany false signals about where an invasion might come. That distinction matters: no surviving calculation can isolate exactly how many soldiers or lives this one briefcase changed.

The real test arrived in July. Allied ships and landing craft approached the coast they had intended to attack all along. On July tenth, the invasion of Sicily began under the name Operation Husky. German and Italian defenders still fought. The landings were neither unopposed nor magically won by a single intelligence trick.

The Allied campaign in Sicily continued through the summer; the island was secured in August.

The operation thus had a demonstrable result before the summer campaign was over: Germany obtained the misleading correspondence and took the supposed alternative targets seriously. The precise extent to which this one operation altered the fighting cannot be separated from the rest of the Allied deception effort.

After the war, Ewen Montagu published an account. Some details of intelligence work remained secret for decades. The fictional officer lived on in books and films, while the man behind that identity was known to the public much later.

The grave in Huelva remained a physical record of the operation. Its inscription eventually included the name Glyndwr Michael, although the identification has been disputed. William Martin was a constructed identity that served a military purpose. The person buried under that name had lived a real life, and it is his grave that survives after the invented officer's mission ended.
