Two American officers were killed while a crew tried to trim a tree on the Korean border.

It was August 1976. The branches blocked the view between two United Nations Command guard posts.

American and South Korean personnel went in to clear them. North Korean guards attacked the work party.

Captain Arthur Bonifas and Lieutenant Mark Barrett died. The branches were still in the way.

The response was planned in Washington. Officials even drew up a contingency for striking nearby North Korean barracks.

Instead, they chose a return mission meant to finish the job without another clash.

Three days after the killings, U.S. Army engineers drove to the tree with chainsaws. This time they weren't alone.

South Korean special forces guarded the work party. Artillery and attack helicopters stood ready.

B-52 bombers flew nearby with fighter jets. An American aircraft carrier was stationed off the coast.

All of that force surrounded a job that had started with a few branches.

The engineers weren't going to trim them anymore. They cut down the whole poplar.

It took less than an hour. The operation ended without another attack.

A memorial near the site now bears the names of the two officers killed there.

So why were bombers guarding a crew with chainsaws?
