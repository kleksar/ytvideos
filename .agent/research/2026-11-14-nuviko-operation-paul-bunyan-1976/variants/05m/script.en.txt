Why did American bombers fly near the Korean border while soldiers cut down a tree? And why did Washington treat a few branches as a military crisis?

In August 1976, there was still no peace treaty ending the Korean War. A ceasefire separated North and South Korea. In one small area called Panmunjom, guards from opposing sides worked within sight of each other.

Near the Bridge of No Return stood a tall poplar. In summer, its branches blocked the view between a United Nations Command observation post and a checkpoint. If guards could not see that checkpoint, they could not properly watch what happened around it.

So on August 18, a work party arrived to trim the branches. Captain Arthur Bonifas and Lieutenant Mark Barrett were among the Americans accompanying the Korean workers. They had come to do a routine maintenance job.

North Korean guards confronted the group. Then more soldiers arrived, and the encounter turned into an attack. The two American officers were killed. Other members of the work party were injured.

The tree was still standing. And the people responsible for the security posts still needed a clear view.

This was no longer just a maintenance problem. Washington had to decide how to respond to two deaths without turning another trip to that same tree into a larger battle.

The United States raised its military alert in South Korea. At a White House meeting the next morning, officials considered several options. One was a possible strike on North Korean barracks near the incident. That strike was discussed as a contingency, not carried out.

But sending a small unprotected crew back could lead to more deaths. What could they do that would finish the job and make another attack less likely?

The decision was to cut down the whole tree. The plan called for a large protective force and a conspicuous display of military power. Washington wanted the first B-52 bomber flight timed to the tree-cutting operation. A naval task force would also move into the region.

The bombers were part of a show of force, not an aerial attack on the tree or the nearby barracks. That difference mattered. The aim of the cutting mission was to complete the work without a new confrontation.

On August 21, three days after the killings, American Army engineers returned with chainsaws. South Korean special forces guarded the area, with artillery and helicopters ready to support them. B-52 bombers and fighter aircraft flew nearby. The USS Midway aircraft carrier group waited offshore.

Imagine the job from the engineers' side: a trunk to cut, people to protect, and opposing soldiers close enough that any renewed violence could change the entire mission.

This time, the crew did not try to reach the branches. They brought down the poplar itself. The cutting took less than an hour.

North Korean forces did not attack the work party again. No strike on the barracks took place. The engineers left after finishing the job.

The operation was called Paul Bunyan, after the legendary American lumberjack. But its lasting meaning came from something much less fanciful: two men had been killed while doing an ordinary task, and the next crew returned under overwhelming protection.

A memorial near the site remembers Arthur Bonifas and Mark Barrett. The tree was removed. The danger of an armed confrontation did not become another fight that morning.
