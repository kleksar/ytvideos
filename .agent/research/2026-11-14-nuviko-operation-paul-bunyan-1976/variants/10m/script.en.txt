Why did the United States send bombers and an aircraft carrier to support men cutting down a tree in Korea? Why did the first crew never finish the job? And how did the death of two men end up changing the rules at a border that had stood for more than twenty years?

The answers begin at a Korean border checkpoint in August 1976. The Korean War had stopped with an armistice in 1953, not a peace treaty. For the soldiers on duty, that distinction was part of everyday life.

At Panmunjom, the two sides had agreed to share a small area for meetings about the truce. The arrangement was unusual. Guards could move around the Joint Security Area instead of remaining on their own side of the military boundary. They worked close to the people they were supposed to watch.

But between them stood a poplar tree roughly thirty metres tall. Its summer leaves and branches obstructed the line of sight. A guard at one post could no longer clearly see what was happening at the other.

That was an everyday problem for the guards. In a place where both sides were still technically at war, a blocked view could leave a post without the observation it was supposed to receive.

On August 18, a group of Korean workers went to prune the tree, accompanied by American and South Korean security personnel. Captain Arthur Bonifas and First Lieutenant Mark Barrett were among the American officers. Their job was to help the workers restore visibility between the posts.

North Korean guards confronted the party and more soldiers arrived. The encounter became a violent attack. Bonifas and Barrett were killed. Other Americans and South Koreans were injured.

The workers had come to remove branches. They left without finishing the job. The blocked view remained, and two officers would never return.

Officials in Washington now faced two separate problems. The checkpoint still needed to be watched. More urgently, two American officers had been killed inside an area where the opposing sides were already dangerously close.

The United States raised the alert level of its forces in South Korea. Aircraft were moved or prepared, and officials debated what kind of response would prevent another attack without causing a much wider war.

At the White House, the decision could not be reduced to sending another maintenance crew. A response that killed more people might start a chain of retaliation. Yet leaving the job unfinished would mean abandoning an ordinary security duty after two officers had died carrying it out.

On August 19, senior officials met in the White House Situation Room. The record of the meeting shows that the question of what to do about the tree was being considered alongside a much larger question: what kind of military response would follow the deaths?

Officials considered a possible strike on North Korean military facilities. That was a contingency, a plan to have available if needed. It was not the operation they eventually chose.

The immediate problem was the work party itself. A second attempt could be stopped just as the first had been. Officials discussed different ways to complete the work, but they also had to consider how the North Korean side might respond to the American response. The more visible the military preparations became, the greater the risk of a misunderstanding.

Eventually the decision came down to a specific physical task. There would be no need for the crew to return every summer to prune those same branches if the entire tree were felled. A large military presence was intended to protect the workers and discourage another attack.

The military preparations extended well beyond the Joint Security Area. American air and naval forces were involved. Their presence was part of the wider response to the killings; they were not being sent to destroy the tree from the air.

Three days after the killings, on August 21, the operation began. It was given the name Paul Bunyan, after the giant lumberjack of American folklore.

U.S. Army engineers came back with chainsaws. South Korean special forces protected the work party.

American aircraft flew over South Korea, and the USS Midway carrier group was offshore. The air and sea movements were part of the wider military response; not every aircraft was physically over the tree at every moment.

All those preparations led back to a crew of engineers doing a job that would normally attract little attention. They had to bring the trunk down safely. Other soldiers were there to protect them. Once the tree was down, there would be no practical reason for the team to stay.

And across the area were North Korean soldiers. Their presence meant that the engineers and guards had to finish while watching for another confrontation.

The saws went to work. The poplar came down. Army accounts give slightly different cutting times, forty-two and forty-five minutes, but both place the work at under an hour.

No new assault stopped the engineers. The military strike discussed in Washington was not carried out. The crew finished the work and withdrew. For the people responsible for the truce, though, the tree had exposed a problem that could not be solved with a saw.

Before August 18, guards from the two sides had been permitted to move within the same small area. The tree was only one obstruction in that arrangement. What could stop another maintenance job, patrol or disagreement from producing the same kind of confrontation?

The dispute went back to the Military Armistice Commission, the body responsible for dealing with violations of the truce. At its first meeting on the incident, the North Korean representatives conveyed a message of regret from Kim Il Sung and proposed talks on the security arrangements. It was not an explicit acceptance of responsibility for the killings.

During the following weeks, representatives of the two sides negotiated a change to the agreement. The issue was no longer which branches to cut. It was whether guards from opposing sides should be allowed to operate across the same area at all.

On September 5, a memorandum reached President Gerald Ford. It reported that the negotiations had produced an agreement expected to be signed the following day. One of its provisions returned to the very practical problem that had brought the first crew to the poplar: the parties were to stop creating obstructions to visibility or passage on the other's territory.

On September 6 the agreement was concluded. Security personnel were to remain on their own side of the military demarcation line inside the Joint Security Area. People assigned to the Armistice Commission and the neutral supervisory commission retained access. The rules for guards had changed; the institutions meant to maintain the truce could still function.

The tree had been brought down in less than an hour. Changing the rules of the area had required negotiations. Neither action ended the division of Korea, and neither made the deaths of August 18 disappear.

But completing the job did not undo August 18. Captain Bonifas and Lieutenant Barrett were still dead. A memorial near the site bears their names.

Today the unusual photograph of a crew with saws can make the whole affair look like a story about military power. But the memorial brings it back to the two men who went out with a work party and did not come home.
