Why would the United States send B-52 bombers, fighter aircraft and an aircraft carrier to support men with chainsaws? How could cutting down a single tree become a question for the White House? And could the crew finish the job without another man being killed?

The answers begin at a Korean border checkpoint in August 1976. The Korean War had stopped with an armistice in 1953, not a peace treaty. For the soldiers on duty, that distinction was part of everyday life.

Near the village of Panmunjom lay the Joint Security Area. North Korean guards and personnel under the United Nations Command operated in close proximity. A checkpoint stood near the Bridge of No Return. An observation post was supposed to keep watch over it.

But between them stood a poplar tree roughly thirty metres tall. Its summer leaves and branches obstructed the line of sight. A guard at one post could no longer clearly see what was happening at the other.

That explains why anyone wanted to cut the tree. It does not yet explain the bombers.

On August 18, a group of Korean workers went to prune the tree, accompanied by American and South Korean security personnel. Captain Arthur Bonifas and First Lieutenant Mark Barrett were among the American officers. They were not there to invade or carry out a strike. Their immediate task was to help restore visibility between the posts.

North Korean guards confronted the party and more soldiers arrived. The encounter became a violent attack. Bonifas and Barrett were killed. Other Americans and South Koreans were injured.

The workers had come to remove branches. They left without finishing the job. The blocked view remained, and two officers would never return.

Officials in Washington now faced two separate problems. The checkpoint still needed to be watched. More urgently, two American officers had been killed inside an area where the opposing sides were already dangerously close.

The United States raised the alert level of its forces in South Korea. Aircraft were moved or prepared, and officials debated what kind of response would prevent another attack without causing a much wider war.

The choices were not simple. Doing nothing would leave the killings unanswered. Sending another small party to the same tree could expose more personnel to an assault. Attacking North Korean military facilities could bring an armed response.

On the morning of August 19, senior officials met in the White House Situation Room. The meeting record survives. It is remarkable for how often the discussion returns to the same practical question: how do you get the tree down and keep the men doing it alive?

One proposal involved hitting North Korean barracks near the Joint Security Area. The meeting directed officials to develop that as a contingency. A contingency is a possible response if circumstances demand it. It was not an order that a strike had already happened.

Another suggestion was to use a very small team, or even explosives, to avoid exposing a large work party. But officials also worried about what would happen if North Korean soldiers approached again. A few workers with no strong protection might be forced to retreat—or become new casualties.

The planners were trying to achieve two things at once. Complete a task that had already cost two lives, and make clear that another violent attack would carry a serious risk of escalation. The danger was that their own demonstration could also make the situation worse.

The final decision was to return in force and cut down the tree entirely. No more attempts to reach selected branches. A stump would leave no leaves between the observation post and the checkpoint.

The plan involved more than the men holding chainsaws. Washington wanted a B-52 flight timed to coincide with the cutting. It also ordered movement of a naval task force and preparations involving additional combat aircraft. The aircraft were to be visible signs of American readiness.

This was not a decision to bomb the tree. And the proposed attack on the nearby barracks was never carried out as part of the cutting operation. The distinction matters, because a show of force and an actual attack can look similar from a distance but lead to very different consequences.

Three days after the killings, on August 21, the operation began. It was given the name Paul Bunyan, after the giant lumberjack of American folklore.

U.S. Army engineers came back with chainsaws. South Korean special forces protected the work party. Artillery and helicopters were positioned to support the operation if trouble started.

Over South Korea, B-52 bombers conducted conspicuous flights, joined by fighter aircraft. Offshore, the USS Midway carrier group stood ready. The air and sea movements were part of the wider military response; not every aircraft was physically over the tree at every moment.

For the people at the base of the poplar, the job remained ordinary in one sense. Someone still had to use a saw, cut through wood, and bring the trunk down safely. Yet now that small work area was the focus of attention from commanders far beyond Panmunjom.

And across the area were North Korean soldiers. Their presence meant that the engineers and guards had to finish while watching for another confrontation.

At the first attempt, a small crew had come to trim the branches and been attacked. At the second, a heavily protected force arrived with orders to remove the entire obstruction. That change was the practical answer to what had happened three days earlier.

The saws went to work. The poplar came down. Army accounts give slightly different cutting times, forty-two and forty-five minutes, but both place the work at under an hour.

No new assault stopped the engineers. The planned contingency against the barracks was not used. The soldiers completed the job and withdrew.

The bombers did not drop bombs on Panmunjom. The carrier did not launch an attack on the tree. Their role was to make a second assault less attractive while a smaller group carried out a very specific task.

That morning answered the first question: the extraordinary forces were there because the previous attempt had ended in murder, and Washington wanted the next crew to finish the work without another fight.

But completing the job did not undo August 18. Captain Bonifas and Lieutenant Barrett were still dead. A memorial near the site bears their names.

Operation Paul Bunyan is often remembered as the day military aircraft backed up a chainsaw. That description is accurate but incomplete. The point was not that a tree was worth a military confrontation. It was that after two people were killed during its pruning, bringing down the tree had become a dangerous test of whether another confrontation could be avoided.

The crews got the tree down. The second attack did not happen. And the two officers' names remain the part of the story that no display of force could change.
