Up a hill outside Constantinople, Ottoman warships moved on wooden rollers.

Why drag a fleet across dry land when a harbour lies right beside it?

It was April 1453. Sultan Mehmed II was besieging the city.

A heavy chain blocked the entrance to the Golden Horn, a sheltered harbour behind the city's sea walls.

On April twentieth, four supply ships fought through the Ottoman blockade and reached the defenders.

But Mehmed already had another route under preparation.

Workers cleared ground behind Galata, the Genoese settlement across the harbour.

They laid wooden tracks and rollers. Teams of people and animals pulled smaller vessels onto them.

The ships went uphill from the Bosporus, then down towards the Golden Horn.

This was planned work, not a road thrown together after the supply ships arrived.

On April twenty-second, dozens of Ottoman vessels entered the Golden Horn without breaking the chain.

The chain still guarded the entrance. But the Ottoman fleet was now on the other side.

The defenders had to watch another stretch of sea wall, taking attention and men from elsewhere.

On April twenty-eighth, they tried to burn the new fleet in a night attack.

The attack failed. The Ottoman vessels remained inside the harbour.

The city held out for several more weeks. Constantinople fell in the final assault on May twenty-ninth.
