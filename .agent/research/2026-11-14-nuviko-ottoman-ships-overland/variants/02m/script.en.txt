How did an Ottoman fleet get inside a sealed harbour when a massive iron chain blocked its only entrance?

In April 1453, Sultan Mehmed II was laying siege to Constantinople. The chain guarded the mouth of the Golden Horn, the harbour beside the city's sea walls. His ships could not simply sail through it.

On April twentieth, four supply vessels fought their way past the Ottoman blockade and into that harbour. For the defenders, it was a rare success.

Mehmed already had another route under preparation. Instead of forcing the chain, his men were going around it.

The route led behind Galata, a Genoese settlement across the water. Workers prepared a wooden track. Men and draft animals pulled smaller warships from the Bosporus shore, up a slope and down towards the Golden Horn.

The road was not invented overnight after the supply ships arrived. The work had started earlier.

On April twenty-second, dozens of Ottoman vessels appeared inside the Golden Horn. The chain was still in place.

Now the defenders had a second problem. Ships threatened a stretch of waterfront they had counted on protecting. Men and attention had to be divided between more positions.

On April twenty-eighth, the defenders tried to burn the new fleet in a night raid. The raid failed, and the Ottoman ships stayed.

Constantinople held out for weeks more. It fell during the assault of May twenty-ninth. The ships did not win the siege by themselves; the land walls remained the decisive battlefield.
