How could an Ottoman fleet enter Constantinople's protected harbour without breaking the chain across its entrance? And why would ships behind that chain matter when the main siege was happening on land?

In April 1453, the city had two very different kinds of defence. Its famous land walls faced Mehmed II's army and artillery. Along another side ran the Golden Horn, a sheltered inlet where the defenders kept ships and could receive supplies. A heavy chain across the harbour mouth kept hostile vessels out. It did not close the whole Bosporus; it shut one critical entrance.

This mattered because the defenders had fewer men than the Ottoman army. The walls were strong, but people still had to stand on them, repair damage and watch for an attack. The chain made one part of that job easier.

On April twentieth, the harbour even received help. Four Christian supply ships fought past the Ottoman fleet. They reached the Golden Horn when the entrance was opened for them. The chain had done its job against the attackers, and the naval blockade had failed to stop the relief ships.

For Mehmed, forcing the harbour entrance was proving costly. But he had been preparing an alternative before that day's fight. It would make the chain irrelevant without cutting a single link.

The alternative began on the opposite shore, behind Galata. That settlement belonged to the Genoese and occupied an awkward position between the two sides. Mehmed could not treat its walls as an ordinary stretch of open shoreline without creating another problem. The route therefore passed around the settlement, across the ground he controlled.

Workers cleared a path between the Bosporus and the upper Golden Horn. They laid a wooden running surface and supports for the hulls. The ships selected for the move were smaller vessels, not the largest ships in the fleet. Men and draft animals hauled them out of the water.

Then came the part that makes this story so strange. A ship built to travel on water had to climb a hill. The hull needed support. The pulling teams needed a continuous route. At the other end, the vessels had to be lowered into the harbour where they could float again. This was engineering and labour, prepared in advance, rather than an order someone could carry out with a few ropes in a single afternoon.

On April twenty-second, dozens of Ottoman ships entered the Golden Horn from its upper side. The precise count and the exact path remain disputed. The fact that matters is simpler: they were inside the protected water, while the chain at its mouth remained intact.

The defenders now faced an unpleasant choice. They still had to hold the battered land walls. But enemy ships were in the inlet beside another stretch of sea wall. Leave that waterfront without defenders, and it could become vulnerable. Move people there, and the land defences had fewer hands. The manoeuvre did not open a gate; it made the defenders' available manpower cover more places.

They tried to undo it. On April twenty-eighth, a naval raid went into the Golden Horn to set the Ottoman vessels on fire. It failed. The enemy ships remained, and the defenders could not restore the harbour to the safety they had enjoyed behind the chain.

Still, this is not a story in which a fleet rolls downhill and the city falls that evening. Constantinople continued to resist. Its defenders repaired walls and fought on. The final Ottoman assault came on May twenty-ninth, more than a month after the ships entered the harbour. The city fell at its land defences, after a wider siege in which artillery, exhaustion and repeated attacks all mattered.

So the chain was not destroyed, and it was not useless from the beginning. It blocked the sea entrance exactly as intended. Mehmed changed the problem: instead of sailing through the barrier, he moved ships around the land beside it.

The consequence was not a magical breach. It was another front for a city already running short of men. The fleet had crossed a hill, but the pressure it created was felt on the walls.
