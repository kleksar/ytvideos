What good is a chain across a harbour if an enemy can put ships on the water behind it? How did Mehmed II manage that in 1453? And did the famous march of ships over a hill actually bring Constantinople down?

To understand the manoeuvre, start with the geography. Constantinople was defended on land by enormous walls. On one side, the Golden Horn formed a long, sheltered inlet. Its entrance could be closed by a heavy chain, supported by the defenders' vessels. This was a barrier across the harbour mouth, not a chain across the entire Bosporus. Ships approved by the defenders could be let through. An attacking fleet could not simply break into the inlet.

That division of the city's defences mattered. Emperor Constantine XI and his commanders had far fewer fighting men than the army surrounding them. They needed people on the land walls to defend weak points and repair artillery damage. A secured harbour reduced the number of places they had to watch at once.

The Ottoman siege began in April. Mehmed's guns struck the land fortifications, but damaged walls could be repaired between attacks. At sea, his fleet also had a problem. It could challenge the approaches to Constantinople, yet the chain and the ships protecting it kept the Golden Horn beyond easy reach. The harbour was still a place where the defenders could shelter vessels and receive help.

Then came April twentieth. Four Christian supply ships arrived near the city. Ottoman ships tried to stop them. The relief vessels managed to get past the blockade, and the chain was opened so they could enter the Golden Horn.

Think about what that meant to both sides. Constantinople's defenders had watched ships carrying supplies fight their way in. For Mehmed, the blockade had failed in full view of his army. Accounts describe a crisis in Ottoman naval leadership after the engagement. The harbour entrance was still guarded, and the defenders had been able to use it.

Now the central question changed. How could Mehmed reach the protected water without winning another fight at the chain itself?

There was a second shoreline: the Bosporus coast to the northeast of the Golden Horn. Between that water and the upper inlet stood land, including a ridge behind Galata. Galata was a Genoese settlement, not simply an Ottoman shipyard. Its position made a direct assault politically and militarily complicated. Yet Mehmed held ground around it.

He had already been preparing a route across that ground. Some retellings make the overland move sound like an instant idea born after the failure of April twentieth. The historical accounts do not require that invention. Work on the road had begun before the relief ships fought their way through.

This was the real solution: take selected, smaller vessels out of the Bosporus, support their hulls on a prepared track, drag them uphill around Galata, and put them back into water inside the Golden Horn.

A description like that can make the work sound effortless. Consider what the teams actually had to accomplish. A hull is designed to be supported by water. On land, its weight has to rest on timber supports without wrecking the vessel. The route has to be cleared and made workable across uneven ground. People and draft animals have to keep the vessel moving, first against the slope, then down towards another shore. Finally the boat has to be launched safely enough to function as a warship again.

Historical narratives describe a wooden track, cradles or rollers, and large teams hauling the vessels. They disagree on the exact route and on the count. That is why a precise number of ships, a single unchallenged street map, or a stopwatch account of every launch would suggest certainty the evidence does not provide. The central event is not in doubt: Ottoman vessels crossed the land and entered the inlet.

On April twenty-second, dozens of them were in the Golden Horn. The chain remained across its entrance. From the defenders' side, the geography of their problem had changed. They had built the sea defence to stop ships sailing through the mouth. Mehmed had put his ships in by coming from a direction that barrier did not cover.

But reaching the harbour was not the same as capturing the city. The newcomers now threatened the sea walls along the Golden Horn, and the defenders still had to resist attacks on the main land walls. The same limited force was being stretched across more positions. This was the manoeuvre's military value: not the spectacle of ships crossing a hill, but the extra pressure those ships created once afloat.

There was a response available, at least in theory. If the Ottoman vessels could be burned before they secured their position, the harbour might become safer again. The defenders debated and prepared a night raid. Each day of delay gave the enemy more time to protect its ships, but a rushed attack was dangerous too.

On April twenty-eighth, the raid went ahead. It failed. Contemporary and later accounts describe Ottoman guns striking the attackers and serious losses, though the details of warning and betrayal are not equally certain. A Venetian commander named Giacomo Coco appears in accounts of the failed operation. The Ottoman ships were not removed.

It is tempting here to end the story with a simple line: the fleet reached the harbour, the defenders failed to burn it, and the city was doomed. But the actual siege continued for a month. The defenders retained capable ships in the inlet, and the new Ottoman fleet did not suddenly destroy them. The Golden Horn had become contested, not automatically conquered.

The Ottomans also worked to link their positions around the inlet. Accounts describe a bridge of floating supports across part of the upper Golden Horn. It was one more way of connecting movement and pressure around the city. The portage, naval fighting, the bridge and the land bombardment were parts of one siege; they were not interchangeable explanations for the outcome.

Meanwhile, the most important fighting still took place around Constantinople's land walls. Ottoman artillery continued its attacks. The defenders repaired damage and countered attempts to undermine their positions. Those actions cost time and manpower on both sides. The city was fighting on through repeated setbacks, not simply awaiting the result of a single trick.

By late May the siege had entered its final stage. On May twenty-ninth, Ottoman forces launched the assault that broke Constantinople's land defences. The emperor died during the fall of the city. That is the end of the siege, but it is not proof that the ships hauled overland were the one action that decided everything.

So what can we actually say about those ships? We can say the chain protected the harbour entrance and remained unbroken during the overland manoeuvre. We can say Ottoman crews and workers moved a significant number of vessels over prepared ground behind Galata into the Golden Horn. We can say that forced an already outnumbered defence to watch additional waterfront and that the attempted counterattack failed.

We should be more cautious about the exact number of ships, the exact path over the ridge, and claims that every step occurred entirely unseen. We should also resist the neat story in which four relief ships arrive and Mehmed invents an impossible road that same night. The preparations were already underway.

The feat changed what the harbour chain could guarantee. It could still stop an enemy fleet approaching from the sea. It could not stop ships that had been hauled around it and launched from the other side.

For Constantinople's defenders, that was the real shock: a familiar barrier remained in place, but one of the advantages they had relied on was gone. The great chain had not failed as a piece of metal. The enemy had changed the route.
