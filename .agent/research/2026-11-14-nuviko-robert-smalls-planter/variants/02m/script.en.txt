An armed Confederate steamer sailed past Fort Sumter carrying sixteen people escaping slavery.

The sentries recognized its flag. They didn't know the man posing as captain was one of the enslaved crew.

In 1862, Robert Smalls worked aboard the Planter, carrying guns and supplies around Charleston Harbor.

Its white officers went ashore for the night. Smalls and his fellow crewmen had prepared for this opening.

Before dawn, they fired the boilers and pulled away from the dock. But the sea wasn't their first stop.

They collected their wives and children at another wharf. The families waited below deck, hidden from the forts.

Smalls put on the captain's straw hat and coat. A wrong signal could bring fire from the shore.

The crew knew the harbor. They used familiar steam-whistle signals as they passed Confederate positions.

At Fort Sumter, Smalls gave the correct signal. The sentry let the ship continue.

The forts were behind them. Ahead, an American warship saw an armed Confederate vessel approaching.

Its captain ordered the guns readied. The escapees hoisted a white bedsheet as a surrender flag.

The guns stayed silent. The Planter came alongside the USS Onward, and Smalls handed over the ship.

All sixteen people were free. Later, Smalls was appointed captain of that same steamer. The hat was no longer a disguise.

But that morning, what exactly had the sentries let sail through?
