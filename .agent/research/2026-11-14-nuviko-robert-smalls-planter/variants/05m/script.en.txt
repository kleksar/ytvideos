How did an enslaved crew take a Confederate military ship out of Charleston without firing a shot? And why did they face another set of guns after the last Confederate fort was behind them?

In May 1862, American Civil War ships were blockading Charleston. For enslaved people working inside the harbor, the Union vessels were close enough to see a possibility of escape, but reaching them meant passing Confederate batteries.

Robert Smalls worked on the Planter, a side-wheel steamer carrying troops, weapons, and supplies between those defenses. He knew the channels and the signals the forts expected to hear. His colleagues knew how to keep the engines working. That knowledge was normally used to serve the Confederate war effort.

Then the Planter's white officers went ashore for the night.

Smalls and other enslaved men had been preparing to use just such an opening. On May 13, before dawn, they got the boilers ready and moved the steamer away from the Southern Wharf. But they did not head straight for the blockade. Their families were still in Charleston.

At another wharf, women and children were waiting aboard the Etiwan. The escapees brought them onto the Planter. Smalls's wife Hannah and their children were among them. The passengers stayed below deck, out of view of sentries along the shore.

The whole operation now depended on a strange deception. The ship was still flying Confederate colors and carrying Confederate guns. From a distance, it needed to look like a transport making an ordinary trip.

Smalls put on the absent captain's coat and straw hat. He was playing the part of Captain Charles Relyea, but he wasn't the only person handling the vessel: the experienced pilot Abram Allston was at the wheel, while others tended the engines and deck.

First the Planter passed other harbor defenses, including positions near Fort Johnson. The crew had to keep moving without doing anything that would make a routine military vessel suddenly look suspicious. Each fort they passed reduced the distance to freedom, but the most dangerous passage was still ahead.

Fort Sumter controlled the main shipping channel. Fort Moultrie lay across the water. A suspicious ship could be caught between their guns. Smalls knew the whistle signal expected at Sumter, because the same crew had regularly worked this route.

At about 4:15 in the morning, he gave the signal. The fort let the Planter pass.

For the first time that morning, the Confederate defenses were falling behind the families. Yet the crew was not safe. The American blockade had no reason to know that the armed steamer approaching from Charleston was bringing people seeking freedom.

On the USS Onward, the acting captain saw what appeared to be an enemy ship. He called his crew to stations and had the guns made ready.

The escapees hurried to raise a white bedsheet as a surrender flag. Their last obstacle was not getting the Confederate forts to mistake them for friends. It was making the Union sailors understand that they were not attackers.

The guns remained silent. The Planter came alongside the Onward, and Smalls handed over the vessel. Sixteen people had crossed the harbor together and reached freedom. The United States had also gained the ship, its guns, and information about Confederate defenses.

That was not the end of Smalls's connection to the Planter. He went on to serve the Union as a pilot and was eventually given command of the same steamer. The captain's hat that had helped him pass a fort was no longer only a disguise.

One crew, one ship, and two very different sets of guns. Getting past the first meant acting as though nothing had changed. Getting past the second meant showing that everything had.
