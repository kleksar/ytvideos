How did sixteen people escape slavery aboard an armed Confederate steamer? Why did the harbor guards let it go? And what happened to the daughter that one of the passengers had to leave behind?

In 1862, the Union fleet was waiting outside Charleston Harbor. Its sailors were there to stop Confederate ships. For the people working aboard one of those ships, getting to that fleet offered a way out of slavery.

The Civil War had begun the previous year. Confederate forces held Charleston, while the United States controlled parts of the South Carolina coast, including the area around Beaufort. People escaping slavery had already made their way to Union-held territory there. Inside Charleston Harbor, however, the Confederate defenses still stood between the city and the blockade.

One of the vessels working inside the harbor was the Planter. Built in Charleston two years earlier, it was a side-wheel steamer used to transport Confederate men, dispatches, guns, and equipment. It sailed between positions that protected the harbor. Its work demanded experience with the channel, the tide, its boilers, and the signals needed to pass military checkpoints.

Robert Smalls had been sent away from Beaufort to work in Charleston at the age of twelve. He found work on the waterfront and married Hannah Jones in 1856, though the state gave their marriage no legal recognition. He took on extra work to try to buy freedom for Hannah and their daughter Elizabeth. In 1861, they had a son as well. Robert knew how to move a ship through Charleston, but he could not freely take his family out of the city.

The steamer's officers had a habit of sleeping ashore, even though standing orders forbade it. They left men aboard who knew how to run the vessel without them. Those men began meeting in secret. Among the people involved were Charles Chisolm, who worked on another vessel, and William Morrison, a tinsmith. The plan reached beyond the crew of the Planter because the escape was to include their families.

On May 12, the crew decided to act. That evening the white officers went ashore again, leaving the men with the ship. There was now a working transport available, with its machinery and a military cargo. The crew had a chance to take their families out on the very vessel they had been forced to operate.

For Hannah, getting on the boat meant leaving someone behind. Her eldest daughter Charlotte had a baby of her own and chose to stay in Charleston. Hannah joined the escape with three of her children. The family had no guarantee of being together again.

That night Chisolm and two other men brought the families to a waiting vessel called the Etiwan. Elsewhere, the crew of the Planter got their ship ready. Before they could escape together, they needed to bring the two groups aboard the same steamer.

The Planter left its berth and picked up the waiting passengers. Hannah was aboard with Clara, Elizabeth and little Robert. Another passenger was Lavinia Wilson, whose name would appear on a list of escapees published in New York. Sixteen people were now on the Planter. The women and children remained below deck while the crew worked above.

From shore, the Planter still needed to appear to be doing its Confederate job. Its flags and guns were familiar. Its crew could not explain the passengers below deck. And its captain was no longer aboard.

Smalls put on Captain Charles Relyea's coat and straw hat. The point was not to convince a sentry standing beside him; it was to resemble the captain at a distance while the ship passed. The rest of the operation depended on the crew. According to the Park Service reconstruction, the experienced pilot Abram Allston was at the wheel, while other men handled the deck and engines. It would be misleading to describe the escape as Smalls steering alone through every position.

The steamer moved past earlier defensive works. It passed near Fort Johnson and a patrol boat without raising an alarm. The next challenge was larger. Fort Sumter occupied the main shipping channel, with Fort Moultrie across the water. A ship suspected of escaping could be exposed to guns from both sides.

The crew knew what the garrison expected to hear. Smalls had worked the route often enough to know the required steam-whistle signal. And as the ship approached Fort Sumter at roughly 4:15 a.m., he gave it.

The sentries let the Planter continue.

There is a revealing detail in the historical record. A report from Fort Sumter describes a sentry identifying a vessel leaving the harbor as a guard boat. The identification was wrong, but the fort did not stop the ship. It had recognized a routine-looking movement where an escape was taking place.

The Planter was beyond the Confederate guns. For the families hiding below, the first danger had passed. But a new misunderstanding was waiting beyond the channel.

The American sailors stationed outside Charleston had no advance notice that the armed Confederate steamer was carrying escapees. From the USS Onward, it looked like an enemy vessel approaching the blockade. Its acting captain, John Frederick Nickels, called his men to their stations and ordered the ship's guns prepared.

The people on the Planter had just survived by keeping Confederate colors visible and appearing to belong to the other side. Now those same signs could get them fired upon.

They rushed to raise a white bedsheet as a surrender flag. A piece of ordinary household fabric carried the message that the Confederate-looking steamer would not attack. There was no exchange of cannon fire. The Planter came alongside the Onward, and Smalls turned it over to the American Navy.

Sixteen people had reached the US forces. They were free from the immediate control of their enslavers, although the legal status of people arriving at Union lines in 1862 was more complicated than a simple declaration of universal emancipation. The Emancipation Proclamation had not yet taken effect. The people aboard had nonetheless crossed a boundary they could not cross freely the previous day.

Their escape had also changed the military picture. The United States received a working transport, its weapons, and information about Confederate operations in the harbor. The achievement was the crew's, not a solitary trick by one man.

Robert Smalls went on to work as a pilot for the Union. In April 1863, he was aboard the ironclad Keokuk when it was damaged by Confederate fire and subsequently sank. Smalls was injured. He returned to service and eventually received command of the Planter. The man who had once worn its captain's hat as a disguise could now command the ship openly.

There was another change taking place ashore. With money awarded for capturing the Planter, Robert bought a house in Beaufort at a wartime tax auction. It had belonged to Henry McKee, the man who had enslaved him as a child. Robert had once lived in a small cabin behind that house with his mother. He and Hannah now had a home there.

The passengers had not all simply disappeared from the record after the escape. In April 1864, Robert and Hannah hosted a wedding in their new home. The bride was Lavinia Wilson, the young woman who had escaped on the Planter with them. A local newspaper described the wedding and reported that Robert had adopted her. Her husband was a soldier in a Black Union regiment.

The rooms had been decorated for the occasion, and the military governor attended with members of his staff. Two years earlier Lavinia had been listed among the people who escaped Charleston. Now the newspaper was recording her marriage in a house once owned by the man who had enslaved her host.

For Hannah, one part of the escape remained unfinished. Her daughter Charlotte and her family had stayed in Charleston. When the war ended, they moved to Beaufort. The parts of the family that had separated before the Planter sailed could finally live in the same town.

Robert later served in the South Carolina legislature and in Congress. But the story of that morning belongs to Hannah, Lavinia, the men who worked the boilers and all the other people who helped get the families out. For some of them the surviving records tell us little about the years that followed.

The Planter had once carried weapons for the army that held these families in slavery. Robert eventually became its captain, and Hannah's daughter Charlotte was able to come to Beaufort, where her mother had made a new home.
