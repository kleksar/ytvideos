How did sixteen enslaved people escape Charleston aboard a Confederate armed steamer? Why did the men who guarded the harbor let it pass? And what made the American sailors waiting outside the harbor nearly fire on the same ship?

The answer involves more than a stolen vessel and a borrowed hat. It depended on what its enslaved crew knew about the jobs they were forced to do, a stop to collect their families, and two opposite signals delivered to two different armies.

In the spring of 1862, the American Civil War was still in its early stages. Confederate forces held Charleston and its harbor forts. United States warships waited outside, enforcing a naval blockade. For enslaved people inside the city, the distance between those two forces was measured in miles. Crossing it safely was another matter.

One of the vessels working inside the harbor was the Planter. Built in Charleston two years earlier, it was a side-wheel steamer used to transport Confederate men, dispatches, guns, and equipment. It sailed between positions that protected the harbor. Its work demanded experience with the channel, the tide, its boilers, and the signals needed to pass military checkpoints.

Robert Smalls was one of the enslaved members of that crew. He had been sent to Charleston as a child and later worked on the waterfront. He married Hannah, and the family lived under a system that did not legally recognize their marriage as equal to that of free people. His position aboard the Planter gave him knowledge of how the harbor operated, but it did not give him control over where he and his family could go.

The white officers frequently went ashore at night and left the enslaved crew with the moored steamer. National Park Service accounts describe the men discussing an escape in secret. Others who did not regularly serve aboard the Planter were drawn into the plan. It required several people, and the families had to be ready elsewhere in the harbor.

Here was the opening: in May, the ship had been moving artillery for the Confederate defenses. On the evening of May 12, the white officers left the Planter at the Southern Wharf. The vessel still carried its own weapons, along with additional artillery intended for shore positions. The men left aboard knew the machinery and the route.

They prepared to move before dawn.

At about three in the morning on May 13, the steamer pulled away from the wharf. The Union blockade lay beyond the forts, but the crew deliberately took another course first. Their wives and children were waiting at the North Atlantic Wharf, aboard a different vessel called the Etiwan.

The Planter arrived, and the families were brought aboard. Hannah and Smalls's children were among them. They went below deck, out of sight of the Confederate sentries. Sixteen people were now trying to leave together. The choice to collect their families made the journey more complicated than simply running an empty ship out of the harbor.

From shore, the Planter still needed to appear to be doing its Confederate job. Its flags and guns were familiar. Its crew could not explain the passengers below deck. And its captain was no longer aboard.

Smalls put on Captain Charles Relyea's coat and straw hat. The point was not to convince a sentry standing beside him; it was to resemble the captain at a distance while the ship passed. The rest of the operation depended on the crew. According to the Park Service reconstruction, the experienced pilot Abram Allston was at the wheel, while other men handled the deck and engines. It would be misleading to describe the escape as Smalls steering alone through every position.

The steamer moved past earlier defensive works. It passed near Fort Johnson and a patrol boat without raising an alarm. The next challenge was larger. Fort Sumter occupied the main shipping channel, with Fort Moultrie across the water. A ship suspected of escaping could be exposed to guns from both sides.

The crew knew what the garrison expected to hear. Smalls had worked the route often enough to know the required steam-whistle signal. And as the ship approached Fort Sumter at roughly 4:15 a.m., he gave it.

The sentries let the Planter continue.

There is a revealing detail in the historical record. A report from Fort Sumter describes a sentry identifying a vessel leaving the harbor as a guard boat. The identification was wrong, but the fort did not stop the ship. It had recognized a routine-looking movement where an escape was taking place.

The Planter was beyond the Confederate guns. For the families hiding below, the first danger had passed. But a new misunderstanding was waiting beyond the channel.

The American sailors stationed outside Charleston had no advance notice that the armed Confederate steamer was carrying escapees. From the USS Onward, it looked like an enemy vessel approaching the blockade. Its acting captain, John Frederick Nickels, called his men to their stations and ordered the ship's guns prepared.

The people on the Planter had just survived by keeping Confederate colors visible and appearing to belong to the other side. Now those same signs could get them fired upon.

They rushed to raise a white bedsheet as a surrender flag. A piece of ordinary household fabric carried the message that the Confederate-looking steamer would not attack. There was no exchange of cannon fire. The Planter came alongside the Onward, and Smalls turned it over to the American Navy.

Sixteen people had reached the US forces. They were free from the immediate control of their enslavers, although the legal status of people arriving at Union lines in 1862 was more complicated than a simple declaration of universal emancipation. The Emancipation Proclamation had not yet taken effect. The people aboard had nonetheless crossed a boundary they could not cross freely the previous day.

Their escape had also changed the military picture. The United States received a working transport, its weapons, and information about Confederate operations in the harbor. The achievement was the crew's, not a solitary trick by one man.

What happened after this morning also matters. Smalls used his knowledge of the South Carolina waters as a pilot for Union forces. He served on several vessels, including the ironclad Keokuk, which came under Confederate fire in 1863. Later he was given command of the Planter itself. The officer's coat and hat had once helped him disguise who was aboard; he eventually commanded that very ship without pretending to be the Confederate captain.

The change went beyond the wheelhouse. Smalls received money connected with the capture of the Planter. In Beaufort, South Carolina, he bought the house of Henry McKee, a man who had enslaved him when he was young. That purchase was possible during a wartime tax sale of abandoned property. It was a stark reversal in ownership, but it did not end the struggle over freedom and political power in the South.

After the war, Smalls entered public life. He supported education for people who had been denied it and served in South Carolina's legislature. He was eventually elected to the United States House of Representatives, serving multiple terms during and after Reconstruction.

Those later chapters should not erase the other people aboard the Planter. The engineers, deckhands, the men who had joined the plan, and the families waiting at the second wharf made the departure possible. Some went into military service; others built lives in a changing South. Their futures were not identical, and the sources do not give all of them equal attention.

For one morning in 1862, their immediate problem was brutally clear. They needed the Confederate batteries to treat the Planter as an ordinary vessel, then needed the American blockade to recognize that it was carrying people seeking freedom.

At Fort Sumter, a steam-whistle signal helped them pass. At the Onward, a white bedsheet stopped the threat of another set of guns.

The men guarding the harbor had seen the ship before. What they did not see was that the people they had forced to operate it had decided where it would go.
