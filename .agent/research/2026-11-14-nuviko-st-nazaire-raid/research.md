## Three short pitches (same decision rubric)

**A. Operation Chariot / St Nazaire (winner).** Target: general English-speaking history-curious Shorts viewers, including non-war buffs, who grasp a damaged ship and hidden hazard without specialist knowledge. Opening (exact): “Four and a half tons of explosives were hidden inside a wrecked British destroyer. German soldiers were searching it in the French port of Saint-Nazaire.” Stop: a searchable wreck has an unseen explosive charge. Continue: why it rammed the gate, ship disguise, costly commando attack, bomb fails to detonate when expected, Germans inspect it, belated explosion. Payoff: dock unusable for remainder of the war but 169 British dead, hundreds captured. Main objection: military-raids saturation in the queue and need to avoid treating casualties as a cinematic twist; ending must acknowledge cost.

**B. James Caird / Shackleton (strongest challenger).** Target: survival/adventure Shorts viewers. Proposed exact opening: “Six men set off across eight hundred miles of Antarctic sea in a lifeboat. Twenty-two people were still waiting for them on the ice.” Stop: distance and tiny boat. Continue: 16 days at sea, landfall on the wrong side of South Georgia, arduous land crossing, multiple unsuccessful rescue efforts. Payoff: the waiting party is rescued; all 28 expedition members survive. Main objection: compressed map changes, visually similar survival/ice story to Togo #26, likely overlong in short.

**C. Great Stork Derby.** Target: odd history and ethics/legal story viewers. Proposed exact opening: “A Toronto lawyer died in 1926 and left his fortune to whichever woman gave birth to the most children over the next ten years.” Stop: real bizarre contract. Continue: Depression-era competition and competing legal claims, court challenge. Payoff: the clause survived the Supreme Court challenge in 1937, leading to division of the estate. Main objection: children and vulnerable families could become spectacle; payout and individual family counts need deeper original records than available here.

**Why A instead of B:** A has a readable object, physical sequence, false lull and a documented second reversal in one location; the post-explosion outcome is conclusive. B has deeper emotional survival but needs an 800-mile ocean leg, the other side of an island, a 36-hour crossing and further rescues; on first read the geography crowds out the final humans. A's weakness is an already military-heavy Nuviko queue, so the angle is **delayed detection in a wreck**, not 'greatest raid' heroics. B could be stronger as a Long or a sharply focused single stage.
