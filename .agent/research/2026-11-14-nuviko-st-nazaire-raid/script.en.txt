Four and a half tons of explosives were hidden inside a wrecked British destroyer. German soldiers were searching it in the French port of Saint-Nazaire.

Earlier that morning, in March 1942, the ship had smashed into the gate of a huge dry dock.

It was the only dock on the Atlantic coast big enough to repair Germany's largest warships. Britain wanted it out of action.

The Royal Navy had taken an old destroyer, removed two of its funnels, and disguised it to look like a German vessel.

Then they packed explosives into its bow and set delayed fuses. Commandos also travelled in small boats to attack the dock's machinery.

At first, German guards were confused by the ship's appearance and its signals. Then the shore guns opened fire.

The destroyer kept coming. It rammed the dock gate, and the commandos scrambled ashore as the smaller boats came under heavy fire.

British teams blew up pumps and other equipment. But most of the boats meant to take them home were destroyed.

The explosives hidden in the destroyer were supposed to go off later. By nine in the morning, they still hadn't.

Many raiders had been killed or captured. German troops began examining the damaged ship, some looking for souvenirs.

A German officer was questioning the British commander who had steered it into the gate. Then the delayed charges exploded.

The blast wrecked the entrance. The dock stayed unusable for the rest of the war.

For the British, the victory had come at a brutal cost: 169 sailors and commandos dead, and hundreds taken prisoner.
