Why would Britain crash a destroyer into the gates of a dock? Why put explosives inside the ship instead of bombing the port? And what happened when the hidden charge failed to go off on time?

In March 1942, the British faced a problem on the Atlantic coast of occupied France. At Saint-Nazaire stood a dry dock big enough for Germany's largest battleships. If Tirpitz, then based in Norway, entered the Atlantic and needed repairs, this was a place it could use.

British planners wanted to deny Germany that option. But the harbour was guarded by guns and searchlights, and the massive dock gates were a difficult target for aircraft.

Their solution was an ageing destroyer called HMS Campbeltown. Workers removed two of its four funnels, angled the others and gave it the outline of a German torpedo boat. Inside its bow, they concealed four and a half tons of explosives with delayed fuses.

The ship would ram the dock gate and stay there. Meanwhile, commandos aboard Campbeltown and small motor launches would land, destroy the machinery that operated the dock, then return to their boats.

Stephen Beattie commanded Campbeltown. Charles Newman led the commandos. Each had a different job, but both depended on reaching the harbour before German gunners understood the threat.

After midnight on March 28, the flotilla moved up the Loire estuary. Campbeltown scraped the shallows twice. An attempted air diversion had already made the Germans suspicious.

The ship's disguise and German recognition signals acquired in an earlier raid bought a little time. Then searchlights swept across the water. Shore guns opened fire.

Beattie kept going. At about half past one in the morning, Campbeltown smashed into the dock gate. The explosive charge was now precisely where it needed to be.

Commandos raced ashore. Stuart Chant was wounded, yet led his demolition team into the pump house. They placed charges on the machinery that emptied the dock. Other groups destroyed equipment that opened and closed its gates.

The essential equipment was being wrecked. But the escape plan was collapsing.

The small wooden boats carrying the commandos had almost no protection. German fire destroyed several before they could reach the jetty where the men were supposed to be picked up.

Newman and a group of survivors tried to fight their way through the town. Most were killed or captured. Beattie left the destroyer, was later pulled from the water by German forces and became a prisoner.

Then daylight brought another problem. The explosives inside Campbeltown had not detonated. The expected deadline had passed. German soldiers boarded the wreck, searched it and even collected souvenirs. They did not find the charge.

As a German officer questioned Beattie about the raid, the hidden explosives finally went off.

The blast demolished the dock entrance. The huge facility remained unusable for the rest of the war. Britain achieved its objective, but 169 British sailors and commandos died and hundreds were captured.

A photograph taken before the explosion shows the crushed ship still wedged against the gate, with people moving around it. The photograph records the apparent failure. It could not show the danger still hidden in the bow.
