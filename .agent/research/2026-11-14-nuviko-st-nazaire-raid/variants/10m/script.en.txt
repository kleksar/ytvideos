Why would Britain deliberately sacrifice a destroyer to damage one dock? How did the ship get through the defences of a German-held port? And why, after its crew had fought their way ashore, did the raid look like a failure?

On the morning of March 28, 1942, a British destroyer sat crushed against a dock gate at Saint-Nazaire in occupied France. German soldiers were inspecting the wreck. A photographer captured the damage. The most important part of the attack, however, was still concealed inside the ship.

The story began with a different vessel: Germany's battleship Tirpitz. From its base in Norway, Tirpitz threatened the supply routes on which Britain depended. If it sailed into the Atlantic and was badly damaged, the availability of a nearby repair dock could change the risk of that voyage.

Saint-Nazaire contained the Normandie Dock, built for a giant passenger liner. More than three hundred metres long, it was large enough to take Germany's heaviest warships. Its vast gates held water back; pumps emptied the basin so a ship could be worked on.

British planners did not need to sink Tirpitz to remove this option. They could disable its potential repair facility.

But the port was defended by coastal guns, searchlights and thousands of troops. A bombing raid did not guarantee that the enormous gate and vital pumping equipment would be destroyed. Planners chose an attack that would place explosives directly against the dock entrance.

The weapon would be HMS Campbeltown, an old destroyer once operated by the United States Navy. It had four funnels, a silhouette that would betray it as it approached. Workers removed two and altered the others to make it resemble a German torpedo boat.

They also stripped away unnecessary weight so the ship could move through shallow water. They added protection for the men still aboard. These changes mattered because the river mouth was full of mudbanks and the approach was guarded.

An explosives specialist, Nigel Tibbits, concealed charges deep in Campbeltown's bow. The final load was roughly four and a half tons. Delayed fuses were intended to give the attackers time to get away. If the plan worked, the battered ship would sit against the gates before the Germans realised what was inside.

There was a second part to the mission. Commandos travelling aboard Campbeltown and a flotilla of small launches were to attack pumps and gate machinery ashore. The two attacks supported each other. If the ship's explosive load failed, the dock might still be crippled by the sabotage.

Stephen Beattie would command the destroyer. Charles Newman would lead the commandos. Demolition teams practised on buildings similar to those they would have to enter in darkness. Their plan allowed only minutes at each target, followed by a hurried return to boats waiting by a harbour jetty.

The force sailed from Britain on March 26. During the approach, a German submarine spotted it but reported an incorrect course. That mistake helped hide the destination. It did not remove the danger waiting at the port.

Clouds disrupted an attempted RAF diversion and increased German suspicions. The ships then entered the Loire estuary under cover of darkness. Campbeltown scraped the shallows twice before reaching open water closer to the port.

German posts challenged the approaching vessels. For a short time the false outline and authentic German recognition signals, obtained by Britain in an earlier raid, created confusion. Campbeltown kept moving.

Then the searchlights found the flotilla.

Shore batteries opened fire. Beattie ordered the German flag lowered and the British naval flag raised. The disguise had served its purpose. Now he had to steer a damaged old destroyer through the gunfire and straight into the gate.

At roughly half past one, Campbeltown slammed into the great metal entrance. The bow was lodged where the explosives could do the most damage. Yet the charges remained silent. Their fuses were meant to ignite later.

Commandos poured over the ship's sides. Some attacked German gun positions. Others went directly to the machinery buildings.

Lieutenant Stuart Chant, already wounded during the approach, led a demolition team into the main pump house. They had rehearsed on a comparable facility in Britain. Now they planted charges against the pumps that normally drained the dock and got clear before the machinery was torn apart.

Other teams destroyed the equipment used to move the dock's gates. The sabotage was working. The dock's operating systems had been hit from more than one direction.

But the return journey depended on small boats in full view of German gunners. Their wooden hulls and exposed fuel tanks made them especially vulnerable. Several launches burned or sank before reaching the jetty from which the commandos expected to be evacuated.

One of the gunners aboard those launches was Sergeant Thomas Durrant. He continued firing despite repeated wounds. He later died of his injuries. His actions earned a posthumous Victoria Cross, with a German officer who had faced his boat supporting the recognition.

Onshore, Newman learned that the evacuation point could no longer be reached. He and some surviving commandos fought their way into the town and tried to escape across occupied France. Only five commandos eventually reached Spain. For most of the men ashore, the attempt ended in death or captivity.

Beattie, meanwhile, had left Campbeltown with members of its crew. The boat carrying him was lost. Germans recovered him from the water and took him prisoner.

By morning, the destroyer still blocked the entrance. Its hull was shattered, but the great delayed charge had not exploded. The moment at which it was expected to go off had passed.

German troops climbed aboard to inspect the wreck. Some searched for souvenirs. The bomb hidden in the bow remained undiscovered.

As a German officer questioned Beattie about the apparent failure, the charge finally detonated. The explosion tore apart the dock entrance. The target the British had reached during the night was finally destroyed after many of the raiders had been captured.

The explosion was not the only delayed danger. Torpedoes fired into another harbour entrance during the raid went off later. Reports describe panic and shooting by German troops, with French civilians among the victims. That human cost cannot be folded into a triumphant final line.

The main military result was nonetheless lasting. The Normandie Dock stayed unusable for the remainder of the war, denying Germany a major Atlantic repair site. It would be wrong to claim that this single raid alone determined where Tirpitz subsequently sailed.

And the cost to the attackers was enormous. A total of 169 British sailors and commandos died; hundreds more were captured. Many of the small boats meant to bring the survivors home did not return.

The photographs from that morning show Campbeltown still in the gate. People stand around the wreck. They show how the raid looked before the final detonation, when the men who had planned the attack could no longer change its outcome, and the decisive charge was still waiting inside the bow.
