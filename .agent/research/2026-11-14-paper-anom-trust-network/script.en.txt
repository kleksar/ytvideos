ANOM phones were not displayed in ordinary shops. Buyers got them from dealers they already trusted. Those dealers recommended the devices to more customers. Every new user unknowingly added messages to a police investigation. How did investigators get criminal groups to distribute their own surveillance? And why did the network keep growing? The story begins in 2018, when American investigators shut down Phantom Secure, a seller of specially modified encrypted phones. The people who had used those phones still wanted private communications. They looked for replacement services.

Closing one provider had not eliminated the market. It had simply pushed its customers somewhere else. Around that time, an experienced seller of encrypted devices was developing another product, called ANOM. He offered it to the FBI. Together with Australia's federal police, investigators turned the product into a covert platform. Instead of hacking a popular existing network, police could operate this particular service from its beginning. That was only half the plan. A police-run phone with no users would tell investigators nothing.

The devices offered almost none of the functions of an ordinary smartphone. Their main purpose was encrypted messaging. For buyers, that was the attraction. They were paying for a channel they believed investigators could not read. But every ANOM message secretly produced a second copy that the sender never saw. One went to its intended recipient. The other travelled through a route built for investigators. The messages were encrypted on the way, but that hidden copy could be made readable to police.

To a person using ANOM, the extra route was invisible. The private messaging service appeared to work. Now investigators needed customers. An obvious public advertisement would have exposed who was behind the product. They relied instead on distributors who already sold encrypted devices inside criminal networks. The first phones moved through a limited group of sellers. Trust came from the person offering the device. A buyer did not need to trust an unknown company. Someone inside the network was recommending its phone.

The next referral carried the same confidence. A sales network began expanding without police openly recruiting anyone. Australian police later named Hakan Ayik, a fugitive accused of drug trafficking, as an unwitting promoter. Police said he recommended the platform to associates without knowing who actually operated it. The operation grew on trust that was already there. Then the market around ANOM started changing. In 2020, investigators disrupted EncroChat. In March 2021, U.S. authorities charged executives of Sky Global.

Customers sought alternatives. U.S. prosecutors said demand for ANOM increased after those other services were dismantled. Investigations against other platforms had helped push customers toward a platform run by investigators. Each new customer meant more possible conversations to examine. The network was helping recruit its own users. Some messages discussed drug shipments. Users sent pictures of what they planned to move. Prosecutors described cocaine hidden among pineapples and bananas, and illegal cargo concealed in tuna cans.

These were investigative allegations, not verdicts against every buyer. A message archive still had to become a case. The devices also promised remote wiping, so data could be erased if police seized a handset. According to an indictment, some distributors took requests to wipe phones that had fallen into police hands. Protection from investigators was being sold through a system those investigators themselves operated. Across the operation, more than twelve thousand ANOM devices and services were sold in criminal networks.

The U.S. Justice Department said investigators collected more than twenty-seven million messages. But those messages were leads and evidence, not twenty-seven million convictions. Investigators still had to identify people. The undercover service could not run forever. On June seventh, 2021, the ANOM platform was shut down. The next day, American prosecutors announced what had been happening. Police operations were unfolding across countries. Thousands of officers took part, and searches were coordinated far beyond a single city.

Officials reported seized drugs, weapons and money, as well as arrests following the investigation. The widely repeated number is eight hundred arrests. That was the reported total for the investigation as a whole. It was neither eight hundred guilty verdicts nor eight hundred arrests on one morning. For people who had trusted ANOM, the public announcement revealed who had been running it. The arrests did not end all the cases. Courts would argue over the messages for years.

Defendants in Australia challenged whether material gathered through ANOM could be used against them. In October 2025, Australia's High Court upheld legislation affecting the use of those messages. The court did not decide every original interception question. The legal aftermath was still more complicated than a raid. None of this means every encrypted app is secretly run by police. ANOM was a particular controlled platform. The weakness here was social as well as technical. Buyers relied on recommendations from people they trusted.

The same trust that helped users avoid outsiders had also helped investigators build the network from within.
