How could a police-run messaging network become popular among people trying to hide from police? Who convinced buyers it was safe? And when investigators revealed the trap, would the messages actually stand up in court?

In 2018, US investigators shut down Phantom Secure, a business selling modified encrypted phones. Its customers did not suddenly stop wanting private conversations. They looked elsewhere. Other suppliers remained, and there was a market for a replacement. That demand created an opening for a very unusual investigation.

An experienced seller of encrypted devices was developing a product called ANOM. He offered it to the FBI. American agents worked with the Australian Federal Police to turn that product into a covert messaging platform. Rather than trying to break into a widely used service owned by somebody else, investigators would operate this particular network themselves. It would look like a confidential service for customers. Behind the service, investigators would have access to its conversations.

That solved one problem, but not the next one. Building a police-controlled platform did not produce evidence unless people chose to use it. And publicly advertising a new service as a police product would destroy the operation before it started. The investigators had to persuade buyers to adopt a system while concealing the only fact those buyers most wanted to know: who controlled it.

ANOM phones weren't normal smartphones with a messaging app added. The devices were modified around private communication. Their restricted functions could look like a selling point to someone who wanted fewer ways to be tracked. Customers paid for a product advertised as a secure channel. The promise was about keeping outsiders away from the conversation.

That promise concealed a second route. When an ANOM user sent a message, the intended recipient received it. But the system also generated a hidden copy. The US Justice Department described it as a blind carbon copy routed through infrastructure that let investigators read it. A message could be encrypted in transit and still reach police through a secret recipient built into the platform. Investigators did not need to claim that every encryption system in the world was broken. They controlled the service delivering these messages.

Picture what that meant for the people selling the phones. From the buyer's point of view, a device could send and receive messages as advertised. The seller had a product that appeared to work. Nothing on the user's screen needed to announce that another copy was leaving the system. The more customers trusted the service, the more conversations accumulated behind it.

But where did that first trust come from? ANOM wasn't on an ordinary retail shelf. Investigators depended on people already distributing encrypted phones inside criminal networks. A buyer who would never trust an unfamiliar company's advertising might trust a dealer who had supplied devices before. That dealer could introduce the service to another buyer, who might recommend it again. The network expanded through existing relationships rather than public recruiting.

Australian authorities later identified Hakan Ayik, a fugitive accused in drug-trafficking matters, as one of the unwitting promoters. According to police, he recommended ANOM to associates without knowing its true operators. The distinction matters. This isn't evidence that he secretly worked for investigators. The police account is that he helped spread the phones precisely because he did not know what was happening.

By 2019 the service had gained a foothold, though it was not yet the only name in encrypted communications. Then events outside ANOM altered the market. Authorities disrupted EncroChat in 2020. In March 2021 US prosecutors charged executives associated with Sky Global. These investigations had their own targets and histories; it would be misleading to say all were staged simply as advertisements for ANOM. But US prosecutors said the loss of alternatives increased demand for the platform investigators already controlled.

The trap now had a feedback loop. A trusted seller recommended the product. More customers came in. Disruption of competing services left people searching again. Some chose ANOM. The system didn't have to recruit every user directly. It benefited from the choices and recommendations of the very networks under investigation.

What were those users actually sending? According to the 2021 federal indictment and Justice Department account, some conversations concerned drug shipments and money laundering. Messages included pictures of narcotics. Prosecutors described cocaine concealed among pineapples and bananas, and illicit cargo packed in tuna cans. The details matter because they show what access to the messages could reveal: alleged plans and supporting images rather than only phone numbers or vague associations.

ANOM also offered remote wiping, a feature intended to remove data if a handset was seized. The indictment described distributors handling requests to wipe confiscated devices. That was a striking reversal. A feature marketed as protection from investigators appeared inside a system those investigators were running. But the charges described alleged conduct. They were not a guilty verdict against everyone who owned a phone.

The volume of collected material presented another problem. Access to millions of messages is not the same as millions of proved crimes. Investigators still had to identify people, connect a conversation to conduct, corroborate evidence and follow the relevant laws in each country. An ordinary-looking photograph in a chat might be important to a case, but the chat itself did not automatically establish every element of an offence.

By the operation's end, the US Justice Department said ANOM had distributed more than twelve thousand devices and services to networks across roughly one hundred countries. Its 2025 account described users in more than three hundred criminal syndicates and more than twenty-seven million messages collected. These are figures reported by the investigators, not measurements of how many individual people were convicted.

The secret couldn't last indefinitely. On June 7, 2021, ANOM was shut down. International police operations unfolded around the takedown. The Justice Department later reported more than ten thousand officers involved, searches of over seven hundred locations, and more than five hundred arrests during the takedown period. The next day, officials publicly explained the platform. People who had recommended ANOM learned that the private channel they were selling had been controlled by police.

Different arrest figures in later reports describe different cutoffs, not necessarily contradictions. At the June 2021 announcement, authorities reported about eight hundred arrests across the investigation. In February 2025 the Justice Department placed the cumulative total at approximately twelve hundred. Arrests, charges, guilty pleas and convictions are different outcomes. Neither number means every message was a crime or every user was convicted.

The legal story continued after the coordinated searches. In the United States, seventeen alleged ANOM distributors were indicted in San Diego. By February 2025, the Justice Department reported that all eight defendants extradited to the US by then had pleaded guilty to a racketeering conspiracy. That is a specific set of defendants and a specific reported date, not a claim that all defendants everywhere received the same judgment.

In Australia, another question emerged: could the intercepted communications be used as evidence? Defendants challenged the collection and use of ANOM data. Parliament passed legislation in 2024 addressing the legal status of material obtained under specified warrants. The dispute reached the High Court, but not as a simple courtroom replay of the operation.

On October 8, 2025, Australia's High Court unanimously upheld that legislation against constitutional challenges. The original question about whether the collection had been unlawful under interception law was no longer decided in that proceeding. The ruling allowed affected prosecutions to proceed, but it was not a universal determination about every accused person or every future method of surveillance. The impressive scale of the police operation did not eliminate the need for legal scrutiny.

So where was the weakness that ANOM exploited? It was not a magical ability to read any encrypted conversation. It was the difference between trusting the mathematics of a private message and knowing who actually runs the service that sends it. People relied on handsets recommended by their own contacts. Dealers passed on that confidence. Investigators built their access into the service and let the network help distribute it.

The final reversal was public: the people who believed they were keeping investigators outside had helped carry the investigators' platform inside.
