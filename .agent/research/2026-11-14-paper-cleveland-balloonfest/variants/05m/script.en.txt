How could a city launch almost a million and a half balloons in one afternoon? And what would happen if those balloons stopped rising before the crowd had even gone home? Cleveland was about to discover that setting a world record and controlling what happens afterward are two very different jobs.

In 1986, Cleveland was tired of being the punchline of jokes about its decline. United Way, a local charity, wanted an event that brought people downtown and made them proud. Its communications director, George Fraser, had seen a photograph of Disneyland releasing a million balloons. Why not do something even bigger?

The man behind that Disneyland display, Treb Heining, knew what the idea required. This was not a matter of buying balloons and opening the boxes. Someone had to fill, hold, count and release them together. He came to Public Square, looked at the space around the Terminal Tower and agreed it could be done.

Workers built an enormous temporary enclosure under a net the size of a city block. Helium lines fed rows of inflation valves. Thousands of volunteers, many of them students, had to tie balloon after balloon without letting the growing mass escape. By the morning of September 27, the square had become a temporary balloon factory.

Then a forecast threatened the entire plan. A cold front and rain were moving toward Lake Erie. The team knew that even a little water could weigh down a helium balloon. They moved the release forward, betting that the cloud would rise before the bad weather arrived. There was no second launch waiting if they got it wrong.

The net opened. A dense cloud of red, yellow, blue and white rose above Public Square and curled around the Terminal Tower. Volunteers could finally see the result of their work. For a short time it looked as if the gamble had worked. The crowd cheered at a sight no one there had seen before.

But the balloons did not simply disappear into the distance. The weather shifted, and rain and downdrafts pushed many down. The same cloud that had filled the sky began spreading over streets and the lakefront. On a nearby highway, drivers were distracted by the balloons; a multi-car crash was reported.

There was a more serious problem on Lake Erie. Two fishermen had gone missing the evening before. Coast Guard crews were already searching for them when the balloons came down on the water. The searchers said the floating balloons made it harder to see what was out there.

The men were later found dead. Their disappearance began before the launch, and there is no sound basis for saying Balloonfest caused their deaths. The documented consequence is that the spectacle interfered with an operation already underway. That distinction matters more than the dramatic version of the story often repeated online.

The trouble did not end at the shoreline. A horse owner said falling balloons had frightened her animal and sued. Other balloons crossed Lake Erie, reaching beaches in Canada weeks later. The moment on Public Square had lasted minutes. Its scattered remains kept turning up far from the people who had applauded.

So did Cleveland actually win? Yes. Guinness recorded 1,429,643 balloons, a world record. Many people who worked on Balloonfest still remember the release as an extraordinary day of cooperation. Others remember traffic disruption, search interference and waste. Both accounts belong to the same afternoon.

The plan succeeded at the one thing it could count: how many balloons rose at once. It had no way to count where all of them would come down.
