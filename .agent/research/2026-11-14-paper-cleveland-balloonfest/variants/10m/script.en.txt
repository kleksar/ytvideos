How do you get almost one and a half million balloons into the air at once? Why did a photographer who came to capture Cleveland's world record end up flying through the display? And why did the very same balloons become a problem for a search crew on Lake Erie?

To understand Cleveland's Balloonfest, start with a photograph taken somewhere else. In 1985, Disneyland had released a million balloons for its anniversary. The photograph reached George Fraser, the communications director at Cleveland's United Way. The charity wanted to interest young people in volunteering, and the city needed an image far removed from the jokes about its industrial decline.

Fraser proposed beating that record and contacted Treb Heining, the man behind the Disneyland release. Heining doubted whether it was possible, but agreed to inspect Public Square. The space around the Terminal Tower persuaded him. There was room for the balloons; what no one could build was a second chance to launch them once the weather turned.

The original ambition was two million balloons. It was reduced to roughly one and a half million while the organizers worked through fundraising and logistics, not because of a last-minute panic on release day. Even at the smaller number, the job was enormous. The team needed a temporary factory in the middle of downtown: helium lines, inflation valves, a structure several stories high and a net covering an entire block.

Operations director Tom Holowach later recalled searching for hundreds of milk crates to support the inflation equipment. The net had to keep the balloons inside while volunteers filled the enclosure. Even before they could start, inspectors wanted the structure to withstand high winds.

That requirement mattered sooner than anyone expected. A permit still had to be sorted out during construction, and the temporary balloon factory was standing in the middle of a busy downtown. The team arranged street closures and transport. Then nature tested the structure before the public even arrived.

On the evening before the release, fierce winds tore parts of the net. Holowach's team spent the night repairing it and needed a fire department lift to reach the top. By the time volunteers started arriving the next morning, the repaired net was ready to hold the balloons. The crew could mend a torn net. They would have no such option once it was lifted.

Volunteers spent hours inflating and tying balloons. Some taped their fingers to avoid blisters. Jennie Jones, the photographer hired by United Way, coordinated a team on the ground and on rooftops; she would shoot from a helicopter. Meanwhile a boy named Max Johnson had come downtown with his family to see the release. They stopped for a meal at a department store restaurant with windows overlooking the city.

But forecasters were following a cold front near Lake Erie. Rain was the particular danger. A helium balloon can rise while dry; extra water on its surface makes that harder, and downdrafts can push it down. Holowach later described speaking to NOAA and being warned that rain could arrive earlier than the scheduled release.

Organizers moved the launch forward rather than wait for the scheduled release. All the work of filling the balloons now came down to a race with the weather. The spectators in the restaurant did not know the schedule had changed.

People in the restaurant rushed to the windows. Johnson later recalled that his father lifted him onto his shoulders so he could see. Outside, the net was rising, and the cloud of color flowed around the Terminal Tower. Above them, Jones was photographing the same event from the helicopter. From two very different heights, they had the sight Cleveland had spent months preparing.

Then the weather caught up. Many balloons stopped climbing. Water on their surfaces added weight, and wind and sinking air carried them toward the streets and Lake Erie. Jones recalled that the helicopter had to fly through the balloons. She was frightened, but the crew got through and landed at the waterfront airport. The photographs survived. The balloons were now heading somewhere the photographers could no longer control.

Johnson's family was heading home. He later described seeing balloons descend onto the interstate and people getting out of their cars to collect them. A multicar collision was reported on the nearby Shoreway. The boy who had needed to sit on his father's shoulders to see the balloons could now see them from the road.

Flights at Burke Lakefront Airport were interrupted around the event. Later accounts disagree on whether the balloons caused the closure or the runway was already closed for the celebration. Jones's account confirms her own encounter in the air, not the reason the airport closed. The lake itself presented a different problem.

On Lake Erie, the consequences were more serious. Two fishermen, Raymond Broderick and Bernard Sulzer, had gone out the previous evening and failed to return. Their boat was discovered, but the men were missing. The Coast Guard was looking for them before Balloonfest's cloud began falling onto the water.

A search crew needs to notice small shapes on the surface. The balloons added thousands of other shapes. Coast Guard personnel said the floating debris made the search harder. The fishermen were later found dead. The available chronology does not show that the balloon release caused their deaths; they were already missing after an earlier storm. Interference with the search is a real and grave enough consequence without inventing a more dramatic one.

One fisherman's widow sued United Way. A horse owner also brought a claim, alleging that the balloons had frightened and injured her animal. Both cases were settled out of court, according to the later account based on local reporting. Neither settlement by itself establishes that the balloons caused the fishermen's deaths.

Meanwhile the balloons kept travelling. Many ended up on city streets and in waterways. Others crossed Lake Erie, and weeks later people found them on beaches in Ontario, Canada. That was another part of the release no one could see from Public Square: the event continued after its audience and cameras had gone home.

Did the attempt set a record? Yes. Guinness recorded 1,429,643 balloons. The lower target had been chosen during planning, and the release still surpassed Disneyland. George Fraser had started with a photograph of balloons in California; now there were photographs of his own event. But the picture he saw in the newspaper afterward was very different.

Fraser recalled opening the local newspaper and seeing the balloons spread across Lake Erie. He said that for years the experience felt disappointing to him, though his view changed with time. That is one organizer's memory. The Coast Guard's account of an obstructed search and the debris found in Canada remain part of the same story.

The Cleveland release became the last holder of that Guinness record, according to a later reconstruction of the event. The category was discontinued over environmental concerns about mass latex releases. Nobody was going to beat Cleveland in that particular record book category again.

The photograph that inspired Fraser showed a million balloons rising above Disneyland. The photograph he remembered opening the newspaper to see showed what had happened after Cleveland's record. He had succeeded in getting people to look up. The next picture showed them where they needed to look down.
