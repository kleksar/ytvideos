Can you build a world record with a net, thousands of volunteers and almost one and a half million balloons? What if the weather turns before you've even finished counting them? And when a celebration goes wrong, how do you tell the difference between what really happened and the disasters added to the story later?

To understand Cleveland's Balloonfest, start with a photograph taken somewhere else. In 1985, Disneyland had released a million balloons for its anniversary. The photograph reached George Fraser, the communications director at Cleveland's United Way. The charity wanted to interest young people in volunteering, and the city needed an image far removed from the jokes about its industrial decline.

Fraser proposed beating that record. He contacted Treb Heining, the man who had organized the Disneyland release. Heining warned that the scale was formidable. But when he visited Cleveland's Public Square and looked up at the Terminal Tower, he saw room for a gigantic installation. The impossible idea now had a location. But on release day a storm would approach that not even the biggest net could solve: once the balloons were free, there was no way to pull them back.

The original ambition was two million balloons. It was reduced to roughly one and a half million while the organizers worked through fundraising and logistics, not because of a last-minute panic on release day. Even at the smaller number, the job was enormous. The team needed a temporary factory in the middle of downtown: helium lines, inflation valves, a structure several stories high and a net covering an entire block.

Operations director Tom Holowach later recalled hunting down hundreds of milk crates to support inflation equipment. The net was a separate engineering problem. It had to hold an enormous, restless collection of helium balloons without letting them escape early. City inspectors also wanted the supporting structure strong enough to withstand severe winds.

At one point the construction was well underway before the organizers had secured the necessary permit. A manager connected with the Terminal Tower hurried to City Hall to straighten it out. Preparations also involved closing streets, rerouting buses and arranging transport for a crowd expected to fill downtown. The event was growing into something closer to a citywide operation than a single afternoon's entertainment.

Then, on the evening before the release, fierce wind hit the area. According to the operations team's later account, parts of the net needed repairs overnight. Workers patched damaged sections, and volunteers arrived early the next morning. The structure had survived its first test. But the storm also showed how little control anyone had over the sky.

Students and other volunteers spent hours inflating and tying balloons beneath the canopy. Some wrapped their fingers in tape to avoid blisters. Photographers positioned themselves on roofs and in a helicopter. Families gathered for the promised sight; local television presenters prepared to broadcast it. The whole undertaking had one irreversible final action: lift the net.

But forecasters were following a cold front near Lake Erie. Rain was the particular danger. A helium balloon can rise while dry; extra water on its surface makes that harder, and downdrafts can push it down. Holowach later described speaking to NOAA and being warned that rain could arrive earlier than the scheduled release.

Canceling would mean leaving the filled enclosure and the enormous crowd without the spectacle they had prepared for. The organizers instead brought the release forward. The decision made sense as an attempt to outrun the weather, but it put the entire result on a very narrow window. Once those balloons were free, nobody could collect them back into the net.

The net rose, and a mass of bright color surged into the air. Witnesses remember it curling around the Terminal Tower and briefly spreading like a vast ceiling of stained glass. From a helicopter, photographer Jennie Jones recorded the sight. For those who had spent the day filling balloons, it was the instant that made the exhausting work worthwhile.

For a few minutes, Cleveland had what it wanted: people looking up at something beautiful, all together. Then the weather reached the balloons. Instead of travelling away and eventually coming down far from the city, many stopped climbing. Rain added weight; wind and sinking air pushed them back toward the streets and the water.

One family who watched from downtown later described heading home along Interstate 90 and seeing balloons already falling while people stopped their cars to pick them up. Other drivers looked up instead of watching traffic. A multi-car collision was reported on the Shoreway. Something that looked harmless in the sky had become a distraction right where vehicles needed clear roads and drivers' attention.

At Burke Lakefront Airport, close to the water, flights were interrupted around the event. Some accounts say the falling balloons closed the runway; an operations participant later disputed whether the closure was caused by them or had been arranged for the event. Without a verified operations log, that narrower claim is unsettled. The balloons themselves, however, certainly did reach the lakefront.

On Lake Erie, the consequences were more serious. Two fishermen, Raymond Broderick and Bernard Sulzer, had gone out the previous evening and failed to return. Their boat was discovered, but the men were missing. The Coast Guard was looking for them before Balloonfest's cloud began falling onto the water.

A search crew needs to notice small shapes on the surface. The balloons added thousands of other shapes. Coast Guard personnel said the floating debris made the search harder. The fishermen were later found dead. The available chronology does not show that the balloon release caused their deaths; they were already missing after an earlier storm. Interference with the search is a real and grave enough consequence without inventing a more dramatic one.

One of the fishermen's families sued. Elsewhere in Ohio, the owner of an Arabian horse said falling balloons frightened the animal and caused harm; that also led to a lawsuit. These were allegations, not evidence that a reporter had watched either incident happen. Both legal claims became part of Balloonfest's history.

Meanwhile the balloons kept travelling. Many ended up on city streets and in waterways. Others crossed Lake Erie, and weeks later people found them on beaches in Ontario, Canada. That was another part of the release no one could see from Public Square: the event continued after its audience and cameras had gone home.

Did the attempt at least set a record? Yes. Guinness recorded 1,429,643 balloons. The original target had been larger, but even the reduced release exceeded the previous mark. The workers had built the temporary installation, thousands had volunteered and the extraordinary sight really had appeared over Terminal Tower. Calling the whole operation a failed record attempt would be inaccurate.

For years, the participants and the later commentators have told rather different versions of the same day. Some volunteers recall a crowded square and the joy of a project completed together. Others remember floating litter, disrupted roads and the rescue search. The people who staged the spectacle do not all agree with the modern description of it as an unqualified catastrophe.

That disagreement does not erase the harms. It helps explain why the event was so memorable: the beauty was visible to everyone at once, while the costs appeared in separate places, to separate people, over days and weeks. A photograph of the release could contain the triumph, but it could not contain the entire aftermath.

Cleveland built exactly the kind of spectacle it hoped people would remember. And they did remember it. The unanswered question was never how many balloons the city could lift. It was what would happen to them after the net opened.
