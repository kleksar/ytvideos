At 4:50 a.m., the vehicles still allowed on Sweden's roads stopped on the left, moved carefully across to the right, and waited.

Ten minutes later, they could drive again. Sweden was changing a rule drivers had followed for generations.

Across the borders, Norway and Finland already drove on the right. So even reaching the next country meant switching sides.

Swedish voters had rejected the idea in 1955. Almost eighty-three percent voted to keep driving on the left.

But in 1963, parliament approved the switch. That left four years to get the roads—and the drivers—ready.

Road crews reworked junctions and bus stops. About eight thousand buses had to be rebuilt or replaced.

And then there were the signs: roughly three hundred and sixty thousand to replace, with new ones installed under covers.

On television and radio, people heard about the change again and again. Special information was prepared for people who needed it.

The night before the switch, workers covered the old signs and uncovered the new ones. By dawn, the roads already looked different.

Most traffic was banned from one until six that Sunday morning. Buses, taxis, emergency vehicles, and other permitted traffic could still move.

At five, those permitted vehicles set off on the right. At six, most other motorists could finally join them.

In central Stockholm, crowds, cyclists, and camera crews packed the street. The carefully planned crossing became a traffic jam.

Police eventually got things moving. There were minor accidents, but the feared wave of crashes never came. Sweden has kept driving on the right ever since.

It had all begun with one carefully timed move by the drivers who were allowed out before sunrise.
