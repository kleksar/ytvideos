How do you move an entire country to the other side of the road without sending cars into each other? And what happens when voters have already said no? Sweden faced both questions in 1967. The answer took four years to build, but the decisive maneuver lasted ten minutes.

Before the change, Swedish drivers kept left. Across the land borders in Norway and Finland, traffic kept right. Crossing a border meant changing sides. That was inconvenient enough. Reversing the rule at home, with roads full of buses and cars, was an entirely different problem.

In 1955, Sweden asked its voters about switching. Nearly eighty-three percent of those who cast ballots said no. The referendum was advisory, and parliament initially kept the old rule. But the disagreement did not disappear along with the ballot boxes.

In May 1963, parliament voted to introduce right-hand traffic in 1967. A vote could change the law. It could not turn a junction around, move a bus door, or teach an entire population a new habit. Those jobs now had a deadline.

Consider the bus waiting by a curb. Its doors were arranged for passengers boarding on the left-hand side. Change the direction of traffic, and the curb is now on the wrong side. Some eight thousand buses needed rebuilding or replacement. Stops and junctions needed work too. In cities, one-way streets and crossings made the puzzle harder.

Then came the road signs. About 360,000 had to be changed, and new signs could be placed in advance under covers. Workers could not simply wake up on Sunday and start choosing where every sign belonged. The new roads had to be prepared while the old rules still worked.

But the hardest object to change was not a sign. It was a driver's reflex. A person could know the new rule and still instinctively steer toward the familiar side of the road. Radio and television carried a sustained campaign. Special material reached people who would not get enough information from ordinary broadcasts.

Finally came the night of September second. Crews worked on the roads while the country slept, hiding old signs and revealing new ones. The Sunday traffic ban ran from one in the morning until six, with exceptions for buses, taxis, emergency vehicles and other approved traffic. Some central city areas had longer restrictions.

Now the ten-minute answer. At 4:50 a.m., the vehicles allowed to be out stopped on the left. They carefully moved across to the right and waited again. At five, they could drive on. At six, most other drivers were allowed out too. They were not all asked to change lanes together in moving traffic.

It was a carefully controlled reset, but the street did not stay empty. On Kungsgatan in central Stockholm, sightseers, cyclists and camera crews crowded around vehicles trying to move through. The first morning produced a real traffic jam. Police eventually restored movement. Contemporary reporting recorded some minor accidents, not the imagined countrywide catastrophe.

Sweden kept driving on the right. The ten minutes mattered because the buses, signs, rules and drivers had been prepared for years. The lesson was not that changing sides was easy. It was that nearly everything difficult had been done before the clock reached 4:50.
