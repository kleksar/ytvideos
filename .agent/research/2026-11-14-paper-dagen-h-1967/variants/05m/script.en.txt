How can a country move its traffic to the other side of the road in ten minutes without cars meeting head-on? And why would it do that after voters had said no? Sweden faced both questions in 1967. The actual switch would take place before dawn. Making it possible took four years.

Swedish traffic had kept left for generations, while Norway and Finland drove on the right. Crossing a land border meant changing sides. Reversing the rule inside Sweden was harder. Every intersection, bus stop and driver's habit had been built around the old arrangement.

In 1955, almost eighty-three percent of voters in an advisory referendum rejected the change. Parliament initially kept left-hand traffic. But international road travel kept growing. In May 1963, lawmakers voted to switch to the right in 1967. That gave the planners a deadline, not a finished road system.

Start with a city bus. Its doors faced the curb when it pulled over on the left. After the reversal, those same doors could open toward moving traffic. Around eight thousand buses needed to be rebuilt or replaced. Crews also had to move stops and alter junctions and one-way streets. Each change had to work with the old rule until the day of the switch.

Then came the signs. Roughly 360,000 road signs needed replacing. Many new ones were mounted early but kept covered. Uncover them too soon and a driver might follow an instruction for traffic that did not exist yet. Leave them hidden too long and the first vehicles would enter an unfamiliar road with the wrong directions.

And a correct sign could not erase a driver's reflex. The authorities used radio, television, schools and reminders to prepare people. The campaign's H symbol even appeared on milk cartons. Detailed instructions for driving on the right came close to the switch, so people would not start applying the new rule while cars were still keeping left.

For some drivers, the morning was something to experience, not just survive. Nineteen-year-old Anders Odéen stayed awake with two friends, drinking coffee and cola. He wanted to drive his Volkswagen Beetle across the Lidingö bridge toward Stockholm before anyone else had crossed it on the right. They had a very specific finish line in mind.

On the night of September second, road crews made their last alterations and uncovered the new signs. From one until six on Sunday morning, most traffic was prohibited. Buses, taxis, emergency services and other specially permitted vehicles could still be out; central areas of some cities had longer restrictions.

At 4:50, those permitted vehicles stopped on the left, carefully moved across to the right, and waited. At five, they could move again. At six, most other drivers were allowed back. This was a pause for a small amount of authorized traffic, not millions of cars swerving at once.

Anders and his friends reached the bridge, but another driver had beaten them there. They crossed second. He later remembered people driving very carefully. In central Stockholm, caution did not prevent a different problem: sightseers, cyclists and television crews crowded Kungsgatan, tangling the newly directed traffic into a jam. Police eventually got it moving.

Contemporary reporting noted minor accidents, not the feared nationwide crash disaster. Officials also imposed strict speed limits and advised drivers to keep their headlights on even by day during the transition. Changing the signs did not mean that people had instantly forgotten their old habits.

Sweden has driven on the right ever since. The ten-minute maneuver worked because the buses, streets, signs and drivers had been prepared before it began. And for Anders, the new era started with a missed first place on a bridge and a drive through a city learning its roads again.
