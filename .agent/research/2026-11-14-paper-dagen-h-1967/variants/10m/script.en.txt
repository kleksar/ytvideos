How can a country change the side of the road in ten minutes? Who gets to move first, when a mistake could send a car into oncoming traffic? And why would lawmakers order such a change after voters had rejected it? Sweden answered those questions on a Sunday morning in 1967. But the clock was the last part of the story.

For generations, Swedish traffic had kept left. Norway and Finland, its neighbors across land borders, kept right. A driver crossing between them had to change habits at the border. More cars and more international travel made that inconsistency harder to ignore. Yet a nationwide reversal would affect far more than the driver behind a steering wheel.

A road is designed for the way traffic moves. A bus stop needs a curb on the correct side. An intersection's turning lanes have to meet the right approach. A warning sign has to face the vehicles that need to read it. None of those things switches merely because a new law is printed.

The argument had lasted decades. Swedish authorities had considered a change long before 1967, but costs and disagreement kept delaying it. Then came a public vote. In October 1955, the country held an advisory referendum. Among those who voted, 82.9 percent rejected the move to the right. Turnout was about 53 percent. Parliament kept left-hand traffic.

For people who opposed the change, it was hardly an imaginary cost. Buses and streets would require expensive work. A new rule could also produce mistakes, especially in the first days. Those concerns were real questions to solve, even if a referendum could not settle the engineering.

In May 1963, parliament voted 294 to 50 to go ahead. The switch was set for 1967. That decision created a striking contradiction: a country whose voters had said no now had a deadline for doing the opposite. The question was no longer whether to move. It was how to make the change function.

The planners divided the problem into roads, vehicles and people. They established a dedicated commission, with regional representatives to coordinate work outside the capital. The scale mattered. A crossroads in a small town and a busy one-way system in Stockholm could not be handled with the same sketch.

Take a normal city bus. Under the left-hand rule, it pulled up beside the curb so passengers could enter safely. Reverse the traffic flow and its doors may face the road, away from that curb. Around eight thousand buses needed to be rebuilt or replaced. Stops had to move, too. Some city layouts needed new turning arrangements, changes to signals and adjustments to one-way streets.

And then there were signs. The transport authorities describe about 360,000 road signs to replace and another 130,000 reminder signs. New signs could be mounted well before the change, but they had to remain covered. Otherwise drivers would see instructions for a rule that had not started yet. The old arrangement had to keep functioning while its replacement was being built around it.

This was an unusual kind of construction project. Usually a new road opens while the old one stays where it was. Here the very meaning of left and right would reverse, on roads people used every day. A sign covered with cloth was not a decorative detail. It let the future system wait without confusing the present one.

There was also the question of cost. The official estimate grew beyond the roughly 400 million kronor discussed when parliament decided. The commission's work was funded in part by a special charge on registered vehicles. For a driver who had voted against the idea, the transition was something they were helping to pay for. The public argument had not magically disappeared.

But engineers could rebuild a junction without changing what a driver did automatically. For years, keeping left had required no conscious thought. Someone could learn the new rule in the evening and still follow the old one at the next bend. That was why information became part of the infrastructure.

Radio, television, newspapers and schools carried the message. Organizers sought out people whom ordinary mass media might miss, including some people at home, and prepared accessible and translated materials. The goal was not to inform most drivers. The authorities wanted every person who could travel independently to understand the change.

They also had to decide when to teach the new movements. Explain them too early, and drivers might start practicing right-hand habits while roads still ran on the left. The commission deliberately held back detailed instructions on how to drive on the right until the final days. In the last weekend, broadcasts intensified. The source describes twelve hours of television programming and forty hours of radio traffic programs across three channels and ten languages.

Finally, the problem moved from paperwork to the streets. On Saturday night, September second, crews worked through the last alterations. Around twenty thousand people were involved in final road preparations, moving or uncovering signs and making the new directions visible. For a moment, the old road network and the new one existed in the same places, separated by covers and barriers.

On Sunday, ordinary traffic was banned from one in the morning until six. Buses, taxis, emergency vehicles and other approved traffic could still use the roads. Some city centers needed longer restrictions so crews could finish intersections. The aim was to keep the roads quiet at the moment of change. It was not a plan to have every Swedish vehicle move sideways at once.

At 4:50 a.m., those vehicles that were permitted to travel stopped on the left. They carefully moved across to the right and stopped again. At five they could resume, now following the opposite rule. At six most other motorists could return, although some central areas remained restricted. The switch of the moving vehicles really did take ten minutes. The larger operation did not.

In central Stockholm, the carefully controlled road met an uncontrolled problem: spectators. People came to watch, along with cyclists and television crews. On Kungsgatan, vehicles trying to take the new side became tangled in a crowd and a traffic jam. A contemporary reporter described police struggling before movement was restored. The plan had accounted for the cars; public curiosity had its own effect.

There were minor accidents. But no nationwide disaster followed that first morning. Reduced speed limits and policing were part of the precautions, and the change held. The success did not prove that a nationwide switch was harmless, or that every accident after it had been prevented by planning. It showed that this particular operation had been prepared in enough detail to take effect.

The most surprising part is what happened before the countdown. A rejected referendum, a later parliamentary decision, buses altered, junctions rebuilt, signs hidden, drivers taught and roads emptied. Every step removed one reason the final ten minutes might fail. On September third, 1967, Sweden did not suddenly become ready at five o'clock. It reached five o'clock already prepared.

That is why the famous photograph of cars crossing lanes captures only the last movement of the change. The country had been changing for four years. The clock merely told its drivers when to use the new road.
