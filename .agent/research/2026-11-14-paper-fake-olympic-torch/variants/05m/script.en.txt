How did a student with a chair leg get an official police escort during an Olympic torch relay? Why did Sydney's mayor accept his flame? And what happened when the real runner arrived?

In 1956, Australia was preparing to host the Olympic Games in Melbourne. The flame was travelling across the country, and Sydney Town Hall was one of its ceremonial stops. Cross-country runner Harry Dillon was supposed to bring it to Lord Mayor Pat Hills.

A group of university students thought the ceremony had become far too solemn. Barry Larkin later pointed out that the modern torch relay had begun at the 1936 Berlin Olympics. The students wanted to make fun of the spectacle.

Their substitute torch took very little engineering. They painted a wooden chair leg silver and fixed an empty plum-pudding tin to the top. Into the tin went old underwear soaked in fuel. Set that alight, and from a distance they had something that resembled a torch.

They had a runner too: a student wearing the white clothes of an athlete. Another friend dressed in an air-force reserve uniform to look like an escort. Barry wasn't the runner. He was wearing ordinary trousers and a tie.

Their first few steps were hardly convincing. People watching the procession understood it was a joke. According to Larkin's later account, even nearby police laughed.

Then the runner swung his arms so hard that the burning underwear flew out of the tin. He dropped the torch and fled.

Peter Gralton went to recover the flaming material. He told Barry to pick up the torch and run. In Larkin's telling, Peter even gave him a shove to get him moving.

So the least convincing-looking athlete became the face of the relay. Barry ran in his tie, holding a painted chair leg topped with a burning tin.

And something changed around him. The crowd closed in, more spectators treated him as the expected runner, and real police officers began clearing a way for him. A prank that had started with people laughing now had the protection of the authorities it was fooling.

The escort delivered Barry to Sydney Town Hall, where the mayor had been preparing for Harry Dillon. The news that a torchbearer was arriving early sent Hills outside before he had finished dressing for the ceremony.

He accepted the homemade torch. Contemporary accounts and later recollections describe silver paint still wet on it. The mayor began his prepared welcome without stopping to inspect the object he had been handed.

Barry didn't stay for a photograph or an explanation. As the speech continued, he walked down the steps, slipped through the crowd and boarded a tram back toward college.

An organiser interrupted the mayor and explained that this was not the official flame. Hills had to tell the crowd they had just watched a university prank.

That could have been the whole joke. But there was still a real torchbearer on the road.

About ten minutes after the fake delivery, Harry Dillon arrived. According to later accounts, the crowd was unsettled and the real runner needed help getting through. The official relay had to resume after the fake one had already received its welcome.

Barry was not punished. In an interview decades later, he recalled his college rector congratulating him the next morning. His classmates applauded when he appeared for an exam. Barry also recalled failing that exam.

The students hadn't taken the Olympic flame. They had delivered an entirely different object to the right place, just early enough for the police, the crowd and the mayor to complete the ceremony for them.
