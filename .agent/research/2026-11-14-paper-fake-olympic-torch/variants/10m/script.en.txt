What does an Olympic torchbearer need to convince an entire city? A uniform? A real flame? Or simply the right people escorting him? In Sydney in 1956, a student arrived at the mayor's reception carrying something made from a chair leg and a pudding tin. Why did the officials welcome it? And how did the genuine torchbearer get through a ceremony that had already happened?

The Olympic Games that year were going to Melbourne. Australia had never hosted them before. The flame's long relay across the country was meant to carry the excitement toward the opening ceremony. On its way south it would pass through Sydney, where the mayor had a formal part to play.

The plan was uncomplicated. Cross-country runner Harry Dillon would arrive with the torch at Sydney Town Hall. Lord Mayor Pat Hills would receive it, speak to the assembled crowd and send the flame on with the next runner. A ceremony like this depends on several people doing their part in the right order.

A group of students at the University of Sydney had decided to test that order. They did not need to intercept the real flame. They only needed to arrive first with something that looked enough like it.

Barry Larkin later described the mood behind the prank. He and his friends thought too much reverence surrounded the relay. Larkin also objected to the fact that the modern Olympic torch relay had been introduced for the 1936 Games in Berlin, where the Nazi regime had used Olympic spectacle for propaganda. That was his retrospective explanation, not a reason we can automatically assign to every student in the group.

Their preparation was gloriously improvised. They found a wooden chair leg, gave it silver paint and attached an empty plum-pudding tin. Instead of an elaborate burner, the tin held old underwear soaked in fuel. Larkin later said he thought the fuel was kerosene. They lit the contents and had a flame.

The idea was crude, but the ceremony was taking place outside, on a public route, among spectators waiting for a moving torch. The impostor would not need to survive a careful examination for long. He would just need to be seen running toward the Town Hall.

The students also thought about the people around their counterfeit. One would wear a white top and shorts, like an athlete. Another wore a reserve air-force uniform and posed as a motorcycle escort. The uniform was part of their performance; it was not an official appointment.

Barry himself was not supposed to be the torchbearer. He turned up in ordinary trousers, a shirt and a tie. Nothing about that outfit made him look like the planned runner.

Accounts of what happened next were told years afterward, and the smallest details do not always agree. But Larkin's own recollection identifies an accident that changed the entire prank.

At first, the students weren't fooling everybody. Their chosen runner began moving with the burning homemade torch, and spectators could see he was joking. Larkin recalled that police nearby laughed too. This was a stunt, not yet a mistaken official procession.

Then the runner swung his arms too enthusiastically. The burning underwear flew out of the tin. The student abandoned the torch and ran away.

The plan had lost both its flame and its athlete, right in the middle of the crowd.

Peter Gralton, another member of the group, retrieved the burning material. Larkin recalled Peter telling him to take the torch and run, and giving him a forceful push to start. Barry did as he was told.

Now the torchbearer was an unplanned substitute, wearing trousers and a tie, with a painted chair leg in his hand.

The result was stranger than the original plan. As Barry moved into the crowd, the people around him stopped responding as if he were just another student playing a trick. The spectators closed around him and treated him as the expected runner.

The fake military-style outrider had helped create the impression of an organised procession. But the most important change came when real police officers started escorting Barry and clearing his path.

Larkin later remembered the noise and flashes from cameras. He was still holding the same improbable contraption. But to someone watching from behind the crowd, what stood out was a man running with a flame while police made way for him.

The students had made the object. The crowd and the authorities were now making the scene look official.

At the Town Hall, Pat Hills had been preparing to receive Harry Dillon at the scheduled time. Someone brought word that a torchbearer was arriving early. Hills hurried out before he had finished putting on his ceremonial clothing.

He received the torch from Barry. The silver paint was reportedly still wet, a detail that makes the mistake especially remarkable. The mayor began his welcome without properly inspecting the thing in his hand.

And that was the decisive moment. The prank was no longer simply a student running down a street. An official had accepted the counterfeit during the very ceremony intended for the real flame.

Barry didn't wait around to see what happened. He turned from the steps, walked back through the crowd and took a tram toward college.

While the mayor was speaking, the organiser of the relay realised something was wrong. Accounts name Marcus Marsden as the man who stopped Hills and explained that this was not the Olympic torch.

Hills then had to announce the hoax to the people who had just watched him receive it. The mayor had welcomed a homemade flame and then told his audience that the welcome had been a mistake.

But the ceremony could not simply be abandoned. The actual runner was still approaching Sydney with the real torch.

About ten minutes later, Harry Dillon arrived. The crowd had already seen one supposed torchbearer; now it was being asked to recognise the real one. Later accounts describe people becoming unsettled and police having to clear a way for Dillon to pass.

The relay continued. The authentic flame was not stolen, and the Olympics were not derailed. What failed was the city's ability to recognise its own ceremony before it took place.

For Larkin, the aftermath was unexpectedly gentle. He said he escaped punishment. In a later interview he remembered his college rector congratulating him at breakfast. His fellow students applauded him when he turned up for an examination.

He also remembered failing the exam. There was no need to invent a heroic escape or a furious courtroom: the public embarrassment had been the main consequence.

The fake torch itself had a much less public ending. According to a later account from fellow participant John Lawler, it remained under his bed for years until his mother threw it away while cleaning. The object that had fooled a mayor did not end up in a museum in this telling.

The story survived without it. In 2000, as Sydney hosted its own Olympic Games, newspapers revisited the old stunt. Organisers took precautions along that year's torch route to discourage imitators. The official flame still attracted attempts at interference, but no identical imitation reached the mayor.

There is a temptation to call this a story about an obviously foolish official. Yet the order of events is more revealing. The first runner was so unconvincing that people laughed. When he dropped the torch, the students' plan nearly ended. Barry, dressed even less like an athlete, took over by accident.

Only then did the crowd close around him and real officers provide an escort. The mayor's mistake came last, after the scene outside already looked legitimate.

A chair leg and a burning tin should never have passed as the Olympic torch. They did for long enough because the people around them supplied every other part of the ceremony.

And when Harry Dillon finally arrived with the genuine flame, Sydney had to welcome an Olympic torchbearer for the second time.
