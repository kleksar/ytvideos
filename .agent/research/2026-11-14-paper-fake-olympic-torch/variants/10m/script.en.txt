Why did Sydney's mayor receive the wrong Olympic torch in 1956? How did the student carrying it end up in the company of real police officers? And what happened to the athlete who was supposed to arrive?

Australia was hosting the Olympics for the first time. The flame had reached Cairns and was travelling south towards Melbourne. At each stop a different runner carried it onward. In Sydney, runners had even practised carrying heavy household objects, according to a later account by historian Harry Blutstein. They had an unusual race to prepare for: their job was to carry a flame, not to cross a finish line first.

Harry Dillon was due to bring the torch to Sydney Town Hall. Lord Mayor Pat Hills had prepared a speech. After receiving the flame, he was to pass it to another runner, Bert Button. Dillon and Button each had a part in a much longer relay. In between them, the city would hold its ceremony.

For some students at the University of Sydney, the excitement surrounding the ceremony was an irresistible subject for a joke. Their classes and exams were still going on. Barry Larkin was one of the students, but he had no part in the real Olympic relay.

Barry Larkin later described the mood behind the prank. He and his friends thought too much reverence surrounded the relay. Larkin also objected to the fact that the modern Olympic torch relay had been introduced for the 1936 Games in Berlin, where the Nazi regime had used Olympic spectacle for propaganda. That was his retrospective explanation, not a reason we can automatically assign to every student in the group.

The students turned a wooden chair leg and an empty pudding tin into a comically crude imitation. The tin contained burning underwear. Years later Larkin could still remember those improbable objects, although he was no longer sure which fuel they had used.

Meanwhile, the real relay was carrying on through the city. The students were not athletes from that relay. The chair leg belonged to a completely separate performance.

The students also thought about the people around their counterfeit. One would wear a white top and shorts, like an athlete. Another wore a reserve air-force uniform and posed as a motorcycle escort. The uniform was part of their performance; it was not an official appointment.

Barry came in his ordinary clothes, including a necktie. He had not expected to be the one carrying the torch.

Accounts of what happened next were told years afterward, and the smallest details do not always agree. But Larkin's own recollection identifies an accident that changed the entire prank.

At first, the students weren't fooling everybody. Their chosen runner began moving with the burning homemade torch, and spectators could see he was joking. Larkin recalled that police nearby laughed too. This was a stunt, not yet a mistaken official procession.

Then the runner swung his arms too enthusiastically. The burning underwear flew out of the tin. The student abandoned the torch and ran away.

The first attempt had collapsed in front of the people who had been watching it. Yet the students did not all leave.

Peter Gralton, another member of the group, retrieved the burning material. Larkin recalled Peter telling him to take the torch and run, and giving him a forceful push to start. Barry did as he was told.

Barry now held the torch. The student who had come along without an athlete's outfit suddenly found himself in the middle of the performance.

The result was stranger than the original plan. As Barry moved into the crowd, the people around him stopped responding as if he were just another student playing a trick. The spectators closed around him and treated him as the expected runner.

The fake military-style outrider had helped create the impression of an organised procession. But the most important change came when real police officers started escorting Barry and clearing his path.

Larkin recalled being surrounded by spectators. Some of the people further away would have been seeing the procession for the first time; the earlier failed attempt was no longer in front of them.

Meanwhile, Harry Dillon was still on the genuine relay. Two men carrying entirely different objects were now associated with the same civic welcome.

At the Town Hall, Pat Hills had been preparing to receive Harry Dillon at the scheduled time. Someone brought word that a torchbearer was arriving early. Hills hurried out before he had finished putting on his ceremonial clothing.

He received the torch from Barry. The silver paint was reportedly still wet, a detail that makes the mistake especially remarkable. The mayor began his welcome without properly inspecting the thing in his hand.

The welcome for the athlete Harry Dillon had become a welcome for a student. And Bert Button was still waiting for the actual relay to reach him.

Barry didn't wait around to see what happened. He turned from the steps, walked back through the crowd and took a tram toward college.

While the mayor was speaking, the organiser of the relay realised something was wrong. Accounts name Marcus Marsden as the man who stopped Hills and explained that this was not the Olympic torch.

Hills had to tell the crowd that the welcome had been a mistake. According to Blutstein's account, he returned inside to finish getting dressed for the genuine ceremony.

The organiser who had corrected the mayor was not a complete stranger to Barry. In his 2004 interview, Larkin remembered playing football with Marc Marsden and credited him with helping him avoid punishment. That is Larkin's own explanation, rather than a proven account of an official decision.

About ten minutes later, Harry Dillon arrived. The crowd had already seen one supposed torchbearer; now it was being asked to recognise the real one. Later accounts describe people becoming unsettled and police having to clear a way for Dillon to pass.

The real ceremony could go ahead at last. The flame continued its journey towards Melbourne. Nothing in the prank had interrupted the continuity of the Olympic flame itself.

For Larkin, the aftermath was unexpectedly gentle. He said he escaped punishment. In a later interview he remembered his college rector congratulating him at breakfast. His fellow students applauded him when he turned up for an examination.

The contrast between the two mornings was quite remarkable. One day he had unexpectedly become the centre of a public ceremony. The next day he was back in the ordinary life of a student, sitting an examination. He remembered failing it.

The fake torch itself had a much less public ending. According to a later account from fellow participant John Lawler, it remained under his bed for years until his mother threw it away while cleaning. The object that had fooled a mayor did not end up in a museum in this telling.

Larkin went on to become a veterinary surgeon. Decades later, when a journalist contacted him about the Games, he still had that failed examination to talk about. And in 2018 Blutstein wrote about the hoax again when Larkin returned to Sydney for a documentary. The student had long since left college, but the city still remembered the day he arrived before the official runner.
