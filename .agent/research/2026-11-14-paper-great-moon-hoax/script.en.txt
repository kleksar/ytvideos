Bat-winged people living on the Moon. That was the discovery a New York newspaper announced in 1835.

The Sun ran six installments, revealing more of this supposed scientific report over the following days.

It credited John Herschel, a real astronomer working in South Africa. His name gave the story weight.

The supposed eyewitness was Herschel's assistant, Andrew Grant. Except Grant was invented.

The articles claimed to come from an Edinburgh science journal. That journal had stopped publishing years earlier.

The paper described a giant telescope with a lens twenty-four feet across. That machine didn't exist either.

First came lunar forests, bison-like animals and strange little goats. Each report revealed more.

Then came beavers that walked upright and carried their young in their arms.

On the fourth day, the report described flying, bat-winged people. It even gave them a scientific name.

Other newspapers reprinted the claims. By the end of the week, some were calling the whole thing a hoax.

The last installment added an accident: sunlight supposedly passed through the enormous lens and set part of the observatory on fire.

The fire was made up. So were the telescope, the assistant, and the animals Herschel supposedly discovered.

A Sun journalist, Richard Adams Locke, denied writing the reports at the time. Years later, he acknowledged doing it.

The Sun never retracted the story. But illustrations of its imaginary Moon creatures have survived.
