In 1835, a New York newspaper announced that people with bat wings lived on the Moon.

The Sun credited John Herschel, a famous astronomer who really was studying the sky in South Africa.

Its report supposedly came from a witness named Andrew Grant and a scientific journal in Edinburgh. Grant was invented, and the journal had stopped publishing.

First the paper described a telescope with a lens twenty-four feet across. Through it, the imaginary astronomers claimed to see forests and herds of animals resembling bison.

Then came beavers walking on two legs with their young in their arms.

By the fourth installment, the paper was describing flying people and giving them a scientific name.

Other newspapers began reprinting the discoveries. But on August twenty-ninth, rivals were already questioning the story. The Sun still had more installments to publish.

Its final report claimed that sunlight had passed through the giant lens and started a fire in the observatory. The fire was fiction, just like the telescope and its discoveries.

On the day that report appeared, Sun journalist Richard Adams Locke wrote to a rival paper denying authorship. Years later, he acknowledged writing the series and described it as satire.

The Sun never printed a straightforward retraction. The reports were republished as pamphlets, and pictures of the creatures were sold separately. The original newspaper series hadn't contained any illustrations.

The discovery had only happened in print. So what was the remarkable news that started it all?
