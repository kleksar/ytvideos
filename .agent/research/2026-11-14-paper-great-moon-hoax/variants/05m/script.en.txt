How did a New York newspaper convince readers that people with bat wings lived on the Moon? And why did the story keep travelling even after other newspapers called it a hoax?

In August 1835, the Sun was a one-cent paper. Its readers could follow the news, a fictional tale, and a report of scientific discoveries in the same small daily edition.

On August twenty-fifth, the paper began a six-part account of discoveries supposedly made by John Herschel, a famous British astronomer working in South Africa. Herschel really was there, studying the southern sky.

But the report did not come from him.

The supposed author was Andrew Grant, described as a companion of Herschel. Grant was invented. The reports claimed to have been copied from the Edinburgh Journal of Science, a real publication that had already ceased publication.

A genuine astronomer, a journal that sounded respectable, and a witness who did not exist: the story had built its scientific credentials before showing readers anything on the Moon.

Then it introduced the machine. The Sun described a telescope with a lens twenty-four feet across, powerful enough, it claimed, to see tiny details on the lunar surface. Herschel had a real telescope in South Africa, but this extraordinary new instrument was fiction.

And here was the turn. Once the imaginary telescope was accepted, its imaginary discoveries could grow from day to day.

First the articles described vegetation, strange landscapes, and herds resembling bison. Next came single-horned goats. Then beavers that walked upright and carried their young in their arms.

On the fourth installment, the newspaper reached its headline-making discovery: human-shaped beings with wings like bats. It even supplied the scientific-sounding name Vespertilio-homo.

The claims were being reprinted elsewhere. Some readers believed them. But others were already asking how a telescope of such astonishing size could have been built without the world hearing about it.

By August twenty-ninth, rival newspapers were publicly calling the reports a hoax. The Moon story did not simply move from universal belief to sudden exposure. Belief and disbelief ran alongside each other while the installments continued.

The final part supplied an ending inside the fiction. Sunlight, it said, had passed through that enormous lens and set part of the observatory on fire, interrupting the work. Even the reason the discoveries stopped had been invented.

Suspicion also fell on Richard Adams Locke, a journalist at the Sun. He denied authorship at the time. Years later, he acknowledged writing the series and described it as satire. His target was speculation about inhabited worlds dressed up as certain science.

Yet the Sun did not publish a straightforward retraction. The story circulated in reprints and pamphlets; pictures of its creatures were made and sold.

The telescope was imaginary. So was the witness. But the newspaper, the competing criticism, and those printed Moon creatures were real. In one surviving image, the winged figures remain exactly where this extraordinary discovery actually happened: on paper.
