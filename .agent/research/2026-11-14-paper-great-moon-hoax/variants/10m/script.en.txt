How could a newspaper report life on the Moon without sending anyone there? Why did rival editors begin doubting the discovery before the story had even ended? And why did pictures of its imaginary inhabitants outlive the supposed evidence?

New York, August 1835. The Sun was a one-cent newspaper, competing for readers who could find local events, entertainment, and accounts of the newest scientific discoveries in its pages.

Scientific news could sound almost unbelievable then. Astronomers were charting the sky. John Herschel, the British astronomer whose father had made important discoveries, had travelled to the Cape of Good Hope to study stars in the southern hemisphere.

That journey was real. It made his name particularly useful to a newspaper writer.

On August twenty-fifth, the Sun began printing what it presented as discoveries from Herschel's South African observatory. The installments would run six times, with each part adding something the previous one had not revealed.

The first report did not immediately present a flying lunar person. It began by making the discovery appear technically possible. That was the foundation on which every later claim would rest.

Readers were told that the report came from an Edinburgh science journal and was written by Andrew Grant, a companion of Herschel. The Edinburgh Journal of Science had existed, but had ceased publication. Grant, the supposed eyewitness, had never existed at all.

The story borrowed a real scientific reputation and attached it to an invented chain of reporting. If you trusted the author, perhaps you would trust the apparatus. If you trusted the apparatus, perhaps you would trust what it supposedly revealed.

Then the newspaper described an extraordinary telescope. Its imagined lens was twenty-four feet wide and weighed about seven tons. The article wrapped the machine in technical descriptions. Herschel did own a real reflecting telescope at the Cape, but it was not this enormous invention.

Another small part of the fictional scientific bureaucracy was equally revealing. The account named Britain's Board of Longitude, which had already been abolished. A reader could check that. A distant astronomer could not instantly correct the New York paper from South Africa.

The main turn came when the instrument stopped being a scientific claim and became a window into an inhabited world.

The article moved across lunar terrain: forests, a pale beach, strange formations. At first it offered animal-like shapes, including herds compared to bison. These were not sightings by Herschel; they were descriptions created for the newspaper.

The next reports raised the stakes. There were goat-like creatures with single horns and beavers that moved on two legs, carrying their young. The accumulation mattered. Every new installment treated the previous impossible observation as established fact.

Meanwhile, other newspapers began reprinting the series. Readers elsewhere could encounter the supposed evidence without seeing the entire sequence that had created it.

By the fourth installment, on August twenty-eighth, the article introduced human-shaped beings with wings stretching like those of bats. The writer supplied anatomical detail and the label Vespertilio-homo.

Now the story had crossed a line. A fantastic claim had been dressed as an observation, and the scientific words made it easy to repeat as a discovery.

But the claim was not accepted by everybody.

Some rival editors had noticed how much the report asked them to believe. How had Herschel transported such a machine? Why had no other scientific publication announced its construction? Why was a supposedly Scottish journal suddenly producing news from a publication that was no longer appearing?

On August twenty-ninth, newspapers including the New York Evening Post and the Gloucester Telegraph publicly raised doubts. Some called the story a hoax. They did so while the Sun still had further installments to publish.

That timing is important. The famous story is sometimes reduced to an entire public fooled by a newspaper and then enlightened by a confession. The contemporary debate was messier. Newspapers argued over the reports in real time. Readers could encounter belief, disbelief, and satire in the same week.

The remaining installments did not withdraw the discovery. Instead, they carried the imaginary expedition further. The writer had to bring the story to a stop without admitting that the telescope and its observer were inventions.

So the final installment reported an accident. Sunlight had supposedly passed through the gigantic lens and started a fire at the observatory. The research was interrupted.

Even that convenient ending belonged to the fiction. No enormous lens had set Herschel's observatory alight, because there was no such instrument. The accident explained why readers would not receive endless further reports from the Moon.

At the same time, the question of the real author was moving from the paper's pages into its rivals.

The journalist Richard Adams Locke was suspected. On August thirty-first, the day of the last installment, he wrote to the Evening Post denying that he was responsible. The form of that denial became part of the controversy.

Years later, Locke acknowledged writing the series. He said he had meant it as satire, taking aim at writers who spoke confidently about inhabited worlds without evidence.

One of the prominent voices of that era was Thomas Dick, a Scottish writer who argued for life on other celestial bodies. This was not a random new obsession invented by the Sun. The newspaper's fantastic discovery appeared in a culture already willing to debate life beyond Earth.

That makes the satire's outcome more complicated. Locke could present the tales as a criticism of overconfident speculation. But whatever his stated intention, the Sun had printed them as scientific discoveries under a famous man's name. His explanation did not turn the printed reports into real observations.

The newspaper never offered a straightforward retraction. Instead, the story continued to travel.

It was copied into other newspapers and printed as pamphlets. Illustrations of the lunar creatures appeared in separate prints and later editions. These were real physical objects, although the creatures on them had never been seen through a telescope.

The argument also produced its own joke. In September, a Philadelphia newspaper published a mock advertisement offering plots of land in the newly discovered lunar territory. The hoax was already becoming something people could parody, not just believe.

Edgar Allan Poe, who earlier that year had written a fictional lunar voyage presented like a report, suspected that the Sun story had borrowed from his work. His complaint became another part of the story's newspaper afterlife. We cannot establish that Locke copied Poe merely from their resemblance.

The contrast is striking. Herschel carried out actual astronomical work in South Africa. At almost the same moment, a fabricated companion, a defunct journal, and an impossible instrument were reporting discoveries in his name thousands of miles away.

The Sun supplied a new claim on each day. Competitors challenged it before the series was over. The writer denied his involvement, then later identified the piece as satire. Copies and pictures continued circulating after the controversy.

So what survives of the great discovery? Not the giant telescope. Not a specimen from the Moon. An actual newspaper series, actual objections from its contemporaries, and engraved images of beings that only ever existed in print.

One of those images shows the winged Moon people. It is evidence of the story's extraordinary journey, but not evidence of life on the Moon.
