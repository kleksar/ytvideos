How did a newspaper turn the work of a real astronomer into a report about people living on the Moon? Why were other editors challenging the discovery before the series ended? And why were the famous pictures of those creatures missing from the original newspaper articles?

New York, August 1835. The Sun was a one-cent newspaper, competing for readers who could find local events, entertainment, and accounts of the newest scientific discoveries in its pages.

Scientific news could sound almost unbelievable then. Astronomers were charting the sky. John Herschel, the British astronomer whose father had made important discoveries, had travelled to the Cape of Good Hope to study stars in the southern hemisphere.

That expedition put the astronomer thousands of miles away from New York. When the newspaper used his name, readers could not simply ask him about the report.

On August twenty-fifth, the Sun began printing what it presented as discoveries from Herschel's South African observatory. The installments would run six times, with each part adding something the previous one had not revealed.

The first report did not immediately present a flying lunar person. It began by making the discovery appear technically possible. That was the foundation on which every later claim would rest.

Readers were told that the report came from an Edinburgh science journal and was written by Andrew Grant, a companion of Herschel. The Edinburgh Journal of Science had existed, but had ceased publication. Grant, the supposed eyewitness, had never existed at all.

No letter from Herschel was needed to make the report look authentic. The paper had supplied a chain of references in his place, starting with a scientific journal that many educated readers could recognize.

Then the newspaper described an extraordinary telescope. Its imagined lens was twenty-four feet wide and weighed about seven tons. The article wrapped the machine in technical descriptions. Herschel did own a real reflecting telescope at the Cape, but it was not this enormous invention.

Another small part of the fictional scientific bureaucracy was equally revealing. The account named Britain's Board of Longitude, which had already been abolished. A reader could check that. A distant astronomer could not instantly correct the New York paper from South Africa.

The report put that enormous instrument to work. Its author could now describe things on the Moon that no ordinary reader could check through a telescope.

The article moved across lunar terrain: forests, a pale beach, strange formations. At first it offered animal-like shapes, including herds compared to bison. These were not sightings by Herschel; they were descriptions created for the newspaper.

The next reports introduced single-horned goats and beavers walking upright, with their young held in their arms. These details made the fictional Moon resemble a place where animals could be watched going about their daily lives.

Meanwhile, other newspapers began reprinting the series. Readers elsewhere could encounter the supposed evidence without seeing the entire sequence that had created it.

By the fourth installment, on August twenty-eighth, the article introduced human-shaped beings with wings stretching like those of bats. The writer supplied anatomical detail and the label Vespertilio-homo.

The columns could now offer something far more startling than a strange animal. Yet there was no picture of the flying people in the original series. The discovery was carried entirely by words.

But the claim was not accepted by everybody.

Rival editors had a way to test the account without seeing the Moon at all. They could ask about the apparatus and the publication that supposedly carried the report, rather than about the strange creatures themselves.

On August twenty-ninth, newspapers including the New York Evening Post and the Gloucester Telegraph publicly raised doubts. Some called the story a hoax. They did so while the Sun still had further installments to publish.

So a reader following the competing papers could encounter the accusation of fraud before finding out how the imaginary expedition ended. The criticism did not stop the Sun from publishing its remaining reports.

The remaining installments carried the imaginary expedition further. The series was about to end, but its characters still had a working telescope inside the story.

So the final installment reported an accident. Sunlight had supposedly passed through the gigantic lens and started a fire at the observatory. The research was interrupted.

The fire belonged entirely to the invented report. It interrupted the observations within the story; the real Herschel's telescope was not the huge instrument described by the Sun.

At the same time, the question of the real author was moving from the paper's pages into its rivals.

The journalist Richard Adams Locke was suspected. On August thirty-first, the day of the last installment, he wrote to the Evening Post denying that he was responsible. The form of that denial became part of the controversy.

The paper did not print a straightforward retraction. And in September a Philadelphia paper was already making a joke out of the news, offering imaginary lunar plots for sale in a mock advertisement. You could laugh at the discovery and still have a reason to talk about it.

By then the reports were taking on a new physical form. Printers put the newspaper story into pamphlets. The editions from eighteen thirty-five and eighteen thirty-six that survive include English, French, German and Italian versions; many shortened the original text.

One sixteen-page English pamphlet from 1835 included a woodcut across its middle pages. The Sun also offered separate lithographs for sale. The drawings gave the readers something that the original columns had never provided: an image of the imaginary discoveries.

In 1836 an Italian edition went further, with six color lithographs. Imagine placing that illustrated book beside the original text-only newspaper: the pictures might look like reproductions of scientific observations, but they were created for the printed story.

The real astronomer eventually heard about the reports. According to a historical account from the library that preserves several of these editions, Herschel was astonished at the public's credulity, but eventually took the satire in good humor.

The man who had written the reports remained a question in the meantime. Edgar Allan Poe had published his own fictional lunar voyage earlier that year and suspected the Sun of borrowing his idea. That suspicion was not proof that Locke had copied him.

Five years after the series, Locke admitted in a private letter that he had invented the account. He later explained his work as satire against confident speculation about populated worlds.

One of the writers associated with that debate was Thomas Dick, who argued for inhabitants on other heavenly bodies. That helps explain what Locke said he was attacking. It does not change the fact that a newspaper had presented the invention under Herschel's name.

The pictures continued to travel long after the installments. Today a separate 1835 lithograph can easily be mistaken for an illustration from the original newspaper, precisely because the famous image and the story are so closely associated.

But those are two different objects. The newspaper shows how the supposed discovery was assembled from borrowed authority and invented observations. The later prints show how other people gave that invention a form readers could keep.

A copy of the newspaper and an illustrated pamphlet can still be laid side by side. The first created the Moon people with words. The second gave them wings you could see.
