A thirteen-year-old boy picked up a black LEGO octopus on a beach in Cornwall.

He'd spent two years looking for one. The toy came from a cargo spill twenty-seven years earlier.

In February 1997, a storm hit the cargo ship Tokio Express off England's southwest coast.

Sixty-two containers went overboard. One held nearly five million LEGO pieces, many intended for ocean-themed sets.

There were tiny flippers, scuba tanks and swords. Among them were just 4,200 octopuses.

Nobody knows exactly how the container opened. But LEGO started turning up on Cornwall's beaches.

Tracey Williams began collecting the pieces, then recording where they appeared alongside other plastic washed ashore.

She found an octopus in 1997. Eighteen years passed before she found another.

Some pieces sank. Fishermen brought them up in their nets, while others lay buried in beach sand.

After high tides and strong onshore winds, more pieces appeared. Tracey believes the water uncovers plastic hidden for years.

In April 2024, the boy finally found his octopus at Marazion. He'd already collected hundreds of other pieces.

Two days later, another octopus turned up on a nearby beach.

That August, a fisherman pulled a LEGO shark from his net, twenty miles off Cornwall. Its dorsal fin was missing.

A separate study of other beach-found LEGO estimates that the plastic could last for centuries. Most of the lost cargo is still unaccounted for.
