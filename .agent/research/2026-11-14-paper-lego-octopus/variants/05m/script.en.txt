How can a toy vanish at sea for twenty-seven years and turn up in a teenager's hand? Why were people hunting one particular black octopus? And what could that tiny piece tell us about everything else the ocean kept?

In April 2024, thirteen-year-old Liutauras Cemolonskas walked a beach at Marazion in Cornwall with his family. He had searched for a black LEGO octopus for two years. He had found hundreds of other LEGO pieces, but not this one.

Then he picked up the octopus.

To understand why that mattered, go back to February 1997. The cargo ship Tokio Express was travelling through heavy seas off England's southwest coast. A powerful wave tipped the vessel, and sixty-two shipping containers went overboard.

One of those containers held 4,756,940 LEGO pieces. Many belonged to ocean-themed sets: flippers, diving tanks, cutlasses, rafts and miniature sea creatures. The ship survived. Its cargo did not all make it to shore as cargo.

The strange part was the inventory. There were hundreds of thousands of common pieces, but only 4,200 octopuses. Nobody knows exactly how that container opened. Yet LEGO soon started arriving on Cornish beaches, piece by piece.

Among the early collectors was Tracey Williams. She initially found a bit of plastic LEGO seaweed and took it home. Once people understood the cargo spill, ordinary beach walks became searches for particular shapes. Tracey found an octopus in 1997. By her account, eighteen years went by before she found another.

But why would pieces appear so far apart in time?

Some plastic floats and follows currents. Other pieces sink and end up in fishing nets. Some become trapped in sand or among rocks. Tracey believes strong onshore winds and high spring tides uncover plastic that has been hidden for years. That is an explanation for patterns of finds, not proof of where any single octopus spent its time.

A storm in March 2020 exposed part of a dune at Perran Sands. Beachcombers found hundreds of LEGO pieces from the spill. The cargo had become an accidental record of what a shifting coastline could release.

Liutauras knew what he was looking for. He searched regularly with his parents, collecting 789 LEGO pieces over two years. His father had shared an interest in searching beaches and finding old objects. The black octopus was unusually elusive among the sea-themed pieces.

In April 2024, the search finally paid off at Marazion. Two days later, another octopus was found at Porthleven. Tracey connected that burst of discoveries to high tides and winds, while leaving the precise journey of each toy unknown.

The story could have ended with two lucky beach finds. Then, in August, fisherman Richard West hauled a LEGO shark from his net about twenty miles south of Penzance. Its dorsal fin was missing. Unlike the octopus on the beach, the shark arrived from a fishing ground offshore.

The two discoveries mattered for different reasons. For the teenager, one small piece ended a two-year search. For the people recording the spill, the shark was another clue that some of the lost cargo remained out at sea.

Tracey began cataloguing finds and the other plastic washing ashore. Her collection grew into a record of much more than toys. A separate University of Plymouth study, based on other beach-found LEGO, estimated that some plastic bricks might persist for centuries. It did not measure the age or future life of Liutauras's octopus.

We still do not know where most of that shipment ended up. Twenty-seven years after the containers fell, a boy could hold one missing octopus while a fisherman found a shark far offshore. The treasure hunt had an answer. The larger question of what the sea keeps had not disappeared.
