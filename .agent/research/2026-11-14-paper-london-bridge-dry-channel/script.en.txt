In Arizona, they built London Bridge on dry ground. There was no river underneath.

The original had stood over the River Thames for more than a hundred years.

By the 1960s, London needed a replacement. A developer named Robert McCulloch bought its outer granite stones.

He was building a new town beside Lake Havasu and wanted a landmark that would draw visitors.

Workers in London numbered the blocks, so they would know where each one belonged.

They dismantled the outer stonework and sent it by ship across the ocean.

From California, trucks took it the rest of the way to the desert.

But those stones weren't the new bridge's main support.

Contractors built five concrete spans, using mounds of earth to support the work.

They covered the concrete with the original granite, fitting the stones back into their familiar pattern.

So now there was a bridge standing in the desert. Under its arches: dirt.

The crews dug that dirt out. Then they excavated a channel through the narrow stretch of land.

Water from the lake filled the channel. What had been a peninsula became an island.

In October 1971, the rebuilt bridge was formally dedicated. The old London granite was crossing water again.

And the bridge still carries traffic across that channel today.

Before they dug the channel, though, there was a very different sight beneath those arches.
