## Three finalist pitches
### A · Mona Lisa: police missed what was in the room
Audience: English-speaking viewers of Paper Detour who know the painting but not the 1911 police mistake. Cold start exact: “Four empty iron hooks at the Louvre. Visitors lined up to stare at them. The Mona Lisa was gone.” Why stop: an instantly recognisable artwork disappears and a crowd stares at an empty spot. Why continue: the thief is a former glazier; the panel is carried out; police find the frame; they even question him in the room holding the stolen painting. Payoff: he approaches a dealer, Uffizi verifies the original, police arrest him and it returns in January 1914. Strongest objection: the heist is well known and may seem retold. Mitigation is to organise around the overlooked investigation scene and the documented empty-hooks audience, not suggest a new conspiracy.

### B · Great Stork Derby
Audience: adults intrigued by strange real legal consequences. Cold start: “A Toronto lawyer died and left his fortune to whichever woman had the most babies in the next ten years.” Why stop: the unusual legal condition; next event: the decade-long competition and disputes over what counted; payoff: four mothers tied at nine eligible children and shared the estate. Strongest objection: the subject concerns real mothers and children under Depression-era economic pressures; sensational packaging can dehumanise them, and qualifying court details are complex. Sources verify the beginning/end but this requires more nuanced treatment than a fast joke.

### C · Pet Rock
Audience: light comedy/business-history viewers. Cold start: “In 1975, Gary Dahl put an ordinary rock in a cardboard box and sold it for $3.95.” Why stop: absurd purchase; next event: a manual and pet carrier packaging; payoff: over a million sold in the short fad, later imitators and follow-ups failed. Strongest objection: after “it was a rock,” the mechanism is mostly a well-told joke, with fewer substantial changes after the opening than A or B.

Decision: **A**. B has an arguably stronger first-line shock, but A supports more distinct evidenced developments and a human-scale detective reversal without treating hardship as a gag. B remains strongest alternative. If an independent reviewer feels A is familiar and has no interest after the theft, reject A rather than manufacturing additional suspense. Selection is a hypothesis, not a popularity score.
