In 1911, people went to the Louvre to see four empty hooks. The Mona Lisa was missing.

Vincenzo Peruggia had worked for a company fitting protective glass to paintings in the museum. He knew the building.

On a Monday when the Louvre was closed to visitors, he took Leonardo's portrait. It was painted on wood, so the thief had a whole panel to carry away.

Staff assumed the picture had been taken for photography. By Tuesday, the discarded frame and glass had turned a missing picture into a police case.

Officers questioned former museum workers, including Peruggia. They came to his Paris room and accepted his explanation.

The painting was in a trunk in that very room. The police left without it.

Over two years passed before Peruggia contacted Alfredo Geri, an art dealer in Florence. He offered to return the portrait to Italy, and expected payment.

He said the painting belonged in Italy. But Leonardo himself had brought it to France centuries earlier.

Geri consulted the director of the Uffizi Gallery, Giovanni Poggi. They examined the panel, confirmed it was genuine and alerted the authorities.

Peruggia was arrested. In January 1914, the painting was back in the Louvre.

By then, the theft had spread its image through newspapers and postcards far beyond the museum. Crowds could finally see the painting again. For a while, though, the Louvre had been drawing visitors to something that wasn't there.
