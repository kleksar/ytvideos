Why did visitors queue to see four empty hooks in the Louvre? And how could the police question the man who stole the Mona Lisa without finding the painting in his room?

In 1911 the Mona Lisa was admired, but it was not yet the worldwide celebrity it is today. It hung among other Renaissance paintings in a vast museum where staff regularly moved works for photography or cleaning. That ordinary practice would buy a thief an extraordinary amount of time.

Vincenzo Peruggia was an Italian tradesman living in Paris. He had worked for a glazing company that installed protective glass around paintings in the Louvre. He knew the building, and he knew what a worker looked like there: a white smock.

On Monday, August 21, the museum was closed to visitors. Peruggia removed the small portrait from its place in the Salon Carré. On a service staircase, its frame and protective glass were separated from the painting. The treasure he carried away was not a rolled canvas. Leonardo had painted it on a wooden panel.

For more than a day, nobody raised the alarm. On Tuesday, an artist noticed the missing portrait. At first, the staff assumed the museum's photographers had taken it. When that explanation failed, the empty frame and glass found near the stairs turned an absent picture into a police investigation.

The Louvre closed while officers searched. Newspapers spread the news far beyond Paris. When the gallery reopened, people came to stare at the blank wall. Four iron hooks became an attraction because they marked the place where the Mona Lisa should have been.

Police interviewed museum employees and former contractors. In November, they reached Peruggia's rented room. He told them he had learned of the theft from the newspapers and explained why he had been late to work on the day it happened.

They accepted his account. What they did not discover was the stolen panel concealed in a wooden trunk in that same room. Investigators had reached the right man and walked away.

The search took stranger turns. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire and the painter Pablo Picasso came under suspicion because of a separate case involving small objects taken from the Louvre. Neither was the Mona Lisa thief. They were released, while Peruggia still had the painting.

For twenty-eight months it stayed out of sight. Public sightings and theories did not bring it back. What finally broke the case was not a brilliant Paris detective, but a letter from the thief himself.

In late 1913, Peruggia contacted Alfredo Geri, an art dealer in Florence, presenting himself under another name and offering to bring the Mona Lisa to Italy. He claimed he wanted to return a stolen Italian treasure to its homeland. The claim concealed an important historical mistake: Leonardo had taken the painting to France himself. Peruggia's stated patriotism was never proof of his only motive.

Geri did not simply accept the story. He involved Giovanni Poggi, director of Florence's Uffizi Gallery. They examined the panel and established that this was the real portrait, not another rumour or a copy. The authorities were alerted, and Peruggia was arrested in December 1913.

The painting was shown briefly in Italy before returning to the Louvre on January 4, 1914. The man who had slipped past museum security was caught after he approached someone who could identify the work.

The theft did more than interrupt the painting's display. Newspapers, cartoons and crowds turned its absence into a story followed across countries. By the time the Mona Lisa returned, many people knew its face who had never thought to seek it out before. And for weeks in Paris, the most famous sight in its gallery had been four empty hooks.
