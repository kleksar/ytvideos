Why would thousands of people visit the Louvre to look at an empty wall? How did the world's most recognisable portrait travel from a French museum to a wooden trunk in a rented room? And why did detectives leave that room without searching the place where the painting lay hidden?

The answers begin before the Mona Lisa became the celebrity it is now. In 1911, the Louvre held an enormous collection in hundreds of rooms. Leonardo's portrait was respected, but visitors were not yet lining up for a glimpse of it. Some other artworks attracted more attention. Security arrangements also belonged to a very different age: objects could be removed for photography, and a worker in the galleries did not automatically look suspicious.

A threat concerning the Mona Lisa had helped prompt the museum to protect valuable paintings with glass. The contractor responsible for such work employed an Italian glazier named Vincenzo Peruggia. He helped install protective fittings and became familiar with the museum. He was not a mysterious stranger discovering its corridors for the first time.

On Monday, August 21, 1911, the Louvre was closed to the visiting public. Staff and contractors still had reasons to be inside. Peruggia wore a white worker's smock, the ordinary clothing that allowed him to pass among them. In the Salon Carré, he removed the Mona Lisa from its place. The picture was small enough to carry once separated from its frame and protective glass.

That distinction matters. The original Mona Lisa is painted on a wooden panel, not canvas that could be rolled into a tube. Peruggia had to take the actual panel out of the building. The discarded frame and glass were later found by a service staircase. The painting itself had vanished.

If a famous painting disappears, you might expect guards to notice immediately. No one did. It was not until Tuesday that an artist looking for the picture drew attention to the empty space. Staff initially assumed it had been removed for photography, a normal museum practice. Only when that explanation failed did they recognise a theft.

The Louvre shut its doors while the police searched. Then the story escaped the building. Newspaper headlines spread from Paris to other countries. When the museum reopened after about a week, visitors crowded into the gallery, not because the Mona Lisa had returned, but because it had not. A bare section of wall and four iron hooks marked the place where it should have been.

The thief had accidentally created a public event that was far larger than a missing museum label. There were cartoons and satirical postcards. Even the writer Franz Kafka and his companion Max Brod joined the curious visitors. The empty hooks became an image people could understand without knowing art history.

And yet the investigation still needed to locate a very real wooden panel. Police interviewed guards, workers and past employees. Peruggia belonged on that list. In November, investigators questioned him in the rented Paris room where he lived. His employer had reported that he came late to work on the day of the theft. Peruggia explained that he had overslept and claimed he only knew of the robbery from the papers.

The explanation satisfied the police. They did not find the Mona Lisa, concealed in a wooden trunk in that very room. This is the central failure of the case: detectives had found a man who knew the building, had handled its protective glass and had an explanation to give. They left without identifying him as the thief.

Meanwhile, a more glamorous line of suspicion occupied the public. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested, and the young painter Pablo Picasso was questioned. That trail came from another affair involving small sculptures stolen from the Louvre. It did not establish that either man had taken Leonardo's portrait. The suspicion collapsed, and both men were released.

For Peruggia, the attention was a problem. The painting was now so well known that simply offering it for sale was no ordinary transaction. Reports sent the missing masterpiece to faraway countries, but the wooden panel was still in Paris. Months passed. Then more months. By the time the case broke, the painting had been missing for twenty-eight months.

The search had reached an unusual dead end: police had visited the room where the answer lay, and the public could recognise the picture everywhere except in the place it actually was. What would finally expose Peruggia was his own attempt to decide what to do with the artwork.

Late in 1913 he contacted Alfredo Geri, a dealer in Florence, using the name V. Leonard. Geri had been seeking works of art. The stranger claimed he possessed the missing Mona Lisa and wanted to bring it back to Italy. It could easily have been another hoax. There were plenty of rumours and false leads surrounding the case.

Geri took the message seriously enough to involve Giovanni Poggi, the director of Florence's Uffizi Gallery. That changed the problem. A dealer could listen to an offer, but the director could help determine whether the thing offered was actually Leonardo's painting. Peruggia travelled from Paris to Florence with his trunk, carrying the original into reach of people who could inspect it.

When Geri and Poggi examined the panel, the search finally had evidence stronger than a story in a newspaper. They recognised and authenticated the painting. The work that detectives had failed to find in Paris was now physically present before two people who understood what it was. The authorities were notified, and Peruggia was arrested in December 1913.

Why had he done it? Peruggia maintained that he wanted to return an Italian masterpiece to its homeland. He apparently believed it had been taken to France through Napoleon's looting. But Leonardo himself had brought the portrait to France in the sixteenth century. Its presence in the French royal collection did not begin with Napoleon stealing it. And Peruggia had approached a dealer in circumstances involving money. His declared patriotism is a documented explanation he offered, not proof of everything he intended.

That uncertainty matters because the thief's version of events became part of the public story. He was tried in Italy in 1914. The court found him guilty, though the patriotic argument contributed to a relatively lenient outcome. He served only months in prison. None of that altered the fact that the painting had been stolen and concealed for more than two years.

Before the portrait went back to France, it was displayed in Italian cities, allowing crowds there to see the recovered original. It returned to the Louvre on January 4, 1914. The police investigation ended because the thief had contacted someone who did not treat the offer as an ordinary sale.

The return brought another wave of publicity. The work already had a remarkable artistic history, but the theft pushed it into newspapers, songs, jokes and photographs for people who might never have entered an art gallery. That does not mean nobody knew the Mona Lisa before 1911, or that this single event explains all its fame. It does explain why the portrait's absence became a public spectacle, and why its recovery felt like international news.

Think of the two images at either end of the story. First, police in a small Paris room, unaware that a wooden trunk contained the object they wanted. Then a crowd in the world's great museum, staring at an empty wall because everyone knew what had been there. Peruggia concealed the painting for twenty-eight months. He could not conceal the attention its disappearance created.
