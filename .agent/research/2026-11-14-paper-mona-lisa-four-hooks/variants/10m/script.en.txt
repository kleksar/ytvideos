Why did people queue to see an empty wall in the Louvre? How could the police question the thief without recovering the Mona Lisa? And how did an art dealer in Italy finally get the painting out of his hands?

The answers begin before the Mona Lisa became the celebrity it is now. In 1911, the Louvre held an enormous collection in hundreds of rooms. Leonardo's portrait was respected, but visitors were not yet lining up for a glimpse of it. Some other artworks attracted more attention. Security arrangements also belonged to a very different age: objects could be removed for photography, and a worker in the galleries did not automatically look suspicious.

A threat concerning the Mona Lisa had helped prompt the museum to protect valuable paintings with glass. The contractor responsible for such work employed an Italian glazier named Vincenzo Peruggia. He helped install protective fittings and became familiar with the museum. He was not a mysterious stranger discovering its corridors for the first time.

On Monday, August 21, 1911, the Louvre was closed to the visiting public. Staff and contractors still had reasons to be inside. Peruggia wore a white worker's smock, the ordinary clothing that allowed him to pass among them. In the Salon Carré, he removed the Mona Lisa from its place. The picture was small enough to carry once separated from its frame and protective glass.

That distinction matters. The original Mona Lisa is painted on a wooden panel, not canvas that could be rolled into a tube. Peruggia had to take the actual panel out of the building. The discarded frame and glass were later found by a service staircase. The painting itself had vanished.

The first reports of the theft tell us something about the confusion inside the museum. In the Manchester Guardian on August 23, the Paris correspondent reported that the absence may already have been noticed on Monday evening, but staff thought the picture had been taken for photography. By Tuesday the missing frame and glass had become evidence of a crime. The ordinary business of the museum had delayed recognition that the painting was gone.

The Louvre shut its doors while the police searched. Then the story escaped the building. Newspaper headlines spread from Paris to other countries. When the museum reopened after about a week, visitors crowded into the gallery, not because the Mona Lisa had returned, but because it had not. A bare section of wall and four iron hooks marked the place where it should have been.

The newspapers carried the image even to people who could not get into the museum. A report dated August 26 described shop windows displaying prints of the portrait and vendors selling postcards in the streets. When the Louvre finally reopened, even the writer Franz Kafka and his companion Max Brod joined the visitors. A person could buy a picture of the Mona Lisa on the way to see the place where the original was missing.

And yet the investigation still needed to locate a very real wooden panel. Police interviewed guards, workers and past employees. Peruggia belonged on that list. In November, investigators questioned him in the rented Paris room where he lived. His employer had reported that he came late to work on the day of the theft. Peruggia explained that he had overslept and claimed he only knew of the robbery from the papers.

The police accepted his account. They did not discover the Mona Lisa concealed in a wooden trunk in the same room. The theft had made the news around the world, yet the investigators had just spoken to the man who had the painting.

Meanwhile, a more glamorous line of suspicion occupied the public. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested, and the young painter Pablo Picasso was questioned. That trail came from another affair involving small sculptures stolen from the Louvre. It did not establish that either man had taken Leonardo's portrait. The suspicion collapsed, and both men were released.

The investigation continued without recovering the portrait. Even before Peruggia's interview, the police had followed reports of a man carrying a frame towards Bordeaux. An August newspaper report described a search of a ship bound for South America. That lead produced no painting. Meanwhile, the portrait remained concealed with Peruggia.

The painting had been gone for more than two years when a dealer in Florence, Alfredo Geri, advertised for works of art. This gave Peruggia an opportunity to approach someone whose business was finding pictures for sale.

A letter arrived from Paris under the name Leonard. Its writer claimed to have the missing Mona Lisa and offered to return it to Italy for payment. Geri showed the letter to Giovanni Poggi, the director of the Uffizi Gallery. In Geri's account, published just after the recovery, neither man initially took the offer at face value. An actual meeting could settle whether the letter was another false lead.

Peruggia came to Florence with the painting. Geri's contemporary account says they met in December, and the dealer saw the picture at Peruggia's hotel. Geri arranged for Poggi to join the next meeting. Seeing the object was only the first step. Before anyone could call it the missing masterpiece, it had to be identified by someone who knew the original.

Poggi examined the panel, and according to the December 15 newspaper account, proposed taking it to the Uffizi to establish its identity. Peruggia agreed. The portrait reached the gallery and was authenticated. Geri and Poggi informed the authorities. The man who had been questioned at home in Paris was arrested in Florence. After all those public appeals and false leads, the investigation ended because two people tested an extraordinary offer instead of dismissing it.

Why had he done it? Peruggia maintained that he wanted to return an Italian masterpiece to its homeland. He apparently believed it had been taken to France through Napoleon's looting. But Leonardo himself had brought the portrait to France in the sixteenth century. Its presence in the French royal collection did not begin with Napoleon stealing it. And Peruggia had approached a dealer in circumstances involving money. His declared patriotism is a documented explanation he offered, not proof of everything he intended.

At his trial in Italy in 1914, Peruggia maintained his patriotic explanation. The court convicted him, but he spent less than a year in prison. Accounts differ over the precise length of time he served. The painting's journey home had already begun by the time the court considered his explanation.

Before the trial, the recovered portrait was exhibited in Florence, Milan and Rome. It returned to the Louvre on January 4, 1914. According to the Smithsonian's account of the return, more than a hundred thousand people came to see it in its first two days back on display. That is a different kind of crowd from the one that had gathered around the empty wall in 1911.

The portrait had been admired long before it was stolen. The theft alone cannot explain its entire reputation. But during those twenty-eight months, newspapers and street vendors had made the absent painting visible in a way the museum never could: even people who had never visited Paris could recognise the face.

In 1911, people had queued at the Louvre to see four hooks. In January 1914, they came back to see the painting. The missing object had returned, and the crowd was larger than before.
