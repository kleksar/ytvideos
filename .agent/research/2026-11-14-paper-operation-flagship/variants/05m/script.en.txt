How do you arrest a hundred wanted people in one building, without knocking on a hundred doors? And why would a giant chicken help?

In Washington, D.C., in December 1985, a free football ticket could be hard to resist. The local team had a devoted following. Deputy marshal Tobias Roche knew that, and he had a problem: people with outstanding warrants did not always stay at the addresses police had for them.

Roche had seen a smaller version of this trick before. In Hartford, marshals had invited wanted people to a meeting with the pop star Boy George and offered concert tickets. For Washington, he needed a local prize people would make an effort to claim. An invitation might even reach someone whose mail had been forwarded after a move. But the number mailed was never the number who arrived.

Roche's answer was to make those people come to the police. He and his colleagues invented a sports television company that claimed to be giving away tickets. More than three thousand invitations went to last-known addresses. Each promised two tickets to a Washington game, a free brunch and a chance at a trip to the Super Bowl.

The company was called Flagship International Sports Television. Its initials were FIST, just like the marshals' Fugitive Investigative Strike Team. The joke was hidden in plain sight.

But an invitation alone might look suspicious. So the team set up a phone line to take reservations, with office music for callers on hold. The fictional manager was named Mr. Cran: NARC backward. Nobody needed to understand the joke for the trap to work.

Now came the part that sounds made up. When guests arrived at the convention center, a deputy marshal was walking around in a giant chicken costume. Contemporary reporting even described a pistol hidden beneath one wing.

The chicken wasn't the only disguise. Officers played ushers, cleaners, caterers and enthusiastic sports-company employees. Others came from outside Washington, because a familiar local officer might have ruined everything.

The team rehearsed before the event, but the real audience did not follow the rehearsal schedule. Some guests arrived before the expected opening. Officers had to be in character as soon as the first visitors came through the doors, with the separate arrest team already out of sight.

There were name tags, coffee, pastries and football decorations. The visitors could register, eat and talk. They had not been summoned for questioning; they believed they had won something.

But why take people upstairs in small groups instead of handing out tickets in the lobby? That was the crucial design. Officers checked arrivals and guided parties of roughly a dozen into a separate room. The people still downstairs could not see what happened next.

Upstairs, a presenter in formal evening clothes talked up the game. Then he announced a surprise.

That word was the signal. Armed marshals and police entered. The guests were placed under arrest, taken out to waiting buses and driven to court, not to the stadium.

The process continued group after group. Contemporary reporting put the total at 101 fugitives arrested. Five other visitors, detained after turning up with letters addressed to wanted people, were released when their identities were checked. The numbers refer to detentions and reported arrests, not convictions.

No one was reported injured in the operation. Two handguns were recovered among those present, a reminder that making the arrests controlled and predictable mattered beyond the absurd costumes.

One arrested guest reportedly still wanted to know whether he could go to the game after getting out. And the next day, according to Roche, two more fugitives called to surrender.

The prize never existed. The brunch, the chicken and the people waiting upstairs certainly did.
