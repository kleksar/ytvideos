How could police arrest a hundred wanted people without first finding their homes? What would make those people come to the authorities themselves? And could a police operation survive the moment its own carefully rehearsed surprise went wrong?

In late 1985, football tickets were valuable in Washington, D.C. The local team had a fierce following. Getting a ticket to a home game wasn't as easy as walking up to the stadium. Deputy U.S. marshal Tobias Roche noticed how much people wanted to go.

Roche also knew something about the people he was supposed to arrest. A warrant tells police whom they are looking for, but it doesn't tell them where that person will be tomorrow. Some had moved. Searching old addresses could mean long investigations and unpredictable confrontations.

In November 1984, Roche had helped run an operation in Hartford, Connecticut. People were promised a chance to meet Boy George and attend his concert. Roche later remembered how the guests had dressed for the occasion and expected a limousine to take them there. For Washington, he wanted a prize that belonged to the city.

In September, Roche brought the idea to his superior, Herbert Rutherford. The Marshals Service teamed up with the local police and assigned senior inspector Robert Leschorn to work on it. In the middle of their planning came Leschorn's peculiar suggestion: one of the marshals should dress as a giant chicken. A football party would be a place where a mascot belonged, and the guests were supposed to believe they were attending a celebration.

The director of the Marshals Service came up with the name of the fictitious sports network: Flagship International Sports Television. Its initials, FIST, were also those of the fugitive task force. The invitation made the recipients sound like lucky names selected at random, rather than people identified through police records.

More than three thousand invitations went to last-known addresses. Each offered two tickets for the Washington–Cincinnati game, a free pregame brunch, and a chance to win season tickets or an all-expenses-paid Super Bowl trip. Those prizes mattered because the offer made sense to fans who had difficulty getting seats.

But the designers faced a problem: a prize letter from an unfamiliar company might raise questions. So the company had a telephone number. Marshals arranged for someone to answer calls. People put on hold heard office music. A supposed manager named Mr. Cran could take a reservation; his name read backward spelled NARC.

Yet the biggest obstacle was still the mail. In an interview just after the event, Roche estimated that only about half the intended recipients had received their invitations. Leschorn recalled decades later that roughly half the letters had come back. The team had no guarantee that the people it wanted would even see the offer, let alone show up.

Roche's colleagues had to solve another problem. What if a guest recognized an officer from an earlier encounter? The operation brought in deputies from outside Washington as well as local police. In total, 166 law-enforcement personnel were involved, although not all were part of the team making the arrests upstairs.

They rehearsed the party before Sunday, December 15. Some officers learned to act like receptionists, caterers or cleaning staff. Others watched the crowd. The room was dressed as a sports promotion, with banners, coffee, food and people cheerfully handling registration.

When the party finally opened, the giant chicken was there, just as Leschorn had suggested. Other marshals were in costume too. A news report at the time described the chicken as carrying a concealed gun. The mascot in the official photographs is real, even though the television network on the banners was not.

The party's promise had now become a room full of actual people. But the organizers knew that trying to arrest everyone in a crowd was a different problem from persuading them to come. They had arranged for the arrests to take place separately from the reception, so that the people who had just arrived would not immediately know what had happened.

The officers reached the convention center early. Their careful schedule was tested immediately: some people arrived before the expected opening. That was a practical risk for a deception built around acting. Staff needed to look ready before their audience appeared.

Roche wore a tuxedo and handled arrivals and name tags. Guests came in winter clothes, some dressed for their team. They found the sort of details that make a free promotion feel genuine: staff, music, pastries, football talk. There was no police desk in sight.

The guests were eventually asked to attend a short presentation before receiving their tickets. This was where Louie McKinney, the marshals' chief of enforcement operations, took over. Wearing formal clothes and a top hat, he had to keep a conversation about football going until the arrest team was ready. There was a single word that was meant to end the performance.

Years later, McKinney described a moment in his memoir when the performance did not go as planned. He had delivered the agreed cue, but nothing happened. The visitors were still talking. The officers who were supposed to come in had not appeared.

McKinney kept talking and repeated the announcement more loudly. This time the arrest team appeared. For the people who had come for a football match, the celebration was over. The elaborate performance had turned into an actual police operation. That is McKinney's recollection of one group, not proof that the cue failed every time.

Outside, buses waited. They carried the arrestees to D.C. Superior Court rather than the football stadium. Meanwhile, reception downstairs could continue, and another small group could be led upstairs. A crowd arriving for one big prize had been divided into a series of controlled arrests.

Contemporary Washington Post reporting put the number of wanted people arrested at 101, associated with more than 170 outstanding warrants. The UPI's early report said 100; later retellings sometimes give different totals. Those are reports and accounting differences, not a claim that all the people were convicted of the offenses listed on their warrants.

Five other people were initially detained but released after officers established that they were not wanted. Some had arrived carrying invitations addressed to someone else. One was reportedly the son of a wanted man. That distinction matters: showing up at the party did not, by itself, make a visitor a fugitive.

Officials reported no injuries during the sting, even though two handguns were seized from people at the event. Officers said one reason for the method was to avoid more dangerous encounters at homes and other unpredictable locations. The staged party concentrated the risk in a place they had prepared.

According to the contemporary account, the operation cost a little over twenty-two thousand dollars. Roche argued that it spared officers some of the long and unpredictable searches for wanted people. That was his explanation of the value of the operation, not proof that every arrest had been justified or that the operation permanently reduced crime. The five visitors who were released were a particularly concrete reminder of that distinction.

As the buses left, one of the arrested men reportedly asked if he'd still get to go to the game after being released. Other passengers were angry. The game itself continued to exist, of course. The promised tickets did not.

And the story didn't quite end when the last bus pulled away. Roche told a reporter that two more fugitives had telephoned to turn themselves in after the operation.

Roche did not need to invent another prize for those two callers. They came forward after the story of the fake party was already public. We do not know why they decided to surrender. But the invitations had answered the problem that started the whole operation: sometimes a person the police could not find would make the journey himself.
