### Three competing pitches, evaluated before full script

**A — Winner: A child gets postage stamps on her coat.** Audience: people interested in the point where a real postal rule meets a bizarre edge case. Exact cold line: "In 1914, a five-year-old girl stepped onto a mail train in Idaho." Why stop: what's a child doing on a mail train? Why postage on a person? What changes: identify May's weight, stamp price and mail-clerk relative; reveal earlier infant case; new ban; 720-mile delivery after ban. Payoff: children really were transported as parcel post with trusted workers, while popular mailbag images were staged. Strongest objection: initial funny fact may exhaust interest, and 1915 follow-up can feel like trivia. We preserve the regulation and later violation as new causes, not another price list.

**B — Strongest alternative: American Falls runs dry (1969).** Audience: people who know Niagara visually but don't know engineering behind it. Exact cold line: "In 1969, engineers stopped the American side of Niagara Falls." Why stop: a giant waterfall is supposedly off. What changes: dam diverts water; exposed rock pile is tested, then found to be stabilizing the cliff. Payoff: officials decide **not** to clear the pile they shut the falls to examine. Strongest objection: the complete Niagara system never stopped, so packaging could mislead; rather more technical, and Short must explain American Falls vs Horseshoe Falls. Reliable reports: https://www.history.com/articles/niagara-falls-dewatering-1969 (updated 2026-08-11) and https://www.niagarafallsusa.com/the-source/when-and-why-the-falls-stopped-flowing/ (local tourism article; date unknown), accessed 2026-11-14.

**C — Victor Lustig's Eiffel Tower auction (1925).** Audience: those who like clever deception. Exact cold line: "A man sent metal dealers invitations to buy the Eiffel Tower for scrap." Why stop: could he sell a national landmark? What changes: fake ministry letterhead, official-looking hotel appointment, selected victim and bribe hint. Payoff: actual payment and no complaint due to embarrassment. Strongest objection: multiple fraud plots already in the queue, and the famous "sold it twice" retelling is less securely evidenced than the first deal. Read https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/man-who-sold-eiffel-tower-twice-180958370/ (2016-03-09) and https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-smoothest-con-man-that-ever-lived-29861908/ (2012-08-22), accessed 2026-11-14.

**Decision:** Parcel child is better for this turn than Niagara for Paper: immediate human action (stamps on a coat), several distinct documentary developments after the cold open, simple prop-led paper dioramas and a resolved staged-photo misconception. Niagara has a stronger instantaneous panorama but its meaningful mechanics demand careful side-by-side geography. Against the queue, May offers family logistics and a social-institutional loophole rather than another catastrophe or public prank. Residual problem: final loop hinges on mail-car specificity, not a measured voice seam; independent reviewer can still reject the concept.
