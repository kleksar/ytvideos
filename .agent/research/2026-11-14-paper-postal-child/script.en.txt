In 1914, a five-year-old girl stepped onto a mail train in Idaho.

She had fifty-three cents' worth of postage stamps stuck to her coat.

May Pierstorff was travelling seventy-three miles to her grandparents. Her parents paid postage instead of buying a passenger ticket.

America's parcel post service had opened just a year earlier. The rules weren't ready for everything people tried to send.

May weighed just under fifty pounds, within the parcel limit. So the stamps went on her sleeve.

They didn't put her in a sack. A relative who worked on the railway mail service rode with her.

He brought May to her grandparents. For her family, the very strange delivery had worked.

But May wasn't the first. The year before, an Ohio family had sent their baby boy to his grandmother through the post.

A rural mail carrier took him about a mile. His parents paid fifteen cents and even insured him for fifty dollars.

Sending children this way was rare. In some rural towns, though, families knew their mail carriers personally.

May's trip, along with another inquiry, prompted the postmaster general to tell post offices not to accept people as mail.

That didn't stop every family. In 1915, another six-year-old travelled from Florida to Virginia by railway mail.

That trip covered about seven hundred and twenty miles, and the postage was just fifteen cents.

The reported practice appears to have ended later that same year.

And those old photographs of babies sitting in mailbags? They were staged for fun.

The children actually travelled with postal workers. One of those trips took place aboard a mail train.
