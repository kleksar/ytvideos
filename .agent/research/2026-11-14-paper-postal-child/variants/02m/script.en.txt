Fifty-three cents in postage stamps. That was the fare for a five-year-old girl's mail-train journey in Idaho in 1914.

May Pierstorff was travelling seventy-three miles to her grandparents. Her parents attached the stamps to her coat instead of buying a passenger ticket.

America's parcel post service was barely a year old. May weighed forty-eight and a half pounds, below the fifty-pound limit. The weight rule was for parcels, but her family used it for a person.

Nobody packed her into a mailbag. A relative employed by the railway mail service rode with her and brought her to her grandparents.

And the idea had started much smaller. In Ohio, a rural carrier had taken a baby about a mile to his grandmother. The parents paid fifteen cents and insured him for fifty dollars.

These were rare arrangements with postal workers the families trusted, not a service designed to transport children.

May's journey, together with another family's inquiry, prompted a decision. In March 1914, the postmaster general told postal offices to stop accepting people as mail.

The order was clear. But the trips continued. A Maryland carrier took a baby to its mother soon afterward. In 1915, six-year-old Edna Neff travelled from Florida to Virginia by railway mail for fifteen cents.

Then, that September, three-year-old Maud Smith was put on a mail train in Kentucky. Postal officials demanded an explanation: why had a child been accepted when the rules expressly forbade it?

That was the last case the National Postal Museum's researchers identified. It doesn't prove that every carrier immediately obeyed.

And the old picture of a baby in a mailbag? The museum dates one famous staged photograph to around 1900 — before parcel post even began.

The children weren't packed with letters. Their families paid postage, but trusted postal workers took them to relatives. The mailbag picture was staged; the real clue was what May wore on her coat.
