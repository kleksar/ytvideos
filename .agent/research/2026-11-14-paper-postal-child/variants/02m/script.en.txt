Fifty-three cents in postage. That was how a five-year-old girl travelled to her grandparents by mail train in Idaho in 1914.

May Pierstorff's family wanted her to visit Lewiston, seventy-three miles from home. They paid the post office instead of buying a passenger ticket.

Parcel post was barely a year old. May and her luggage came in under the local fifty-pound limit. But the weight rule was written for packages.

So who actually looked after her? A railway mail clerk named Leonard Mochel, a relative, rode with her and took her to her grandmother's house.

May wasn't the first. In Ohio the year before, a rural carrier had taken a baby to his grandmother in a mail wagon. The family paid fifteen cents and insured him for fifty dollars.

By the time May travelled, officials had already been confronted with a question about mailing a two-year-old. A Washington newspaper reported a prohibition on human parcels six days before her trip.

The Postal Museum also records a formal notice in March 1914. Yet the journeys continued.

A Maryland carrier delivered a baby to its mother. In 1915, six-year-old Edna Neff travelled by railway mail from Florida to Virginia.

Then a three-year-old named Maud Smith rode a mail train to her mother in Kentucky. A postal superintendent demanded an explanation from the local postmaster.

It was the last such trip the museum researchers identified. We don't know that every carrier obeyed immediately afterwards.

And that famous photograph of a baby in a mailbag? It was posed, and the museum dates it to about 1900, before parcel post began.

May really made the trip, but a relative took care of her, and her family paid for the journey with stamps. How much did they pay?
