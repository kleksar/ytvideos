How could a five-year-old girl travel seventy-three miles by train for the price of postage? Who made sure she reached her grandparents? And why did these journeys continue after the Post Office forbade them?

In January 1913, the United States introduced parcel post. Families in small towns and on farms could now send packages through a network of rural carriers. Some of those carriers were people the families already knew.

Within weeks, parents in Ohio handed an infant to a rural mail carrier. He took the boy about a mile to his grandmother. The parents paid fifteen cents in postage and insured him for fifty dollars. The insurance was not the fare.

It was an unusual arrangement, but the baby went with a known adult along a familiar local route. Then another family took that idea much farther.

On February nineteenth, 1914, five-year-old May Pierstorff was due to visit her grandparents in Lewiston, Idaho. They lived seventy-three miles from her parents' home in Grangeville.

The National Postal Museum puts May's weight at forty-eight and a half pounds. The local parcel limit was fifty. Her family paid fifty-three cents in postage rather than buying a passenger ticket. The museum describes stamps on her coat, but a report from her hometown newspaper on the day of the journey puts them on her small suitcase. Either way, it was the girl's journey that was paid for with stamps.

A weight rule for packages had become the basis for a child's railway journey. But how could that possibly work?

May wasn't placed in a mailbag or sent alone. She rode in the railway mail compartment with a postal clerk who was also a relative. Museum research names him Leonard Mochel. He accompanied May to Lewiston and delivered her to her grandmother's home.

That is the actual human outcome: a little girl reached her family with a trusted escort. The postal system had not invented a way to ship unattended children.

But May's successful trip revealed a problem for officials. Was anything in the rules actually stopping another family from mailing a person much farther?

An Oklahoma postmaster had been asked whether a two-year-old could be sent from Idaho to Oklahoma. He searched the postal regulations and found no explicit ban. That proposed long journey is not a recorded delivery. The inquiry alone exposed the gap.

And the answer had already made the news before May got on the train. On February thirteenth, a Washington newspaper reported that people were barred from parcel post. The museum also records Postmaster General Albert Burleson's notice of March twenty-eighth. We cannot tell whether the railway clerk had seen the earlier announcement when he accompanied May.

That should have put an end to the arrangements. Yet about a month later, a Maryland rural carrier transported a baby twelve miles from the grandmother's house to the mother's. The museum records this as a delivery after the order.

Then in 1915, six-year-old Edna Neff travelled by railway mail from Florida to Virginia. A later history account estimates the trip at roughly seven hundred and twenty miles. The museum documents the journey but admits that little survives about what happened inside the train.

More distance didn't mean that the national rule had changed. It meant the prohibition hadn't immediately ended individual carriers' arrangements.

In September 1915, three-year-old Maud Smith travelled from her grandparents to her mother in Kentucky by mail train. This time we know of an explicit official response: a railway-mail superintendent demanded to know why the local postmaster had allowed a child aboard despite the regulation.

That is a recorded demand for an explanation, not evidence of a conviction or punishment. The National Postal Museum treats Maud's journey as the last case its researchers located. No one can infer that every carrier obeyed from that day forward.

Now consider the photograph often used to illustrate this story: a uniformed carrier carrying a baby inside a mailbag. The museum catalogues a famous example as a posed joke from around 1900, before parcel post even existed.

Children really did travel with postage paid on their behalf. But the records describe accompanied journeys, and in May's case they identify the worker who brought her all the way to her grandmother's home. The mailbag picture shows a joke. The fifty-three cents and the name of the man who brought May home tell us what really happened.
