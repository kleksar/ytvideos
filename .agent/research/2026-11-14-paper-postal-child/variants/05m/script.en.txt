How did a five-year-old girl travel seventy-three miles as a postal parcel? Who made sure she arrived safely? And why did the post office keep delivering children even after it told everyone to stop?

In 1913, the United States had just opened its parcel post service. Families could send packages through a network that already reached their towns and farms. But a new service meant new questions about what counted as a package.

One Ohio family tested that question almost immediately. They handed their infant son to rural letter carrier Vernon Little, who took him about a mile to his grandmother. They paid fifteen cents in postage and insured him for fifty dollars. The insurance was not the fare. It was coverage on the most unusual delivery of the day.

The following year, a family in Idaho faced a longer trip. Five-year-old May Pierstorff was going from Grangeville to her grandparents in Lewiston.

The records put her weight at forty-eight and a half pounds. The parcel limit was fifty. And instead of buying a passenger ticket, her parents paid fifty-three cents in stamps, attached to her coat sleeve.

That is the astonishing part. A child was being treated as mail under a weight rule written for parcels.

But May was never sealed inside a bag. She travelled in the railway mail car alongside a relative who worked for the mail service. He accompanied her to her grandparents. The delivery worked because there was a person she could travel with, not because anyone had invented a way to ship an unattended child.

And May was not the first case. In January 1913, a family in Pennsylvania entrusted their daughter to rural carrier James Byerly. He brought her to relatives that same afternoon. The postage was forty-five cents. That was already the second kind of example: an infant taken about a mile in Ohio, and a girl delivered by a carrier in Pennsylvania. These were unusual local arrangements, not a regular national service for children. In small communities, the carrier was often someone the family knew.

Postal headquarters had to draw a line. The inquiry was not just a vague complaint. An Oklahoma postmaster had been asked about mailing a two-year-old from Idaho to Oklahoma. When he checked, he could not find an explicit ban on human parcels. That proposed journey is not a documented trip. It was a request that showed why a clear national decision was necessary. May's completed journey added urgency. On March twenty-eighth, 1914, Postmaster General Albert Burleson told post offices that human beings could not be accepted through the mail.

That should have ended the story. It didn't.

Roughly a month later, a rural carrier in Maryland transported a baby twelve miles between family homes, despite the new instruction. And in 1915, six-year-old Edna Neff travelled by railway mail from Florida to Virginia. Her journey was reported at about seven hundred and twenty miles. The postage was fifteen cents.

And the rule failed outside the trains as well. In March 1915, rural carrier Charles Hayes in Missouri brought a girl named Helen Combs to her grandmother, who lived on his route. That family paid ten cents. This was a familiar local delivery rather than Edna's long railway journey, but both fell under the same prohibition. Later that September, three-year-old Maud Smith was put on a mail train in Kentucky. Postal officials demanded an explanation for a journey that plainly broke their rules.

After that, the documented practice appears to have stopped. The ban had arrived earlier than the last recorded delivery.

There is one more twist. You may have seen an old photograph of a postal worker carrying a baby in a mail sack. Those pictures are not evidence that children were stuffed into bags. Some were staged jokes; one famous museum photograph is dated to around 1900, before the parcel post service even existed.

So yes, children really travelled as mail. But they went with postal workers, and the most famous picture of how it supposedly happened is the misleading part of the story.
