Could a parent really put postage on a child and send her across America? What did the post office mean by accepting a person as a parcel? And how could a rule banning the practice fail for another year?

On January first, 1913, the United States launched a new parcel-post service. A farm family could finally send packages from its own mailbox instead of carrying produce to a town with an express office. About three hundred million parcels went through the system in the first six months. The initial weight ceiling was eleven pounds; later in 1913 it rose to twenty for nearby destinations. At the beginning of 1914 it reached fifty pounds for local zones. More families could use the service for heavier loads. Then a postman in Ohio was asked to carry something no scale could properly classify.

One family in Ohio found a particularly strange omission. They wanted their baby to get to his grandmother, who lived about a mile away. Rather than arrange an ordinary trip, they entrusted him to a rural mail carrier.

The carrier delivered the child. The parents had paid fifteen cents for postage. They had also bought fifty dollars of insurance for the boy. Those are two different amounts: a tiny delivery charge and a much larger insurance value.

It sounds like a story invented years later to make the old post office look foolish. But the National Postal Museum traces the case to January 1913, just weeks after parcel post started. The baby is the first known example, not proof that everyone was doing this.

A second recorded arrangement came later that same month. In Pennsylvania, a family gave their daughter to rural carrier James Byerly. He brought her to relatives the same afternoon. The postage in this case was forty-five cents.

These children were not dropped into a sorting machine with letters. The carrier was the journey. The family knew which adult would take the child and where that person would deliver her. That personal connection made these rare choices imaginable, even though the system was never designed to transport passengers.

For most of the rest of 1913, the museum's account reports no further child deliveries. The next famous case would stretch the idea much farther than a short walk.

In February 1914, five-year-old May Pierstorff needed to travel from Grangeville, Idaho, to her grandparents in Lewiston, about seventy-three miles away. This time the route involved a train.

The hometown newspaper, the Idaho County Free Press, ran the story on its front page that day. It reported a combined weight of forty-nine pounds for the girl and her suitcase, and postage of fifty-three cents. The museum gives May's own weight as forty-eight and a half pounds. Both figures fit below the local fifty-pound limit.

The two accounts differ on one memorable detail. The museum says the stamps were put on her coat; the newspaper report places them on the suitcase she carried. We cannot settle that from the surviving accounts alone. What they agree on is that the child's trip, rather than just her luggage, was paid for with postage.

The man who made the arrangement work was Leonard Mochel. He was a relative and a railway mail clerk. May travelled in his mail compartment, and the museum records that he delivered her to her grandmother's house.

The stamps did not turn her into an object. A trusted person made the actual journey possible. Without that detail, the story changes into a much more frightening claim the evidence does not support.

Think about how the two kinds of travel differed. In Ohio, the reported delivery covered about a mile. In Idaho, a railway mail worker could accompany May much farther. The same administrative idea — marking a person as a parcel — was stretched from a local walk to a train journey between towns.

At about this time another inquiry reached postal officials. A postmaster in Oklahoma had been asked whether a two-year-old could be sent from Idaho to Oklahoma. He searched the rules and found no explicit ban on mailing human beings.

This was a question about what the service would allow, not evidence that the proposed long journey actually happened. But it exposed the problem clearly. Postal employees needed more than local judgment about whether a child fitted within a package weight limit.

Here the chronology becomes surprising. The Washington Times had reported an answer on February thirteenth, six days before May's journey: humans were excluded from parcel post. The Postal Museum's timeline also records a March twenty-eighth notice from Postmaster General Albert Burleson. The earlier newspaper report makes it unsafe to claim May's trip was what first created the prohibition. There is no record here of what Mochel knew about the February announcement.

On the same front page as May's story, the Idaho County Free Press reported another local test of the expanded postal limits: a rancher's shipment of about two thousand pounds of oats. The mail contractor quit, and the postmaster arranged for other men to move the load. This episode shows why the authorities had to confront problems their original rules had not anticipated. It does not explain why Mochel carried May.

There was no newly created children's travel program to close. There were a handful of unusual deliveries and a regulation attempting to shut the gap.

Now comes the part that makes this more than one amusing anecdote.

Only about a month after that instruction, a rural carrier in Maryland carried a fourteen-pound baby from the grandmother's home at Clear Spring to the mother at Indian Springs. The trip was reported as twelve miles. A local newspaper said the baby slept through the journey.

That detail belongs to a newspaper account; we cannot independently know what the infant experienced. What is clear is that a reported child delivery happened after the new prohibition.

The following year brought an even greater distance. Six-year-old Edna Neff travelled from her mother's home in Pensacola, Florida, toward her father's home in Virginia. Museum research describes the trip as the longest known child parcel-post journey. A later history account puts it at roughly seven hundred and twenty miles.

Again the recorded weight was just below the fifty-pound parcel limit. The postage was only fifteen cents. The museum also says that little survives about exactly how the journey unfolded. It would be easy to invent a scene inside the train. The records do not give us one.

The problem had changed. At first, the question was whether people were allowed to mail a child at all. By 1915 the answer from headquarters was no. And still these arrangements were being reported.

In March that year, rural carrier Charles Hayes in Missouri transported a girl named Helen Combs to her grandmother, who lived on his mail route. Her parents paid ten cents. Another trip, another local family using a familiar carrier.

Then in September, three-year-old Maud Smith travelled from her grandparents back to her mother in Kentucky. This time the official response was especially visible. John Clark, superintendent of the Cincinnati division of the Railway Mail Service, asked the postmaster in Caney, Kentucky, to explain why he had admitted Maud to the train in violation of postal regulations.

The museum treats that September 1915 case as the apparent endpoint. That means the final recorded example it could identify, not proof that every letter carrier in America obeyed from the next morning onward.

Taken together, the cases tell a different story from the familiar image of a baby in a sack. We have ordinary relatives expecting a visit, postal workers whom some families trusted, a new system with unclear boundaries, then a rule that took time to reach practice.

But where did the sack image come from?

Postal workers and photographers posed humorous scenes of babies with mailbags. The Smithsonian holds one widely shared photograph of a uniformed carrier with a child in a bag. Its museum catalogue dates that image to approximately 1900 — before the parcel post service opened in 1913.

A picture that looks like evidence of a bizarre practice can predate the actual practice by more than a decade. The museum identifies the scene as posed. It is an illustration of the joke, not a record of May being carried like a parcel.

We should also be careful with the word 'mailed.' It describes how the delivery was paid for and handled by the postal service. It does not describe a child being packed, sorted and thrown into the back of a mail truck.

The front page from Grangeville is a better ending than the staged picture. It announced a real child's journey and even named the relative working on the train. The same paper doubted that the idea would become popular. The handful of documented journeys that followed, and the official inquiry into Maud's trip, bear out how unusual the practice remained. The fifty-three cents were real; the famous mailbag scene was a joke.
