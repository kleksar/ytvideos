Seattle police kept getting calls about tiny pits in car windshields. Soon the mayor asked President Eisenhower for help.

It was April 1954. The complaints had begun farther north, where police suspected vandals firing pellets.

In Anacortes, officers set up roadblocks and checked passing cars. No culprit appeared.

At a nearby naval air station, Marines searched for five hours. They found damaged glass, but nobody causing it.

Then Seattle drivers began stopping patrol cars in the street to show officers their windshields.

By April 15, nearly three thousand windshields had been reported.

Some residents feared radioactive fallout. Others blamed particles no one could explain.

University of Washington scientists examined eighty-four cars. The pits looked like ordinary wear from small objects striking glass.

Most were on front windshields, where road grit would hit first.

But the county sheriff wasn't convinced. His deputies inspected more than fifteen thousand cars and found thousands of damaged windshields.

Seattle police compared cars at dealerships. Older windshields were pitted. Brand-new ones generally weren't.

There was no convincing evidence of a new kind of damage sweeping the region. Many marks had been there for years.

By April 17, reports had faded. People were finally looking at the glass they'd spent years looking through.
