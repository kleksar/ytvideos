Why did a few tiny marks on car windows make the mayor of Seattle ask the president for help? And how could police search thousands of cars without finding who was doing it?

The answer begins north of Seattle, in March 1954. Drivers around Bellingham were noticing small pits in their windshields. The first explanation sounded ordinary enough: someone might be firing pellets at parked cars.

More towns reported the same thing. On April 13, officers in Anacortes set up roadblocks at bridges and inspected vehicles coming and going. If a person was shooting glass, this was a chance to catch them.

Nobody was caught. At the naval air station on Whidbey Island, cars bore similar marks. Around seventy-five Marines searched the base for five hours. Still no attacker. By that evening, more than two thousand cars in the northern part of the region had been reported damaged.

And the stories were heading toward a bigger city.

Seattle newspapers put the mystery on their front pages. On April 14, drivers started reporting pits in parking lots and on streets. Some stopped police patrol cars to show the damage. Even police vehicles outside their own stations appeared affected.

By April 15, nearly three thousand windshields had been reported. Mayor Allan Pomeroy sent appeals to Washington's governor and President Eisenhower. An investigation of tiny marks had become a request for emergency government cooperation.

But what emergency was this? Nobody could identify an attacker or a new material damaging the glass. People suggested radioactive fallout from nuclear tests, strange airborne particles, even cosmic rays. The more explanations appeared, the less convincing the original vandal theory seemed.

Investigators tried testing one of the frightening possibilities. Geiger counters found no radioactivity on the examined glass or on people who had touched the marks.

The governor asked the University of Washington to investigate. Its scientists examined eighty-four cars. They found a pattern: pits appeared mainly on the windshields facing the road, not on the rear glass. The marks looked consistent with ordinary damage caused by small objects striking a car during years of driving.

That would explain how pits formed. It did not yet explain why so many people noticed them in one week.

And not everyone accepted the scientists' conclusion. King County Sheriff Harlan Callahan had deputies inspect more than fifteen thousand cars. They found damage on thousands. To him, that number still looked far too large for everyday wear.

Then Seattle police made a simpler comparison. They looked at cars in dealerships. Used cars had pitted windshields; brand-new cars generally did not.

That difference mattered more than the alarming total. If a new airborne threat had just swept through Washington, why would it seem to spare the newer glass? But if tiny stones and road debris had left marks over time, older windshields should show more of them.

The evidence pointed toward an outbreak of attention, not an outbreak of fresh damage. Newspaper reports sent people out to inspect glass they usually looked through. They found real pits, assumed the marks were new, and reported them. Those reports became new stories, sending more people to check.

There may have been isolated acts of copycat vandalism. The investigation did not prove every mark had the same origin. But it found no convincing evidence of a region-wide new attack.

By April 17, reports around Seattle had sharply faded. The marks had not needed to heal. People had changed what they were looking for.

Thousands of windshields really were scratched. The mysterious part was never the glass. It was how an old, everyday detail became everyone's urgent new discovery.
