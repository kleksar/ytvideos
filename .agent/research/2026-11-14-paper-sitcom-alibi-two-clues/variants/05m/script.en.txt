How could a comedy show help clear a man accused of murder? Why wasn't the footage enough on its own? And which clue finally made the timing matter?

In May 2003, Juan Catalan took his six-year-old daughter to a Los Angeles Dodgers game. They watched the Dodgers lose to the Atlanta Braves. That was an ordinary family outing. Three months later, the same evening became the central question in a murder case.

Sixteen-year-old Martha Puebla had been shot outside her home that night, roughly twenty miles from the stadium. She had testified in a separate case involving men connected to Catalan's brother. Police believed Catalan had helped retaliate against her. They arrested him in August.

Catalan denied the accusation. He told his lawyer, Todd Melnik, that he was at the ballpark with his daughter. He had ticket stubs. Relatives could support his account. But the investigators also said they had a witness placing him near the killing. A ticket could show that he entered a stadium; it could not establish where he was at the precise moment Puebla was shot.

Melnik went looking for pictures. He obtained footage from the Dodgers' own stadium cameras and the televised game. He knew roughly where Catalan had sat. Some views showed the seats, but the resolution could not identify the person in them.

Then he learned something extraordinary: another crew had been filming at the same game. It was shooting an episode of Larry David's comedy series Curb Your Enthusiasm. A man facing a murder charge might have appeared in the background of a sitcom that had nothing to do with him.

The HBO crew allowed Melnik to inspect material that had never appeared in the episode. In one shot, Catalan and his daughter were visible near the edge of the image. The lawyer could finally point to a recording and identify his client at the game.

There was a catch. The footage included a time code, and it had been shot roughly an hour and a half before the murder. It settled whether Catalan was at the game, but it did not close the gap between the image and the shooting. The distance was about twenty miles. That left a question the funny coincidence could not answer.

Melnik turned to telephone records. A call to Catalan's phone connected through a cell tower serving the area near Dodger Stadium late that evening, only about twenty to thirty minutes before the killing. A tower connection is not GPS, and it did not identify his exact seat or route. But it was a second, later piece of the timeline, not merely another image from the same early moment.

The lawyer examined additional calls as well. In a later account, he said the records from Catalan's cousin and from the victim's household also helped him reconstruct the critical period. He needed to test the prosecution's sequence against actual times, not just repeat that his client remembered a baseball game.

The case presented two competing explanations: police alleged that Catalan had reached the murder scene; the defense assembled evidence showing where he was and when. The court did not have to decide that a sitcom magically proved everything. It had to decide whether the case against him could stand.

In January 2004, a judge dismissed the murder charge. Catalan had spent about five and a half months behind bars. Years later, Los Angeles agreed to a settlement over his treatment. That outcome did not undo Martha Puebla's death or answer every question about her case.

The detail people remember is the television camera. But the decisive story is what happened after the lawyer found his client in the frame: he noticed what the image could not prove, then looked for evidence that could fill the missing time.
