How could a television comedy help a man accused of murder? Why did the footage fail to cover the most important part of his evening? And what did his lawyer find after the cameras stopped rolling?

On May 12, 2003, Juan Catalan took his six-year-old daughter to watch the Dodgers play baseball in Los Angeles. They saw the home team lose to Atlanta. It was an ordinary trip for a father and child. That night, twenty miles away in Sun Valley, sixteen-year-old Martha Puebla was shot and killed outside her home.

Three months later, police arrested Catalan. They believed Puebla had been killed in retaliation for testimony in another case involving people connected to his brother. Investigators said a witness placed Catalan near the crime. He denied killing her. The charge exposed him to a possible death sentence.

Catalan told defense lawyer Todd Melnik where he had been: at Dodger Stadium with his daughter. He had tickets and relatives who backed up the trip. But the accusation concerned a particular moment that night. A ticket could not show whether he had remained at the stadium until then.

Melnik requested the stadium's crowd-camera recordings and the sports broadcast. He located the rows where Catalan had sat, then studied the images. The seats were visible. The faces were not clear enough for him to identify his client. The most obvious source of an alibi had led to a dead end.

Then the lawyer learned that a completely different camera crew had been at the same game. HBO was filming scenes for Larry David's comedy, Curb Your Enthusiasm. Somewhere among that crew's unused takes might be a stranger who had never expected to appear on television.

The production allowed Melnik to inspect the footage. There, near the edge of a shot, he recognized Catalan with his daughter. At last he had a clear image connecting his client to the ballpark. But there was a detail he couldn't ignore: the recording's time code.

The sitcom footage had been shot about ninety minutes before Puebla was killed. It proved that Catalan had attended the game, not that he could not have left afterward. The murder scene was across the city. A camera that seemed to solve the case had instead exposed the missing hour and a half.

Melnik went back to the timeline. Telephone records showed a call connecting to Catalan's phone through a cell tower serving the stadium area at about 10:11 that night, roughly twenty to thirty minutes before the killing. That was a much later sign of where the phone had been than the television footage.

But a cell tower is not a GPS tracker. Its coverage is an area, not a single seat, and a phone record alone cannot prove every movement made by its owner. The defense needed to put several records together.

Years later, Melnik described how complicated this had been. In his recollection, Catalan had also dropped off a cousin near the neighborhood where Puebla was killed. That made a simple claim that Catalan had never approached the area untenable. Melnik said he checked calls associated with the cousin and the victim's household to examine the sequence more closely. Those are the lawyer's later recollections, not details independently established here from a full trial transcript.

The unusual television outtake answered one question: was Catalan really at the game? The later phone evidence helped address a different one: what did the timings permit, and what did the prosecution's account require? Melnik's work was to test those accounts rather than rely on one spectacular image.

In January 2004, at a preliminary hearing, a judge dismissed the murder charge for insufficient evidence. Catalan had spent about five and a half months in jail. The dismissal was not a jury acquittal, and it did not undo Puebla's murder. But it ended the prosecution against him. Los Angeles later settled a related lawsuit for $320,000.

A comedy camera gave the lawyer a face in a frame. What helped free Catalan was recognizing that the frame had a timestamp — and continuing the investigation when that timestamp wasn't enough.
