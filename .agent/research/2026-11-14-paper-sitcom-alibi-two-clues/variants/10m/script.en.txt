What would you do if someone accused you of a murder committed on the same night you took your child to a baseball game? How do you prove an ordinary evening months later? And why did the most famous piece of evidence in this case still leave a dangerous gap?

On May 12, 2003, Juan Catalan was at Dodger Stadium in Los Angeles with his six-year-old daughter. The Dodgers were playing the Atlanta Braves and lost eleven to four. For Catalan, the game would become important for a reason nobody in the crowd could have predicted.

That night, sixteen-year-old Martha Puebla was shot outside her home in Sun Valley, around twenty miles away. Her death was connected in investigators' minds to earlier testimony. She had appeared as a witness in proceedings involving alleged gang violence, and police suspected retaliation. They focused on Catalan, whose brother was connected to defendants in the earlier case.

Three months after the game, officers arrested Catalan for Puebla's murder. He faced a charge with the possibility of the death penalty. He denied killing her and said he had been at the stadium with his daughter. But an explanation is not the same thing as proof that can survive a courtroom.

The police believed they had a witness connecting him to the shooting. Catalan could offer ticket stubs and testimony from people who knew he went to the game. Those facts mattered, yet a person could attend a game and later go somewhere else. The disputed part was the clock: where had Catalan been when Puebla was killed?

Defense attorney Todd Melnik began with the most obvious material. A baseball stadium is full of cameras. There were images for the stadium's own screens, a sports television broadcast and other recordings of the crowd. If Catalan really sat where he said he did, perhaps one camera had caught him.

Melnik obtained tapes and looked for his client's section. He could see the area. He could not reliably pick out Catalan's face. The video showed spectators, but not the identity he needed to establish. The stadium was an unusually well-recorded place, and still its cameras offered him no clear answer.

During the search, Melnik heard that the Dodgers were not the only people filming that evening. An HBO crew had been shooting a scene for Curb Your Enthusiasm, Larry David's comedy show. The storyline involved a visit to a baseball game; Juan Catalan was not part of the production. He simply happened to be among the people sitting nearby.

The series footage was a peculiar kind of evidence. It had been made for a joke, not an investigation. The shots nobody used might be more valuable in court than the scenes viewers would eventually see on television.

Melnik got access to the outtakes. The crew and David cooperated with his search. After reviewing the material, he found Catalan with his daughter close to the edge of a shot. Another account described the pair returning to their row with snacks. The lawyer could identify the man whose presence had been difficult to establish using the baseball cameras.

That discovery is the part usually told as the whole story. A famous television comedian accidentally filmed an innocent man; the man went free. But consider what a timestamp does and does not establish.

The comedy footage had time codes, and the relevant frames were from roughly ninety minutes before Puebla was killed. So it verified that Catalan was at Dodger Stadium earlier in the evening. It did not place him at the stadium throughout every minute afterwards. The prosecution could still ask whether he had driven the twenty miles and reached the neighborhood in time.

That gap changed the task. Melnik no longer needed only to prove that Catalan had attended a baseball game. He needed an independent indication of where Catalan was much closer to the time of the shooting.

He turned to cell phone records. One call to Catalan's phone late in the evening was registered through a tower serving the vicinity of Dodger Stadium. Later accounts differ slightly on the exact minute, but put it around 10:11 or 10:12 p.m. — within roughly twenty to thirty minutes of the murder. That was much later than the sitcom footage.

A cellular record is not a photograph of somebody standing under a tower. It shows that a phone used a particular part of a network. Its coverage can include an area, not a single spot on a map. It cannot tell a court what every passenger in a car was doing. The defense still had to explain why this information mattered.

Here is the practical difference. The earlier video confirmed Catalan at the ballpark. The later network record helped narrow the interval during which he would have needed to leave the stadium, travel across the city and appear at the murder scene. The two forms of evidence answered different questions. Neither should be silently substituted for the other.

And Melnik's reconstruction did not stop there. When he revisited the case many years later, he said he had examined calls associated with Catalan's cousin and the household telephone of the victim's family as well. Those records allowed him to test additional points in the timeline. His later recollection included an awkward detail: he believed Catalan had dropped off a cousin near the area where the crime occurred, very close to the critical time.

That last detail is important because it resists a neat version of the story. A simple image of an innocent man never coming near the neighborhood would have been easier to explain. It was not the account Melnik later gave. According to that account, the case required distinguishing who was where, and at what time, rather than treating a stadium picture as proof of every subsequent movement.

The defense also questioned the prosecution's theory of motive. Police had tied Catalan to the idea of retaliation for Puebla's prior testimony. Contemporary reporting described a more complicated earlier proceeding, including disputes about whom she had actually testified against. Whether that theory could support a murder prosecution was a separate question from whether a witness said he had seen Catalan near the killing.

The point of an alibi is not that one item looks persuasive to the audience. It is whether the evidence, considered together, holds up when its timing, identification and limitations are tested. A ticket stub, a sports broadcast, a television outtake and a phone record each have different strengths and weaknesses.

At a preliminary hearing in January 2004, the defense presented its material. The judge dismissed the murder case against Catalan, finding insufficient evidence to proceed. He had been held for about five and a half months. The decision freed him; it did not mean that the show's outtake had been a complete alibi in isolation.

The murder remained a separate and grave crime. Later reporting said that people other than Catalan were convicted in connection with Martha Puebla's killing, and that Jose Ledesma was sentenced for ordering it. The dismissal of one man's case should not erase the victim or imply that her death was just the setup for an amusing television coincidence.

Nor did the consequences for Catalan end when the charge was dropped. He challenged the actions taken against him. In 2007, Los Angeles agreed to settle his police-misconduct lawsuit for $320,000. A payment cannot return months spent in jail or remove the possibility that he once faced a capital conviction. It is nevertheless a documented part of what followed.

The story found another audience years later through the documentary Long Shot and interviews with Catalan and Melnik. Those retellings often emphasize Larry David, the unlikely camera and the moment Catalan appears on screen. It's an extraordinary coincidence and a legitimate part of the case.

But Melnik later singled out the cellular records as particularly important. The sitcom supplied a face in a frame. The rest of the investigation supplied the times against which that frame had to be judged. An early image might have made his client visible, yet the later call data helped make the sequence difficult to reconcile with the accusation.

That is why the case is more interesting than a celebrity accidentally saving somebody. A lawyer followed one lead, discovered that it did not answer the whole question, and went looking for the missing evidence rather than stopping at the most dramatic discovery.

A comedy camera caught Catalan by chance. What helped get him out of jail was the work of finding exactly what that image proved — and exactly what it didn't.
