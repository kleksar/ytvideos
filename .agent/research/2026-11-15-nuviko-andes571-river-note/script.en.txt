A Chilean herdsman looked across a river and saw two strangers waving at him. They were trying to tell him about fourteen people still trapped in the Andes.

The strangers had walked out of a crashed airplane. Rescue teams had stopped searching for that plane weeks earlier.

On October 13, 1972, a plane carrying forty-five people, including members of a Uruguayan rugby team, struck the Andes while flying toward Chile.

Some people died in the crash. Those who lived sheltered in the broken fuselage, high in the mountains and far from any settlement.

They used seats and luggage to block the wind. There were sweets, a little wine and jam, but very little food for so many people.

A small radio brought news from outside. Then the survivors heard that the official search had been suspended.

The food ran out. Around the wreck were only snow, ice and rock, and the people inside kept getting weaker.

Eventually, the survivors ate flesh from those who had died. Many of the dead were their friends. They later spoke openly about how difficult that choice had been.

It kept some of them alive, but it could not bring rescuers to the wreck. And the mountain had another danger waiting.

On October 29, an avalanche buried the fuselage and killed eight more people. Even the shelter was no longer safe.

The others dug through the snow and remained with the aircraft. To leave, somebody would have to cross the mountains on foot.

They prepared a sleeping bag from insulation taken from the plane. Without protection at night, a long walk would be nearly impossible.

On December 12, three survivors set out: Nando Parrado, Roberto Canessa and Antonio Vizintín. The others waited by the wreck.

Their food was limited. Three days later, Vizintín turned back, leaving Parrado and Canessa to continue with more of the remaining supplies.

They climbed toward a ridge, hoping the far side would lead to a way out. Beyond it, they saw still more mountains.

There was no road and no town in sight. The two men kept walking, while their friends had no way to know whether they were still alive.

The terrain gradually became less frozen. They kept descending until they reached a river running through lower ground.

On December 20, they spotted a man on the opposite bank. It was the first person they had found outside their own group.

The river separated them, and they couldn't simply walk across to explain. They needed a message the man could carry away.

They sent a handwritten note tied to a stone. It said their plane had crashed and fourteen people were still waiting at the wreck.

The note explained that those people were injured, had almost nothing to eat and could not walk out. This was the information the search teams had never received.

The herdsman's name was Sergio Catalán. He went for help, and the authorities learned that survivors of Flight 571 were still alive.

Parrado helped guide the rescuers back toward the wreck. Helicopters began bringing the people who had remained there down from the mountains.

The evacuation took two days. By December 23, all sixteen survivors were safe, seventy-two days after the crash.

Forty-five people had boarded the plane. Sixteen came back. The others had died in the crash or during the weeks that followed.

News of how the group had found food caused shock. The survivors later described the choice in their own words, alongside the effort to save one another.

But the fourteen who could no longer leave the wreck had been found for one very specific reason: two men managed to get a note across a river.
