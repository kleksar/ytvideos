How do you ask for a rescue when the only person who can help is across a river? And how do you explain that fourteen people are still alive in mountains where the search has already ended?

In December 1972, two exhausted men reached a river in Chile. They had walked out of a plane crash in the Andes, leaving fourteen companions behind.

The plane had struck the mountains on October 13 with forty-five people aboard. Survivors sheltered inside the broken fuselage, using seats and luggage against the cold. Food soon ran out. They heard on a radio that the official search had been suspended.

With no other source of food, the group ate flesh from people who had died in the crash and afterward. Then an avalanche buried the fuselage and killed eight more. Waiting for rescuers was no longer a plan.

Nando Parrado, Roberto Canessa and Antonio Vizintín set off on December 12. They carried improvised equipment, including a sleeping bag sewn from material taken from the plane. Vizintín eventually turned back so the other two could continue with the remaining supplies.

After climbing one ridge, Parrado and Canessa discovered more mountains, not the nearby help they had hoped for. They kept going downhill until they found a river—and a Chilean herdsman on the far bank.

The river drowned out their voices. The herdsman, Sergio Catalán, sent paper and a pencil across; Parrado sent a reply back with a stone. It explained that fourteen injured companions were still at the aircraft, unable to walk out.

Catalán rode for hours to reach the police. The written message helped turn an improbable claim into an urgent search. Parrado then guided helicopters to the wreck. The airlift needed two days; by December 23, all sixteen survivors were safe, seventy-two days after the crash.

The river had stopped two men from simply speaking. Their message crossed it—and brought rescuers to fourteen others.
