Why would a rescue crew give up looking for people who were still alive? How could two of them cross the Andes without climbing equipment? And when they finally found somebody, would a river keep the message from reaching him?

On December 20, 1972, two men spotted a Chilean herdsman across fast-moving water. Fourteen companions were still waiting beside a wrecked plane high in the mountains. The herdsman did not know that yet.

The story had begun on October 13. A Uruguayan air force plane was taking rugby players, relatives and friends toward Chile. There were forty-five people aboard. The pilots misjudged their position in the mountains and began descending before they were clear of the high ridges. The aircraft struck the Andes.

People who survived the impact pulled others from the wreckage. They turned the remaining fuselage into a shelter, piling luggage and seats against openings. They had almost no warm clothing and very little food. As the days passed, even melting snow for water and getting through the night demanded work.

They heard aircraft overhead but were not found. Then a transistor radio brought devastating news: the official search had been suspended. From outside, their disappearance looked like a fatal crash. Inside the wreck, people were still counting the days.

At first, they stretched a small supply of sweets, wine and other food. Eventually there was nothing edible around them—only rock and snow. The survivors faced the decision to eat flesh from companions who had already died. They later spoke about that choice themselves. It was a means of surviving, not an easy resolution of their problem. Somebody still had to get help.

On October 29, an avalanche struck the fuselage. Eight more people died. Snow filled the shelter. Those left alive had to dig their way out after the storm. The place they had made safe enough to sleep was no longer dependable.

Several survivors explored what lay beyond the wreck. They also needed protection for nights outside. They stitched together a sleeping bag from materials recovered from the plane. It was improvised, but without it a long journey on foot had little chance.

On December 12, Nando Parrado, Roberto Canessa and Antonio Vizintín began climbing away from the aircraft. They hoped the other side of a ridge would bring them closer to Chilean settlements. Instead, there were more mountains. They had limited food and no proper mountaineering equipment. Vizintín returned to the wreck so the other two could take his share of the supplies onward.

Parrado and Canessa kept climbing, then descending. The landscape gradually changed. Snow gave way to lower, more open ground. Their companions at the fuselage could not know whether the pair were alive, let alone whether anyone would come back.

On December 20 the two walkers finally saw people across a river. The water drowned out their calls. Sergio Catalán, a Chilean herdsman on the other bank, had no reason to know that the strangers had crossed the Andes from an aircraft everyone had stopped searching for.

Catalán supplied paper and a pencil across the water. Parrado wrote down what shouting could not explain: fourteen people were still at the wreck, hurt, weak and unable to walk out. He sent the note back across attached to a stone. Their first task was no longer reaching a person. It was making him understand the people who were missing.

Catalán carried the written evidence on a long ride to the police. The claim sounded hard to believe after so many weeks, but the note gave authorities something specific to act on. Now rescuers had another problem: the aircraft was hidden among peaks. Parrado went with the helicopter crews to guide them toward the right valley.

They could not carry everyone out in one flight. The operation continued over two days, and by December 23 all sixteen survivors were safe. Twenty-nine of the forty-five people aboard had died.

News later focused on what the group had eaten. But the rescue itself depended on a separate chain of actions: the people at the wreck prepared two walkers for the cold; the walkers refused to turn back when the ridge revealed more mountains; Catalán carried their message; and rescuers reached the fourteen who could not walk.
