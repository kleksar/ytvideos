A search can end while the missing people are still alive. So how do those people tell the world it has stopped looking too soon? What made two starving survivors leave a crash site and cross mountains without proper climbing equipment? And why would getting one handwritten note across a river become the final obstacle?

In December 1972, two survivors of a plane crash stood beside a river in Chile. Across the water were men who could reach the authorities. Back in the Andes, fourteen people were waiting inside the remains of an airplane. They had no way to send the message themselves.

Two months earlier, a chartered Uruguayan air force plane was taking a rugby team, relatives and friends toward Santiago, Chile. Forty-five people were aboard. Bad weather had already delayed the journey, and the aircraft was supposed to follow a route through a lower pass in the Andes.

On October 13, the crew misjudged where the plane was. They began descending while high mountains still lay ahead. The aircraft hit a ridge. Its wings and tail broke away, and the remaining fuselage slid down the snow before stopping.

People who could move tried to free the trapped and tend to the injured. Among them were two medical students, Roberto Canessa and Gustavo Zerbino. They had neither a proper hospital nor the supplies one would expect after such a crash. Clothes became makeshift bandages. Seats and baggage became parts of a wall against the wind.

Thirty-three people survived the immediate crash. The broken fuselage was their only substantial shelter. Outside, the sun reflected harshly off the snow in daytime, and the cold took over at night. Nothing around them looked like a road, a farm, or a route that a rescue party could easily follow.

Nando Parrado had travelled with his mother and sister. His mother died in the crash. He was unconscious for days, and when he woke up he learned what had happened. His younger sister was badly injured and died later. Those losses belonged to the same unfolding emergency as the struggle for food and warmth.

The group improvised ways to stay alive. They blocked openings in the fuselage with luggage, pulled fabric from the seats, and tried to turn snow into drinking water. One injured person could need help from someone who was barely strong enough to stand. A decision that helped a single person also used up the strength of the group.

They saw aircraft above the mountains. But the wreck was hard to distinguish against the snow, and the aircraft did not find them. The people inside could not guide the search from the ground. Then a small radio brought news that changed their calculation: the official operation to locate them had been suspended.

Think about what that meant at the wreck. There was no scheduled rescue to wait for, but leaving immediately was not possible either. Some survivors were injured. The weather was dangerous. They did not know a safe path to a village. There was no useful food growing or moving anywhere near them.

The original supplies amounted to a little wine, candy, jam and other small items. These were gone long before rescue. The survivors debated a terrible option: eating flesh from those who had already died. They eventually did so. In later accounts, survivors described the decision in different personal terms, but it came out of the same material fact: without food, the people still alive would also die.

That decision kept the group going. It did not get them off the mountain.

On October 29, a new disaster struck. An avalanche swept over the fuselage while people were sheltering inside. Eight more died. Snow filled the cramped space. Some of those buried were still breathing, and others had to clear snow from them before it was too late.

Nando later recalled that they remained trapped for days as the storm continued. They made a small air opening and finally dug out when conditions allowed. The wreck had saved them from the weather, yet it was also a place where the next snowfall could bury them. Still, none of them could simply walk away on his own.

As weeks passed, the survivors explored the surrounding terrain. They recovered material from another part of the aircraft and considered routes beyond the peaks. A radio transmitter they found could not give them a working link to rescuers. The practical solution was becoming clearer, however brutal: a few people would have to walk far enough to meet somebody.

But the mountain did not offer ordinary camping equipment. At that height, they needed to survive freezing nights outside the fuselage. Working with recovered materials, the group stitched together a sleeping bag. They assembled clothes in layers and planned provisions for a journey whose length they could only guess.

By December, the group had diminished to sixteen. On December 12, Parrado, Canessa and Antonio Vizintín set off. They were not trained mountaineers, and they had no reliable map of the route. They hoped that by climbing the mountain above the crash site, they would reach the edge of the high range and find a descent toward Chile.

The climb took longer than expected. Parrado later described the overwhelming physical effort of gaining height. The men carried limited food and equipment. To make the remaining journey possible, Vizintín turned back, leaving more of the supplies for Parrado and Canessa. He returned to the wreck, where the others could only wait.

Parrado reached a high ridge expecting the landscape to change. Instead he saw peak after peak. The survivors had based their plan partly on the position they believed the plane had reached before it crashed. Now the view told them that they were much farther from a way out than they had imagined.

They had already spent several days climbing. Turning back would put them at the same wreck, with the same cancelled search. Continuing meant accepting that they still did not know how many more nights the walk would take. They chose to keep moving.

The descent was another journey rather than the last easy step. Their route took them through more mountains and finally into lower ground where the landscape began to change. There were traces of animals and human activity. After weeks of seeing almost nothing but snow and rock, those details meant there might be people somewhere ahead.

On December 20 they saw men across a river, among them a Chilean herdsman named Sergio Catalán. The rushing water made ordinary speech useless. Catalán could see two exhausted strangers, but their companions were beyond his sight, high in the mountains. One more obstacle lay between the walkers and a rescue: making this stranger understand who was still missing.

Catalán sent paper and a pencil across the river. Parrado wrote down that he had come from a crashed aircraft after ten days of walking. Fourteen people were still beside the wreck, injured and unable to leave. Parrado fastened his reply to a stone and threw it back. Without that exchange, Catalán might have known only that two lost men needed food, not that a rescue operation had to reach another fourteen.

Catalán rode for hours to bring the message to a police post. After weeks without any sign of survivors, his account was difficult for authorities to believe. He could show them the note. A chance encounter had become specific evidence of a second, much larger emergency.

The helicopters still needed an exact location. The note did not contain a usable route through the peaks. Parrado joined the crews and used what he remembered of the walk and the mountains to help guide them toward the fuselage.

When the aircraft finally reached the valley, the survivors still at the wreck could be seen emerging toward their rescuers. There was not room to evacuate everybody at once. Some remained another night with members of the rescue team. The airlift continued, and by December 23 all sixteen survivors were safe.

Seventy-two days had passed since the crash. Twenty-nine of the forty-five people on board had died, from the impact or during the ordeal that followed. News coverage soon turned toward the decision to eat the dead, and the survivors had to explain that part of their experience as well.

The survivors kept in touch with Catalán. In 2007, Canessa learned that the herdsman was in severe pain and could hardly walk. By then Canessa was a doctor. He called a Chilean surgeon, Felipe Jugo, who had been among the rugby players waiting for the Uruguayan team in Santiago in 1972. Now Jugo had a different reason to meet someone connected to that unfinished journey. Canessa and his fellow survivors helped arrange a hip replacement for Catalán. On July 3 the operation was reported successful.

Three months later, Catalán travelled to Uruguay for the first time. The two rugby clubs gathered again for a commemorative match, and Catalán was there with the survivors. The man who had once received a message from strangers across a river had finally made the journey to their country.
