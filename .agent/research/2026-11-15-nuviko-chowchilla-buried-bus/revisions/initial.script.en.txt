In 1976, police found a school bus hidden beneath branches in a California ditch. Every seat was empty.

Twenty-six children and their driver had vanished on the way home. Nobody knew where the busload of people had gone.

They were more than a hundred miles away, inside a truck that their kidnappers had buried under a quarry.

How did all twenty-seven escape that buried truck before the kidnappers could even demand a ransom?

It started on July 15, when the children were returning from a swimming trip near the town of Chowchilla.

A van blocked the rural road. Driver Ed Ray stopped, and three armed men took control of the bus.

They drove the bus into a dry riverbed, covered it with brush, and transferred their captives into two vans.

The windows had been covered. The children couldn't see where they were going as the vans drove for about eleven hours.

Before dawn, the vehicles reached a rock quarry near Livermore, far from the children's homes.

One by one, the kidnappers sent the children and Ray down a ladder into a moving van placed in a pit.

There were mattresses inside, some food and water, and pipes that let air through. This prison had been prepared in advance.

When the last person climbed in, the men removed the ladder, closed the hatch, and covered the entrance.

Back in Chowchilla, police had found the empty bus. But it gave them no answer about where the children were being held.

Underground, the captives had air to breathe for a while. They had no way to contact anyone outside.

As the hours passed, the roof began to sag. Waiting for someone to find them was becoming a dangerous gamble.

The only possible escape route was the hatch overhead. The children and Ray began dragging mattresses beneath it.

They stacked the mattresses so someone could reach the ceiling. Then they discovered what was keeping the hatch shut.

Above it sat a heavy cover and two batteries. Opening the hatch meant pushing that weight out of the way.

Ed Ray and the older children worked at the obstruction. Survivors later differed on exactly who began leading the escape.

Eventually the cover shifted. But getting through the hatch only brought them to another barrier: a wooden box above the roof.

Fourteen-year-old Michael Marshall worked at the wood until he broke through. There was still earth above them.

Now the group had to dig upward with their hands. Every handful removed brought them closer to the surface.

At last, they broke through. After roughly sixteen hours underground, the first captives climbed out into the quarry.

The others followed. All twenty-six children and Ed Ray had escaped alive, without rescuers opening the hatch for them.

For their families, the search that began with an empty bus finally had an answer. The children were alive.

But there was another unanswered question. Why had nobody received a ransom demand for twenty-seven hostages?

The kidnappers had planned to ask for five million dollars. They tried to telephone the police in Chowchilla.

News of the missing children had filled the town with panic. Families and reporters overwhelmed the police phone lines.

The kidnappers couldn't get through. Their captives escaped before the men could deliver the demand.

Once the survivors were found, investigators focused on the quarry where the van had been buried.

They learned that a son of the quarry's owner had been seen digging a large hole there months earlier.

A search of the wealthy family's estate turned up plans for the kidnapping and a draft ransom note.

The suspects were Frederick Woods and two brothers, James and Richard Schoenfeld. All three were arrested that July.

In 1977, they pleaded guilty to twenty-seven counts of kidnapping for ransom. They were sentenced to prison for life.

Their sentences later changed, and the three men were released on parole between 2012 and 2022.

For the people they buried, getting out of the truck was only the end of the immediate danger.

Years afterward, survivors described nightmares and other lasting effects. They had escaped physically, but the experience stayed with them.

The kidnappers never collected their millions. The children and their driver had escaped a prison hidden under the ground.

It took hours to move a hatch cover, break through a wooden box, and dig out. The ransom call never even connected.
