How did twenty-six children and a school-bus driver escape a truck buried underground? And why did their kidnappers never manage to deliver a five-million-dollar ransom demand?

On July 15, 1976, the children were returning from a swimming trip near Chowchilla, California. Three armed men stopped their bus on a rural road. The men hid the empty bus under branches, put the passengers in two vans and drove for hours.

At a rock quarry near Livermore, they made the children and driver Ed Ray climb into a separate moving van, already buried in a pit. Inside were mattresses, water, food and air pipes. Then the kidnappers shut the hatch and covered the entrance.

The captives had no telephone. As hours passed, the roof began to sag. Ray and the older children stacked mattresses to reach the hatch above them. A heavy cover and batteries blocked it. They pushed the obstruction aside, only to find wood and earth still between them and daylight.

Fourteen-year-old Michael Marshall helped break through the wood. The group dug upward with their hands. After roughly sixteen hours below ground, all twenty-six children and Ray climbed out alive.

But the kidnappers' other plan had already failed. They had intended to call the police with a demand for five million dollars. Families and reporters had overwhelmed the phone lines, and the men could not get through before their prisoners escaped.

The three kidnappers were eventually convicted. For many survivors, the fear lasted much longer than their time underground.
