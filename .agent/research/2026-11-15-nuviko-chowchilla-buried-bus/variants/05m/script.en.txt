How do twenty-six children and their bus driver escape a prison buried beneath a quarry? Why can't three kidnappers deliver a ransom demand to the town searching for them? And what happens to the people who survive when the headlines finally end?

On July 15, 1976, a bus carried children home from a swimming trip near Chowchilla, California. The driver, Ed Ray, saw a van blocking the rural road. Three armed men took over the bus.

The men drove it into a dry riverbed, hid it under branches and moved all twenty-seven passengers into two other vans. The windows were covered. For about eleven hours, the children could not see where they were going.

The police would later find a school bus with empty seats. But the missing passengers were already far away. Before dawn, the vans reached a rock quarry near Livermore. The kidnappers took the children and Ray out one at a time and sent them down a ladder into a moving van buried in a pit.

There were mattresses, food, water and pipes bringing in air. The place had been prepared. When everyone was inside, the men took away the ladder and closed the entrance.

At first, the room had what people needed to stay alive. It had nothing that could tell rescuers where they were. The children were between five and fourteen years old. Ed Ray was the only adult with them. And after hours inside, the ceiling began to sag.

The way out was directly overhead, but they could not simply stand up and open it. Ray and the older children dragged mattresses under the hatch and stacked them to make a platform. Even then, a heavy cover and two batteries held the opening shut.

They managed to move the weight. That should have been their exit. Instead, there was a wooden box above the roof, and beyond it, packed earth.

Fourteen-year-old Michael Marshall worked at the wood until they could get through. Ray and the older children helped force a route upward. Survivors later remembered the leadership differently; this wasn't an escape planned by one undisputed hero.

They dug with their hands. When they finally broke through to the quarry, around sixteen hours had passed underground. The rest followed until all twenty-six children and Ed Ray were out alive.

The discovery solved the question torturing families in Chowchilla: where were the children? It left a different question for police. Why had the kidnappers not demanded anything?

The men had planned to ask for five million dollars. But as the town searched, parents and reporters jammed the police telephone lines. Later accounts say the kidnappers tried to call and could not get through. No verified recording of those attempts is available. By the time they could make their demand, the captives had escaped.

The location of the buried van helped investigators. They learned the quarry owner's son had been seen digging a large hole months before the crime. A search of his family's estate uncovered a draft ransom note and planning material.

Police identified Frederick Woods and brothers James and Richard Schoenfeld. All three were arrested in July. In 1977, they pleaded guilty to kidnapping for ransom and received life sentences. The legal consequences changed over time; all three were released on parole between 2012 and 2022.

For the children, freedom was real, but it didn't erase the experience. Survivor Jodi Heffington later described how the kidnapping affected her life for decades. Other survivors reported nightmares and continuing fear.

Twenty-seven people escaped because they worked through a hatch, a wooden barrier and the earth above it. The ransom never reached the police. And for some of those children, getting out of the quarry was the beginning of a much longer recovery.
