How did twenty-six children and their bus driver get out of a buried van without anyone coming to rescue them? Why did their kidnappers never get their ransom demand through? And how did the escape lead to a legal fight that would last decades?

On July 15, 1976, a bus carried children home from a swimming trip near Chowchilla, California. The driver, Ed Ray, saw a van blocking the rural road. Three armed men took over the bus.

The men drove it into a dry riverbed, hid it under branches and moved all twenty-seven passengers into two other vans. The windows were covered. For about eleven hours, the children could not see where they were going.

Police would later find a school bus with empty seats. Its passengers had been taken to a rock quarry near Livermore. The children and Ray were forced into a separate moving van buried in the ground.

There were mattresses, food, water and pipes bringing in air. The place had been prepared. When everyone was inside, the men took away the ladder and closed the entrance.

The children were between five and fourteen years old. Ed Ray was the only adult with them. They had no way to tell the people searching for them where they were. After hours underground, the ceiling began to sag.

The way out was directly overhead, but they could not simply stand up and open it. Ray and the older children dragged mattresses under the hatch and stacked them to make a platform. Even then, a heavy cover and two batteries held the opening shut.

They managed to move the weight. That should have been their exit. Instead, there was a wooden box above the roof, and beyond it, packed earth.

Fourteen-year-old Michael Marshall helped break through the wood. Ray and the older children worked together to free the group. Survivors later disagreed about who led the effort.

They dug with their hands. When they finally broke through to the quarry, around sixteen hours had passed underground. The rest followed until all twenty-six children and Ed Ray were out alive.

The discovery solved the question torturing families in Chowchilla: where were the children? It left a different question for police. Why had the kidnappers not demanded anything?

The men had planned to ask for five million dollars. According to later reports, worried families and reporters overwhelmed the police phone lines. The kidnappers reportedly tried to call and could not get through. No original call record has been verified. By the time the captives escaped, no ransom demand had reached the police.

The location of the buried van helped investigators. They learned the quarry owner's son had been seen digging a large hole months before the crime. A search of his family's estate uncovered a draft ransom note and planning material.

Police identified Frederick Woods and brothers James and Richard Schoenfeld. All three were arrested in July. In 1977 they admitted their guilt on twenty-seven kidnapping charges. The following year the court imposed life terms without parole on three of those counts. For the families, it appeared the men would never be released.

Then in 1980 an appeals court overturned the findings of bodily harm on which parole ineligibility depended. The convictions and life sentences remained, but parole became possible. One of the children covered by those findings was Jodi Heffington. As an adult, she repeatedly attended parole hearings to oppose the men's release.

The first brother was released in 2012, the second in 2015. Heffington died in 2021; Woods was released in 2022. All twenty-seven captives had made it out alive. For Jodi, the effort to keep the men in prison had become part of her adult life.
