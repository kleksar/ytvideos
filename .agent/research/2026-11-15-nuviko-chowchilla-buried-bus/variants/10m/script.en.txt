How can twenty-six children and their bus driver escape a vehicle buried underground, when nobody outside knows where to look? Why do three kidnappers fail to collect a ransom even though they have taken an entire busload of people? And what does surviving such an ordeal mean years after the rescue?

The story begins on July 15, 1976, in California's farming country. Children aged five to fourteen were returning from a swimming trip near Chowchilla. Their school-bus driver was fifty-five-year-old Ed Ray. The bus was on a rural road when a van appeared ahead, blocking the way.

Three armed, masked men took control. They forced Ray and the children off their route, hid the bus in a drainage ditch and covered it with brush. Then they loaded their captives into two different vans. It was the first deliberate separation in a plan built to make the passengers disappear.

The windows of those vans were covered. The journey went on for about eleven hours. The children could not follow road signs or tell their families where they were being taken. Police eventually found the school bus, but its empty seats answered only one question: the passengers were no longer there.

Ten-year-old Jodi Heffington was among the missing children. Jennifer Brown Hyde, who was nine, later remembered Jodi trying to comfort the younger ones during the journey. The two girls would eventually have to confront what happened to them again, in a courtroom.

Before dawn the vans arrived at a rock quarry near Livermore, more than a hundred miles from the children's homes. One by one, the captives were sent down a ladder into an old moving van in a prepared pit. The kidnappers weren't burying the school bus. They had built a second place to hold the people who had been riding it.

The moving van had mattresses, food, water and pipes bringing air in from outside. Those details mattered: the men were planning to keep twenty-seven people alive long enough to bargain over them. Then the ladder was taken away, the hatch was blocked and the entrance was covered.

For a while, the captives could breathe and wait. They couldn't communicate with the search parties. The room was crowded, and as hours passed, its ceiling began to sag. None of the people inside knew when, or whether, anyone would discover the quarry. Staying still and waiting was becoming a risk in its own right.

Ray was the only adult among the captives. Around him were children who had begun the day on a summer outing and now had to work out a way to open an exit above their heads. The hatch was too high to reach effectively from the floor. So they dragged mattresses into a stack and climbed on them.

That solved the height problem, but not the weight. Above the hatch were a heavy cover and two batteries. The people standing on the mattresses still had to shift the obstruction while working in a confined space. Ray and the older children pushed until the covering moved enough to make an opening.

It was not daylight yet. Above the metal opening stood a wooden box. Even if they cleared that, soil lay between them and the ground. The escape had turned from reaching a hatch into breaking through one barrier after another, with no certainty about how much material remained.

Fourteen-year-old Michael Marshall helped break through the wood. Ray and other older children also worked to free the group. Later accounts differ about who first took charge, but the court record credits Ray and two older children for the escape.

After the wood gave way, they dug upward with their hands. There was no machine lowering a rescue tunnel toward them. They were opening a passage from below, with the rest of the group still waiting in the van. Eventually, after roughly sixteen hours underground, the first captives reached open air.

All twenty-six children and Ed Ray followed. The escape had taken place without the kidnappers releasing them and before outside rescuers opened the hatch. Quarry workers alerted authorities. The police search that had begun with an abandoned bus finally had a place, a set of survivors and a clear result: everyone was alive.

Yet there was a strange absence in the case. A mass kidnapping for money is usually followed by a message: a number to call, a deadline or a demand. Chowchilla had none of those. The three men had intended to demand five million dollars. So why was no demand delivered?

Back in town, the news of the missing children had brought families, journalists and police into the same frantic search. Calls crowded the small police telephone system. Later reports say the kidnappers tried to reach the department but could not get through. There is no verified original call log establishing every attempt or its exact timing.

No demand had reached the police before the escape. But the missing message was about to turn up in a different form: as evidence in the investigation.

Investigators now had something concrete that was missing when they found the empty bus: the quarry itself. They learned that the quarry owner's son had been associated with digging a large hole months earlier. Police searched the wealthy family's estate and found a draft demand for five million dollars along with materials associated with the plot.

The suspects were Frederick Woods, the quarry owner's son, and brothers James and Richard Schoenfeld. All three were arrested that July. Police now had a proposed ransom amount and evidence of the plan. The case moved to court.

In July 1977, the men pleaded guilty to twenty-seven counts of kidnapping for ransom. That December, the trial judge found that three girls, including Jennifer Brown and Jodi Heffington, had suffered bodily harm under the law then in force. In February 1978 the men received life terms without parole on those three counts. The convictions themselves were no longer in dispute.

The children were home, and the men were in prison. But the trial judge's finding would determine whether they could ever be considered for release. The defendants challenged it on appeal.

After the escape the children and Ray had been taken to the Santa Rita facility for checks and questioning. A police escort brought them back to Chowchilla early on July 17, where they were reunited with their families. Five weeks later there was a public celebration honoring the survivors and their driver.

There was even a trip to Disneyland. But in later interviews, survivors described how difficult it had been to return to childhood. Jennifer Brown Hyde recalled that for decades she still needed a light on to sleep. The public celebration and what happened at home were two different parts of the same recovery.

In November 1980 California's Court of Appeal acknowledged the children's suffering but held that the physical injuries did not meet the statutory threshold for the harsher sentence. It struck down the findings that barred parole while leaving the life sentences intact. That ruling changed the future for the very girls whose suffering had been considered at the original sentencing.

As an adult, Jodi Heffington went to nearly every parole hearing for the three men. At a 2018 hearing, she addressed Woods directly, challenging his account of his own childhood by reminding the board of what she had lost. That time, parole was denied. The Schoenfeld brothers had already been released, in 2012 and 2015.

Other survivors found different ways forward. Jennifer described the help she received from people around her. Michael Marshall eventually overcame his struggles with alcohol and found a life as a father and truck driver. He did not share the view that forgiving the kidnappers was necessary for recovery.

Jodi died in January 2021, aged fifty-five. In 2022 Woods was granted parole. The girl who had tried to comfort younger children during the journey had spent much of her adult life trying to keep the three men behind bars. The escape was a victory for every child on that bus. What followed was a different story for each of them.
