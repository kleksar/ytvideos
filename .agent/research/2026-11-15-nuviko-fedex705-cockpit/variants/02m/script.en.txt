A wounded FedEx pilot deliberately banked a cargo jet beyond ninety degrees. The danger was inside the cockpit: two injured crewmates were fighting an attacker behind him.

On April 7, 1994, Flight 705 left Memphis for California. Captain David Sanders, first officer Jim Tucker and flight engineer Andy Peterson operated the DC-10. A fourth FedEx employee, Auburn Calloway, was riding in a spare seat.

Calloway was a flight engineer, but he wasn't working this flight. The crew didn't know he had brought hammers and a spear gun. The following day, he was due at a company hearing about irregularities in his reported flight hours.

After takeoff, Calloway attacked all three with a hammer. Tucker suffered skull fractures, and his right arm and leg barely worked. Peterson and Sanders were badly injured too. No replacement crew was there to take over.

Then Calloway came back with the spear gun. Peterson grabbed for the weapon, Sanders joined the fight, and Tucker stayed at the controls. He couldn't abandon the seat to help them.

Tucker had taught combat maneuvers in the Navy. He put the loaded DC-10 into an extraordinary bank. Sanders later estimated the angle at roughly 135 degrees. The men fighting behind Tucker lost their balance.

The maneuver interrupted the attack. It didn't end it. Sanders took over the controls, while Tucker went back to help Peterson restrain Calloway.

Now the injured captain had to land an aircraft still heavy with cargo and fuel. He was coming in too high for the runway first assigned, so he requested a longer one.

Sanders landed safely in Memphis. Emergency responders boarded and handcuffed Calloway. All three crewmen survived, but their injuries ended their commercial flying careers.

A federal appeals court later upheld Calloway's life sentence for attempted aircraft piracy.

In the air, Peterson and Sanders could reach the attacker. Tucker could barely use his right side, and he couldn't leave the controls. There was only one way he could help them from his seat.
