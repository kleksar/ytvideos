Why would an injured pilot deliberately roll a cargo jet past ninety degrees? Who was he trying to stop? And once the aircraft was flying again, who was still able to land it?

On April 7, 1994, a Federal Express DC-10 lifted off from Memphis, carrying freight toward California. There were three men working the flight: Captain David Sanders, first officer Jim Tucker, and flight engineer Andy Peterson. Another FedEx employee was aboard, but he was not part of this crew.

His name was Auburn Calloway. He was a flight engineer himself. That mattered: he looked like someone who belonged on a company aircraft. The crew accepted him as a colleague riding in the spare seat. They did not know what was in his luggage.

Calloway was due at a company hearing the following day over irregularities in his reported flight hours. Before the flight he had brought weapons aboard, including hammers and a spear gun. The crew had taken off without knowing it was carrying an attacker.

After takeoff, Calloway came into the cockpit with a hammer. He struck the three men who were supposed to keep a huge cargo aircraft in the air.

Tucker suffered serious injuries to his head. His right arm and right leg were impaired. Peterson was badly wounded, as was Sanders. The attack had left all three needing help, but no replacement crew was waiting behind the cockpit door.

Calloway then left and returned with a spear gun. Peterson grabbed at the weapon. Sanders joined him. For the next moments, two injured men were trying to prevent another attack, while Tucker remained at the flight controls.

That left Tucker with a problem no normal flight lesson prepares a pilot for. Flying straight gave the attacker room to fight. He needed to disturb the fight without giving up control of the plane.

Tucker had flown in the U.S. Navy and taught combat maneuvering. He pulled the DC-10 into an extreme bank. According to Sanders's later account, the aircraft reached roughly 135 degrees from level flight: well past the point where one wing is pointing straight down, though it did not perform a complete barrel roll.

The people fighting behind Tucker lost their balance. The airplane itself had become a way to interrupt the attack. It was an opening, not a victory. Calloway was not yet safely restrained.

Meanwhile, the aircraft still had to be flown. Tucker's injuries made operating the controls harder, and the cabin fight had not ended. Sanders took the pilot's seat to bring the jet back to Memphis. Tucker went back to help Peterson keep Calloway from attacking again.

Sanders was injured too. And the aircraft was heavy with its fuel and cargo. He could not simply point it at any runway and expect a routine landing. In his later account, Sanders said he was coming in high and requested the longer runway instead of the one initially assigned.

He had to judge the approach while the struggle continued behind him. The cargo jet landed safely at Memphis. Emergency personnel came aboard; a paramedic handcuffed Calloway.

Only then was the full cost to the crew apparent. All three had survived an attack severe enough to leave Tucker with motor problems on his right side. The injuries ended the men's commercial airline flying careers.

There was a second investigation after the landing. FedEx personnel found multiple hammers and the spear gun. Investigators learned that Calloway had changed insurance beneficiaries and left notes; a roommate told the FBI about a paper listing members of the crew. These were facts examined in court, not permission to invent what went through Calloway's mind during the attack.

A jury convicted him. In 1997, a federal appeals court upheld his conviction and life sentence for attempted aircraft piracy. It also removed a separate, overlapping crew-interference conviction. The life sentence stood.

The strange image from this flight is an enormous cargo plane tipped far past its side. But that was not the ending. It was the brief chance Tucker created so his wounded colleagues could keep fighting—and still have a plane to bring home.
