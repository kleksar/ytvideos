How do you fight someone inside a flying cargo jet when all three crew members have been badly injured? Why would the pilot deliberately bank that giant aircraft beyond ninety degrees? And after that maneuver, could anyone still bring it down safely?

The flight was Federal Express 705, on April 7, 1994. It was a DC-10 carrying freight from Memphis toward California. There was no cabin full of holiday passengers: the people who could keep this aircraft in the air were Captain David Sanders, first officer Jim Tucker, and flight engineer Andy Peterson. A fourth FedEx employee had a spare seat.

His name was Auburn Calloway, and he was a flight engineer too. He wasn't assigned to operate this flight. The crew treated him as a colleague hitching a company ride. Before they boarded, Calloway had been in the cockpit, in flight gear, touching instruments and controls as though he were one of the working crew. Those details later appeared in a federal court's account of the evidence.

For Sanders and his colleagues, it was another cargo assignment. An account of the flight written years later says the original scheduled crew had exceeded its permitted duty time by just a minute, so a replacement crew was called. A small scheduling detail had put three different men in the seats that afternoon. There is no way to prove what would have happened to anyone else.

Calloway had a different appointment on his calendar. The next day he was supposed to attend a company hearing about irregularities in the flight hours he had reported. That was an investigation, not a proven decision to dismiss him. Before the flight he had also changed beneficiaries on insurance policies and transferred money to his former wife. Those arrangements became evidence in a later criminal case; by themselves they do not tell us every motive behind the attack.

In his belongings were hammers and a spear gun. The crew did not know that. They had accepted a fellow employee onto a working cargo flight, and they left Memphis believing they had a fourth person aboard, not an attacker.

Then the man in the spare seat entered the cockpit carrying a hammer.

Calloway struck the three men who operated the airplane. The effect was immediate and devastating. Tucker suffered fractures to his skull and problems controlling his right arm and leg. Peterson suffered a skull fracture and a severed artery at his temple. Sanders received serious head wounds and other injuries. This was no ordinary fight in which someone could step away and call for another driver. They were flying.

Calloway left the cockpit and returned armed with a spear gun. Peterson grabbed at the weapon. Sanders joined the struggle. One wounded man was still at the controls: Tucker. Behind him, the two others were trying to prevent the next strike, without knowing whether they could stop Calloway at all.

Tucker had been a Navy aviator before his airline career, and had taught combat maneuvering. That experience is part of why his next action makes sense. But he was sitting in a huge, loaded DC-10, not the small military aircraft on which such maneuvers would normally be practiced.

He pulled the aircraft into an extreme bank. According to Sanders, who later described the fight at an aviation conference, the bank reached roughly 135 degrees from level. Imagine the wings level, then one wing pointing almost straight down at ninety degrees. Tucker pushed the bank farther still. It was close to upside down, not a completed 360-degree roll.

That movement changed the fight. The men struggling behind the cockpit lost their balance as the aircraft swung. For a moment, Calloway's ability to attack was disrupted. Tucker had made the airplane itself part of the crew's defense.

But the airplane still needed defending from the maneuver. And the attacker was still fighting.

Sanders later described the aircraft pitching and rolling while he and Peterson struggled with Calloway. The bank gave them an opportunity to resist and restrain him, not a clean ending in which the attacker simply dropped his weapon. Exact positions and seconds differ between retellings; what is clear is that the struggle continued after the most extraordinary movement of the aircraft.

The first danger had been a man with weapons in the cockpit. Now there were two problems at once: keeping Calloway from renewing his attack, and flying an injured crew back to an airport.

Tucker's impaired right side made the controls difficult. Sanders returned to the pilot's seat to fly the approach. Tucker went back to help Peterson restrain Calloway. That handover had a cost: Sanders was injured and had to concentrate on the aircraft while the two men behind him continued a physical struggle. He could not abandon the controls to help them.

The DC-10 was also loaded with cargo and fuel. Its condition was nothing like a normal landing at the end of a long trip, when much of the fuel has already been burned. A heavy airplane needs care on approach, and Sanders was returning soon after departure, with a wounded crew.

In his later account, Sanders said the controllers had initially cleared runway nine. He knew he was coming in high. Rather than force the approach onto that runway, he asked for the longer runway 36. He got clearance. He also recalled warning alarms sounding as he worked to bring the plane in.

The important decision here was not another cinematic stunt. It was to keep flying an imperfect, difficult approach until the aircraft could be put on the ground. The man who had taken back the controls was trying to land while the danger behind him remained unresolved.

The DC-10 reached Memphis and landed safely. Emergency responders boarded. A paramedic handcuffed Calloway. Everyone who had been operating Flight 705 was alive when the ordeal ended.

The word “survived” can hide what those men had suffered. The court's account records Tucker's fractured skull, loss of control on the right side and injury to an eye. Sanders needed treatment for deep head wounds, an injured ear, and other injuries. Peterson had also suffered a fractured skull and a severed artery. All three were unable to return to professional commercial flying because of the injuries.

For Tucker, flying had been more than a job title. He had flown Navy aircraft, taught maneuvering, and made a career flying airliners. A later aviation profile reported that he eventually continued to fly small aircraft privately. That does not change the loss of his commercial career, or mean the three returned to their old jobs.

Meanwhile, investigators had to answer a question the safe landing had not resolved: what evidence showed this assault was an attempt to take control of an aircraft?

After the landing, FedEx personnel found two claw hammers, two sledgehammers, a spear gun and a spear. They also found a note addressed to Calloway's former wife. A man who shared his apartment told the FBI he had seen a note listing the names of Flight 705's crew. Investigators also learned about the changed insurance beneficiaries.

Those pieces led to a search warrant for Calloway's apartment. Agents found a note with crew names, another listing weapons, bank receipts and other documents. In the courtroom, these papers mattered because they connected preparations before the flight with what had happened aboard. But a court ruling establishing their admissibility is not a licence to invent a verbatim confession or inner monologue.

Calloway challenged the search and several aspects of his conviction. The appeals court considered whether the warrant had been justified, whether some seized documents could be admitted, and whether the evidence supported attempted aircraft piracy. It rejected those challenges.

One part of the decision was narrower than some later accounts make it sound. The court vacated the separate conviction for interfering with flight crew members, because it overlapped with the more serious attempted-aircraft-piracy offense. It did not overturn the central verdict. In June 1997 it upheld Calloway's conviction and life sentence for attempted aircraft piracy.

The investigation could explain what the documents showed and what the court found. It could not undo the injuries. Three men had been assigned to move freight across the country; by the end of the day they had fought to survive and lost the careers that had put them together in that cockpit.

A wildly banked DC-10 makes a striking picture. But the more consequential sequence is what came after it: the struggle did not stop, one injured pilot took over, another went back to the fight, and they still made the runway. Tucker's extraordinary bank bought time. Bringing the airplane home is how that time was used.
