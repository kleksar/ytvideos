The commander who sank an American warship was asked to testify against its captain. His answer weakened the Navy's case.

The captain was convicted anyway. Why did that happen? And how did an eleven-year-old later help change the official record?

In July 1945, the USS Indianapolis had just delivered components for the atomic bomb to the island of Tinian.

Its secret assignment was finished. The cruiser was now heading from Guam toward the Philippine island of Leyte.

Captain Charles McVay had asked for an escort. None was assigned, and the ship had no equipment to detect submarines.

He also wasn't given important intelligence about Japanese submarines operating in the area he was crossing.

After dark, McVay stopped zigzagging. He later said poor visibility made that course change unnecessary.

Soon after midnight on July 30, the Japanese submarine I-58 located the cruiser and fired a spread of torpedoes.

Two torpedoes struck. The Indianapolis sank in roughly twelve minutes, leaving hundreds of sailors in the water.

Around nine hundred men had escaped the sinking. There were nowhere near enough life rafts for all of them.

The survivors faced exposure, dehydration, injuries, and sharks. As the days passed, the groups grew smaller.

Meanwhile, the cruiser failed to arrive at its destination. No search began simply because the ship was overdue.

On the fourth morning, a patrol pilot spotted men in the sea. Their discovery finally brought rescue ships and aircraft.

Only about three hundred and sixteen survived. The exact totals differ slightly across historical accounts.

Once McVay was back on land, the Navy accused him of negligence. There were two charges.

One was that he had not ordered the men to abandon ship quickly enough. The court rejected that charge.

The other charge concerned zigzagging. The Navy argued that changing course might have made the cruiser harder to hit.

Prosecutors called Mochitsura Hashimoto, the Japanese submarine commander who had fired the torpedoes.

Hashimoto confirmed that the cruiser was not zigzagging. But he said zigzagging would not have changed his attack.

The court still found McVay guilty of negligently exposing his ship to danger. His punishment was set aside, but the conviction remained.

He was the only American naval captain court-martialed for losing his ship to enemy action during that war.

Yet the trial had not answered why the ship went missing for days without anyone launching a search.

Nor had McVay been told about all the submarine intelligence available to naval authorities before the voyage.

Survivors kept arguing that their captain had been treated unfairly. For years, official attempts to reconsider the case went nowhere.

McVay died by suicide in 1968. The finding of guilt was still part of his record.

Then, in 1996, an eleven-year-old boy named Hunter Scott began studying the Indianapolis for a school history project.

He interviewed survivors, worked through the evidence, and began asking why the Navy had never corrected the verdict.

His work helped bring new attention to the campaign that the veterans and their supporters had already been pursuing for decades.

Retired naval officers also examined the case. One analysis challenged the idea that zigzagging would have prevented the torpedo hits.

In 1999, survivors testified at a Senate hearing. Now the old charge faced scrutiny far beyond the Navy.

Hashimoto, the man who had launched the attack, also urged American lawmakers to clear McVay's name.

He had tried to help at the court-martial in 1945. More than fifty years later, he was still making the same argument.

Congress passed a resolution in 2000 stating that McVay should be exonerated. But that didn't automatically remove the conviction.

The Navy finally changed his personnel record in 2001. The crew also received a Navy Unit Commendation.

The captain had not lived to see it. The survivors, a schoolboy, and even the former enemy commander had helped bring the correction about.

The ship was lost in twelve minutes. It took the Navy fifty-six years to change what it had written about its captain.
