Why did the U.S. Navy convict a captain after an enemy submarine sank his ship? Why did the man who fired the torpedoes dispute the charge? And how did an eleven-year-old help change the captain's record fifty-six years later?

In December 1945, Japanese submarine commander Mochitsura Hashimoto appeared at a U.S. Navy court-martial. The officer on trial was Captain Charles McVay of the USS Indianapolis. The Navy wanted to know why his cruiser had not zigzagged.

Five months earlier, the Indianapolis had delivered components of an atomic bomb to Tinian. The secret job was finished; the cruiser was sailing from Guam toward Leyte in the Philippines.

McVay had asked for an escort, but none was assigned. His cruiser also lacked equipment for detecting submarines. Important reports about Japanese submarines in the area were not passed to him.

That night McVay stopped zigzagging. He later said visibility was poor. Whether it was good enough to require zigzagging became central to the case against him.

Shortly after midnight on July 30, the Japanese submarine I-58 found the Indianapolis and fired. Two torpedoes struck the cruiser.

The ship sank in about twelve minutes. Hundreds of sailors made it into the water, but there were not enough life rafts. Many were wounded.

The survivors waited through days of exposure, thirst, injury, and shark attacks. Meanwhile the cruiser missed its expected arrival. That alone did not set a rescue search in motion.

On the fourth morning, a patrol pilot happened to spot men in the sea. Rescue aircraft and ships were sent. About 316 survived; historical accounts differ slightly on the total.

McVay survived too. But when the Navy convened his court-martial, it brought two separate charges. One said he had been too slow to order the ship abandoned.

The court acquitted him of that charge. It convicted him on the other: negligently endangering the ship by failing to zigzag when, the Navy argued, he should have done so.

The prosecution had called Hashimoto to confirm that the cruiser sailed straight. Hashimoto agreed on that fact. Then he said the zigzags would not have changed his torpedo attack.

The court still found McVay guilty of negligently hazarding his vessel. The Navy later remitted his punishment, but the guilty finding stayed in his record.

There was a crucial limit to that verdict. McVay had not been tried for the enemy sinking itself. Nor did the court-martial decide why the overdue cruiser had gone unsearched.

Families had lost hundreds of sailors, and the conviction remained attached to McVay's name. Survivors who defended him kept asking the Navy to reconsider.

McVay died by suicide in 1968. The official finding against him had not been removed.

The survivors did not stop. They gathered support and pressed their case for years, although the court-martial result stayed on the books.

In 1996, eleven-year-old Hunter Scott chose the Indianapolis for a school history project. He began speaking with survivors and examining what had happened to their captain.

His questions brought attention to the campaign the sailors had already carried for decades. The boy had not started that campaign; he helped bring new people to it.

Retired naval officers also examined the evidence. One analysis challenged the claim that zigzagging would have prevented the submarine attack.

In 1999, the survivors' case reached a Senate hearing. It was no longer only a dispute among the people who had been aboard the cruiser.

Hashimoto also urged U.S. lawmakers to clear McVay's name. More than fifty years after the attack, the former enemy commander still stood by what he had said about zigzagging.

In 2000, Congress declared that McVay should not be held responsible for the loss of the Indianapolis. But this was a sense-of-Congress provision, not an order vacating the court-martial.

On July 11, 2001, Navy Secretary Gordon England directed that Congress's statement be added to McVay's personnel file. The crew was also granted a Navy Unit Commendation.

That was the correction the survivors had spent decades seeking. But the Navy did not erase the 1945 conviction: the new statement sat in the file alongside the old finding.

McVay never saw that page. He had died thirty-three years earlier. A Japanese commander, surviving sailors, and a boy with a school project had helped put it there.
