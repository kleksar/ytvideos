How could the man who sank an American warship become a witness against its captain—and still end up defending him? And why did the Navy change the captain's record more than fifty years later?

In July 1945, USS Indianapolis finished a secret delivery to Tinian and sailed toward the Philippines. Captain Charles McVay had no escort. Reports of Japanese submarines on the route had not reached him.

A Japanese submarine, I-58, found the cruiser at night. Two torpedoes struck. Indianapolis sank in about twelve minutes, leaving hundreds of sailors in the sea.

The ship failed to arrive, but no search began simply because it was overdue. Days passed before a patrol aircraft unexpectedly spotted survivors. Only about 316 men lived; accounts differ slightly on the exact total.

McVay survived. Five months later, the Navy put him on trial. He faced two charges, including an allegation that he had failed to order the ship abandoned quickly enough. On that charge, he was acquitted.

The other charge concerned zigzagging. McVay had stopped changing course in poor visibility. Prosecutors argued that this had negligently endangered his ship.

They called the Japanese submarine commander, Mochitsura Hashimoto, to establish that the cruiser had sailed straight. But Hashimoto also maintained that zigzagging would not have stopped his attack. The court nevertheless convicted McVay of negligently hazarding the vessel. His punishment was remitted; the conviction stayed.

The surviving sailors kept challenging that result. McVay died in 1968, long before their campaign succeeded.

In 1996, eleven-year-old Hunter Scott took up the story for a school project. He interviewed survivors and helped draw attention to the work they had been doing for decades. Hashimoto later supported their appeal too.

Congress said in 2000 that McVay should not be held responsible for the loss. In 2001, the Navy added that statement to his personnel file.

It did not overturn the 1945 court-martial. The two records remained together: a conviction, and a later acknowledgment that the captain should not carry the blame.
