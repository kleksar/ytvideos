Why did the Navy call an enemy officer to testify against an American captain? And why did that officer later ask America to clear the captain's name?

In July 1945, USS Indianapolis finished a secret delivery to Tinian. Captain Charles McVay sailed toward the Philippines without an escort. The Navy had not passed on warnings about Japanese submarines.

A Japanese submarine sank his cruiser. Hundreds of sailors were left in the sea. The ship missed its arrival, but no search began on that account. Days later, a patrol plane spotted the survivors by chance.

McVay survived, and in December the Navy put him on trial. It acquitted him of delaying the order to abandon ship but convicted him of endangering the cruiser by failing to zigzag.

The prosecution brought in the Japanese submarine commander, Mochitsura Hashimoto. He could not tell whether the ship was zigzagging when he first saw it, and he acknowledged that a small change of course might have occurred. His evidence was more qualified than a simple claim that the ship never changed direction.

The Navy remitted McVay's sentence, but the conviction stood. He died in 1968. Survivors kept asking for his name to be cleared.

In 1996, eleven-year-old Hunter Scott chose their ship for a school project and began interviewing the veterans. Their case reached Congress. In 1999 Hashimoto himself wrote to a U.S. senator, supporting the survivors' appeal.

In 2000 Congress said McVay should not be blamed for the loss. The Navy added that statement to his file in 2001. It did not overturn his conviction.

The sailors had won a new page for their captain's record. He never lived to see it.
