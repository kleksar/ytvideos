Why would the U.S. Navy prosecute a captain whose ship had been torpedoed by an enemy submarine? Why did that submarine's commander eventually join the sailors' appeal? And why did the captain's case take fifty-six years, a group of survivors, and an eleven-year-old student to change?

In December 1945, Japanese officer Mochitsura Hashimoto entered an American naval court as a witness. Five months earlier, his submarine had sunk USS Indianapolis. The man facing trial was the American cruiser's captain, Charles McVay. One question before the court was whether McVay should have ordered his ship to zigzag across the ocean.

That sounds like a question about tactics. But to understand what the court was actually deciding, we have to go back to the voyage—and then follow what happened to the men who survived it.

Indianapolis had just delivered components of an atomic bomb to the island of Tinian. That secret mission was completed before the attack. The cruiser then sailed from Guam toward Leyte in the Philippines. It was moving between American bases, not attacking the Japanese submarine that would find it.

McVay had asked for a destroyer escort, but his request did not result in one being assigned. The cruiser did not have the submarine-detection equipment that an escort could provide. Intelligence about Japanese submarines operating in the area was not passed on to him.

Those facts matter because they were decisions made beyond the bridge of Indianapolis. They would become part of the argument over who should bear responsibility when the voyage ended in disaster.

There was another disputed decision aboard the cruiser itself. McVay stopped zigzagging during the night. He later said visibility was poor, and the instructions governing evasive course changes depended in part on conditions. Whether he could see well enough to require zigzagging became the prosecution's central issue.

Shortly after midnight on July 30, 1945, the Japanese submarine I-58 detected the cruiser. Hashimoto attacked. Two torpedoes struck, and within roughly twelve minutes Indianapolis sank.

There was almost no time to organize an abandonment. Hundreds of men entered the water. Some were injured; some had flotation gear, while others struggled to stay afloat. There were not enough life rafts to shelter everyone.

The next stage was a different failure. Indianapolis did not arrive when expected, yet that absence did not automatically trigger a search. There is a distinction between a ship sending a distress message and a command system noticing that an overdue ship may be missing. Neither gave the sailors the prompt rescue they needed.

The men at sea endured exposure, dehydration and injuries. Shark attacks also killed sailors, but this was not a disaster that can be explained simply as a shark story. Time without water or rescue was itself deadly, and the number of those who survived kept falling.

On the fourth morning, a patrol aircraft unexpectedly spotted men in the ocean. Aircraft and ships began converging on the area. The discovery was not the result of a search launched promptly when the cruiser missed its arrival.

Among the rescuers was pilot Adrian Marks. He brought a PBY flying boat down on the open sea to take exhausted sailors from the water, despite the danger to his aircraft. More than fifty men were helped aboard or onto the plane. Their rescue is one concrete part of a much wider effort that continued as ships arrived.

By the end, roughly 316 men had survived. Different historical accounts give slightly different totals; the exact count does not change the scale of the loss. McVay himself came back alive, along with only a fraction of the cruiser's crew.

At this point, the story could have ended with the inquiry into a wartime catastrophe. Instead, the captain faced a court-martial. And the charges were narrower—and more complicated—than the familiar claim that he was simply blamed for losing his ship.

One charge accused McVay of failing to order an abandonment soon enough. Another alleged that he had negligently endangered the ship by not zigzagging when conditions required it.

The court acquitted him of the abandonment charge. It convicted him of the negligence allegation concerning zigzagging. That is important: the proceedings were not a trial for being hit by an enemy torpedo, and they were not a full judicial investigation of why the missing sailors had gone unsearched.

The prosecution called Hashimoto as a witness. The Navy's press announcement said his submarine's official documents had been destroyed before its surrender. He would give his evidence from memory, through interpreters.

Asked whether Indianapolis was zigzagging when he first saw it, Hashimoto said he could not tell. He saw no major course change afterward, though he could not rule out a small one. He distinguished the effect of zigzagging on his submarine's maneuvers from its effect on how he fired. Those are the limits of the surviving courtroom answer. Later accounts made stronger claims about what he believed would have happened, and in 1999 he would put his own position to an American senator.

A second experienced submarine officer, Glynn Donaho, also argued that such changes of course would not necessarily have thwarted the attack. That did not mean every submarine would always hit a zigzagging ship. It meant the prosecution's assumption about this specific danger was contested by men who knew submarine attacks.

And McVay was being judged without the benefit of submarine intelligence he had never been given. A captain could not base his decisions on reports withheld from him. Those circumstances did not erase the court's authority, but they mattered to the veterans who later asked whether its conclusion was fair.

The Navy remitted McVay's punishment. It did not remove the conviction. The distinction followed him long after his active service: no ongoing sentence to serve, but an official finding that he had negligently endangered his ship.

The survivors also lived with that distinction. They had lost shipmates during the sinking and the days in the water. They disagreed that their captain should carry the official blame, and they kept pressing for reconsideration. Their campaign did not start with a child decades later.

McVay died by suicide in 1968. He did not live to see the later effort succeed, and nothing in the historical record permits us to reduce his death to one proven cause. The conviction was still in his file.

In December 1990, Hashimoto met some of the survivors at Pearl Harbor. One of them was Giles McCoy. Through an interpreter, the Japanese commander expressed his wish to honor the shipmates they had lost. McCoy told him he forgave him. The former enemies could now speak to each other in person, while the American captain's conviction remained on the record.

In 1996, eleven-year-old Hunter Scott chose Indianapolis for a school project. He began interviewing the sailors who were still alive and examining what the Navy had done to their captain. His questions helped the veterans bring the case to people who had not followed it for half a century.

Scott's school assignment soon became correspondence with people in government. In 1997 he sent a letter to Congresswoman Patsy Mink, enclosing articles about his research. The following year Mink had his letter entered into the Congressional Record. A seventh-grader's history project had become part of the public record in Washington, alongside the veterans' longstanding campaign.

Retired naval officers reexamined the zigzagging claim. In 1999, the survivors' case reached a Senate hearing. Lawmakers could now hear arguments about the submarine attack, the withheld warnings and the original court-martial in one place.

Hashimoto sent Senator John Warner a letter in 1999. He asked the United States to restore McVay's reputation, invoking the reconciliation that had already taken place between the two nations. The man who had once appeared as a prosecution witness was now asking Congress for the same result as the men he had once left in the sea.

There were two different kinds of correction available. Congress could express its judgment about responsibility. But a congressional statement was not the same thing as a new naval court setting aside the 1945 conviction.

In 2000, Congress adopted language saying McVay should not be held responsible for the loss of Indianapolis. It was a formal statement of Congress's view, not a judicial reversal.

Then, on July 11, 2001, Navy Secretary Gordon England ordered that this congressional statement be added to McVay's personnel record. The Indianapolis crew also received a Navy Unit Commendation.

The new statement joined the old guilty finding in the file. The 1945 conviction was never overturned. McVay never saw the addition; he had been dead for thirty-three years. Hashimoto had died in 2000, too.

In 2023, another letter traveled between the two former crews. Harold Bray, the last surviving sailor of Indianapolis, wrote to Kunshiro Kiyozumi, the last surviving member of Hashimoto's submarine. Bray offered friendship and thanked Kiyozumi's captain for speaking up for McVay. Kiyozumi replied.

The men who had commanded the two vessels were gone. The two sailors who remained could still reach each other, and a letter asking America to remember one of their captains had become part of their shared history.
