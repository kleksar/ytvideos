Fifteen hostages and two rebel commanders boarded a helicopter in the Colombian jungle. Everyone believed the prisoners were being flown to another rebel location. But the helicopter belonged to the army that had spent years trying to free them. Why did the guards hand over their captives without a single shot?

By July 2008, some prisoners of the FARC guerrillas had been held for over a decade. Among them was Ingrid Betancourt, a former presidential candidate captured in 2002. Three Americans and eleven Colombian soldiers and police officers were held alongside her. An armed raid into their jungle camp might have endangered all fifteen.

Colombian military intelligence pursued a different route to reach them. According to Colombian officials, agents had penetrated rebel communications and command networks. A commander known as César believed he had received an order from FARC leadership. He was to gather the prisoners for a flight to the guerrilla movement's chief.

César accepted the supposed order and assembled the people under his control. Now the guards expected an aircraft. That was the first part of the deception. The army prepared two Mi-17 helicopters to resemble aircraft from a humanitarian mission. Soldiers dressed as medical workers, journalists, pilots and other mission personnel.

Even the camera crew was part of the deception, filming what appeared to be a transfer. The scheme depended on everyone in the clearing accepting the same story. On July second, the helicopters arrived near the holding area in Guaviare. One came down in a clearing surrounded by fighters who still controlled the hostages.

The visitors stepped out looking like an international mission, not an armed rescue force. The people being held did not know the operation was underway. Their hands were bound as they were moved toward the waiting helicopter. César boarded with another rebel commander. Both expected to accompany the transfer.

They had willingly entered an aircraft crewed by the very soldiers hunting them. The helicopter lifted off. Now the guards were away from the fighters on the ground. Inside, Colombian soldiers suddenly overpowered and restrained the two rebel commanders. Betancourt later said she did not immediately understand what had happened.

Then a crew member announced that the passengers were free and the crew was the Colombian army. The fifteen captives had been rescued. No one had fired a shot. Betancourt described people crying, embracing and jumping with relief inside the aircraft. After more than six years in captivity, she was on her way back to her family.

The two men who had guarded them were now the prisoners. On the ground, fighters had watched their captives leave with two trusted commanders. They had seen a transfer. The captives had been flown to freedom. But one detail of the disguises later brought a different kind of scrutiny.

A Colombian soldier had worn the protected emblem of the Red Cross. The country's president acknowledged the misuse and apologized after it became public. The International Committee of the Red Cross said it had not participated in the mission. The emblem identifies neutral humanitarian workers who need access to people in war zones.

When soldiers borrow that protection, genuine aid workers may be less trusted later. The rescue freed fifteen people, but the Red Cross dispute could not be dismissed as decoration. Back in the jungle, the captives had boarded with their hands tied. They thought another transfer had begun. Instead, they were finally flying home.
