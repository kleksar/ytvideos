How did two guerrilla commanders step onto a Colombian army helicopter without knowing who was flying it? And how did fifteen people held in the jungle leave without a gunfight?

In July 2008, the FARC guerrillas were holding former presidential candidate Ingrid Betancourt, three Americans, and eleven Colombian soldiers and police officers. Some had spent years moving between jungle camps. An armed rescue could have put them in danger.

Colombian intelligence chose deception. According to the military, operatives made a local commander named César believe that his own leaders wanted the prisoners flown to another rebel chief. The army sent a helicopter disguised as a humanitarian flight. The people on board looked like aid workers, journalists, and crew.

On July second, the aircraft landed in Guaviare. The prisoners were led aboard with their hands restrained. César and another rebel went with them. Neither man knew that the crew were Colombian soldiers.

Only after takeoff did the rescuers overpower the two guards. The captives learned that the flight was taking them to freedom. All fifteen were rescued without a shot being fired.

But there was a second story behind the disguise. A soldier had worn a Red Cross emblem, even though the real Red Cross had no part in the operation. Colombia's president apologized. The concern was serious: misuse of a protected symbol could undermine trust in genuine humanitarian workers.

Those passengers had climbed aboard expecting another transfer inside the FARC's captivity. Instead, they were finally leaving it.
