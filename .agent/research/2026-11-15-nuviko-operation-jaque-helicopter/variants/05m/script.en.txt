How could fifteen prisoners be rescued from an armed guerrilla camp without a single shot? Why did two of their captors willingly fly out with them? And what was the cost of making a military helicopter look like humanitarian help?

In July 2008, fifteen captives were still under FARC control in remote parts of Colombia's jungle. Their captors could move them between camps, and armed fighters guarded the places where they were held. Even if the army found the right location, a rescue by force could turn into a firefight around the prisoners. The government needed a way to move them without starting that battle.

Among them was Ingrid Betancourt, kidnapped in 2002 while campaigning for Colombia's presidency. Three Americans had been captured the following year; eleven Colombian soldiers and police officers were also held. They had come into captivity by different routes and had spent years under guard. None of them knew that the army was preparing to use their captors' own expectations against them.

The army pursued a plan built on what the captors expected to hear. According to Colombian officials, military intelligence had penetrated FARC communications. A commander called César received what he believed was an instruction from higher up: gather the hostages and send them by helicopter to the guerrilla leadership. The order appeared to come from his side. That made the strange flight sound like another transfer, not a rescue.

The aircraft also had to look credible. Earlier in 2008, other FARC prisoners had really been freed through a humanitarian handover. The army prepared two Mi-17 helicopters and dressed personnel as aid staff, journalists and flight crew. A staged camera team would record the supposed transfer. Colombia's president later said that the fictitious humanitarian organization even had a website and phone number; that is not evidence César checked either one. The people flying this apparent aid mission were Colombian soldiers. The real Red Cross had not authorized it.

On July second, one of those helicopters landed near the captives in Guaviare. To the fighters watching, the arriving team appeared to be there to collect prisoners for their own commanders. The hostages, whose hands were restrained, had no reason to recognize the disguised soldiers. They boarded under the eyes of the same guerrillas who had been guarding them.

Then César made the decision on which the operation depended. He boarded with a second rebel commander to accompany the transfer. According to accounts of the operation, the two were separated from their weapons before the aircraft left the clearing. The disguised crew now had the prisoners and both guards inside one helicopter.

The aircraft rose away from the jungle. Only then did the team overpower and restrain the two rebel commanders. Betancourt later recalled struggling to understand the sudden change. The people who had looked like humanitarian workers revealed themselves as Colombian soldiers and told the prisoners they were free.

All fifteen were freed without a shot being fired. Contemporary reporting described Betancourt and the others embracing and crying with relief inside the aircraft. They reached San José del Guaviare and could begin the journey home. Those who had been ordered from one remote camp to another were now beyond their guards' control. The two rebels who had flown along to supervise the transfer were the men being detained.

But the cover had created a problem that could not be resolved by a successful landing. Footage later showed that a member of the military team had worn the protective Red Cross emblem. President Álvaro Uribe acknowledged the misuse and apologized. The International Committee of the Red Cross said it had neither taken part nor authorized the mission. Its emblem is not a general costume for people claiming to offer help; in a war zone, its protection matters to workers who must approach opposing sides.

The fighters in the clearing had watched their own commanders escort the prisoners onto what seemed to be a routine transfer. Only after takeoff did the guards lose control and the captives learn who was flying them. For the fifteen passengers, this was finally a journey out of captivity rather than deeper into it.
