How did fifteen people escape years of captivity in the Colombian jungle without a single shot being fired? Why did two of their own captors go with them? And why did a rescue celebrated around the world end with an apology to the Red Cross?

In February 2002, Ingrid Betancourt was campaigning for Colombia's presidency when she encountered a roadblock. She noticed that the men there wore rubber boots rather than the leather boots of Colombian soldiers. They were members of the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, better known as the FARC, an armed guerrilla organization fighting the government. Betancourt was taken prisoner. Her son and daughter would grow into young adults before she saw them again.

The following year, an aircraft carrying American contractors came down in FARC territory. Three of the men, Keith Stansell, Marc Gonsalves and Thomas Howes, were taken captive. Eleven Colombian soldiers and police officers would eventually be rescued alongside them and Betancourt. Some of the Colombians had been prisoners for more than a decade. Their families had no release date to count down to.

Stansell's partner, Patricia Medina, was pregnant when his aircraft crashed. While he was in captivity, she gave birth to twin boys. By 2008, they were five years old, and their father had never met them. In an interview after his rescue, Stansell described hearing messages from his children on Colombian radio. He could listen to their voices without being able to go home to them.

The years were also passing for his fellow prisoners. Gonsalves spent months making a chess set. In a later television interview, he explained that playing gave the captives something to concentrate on besides their circumstances. Stansell recalled playing while still in chains. A game could end, and then another could begin, but the men were still in the jungle.

By July 2008, the Colombian army was preparing an operation to recover fifteen captives. Finding them was only part of the problem. Their guards were armed, and a battle around the people being rescued could have cost those people their lives. Even reaching the right camp was no guarantee of bringing everybody out. The army's remarkable decision was to attempt a rescue in which the captors themselves would take part, without realizing they were doing so.

According to the Colombian military, its intelligence officers had penetrated the guerrillas' communications. The officer in charge of the captives, Gerardo Aguilar Ramírez, known as César, received what appeared to be an instruction from the FARC's leadership. The prisoners were to be transferred to the organization's senior command. The military later said that the deception allowed people held in three different groups to be brought together. The account comes from the army; the individual radio messages have not been independently established here.

The appearance of a humanitarian flight had a precedent. Earlier that year, the FARC had released other prisoners through a real humanitarian handover involving international intermediaries. It was an important part of the setting in which this new transfer took place. The proposed flight appeared to be another movement of prisoners under guerrilla authority, rather than the arrival of an army rescue team.

There was, however, no real humanitarian organization directing this flight. The rescue team was made up of Colombian military personnel. Their appearance and the aircraft had been altered for the operation. The genuine International Committee of the Red Cross was not involved. That distinction would become important after the hostages were safe.

On July second, 2008, a helicopter arrived to collect the captives in the Guaviare region. Among the people who saw it were Betancourt and the three Americans. They had spent years under the authority of armed guards. The approaching aircraft brought no announcement that their captivity was about to end. What looked like another transfer was in fact the rescue.

The fifteen prisoners were taken aboard. César and a second FARC commander, known as Enrique Gafas, also went with them. It was this extraordinary reversal of roles that made the operation possible: the men charged with keeping the hostages under guerrilla control accompanied them onto a Colombian military aircraft.

Once the helicopter was away, the military team detained the two commanders. The rescuers revealed their identity to the hostages and told them that they were free. The people who had been taken from place to place under guard had finally left the custody of the FARC. Betancourt later described how difficult it was to take in what had just happened. Contemporary reporting described embraces and tears aboard the aircraft.

All fifteen hostages were rescued without gunfire. Some had lost five or six years of their lives, and some much longer. Their release was also the end of a long wait for people far from the jungle. For the families, the helicopter flight was only the beginning of the reunion.

The day after the rescue, Betancourt's children, Melanie and Lorenzo, arrived from France. Reporters saw her hurry up the steps of their aircraft to meet them. The woman who had been taken away during an election campaign six years before was now embracing the two adults her children had become. There was no need to explain what those six years meant: the reunion showed it.

Meanwhile, the three Americans were receiving medical care in Texas. Stansell's family traveled to join him. He later described opening a door and seeing his twin sons for the first time. They recognized him and called out to him as their father. He credited their mother for the bond they already had. He also recalled that the first visit was deliberately limited; after years apart, even a reunion took adjustment.

Gonsalves appeared in a television interview with the chess set he had made in captivity. The pieces had survived the years in the jungle. Here was something tangible from the prisoners' former lives that could be shown to the world without pretending to know every private thought they had had.

The rescue was celebrated, but there was still a question about the way it had been carried out. In mid-July, evidence emerged that a member of the military team had worn the protected Red Cross emblem. Colombia's president, Álvaro Uribe, acknowledged the misuse and apologized. The International Committee of the Red Cross emphasized that it had not participated.

The emblem matters because neutral medical and humanitarian workers rely on it for protection. Its misuse in a military operation can undermine confidence in the sign. That is a real concern even when a particular rescue has saved lives; it does not prove that any later attack on an aid worker was caused by this operation.

There was also a dispute over when the emblem had been put on. The government's initial explanation described an individual decision during the operation. Subsequent reporting on footage of the preparations challenged that account. Other reporting alleged a secret payment for the release, which the Colombian authorities denied. The available evidence does not establish that allegation as fact. What remains clear is that the fifteen people were freed, and that the use of the emblem was acknowledged.

The operation was named Jaque, the Spanish word for check in chess. There is no evidence that its name had anything to do with Gonsalves's homemade set. But the chess set and the rescue belong to the same true story. For years, he could put the pieces back on the board and begin another game, while the guards still decided where he could go. In July 2008, he could finally leave the game behind and go home.
