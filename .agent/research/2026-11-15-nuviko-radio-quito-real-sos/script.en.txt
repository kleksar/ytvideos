How did a fictional invasion on the radio end with a real fire? Why were firefighters kept away from the building? And when the announcer asked for help, did listeners believe him?

On February twelfth, 1949, Radio Quito was broadcasting from the same building as El Comercio, one of Ecuador's best-known newspapers.

Listeners had tuned in for the singing duo Benítez and Valencia. Their music was interrupted by an urgent bulletin about an attack near the city.

There was no attack. The station was performing its own version of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds, with Martians supposedly advancing toward Quito.

The actors impersonated reporters and officials. A contemporary newspaper report even described an actor playing the interior minister. The place names were familiar to local listeners.

The broadcast sounded enough like news that people took to the streets. The same contemporary report said police units went toward Cotocollao, where the fictitious invaders were supposed to have landed.

Radio Quito eventually announced that the invasion was only a play. For people who believed the warnings, that explanation brought anger rather than relief.

José Laso, who was listening at home, later recalled seeing furious crowds moving toward the station. Other witnesses remembered people carrying kerosene torches.

The crowd surrounded the newspaper building. Stones hit the windows. Then people set fires inside, while radio and newspaper employees were still working upstairs.

Guillermo Villalba had been invited from another station to take part in the drama. Now he saw smoke rising toward the floor where Radio Quito operated.

A Radio Quito announcer returned to the microphone, asking police, firefighters and anybody listening to rescue the people trapped inside.

Years later, Laso remembered that some people treated those pleas as part of the performance. We do not know how many shared that confusion, and it cannot by itself explain the deaths.

Firefighters did arrive. But accounts gathered afterward say people in the crowd threatened them if they tried to spray water. The crews withdrew.

Inside, workers tried to escape. Protesters eventually said employees could leave through one doorway, but the flames had already made reaching that exit dangerous.

Villalba escaped by leaping onto a hot metal roof beside the building. He badly burned his hands and body; someone later took him to a hospital while he was unconscious.

Another announcer, Luis Beltrán, survived a fall from the burning building, according to his daughter's later account. Not everyone inside had a way out.

By late evening, police and soldiers had cleared the crowd. Firefighters could finally approach the building, but much of its interior was already burning.

A newspaper report published days later counted at least six dead and fifteen injured. Later accounts reported eight deaths; other totals circulated. The final figure is not firmly settled.

The blaze also destroyed the station's recording of the drama and its adapted studio script. What listeners heard in 1949 cannot simply be replayed from an original tape.

The invasion broadcasts used in later retellings are reconstructions. The real fire, the blocked firefighters and the cries for help are documented by contemporary reporting and later testimony.

The station's artistic director, Leonardo Páez, was investigated after the disaster and later acquitted. Radio Quito began broadcasting again in 1951.

But among those who died were two musicians who had been in the building that night: violinist Perfecto Alvarado and pianist Raúl Molestina. The performance that began as entertainment cost them their lives in the real fire.
