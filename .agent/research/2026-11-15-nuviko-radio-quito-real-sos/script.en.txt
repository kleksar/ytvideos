In 1949, a radio station in Ecuador was asking for help while its building burned.

One listener later recalled that some people mistook the pleas for another scene in a radio drama.

How did a real emergency become difficult to believe on the city's own trusted frequency?

Quito is Ecuador's capital, high in the Andes. In 1949, radio was a central part of city life.

Radio Quito shared a building with El Comercio, an influential newspaper. People knew both names.

On the evening of February twelfth, listeners expected music from Benítez and Valencia, a popular singing duo.

Instead, the performance was interrupted by an urgent bulletin about an attack near Quito.

Nothing had attacked the city. The station was performing a version of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds.

The characters spoke about Martians arriving in familiar Ecuadorian places, then advancing toward the capital.

Actors sounded like reporters and government officials. The program borrowed the form of a real news emergency.

And the supposed invasion was happening right around the towns and streets listeners recognized.

For those who did not know it was fiction, the station gave them reasons to believe every new report.

Witnesses later described frightened residents going into the streets. Some looked for information or safety.

Contemporary news reports also said some police had gone toward the place where the fictional attackers were supposedly landing.

When the station finally explained that the invasion was imaginary, the panic should have ended.

Instead, the announcement changed fear into anger. People who felt deceived headed for Radio Quito.

The station wasn't in a separate little booth. It was inside the newspaper building, with workers still there.

Stones struck the windows. People arrived with torches. Soon there was fire inside the building.

Staff upstairs could see smoke climbing from below. Their exits were becoming dangerous.

The radio that had just reported an imaginary disaster now had a real one unfolding around its microphones.

One of the announcers went back on air to ask police, firefighters and anyone listening for help.

Years afterward, José Laso recalled hearing that plea and believing some listeners thought it was still part of the performance.

His memory does not tell us how many people shared the confusion. But the calls for help were real.

Firefighters did reach the building. Witness accounts say people in the crowd kept them from fighting the blaze.

Inside, the fire kept spreading while radio and newspaper employees tried to find a way out.

One radio worker, Guillermo Villalba, escaped over a neighboring roof and suffered severe burns.

Another announcer, Luis Beltrán, survived a fall from the burning building, according to his daughter's later account.

Eventually police and soldiers cleared access for the fire crews. The rescuers could finally work.

The fire gutted both the station and the newspaper's offices. Some people inside never escaped.

A news report from February 1949 counted at least six dead and fifteen injured.

Later reporting counted eight deaths in the fire; other figures also circulated. The exact overall toll is contested.

Two of the dead were musicians: violinist Perfecto Alvarado and pianist Raúl Molestina.

The original recording and the studio script were lost in the blaze, according to later historical reporting.

So recordings used to illustrate the story today are reconstructions, not what Quito's listeners actually heard that night.

The radio's artistic director, Leonardo Páez, was investigated after the disaster and later acquitted in court.

Radio Quito returned to broadcasting in 1951. None of that changed what happened to the people in the building.

The first false alarm made people afraid of an invasion. The real alarm came after they learned the invasion was fake.

And when a voice from Radio Quito asked for rescue, some people no longer knew which kind of emergency they were hearing.
