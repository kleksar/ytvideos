How could a fake Martian invasion on the radio lead to real deaths? And why did a genuine call for rescue become hard to believe?

On February 12, 1949, people in Quito, Ecuador, tuned in to Radio Quito for live music. The singers were interrupted by what sounded like breaking news. Martians had supposedly landed near the city.

Actors played reporters and government officials. They named familiar places, including Cotocollao. A newspaper report published days afterward said police actually headed toward the supposed landing site.

There were no Martians. This was a radio adaptation of The War of the Worlds. After roughly fifteen minutes, the station explained that it was a play.

That announcement changed the danger. Angry listeners gathered at the building shared by Radio Quito and the newspaper El Comercio. Stones broke windows. People brought torches, and fire spread through the building while employees were still inside.

An announcer asked for real help over the same microphone. One listener, José Laso, recalled years later that some people thought even those pleas belonged to the drama. We cannot know how many did.

Firefighters arrived, but witnesses later described threats from the crowd that forced them back. Guillermo Villalba, an invited performer, escaped by jumping onto a nearby metal roof and suffered serious burns.

Police and soldiers eventually cleared the area so crews could fight the fire. A contemporary report counted at least six dead; later accounts gave different totals.

Among the dead were violinist Perfecto Alvarado and pianist Raúl Molestina. The original broadcast recording burned too. The invasion was fictional; the musicians' deaths and the desperate rescue calls were real.
