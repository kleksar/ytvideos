Why did a radio station convince people in Quito that Martians were coming? How did admitting it was a play lead to a fire? And what happened when the station's announcers needed real help?

In February 1949, a radio could bring a family the news, a concert, or a drama without any change in the box on the table. Not everyone in Quito owned a set. Some people listened with neighbors or around speakers placed in shops. A trusted station reached more than the people sitting beside its microphone.

Radio Quito was one such station. It shared a building with El Comercio, one of Ecuador's major newspapers. That connection gave its name a familiarity extending beyond entertainment.

On Saturday, February twelfth, listeners expected music from the singing duo Benítez and Valencia. But the programme was interrupted. The station appeared to be carrying urgent reports about an attack near the capital.

What the audience did not yet know was that the station had prepared a local adaptation of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds. In 1938, Orson Welles had staged a well-known American radio version. Quito's programme replaced distant locations with places its own listeners recognized.

The artistic director, Leonardo Páez, was involved in arranging the production. Actors played correspondents and officials, including a supposed interior minister. The invasion moved across a map that sounded familiar: Latacunga, Cotocollao, and the approaches to Quito.

To the performers, those names belonged to a script. To a listener who had caught only the interruption, they could sound like urgent details of a nearby event. The microphone was the same one that ordinarily carried the station's news.

Guillermo Villalba was a broadcaster invited from another station to participate. He later described staged exchanges between radio voices. The effect suggested that reports were arriving from different places, when the voices were part of the same performance.

Another detail survives in later accounts from Páez: the programme even included a soft-drink advertisement. That is a recollection, not audio that can now be checked against an original recording. The strange mixture of fictional emergency and ordinary radio routine helps explain how uncertain the boundary could feel.

At a restaurant, a teenage listener named José Antonio Gross later remembered hearing the broadcast with other young people while a radio played for the neighborhood. For them, the story was not confined to a family sitting quietly in one room.

And people began to act. A newspaper published just days after the events reported that police units went toward Cotocollao, the site of the supposed landing. That specific response is more securely described than the common claim that an entire city believed the invasion.

Other memories describe ordinary households reacting to what they heard. Aurora Pazmiño, then eleven, later recalled her family praying. José Laso remembered his mother wanting the family to take refuge in a basement.

These recollections do not measure how many listeners believed the story. They show something narrower and important: fictional reports were affecting real decisions.

Then the station learned that its programme was causing alarm. After roughly fifteen minutes, Radio Quito announced that the attack was fictitious. The listeners had been hearing a play.

The announcement should have ended the danger. Instead, for some who had believed the warnings, it replaced fear with anger. They had changed their behavior because of a broadcast that now turned out to be entertainment.

Here the story changed direction. People were no longer reacting to imaginary invaders. They were heading toward the very building that had produced the illusion.

Laso later recalled crowds moving through Quito. Other witnesses described sticks and kerosene torches. The crowd gathered at the building shared by the station and the newspaper.

Stones broke windows. People entered the lower part of the building, damaged equipment, and started fires. Radio and newspaper employees were still working above. The emergency had moved from a radio script into a real building full of people.

Villalba had come to perform an imaginary emergency. Now, according to his later testimony, he watched smoke move toward the radio floor. Employees tried to work out how to escape.

The station returned to the air with an entirely different message. An announcer called for the police, firefighters, and anyone listening to rescue the people trapped inside.

But what could the audience make of another urgent announcement coming from the same station? Laso remembered years afterward that some people treated those real pleas as another part of the show.

It is a troubling detail, and it has limits. We do not know how many people shared that confusion. We cannot say the misunderstanding, by itself, explains the deaths. There was also an immediate obstacle that did not depend on anyone's interpretation of the broadcast.

Firefighters arrived at the scene. Interviews gathered years later describe members of the crowd threatening the crews if they tried to spray water on the building. The firefighters withdrew.

Inside, the fire continued to spread. Outside, the people able to fight it could not safely approach. These were separate failures: a genuine message not understood by some listeners, and physical intimidation of the rescuers.

Some protesters eventually said the employees could leave by a single doorway, while demanding Páez. But permission did not clear the flames. The route to that door had become dangerous.

Villalba needed another way out. Beside the newspaper stood a building with a metal roof. He jumped onto it. The roof was hot; he burned his hands and body and later lost consciousness after inhaling smoke. He was taken to a hospital.

A different announcer, Luis Beltrán, survived a fall from the burning building, according to an account later given by his daughter. Not everyone inside escaped. The surviving testimony cannot tell us the movements of every person trapped there.

From outside the city center, Pazmiño later remembered seeing flames over Quito. People who had not been inside the studio could now witness the actual disaster.

By late evening, the authorities moved to clear the streets. Later reporting describes police using tear gas around eleven and soldiers arriving to help disperse the crowd. Firefighters were finally able to reach the building, but much of its interior had already burned.

What happened there cannot be completely reconstructed from the broadcast itself. The original recording of Radio Quito's adaptation and its studio script were destroyed in the fire. Audio used in later retellings is reconstruction, not an untouched recording of the programme heard that night.

The evidence for the deaths also requires care. A contemporary newspaper report counted at least six people killed and fifteen injured. Radio Ambulante's much later investigation reported eight deaths in the fire. Other totals have circulated. The final number is not firmly established by those sources.

But several of the people who died can be named. Among them were violinist Perfecto Alvarado and pianist Raúl Molestina. They were musicians in the building, not anonymous figures in a statistic about a crowd.

The disaster also followed the people who survived. Villalba later said the burns damaged his fingerprints, while smoke had injured his lungs. Escaping the building did not make the night disappear from his life.

Páez, the artistic director, also faced consequences. A later investigation says he hid after the disaster and eventually went to court. He was acquitted after the court considered the station management's authorization of the production. His reputation, however, was badly damaged; he later moved to Venezuela.

Radio Quito resumed broadcasting in 1951. The station could return to air, but the destroyed original programme and the lives lost could not be restored.

So the answer is not that every listener believed the fake invasion, or that no one believed a real call for help. Specific people later described confusion; others threatened firefighters who tried to intervene. The broadcast triggered actions the station could not reverse by announcing that the invasion was a play.

The evening began with music interrupted by fictional danger. It ended with two musicians dead in a real fire, and rescuers struggling to reach the people who had been asking for help.
