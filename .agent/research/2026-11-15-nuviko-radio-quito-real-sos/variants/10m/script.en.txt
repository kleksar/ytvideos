How could a story about visitors from Mars leave an Ecuadorian radio station with real victims? Why did a genuine appeal for help become so difficult to understand? And how do we know what happened when the recording itself is gone?

In Quito in 1949, listening to the radio was often a shared activity. Not every household had electricity or a receiver. Neighbors could listen together, and some people heard programs through speakers outside their homes.

One of the stations they knew well was Radio Quito. The company behind it also ran El Comercio, a major Ecuadorian newspaper. Its broadcasts were a familiar source of music and information about the country.

On February twelfth, the singers Benítez and Valencia were performing. The program was interrupted by an apparent news bulletin. People listening for a concert suddenly heard that strange objects had arrived in Ecuador and that the capital was in danger.

An early newspaper account described police responding to the supposed emergency. The news was already affecting decisions outside the studio. Yet the danger described on the air existed only in a work of fiction.

Eleven years earlier, the American broadcaster Orson Welles had adapted H. G. Wells's novel The War of the Worlds for radio. In Quito, artistic director Leonardo Páez and the playwright Eduardo Alcaraz prepared an Ecuadorian adaptation of the same story.

Páez had worked as a singer, actor and writer before becoming known for his work in broadcasting. He was part of a local entertainment industry in which an evening's radio drama could bring families and neighbors together without anyone leaving the neighborhood.

For the young people listening outside a restaurant, the program was a shared experience. José Antonio Gross, who was among them, remembered it decades later. Another listener, Aurora Pazmiño, was eleven years old at the time. Her family began to pray.

José Laso was at home. He remembered his mother considering whether the family should shelter in the basement. The experiences of these households show how a fictional emergency could become part of somebody's real life, even before the audience knew it was a play.

These are individual memories, collected long after 1949. There was no reliable count of everyone who believed the invasion. It is perfectly possible for one family to have been frightened while another recognized the story as entertainment.

Radio Quito acknowledged that the invasion was fictional. For listeners who had been frightened, the clarification did not necessarily undo what they had experienced. Anger over the broadcast became part of a public crisis, and Radio Quito and El Comercio suffered a fatal attack.

Guillermo Villalba, a broadcaster invited to participate in the drama, was among the people in danger. The studio, which had been a place of work and entertainment for him, was now part of a genuine emergency.

An announcer made an actual appeal for rescue on Radio Quito. The audience had just been told that the extraordinary reports they had heard were fiction. Now it was being asked to take another urgent announcement seriously.

Laso later recalled confusion over whether the requests for help were still part of the drama. His testimony makes the reversal real without establishing how many people misunderstood the announcement. There is no surviving audience survey that could turn that recollection into a citywide statistic.

The fire was real, and the rescue effort faced serious obstacles independent of what radio listeners believed. Reports about those difficulties matter because it would be wrong to attribute the deaths simply to the audience's confusion. The emergency was no longer a question of interpreting a performance.

Villalba survived, but he was taken to hospital with serious injuries. In a later interview he described lasting damage to his hands and lungs. His survival therefore did not mean that his working life could simply return to the way it had been before the broadcast.

Another broadcaster, Luis Beltrán, also survived, according to his daughter's account given many years later. Those family recollections preserve a part of the human story that a short report about a destroyed building cannot convey.

Aurora Pazmiño remembered seeing the actual fire from a distance. The danger her family had feared earlier was imaginary. What she could now see over the city was the consequence of a real disaster, with no connection to visitors from another planet.

The early reports and later histories disagree about the precise death toll. It remains important to distinguish what is uncertain from what is known: people lost their lives, and surviving broadcasters lived with serious consequences.

The station's original recording was also lost in the fire, along with its studio script. That loss created a problem for everyone who later wanted to understand how the broadcast had sounded. A reconstructed scene cannot tell us exactly what somebody heard at their kitchen radio.

The artistic director faced a different consequence. Páez eventually appeared before a judge. He and his defense pointed to the station's authorization of the production. He was acquitted, but a legal decision could not immediately restore his position in the city's broadcasting industry.

His professional difficulties were concrete. Quito's Museo del Pasillo records that work became scarce and that he found temporary employment at another station. He continued writing plays, and a prize eventually gave him the resources to move to Venezuela with his family.

Radio Quito itself was able to resume broadcasting in 1951. Its artistic director, however, eventually settled abroad. The station's return to the air and his departure were two different outcomes of the same episode in the city's history.

Then came something that the people seeking an original script could not have found in the burned studio. In 1982, more than three decades after the broadcast, Páez published a book in Venezuela about what had happened in Quito.

It was called Los que siembran el viento, or Those Who Sow the Wind. It was a novelized account by a participant in the events. It also reproduced parts of the radio play, preserving a written version of material whose original studio copy had been destroyed.

That is a valuable distinction. The loss of the original recording does not mean the words of the play vanished from every later account. But a novel written decades afterward cannot replace the missing recording. Its author was describing an episode in which he himself had been involved.

Páez died in Venezuela in 1991. In March 2016 a publisher in Ecuador announced a presentation of the novel in Quito. The book containing his version of the events was returning to the city where the story had begun. It was a literary work, not a rediscovered recording.

The book gave readers another way to approach the story, while the memories of listeners and surviving broadcasters gave them other perspectives. Neither can establish the private reactions of an entire audience. Together they help explain why the actual call for help is remembered as such a troubling moment in radio history.

For two families there was no eventual return to ordinary life for the people they had lost. Among those who died were the violinist Perfecto Alvarado and the pianist Raúl Molestina. They had been at the station for a musical program. Their names are a part of the history that matters even though the original sound is gone.
