Why did a department store keep taking customers when its upper floor was cracking? How did changes made years before turn those cracks into a catastrophe? And why did rescuers find a nineteen-year-old employee alive sixteen days later?

In the summer of 1995, Sampoong was a luxury department store in Seoul. It was only a few years old. On June twenty-ninth, people were still shopping while staff upstairs were dealing with damage serious enough to close part of the fifth floor.

The most disturbing fact is what happened next. Some senior managers left, but customers were not told to evacuate. To understand that decision, we have to look at a building whose problems began before it opened.

The plans had been changed during construction. Wide retail floors looked good to customers, but the changes left some concrete columns smaller than the original design required. Upstairs, a proposed skating rink became a set of restaurants. The heavier construction increased the permanent load on that floor by roughly thirty-five percent, according to a later engineering study.

Then there were the air-conditioning units on the roof. Neighbours complained about their noise, and the heavy equipment was moved across the rooftop rather than lifted clear of it. Later accounts connected that move with damage to the concrete. These were not separate problems: extra weight was being carried by a structure with reduced margins of safety.

A flat concrete floor transfers weight into the columns supporting it. When the forces around a column become too great, the slab can fail at that connection. That kind of failure would eventually help explain how the upper floors came down.

But the people entering the shop on June twenty-ninth did not need an engineering report to see that something was wrong. Cracks on the top floor had widened. Management closed part of it and switched off the air conditioning. Neither action took the weight off the roof or strengthened the supports beneath.

Engineers were called in. A later account of court documents says some board members urged evacuation, but chairman Lee Joon rejected it, citing the loss of business. Police also said executives had been aware of the danger. Those are findings and reported statements about management decisions, not proof that every employee understood the structural risk.

By early evening, the danger was no longer confined to the fifth floor. Around six o'clock, a large section of the store collapsed down through the levels below. People in shopping halls and work areas were buried under concrete.

The final count was five hundred and two dead and nine hundred and thirty-seven injured. The building's failure was now a rescue problem, with a new danger: moving one slab could bring another down onto anyone still alive.

Teams searched through pockets in the wreckage. After roughly two days, they reached workers trapped together below ground. Rescuers supplied air, food and water through a pipe while they worked to open a passage. That rescue showed the collapsed floors had not crushed every space beneath them.

Yet as the days passed, the odds grew worse. On July ninth, a young part-time worker, Choi Myong-sok, was pulled out alive after more than nine days. Contemporary reports said he had managed to drink rainwater that entered his small space. His rescue proved there were still places nobody had reached.

Then another week went by.

On July fifteenth, searchers heard a response from beneath the concrete. They found nineteen-year-old Park Seung-hyun, who had worked in the store's children's clothing department. Rescuers opened a way through and carried her out after sixteen days trapped beneath the building.

At the hospital she asked for water and wanted to know the date. Contemporary accounts say she had believed far fewer days had passed. The darkness had taken away her calendar, but she had survived.

Investigators later described overloaded floors, inadequate columns and poor construction. The company's chairman and others were convicted. The decisions and failures that kept the store standing only until that June evening were finally examined in court.

Park was the last person brought out alive. When she asked what day it was, the answer was July fifteenth. The rescue ended a search; it could not undo the choice to leave the store open.
