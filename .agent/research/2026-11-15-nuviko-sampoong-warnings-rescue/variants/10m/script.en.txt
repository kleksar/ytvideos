Why did the people running a department store wait so long to evacuate their customers? How could a building only a few years old fail so completely? And how did rescuers hear a living voice beneath it sixteen days after the collapse?

To answer those questions, start with an ordinary Thursday in Seoul. June twenty-ninth, nineteen ninety-five. Sampoong Department Store had restaurants above its shopping floors, expensive goods inside and customers going about their afternoon. Nothing about a normal purchase told them what was happening to the concrete overhead.

The upper floor was cracking. Parts of it were closed. Engineers were asked to inspect the damage. Yet the store continued to sell goods. Later accounts of court documents describe senior staff discussing evacuation and chairman Lee Joon refusing it because of the business the store would lose. At that point the people shopping below were still not told to leave.

That decision would be examined after the disaster. First, though, there is a different question. Why was a large concrete building so close to failure before anyone entered it that morning?

The original construction scheme was altered to make a department store. A retail business wanted large open floors, where customers could move between displays without a forest of columns. But that space had a structural price. Some supporting columns were made smaller than the original design called for. The slabs above were expected to carry loads into a support system with less margin than it needed.

One connection carried much of the risk: the place where a concrete floor met a column. Sampoong used flat slabs. The weight of each floor had to pass through the concrete around its supports. If that joint was too weak for the load, the column could push through the slab. And when an upper slab fell, the floor below would suddenly be asked to carry its weight too. Engineers call the failure at the joint punching shear. It helped explain how the first failure could bring down so much of the building.

Not every slab in a building carries the same weight. At Sampoong, the fifth floor had originally been intended for a skating rink. It was turned into restaurants. Kitchens, fittings and heavier floor construction were added. A later engineering study calculated that the change raised the floor's permanent load by approximately thirty-five percent. That was weight resting on the structure before a single customer sat down.

Then there was machinery on the roof. Heavy cooling units were installed above the shopping levels. Neighbours complained about their noise. The equipment was moved to a different part of the roof, but accounts of the move say it was dragged across the concrete instead of being lifted over it by a crane.

The roof move added another problem to a structure with too little reserve. Even switched off, the cooling units remained heavy. Dragging them could damage concrete along the way, and their vibrations later became part of the inquiry. Investigators also found deficiencies in the supports and excessive floor loads. There was no single switch that could reverse years of accumulated changes. The question was what the managers did when the consequences became visible.

Cracks were visible near the upper level by April 1995. They were a warning, not a clock: nobody downstairs could tell from a crack exactly when the structure might fail. But the store's managers could not treat growing damage as an ordinary problem with the decor. By the last Thursday in June, the cracks had worsened while the sales floors remained open.

On the morning of June twenty-ninth, the cracking became harder to ignore. Staff saw damage spreading upstairs. Management shut sections of the fifth floor. The air-conditioning equipment was switched off after concerns about vibration. But switching off a motor does not remove its weight. Closing a restaurant does not make the reinforced concrete stronger. Customers kept shopping downstairs.

Engineers inspected the building. A later Korea Herald account of court records says an emergency board meeting began that afternoon and that some directors recommended evacuation. It also records chairman Lee Joon declining because of lost sales. Contemporary police reports attributed knowledge of the danger to company executives. These sources support a serious failure of management, without proving that every person working there shared the same knowledge or intention.

Later that afternoon, the ceiling of the fourth floor began to sink. That level was closed too. Only when the danger became impossible to ignore did the alarms sound and employees start to evacuate shoppers. Almost immediately, one of the store's two wings collapsed through the levels below. Where sales floors and work rooms had stood, slabs of broken concrete lay on top of each other.

For some people the collapse was fatal immediately. Others were caught in spaces that the falling floors had not completely crushed. The final toll reached five hundred and two dead and nine hundred and thirty-seven injured. The numbers are the final figures; early reports during the search listed much smaller death counts because so many people were still missing.

The first challenge for rescuers was locating anyone alive. The second was reaching them without causing another collapse. A heavy slab might protect a small space beneath it. Shifting that slab to open a route could destroy that space. The search involved listening, cutting through barriers and working in a structure whose remaining parts were not stable.

On July first, roughly two days after the collapse, rescue teams reached a group of workers in a basement area. Contemporary reports described more than twenty people brought out after around fifty-two hours. A pipe delivered air, water and food to trapped people while crews worked through the concrete. That rescue proved the wreckage contained pockets in which survival was possible.

Other spaces had not even been located, let alone reached with a pipe. In one of them, a young man later recalled hearing crews dig nearby and then move away. He had no way of knowing when they would return. By the time rescuers found him, they had begun clearing the wreckage with heavy machinery.

On July ninth, an excavator operator clearing broken concrete noticed someone alive beneath the debris. It was twenty-one-year-old Choi Myong-sok, a student working part-time at the store. He had been trapped for more than nine days. Finding him with machinery was one thing; getting him out was another. Rescuers had to cut through concrete and reinforcing bars to reach his confined space. This was not a man simply walking out of a hole uncovered by a machine.

Choi said that rainwater had seeped into the ruins. Contemporary reporting also described him eating pieces of cardboard when he was hungry. His father and relatives had joined the search as volunteers. He was carried out on a stretcher and reunited with his parents at the hospital. After nine days in the ruins, he said he wanted something to eat and a cola. The contrast with the July first group was striking: those people received water through a rescue pipe; Choi had survived on what found its way into the debris. No one knew which conditions the next missing person faced.

Later that week, another young survivor was brought out. After these rescues, one fact was clear: even this late in the search, removing the debris was still a search for living people.

Then came July fifteenth, sixteen days after the building had fallen. Searchers working through the wreckage heard a response. They stopped to investigate and opened a way into a trapped space. There they found a nineteen-year-old shop assistant named Park Seung-hyun. She had worked in the children's clothing department.

Park was weak but conscious when she came out. Contemporary reports describe her asking for water and wanting to know the date. She had apparently thought far fewer days had passed. Accounts differed on the exact number of hours she had spent underneath, and none established a simple, certain explanation of how she survived. But the rescue itself was clear: she was alive after more than two weeks beneath the store.

A nurse later recalled telling her the date. For Park, it was an answer she could not have worked out from the darkness.

Investigators eventually tied the collapse to inadequate supporting columns, altered loads, poor construction and overloaded slab-to-column joints. The chairman and others were convicted. These findings answered how a relatively new building could fail, but they came after the decision to delay evacuation. The people who kept shopping had not chosen the structural changes above them.

Park was the last person rescued alive from Sampoong. Five hundred and two people had died, and hundreds more were injured; her survival could not undo that. But a search that had continued through successive discoveries had reached one more person.

She asked what day it was. Outside the rubble, the answer was July fifteenth.
