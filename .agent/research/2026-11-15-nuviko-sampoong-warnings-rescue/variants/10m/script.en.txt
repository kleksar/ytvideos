Why did the people running a department store leave when the shoppers were still inside? How could a building only a few years old fail so completely? And how did rescuers hear a living voice beneath it sixteen days after the collapse?

To answer those questions, start with an ordinary Thursday in Seoul. June twenty-ninth, nineteen ninety-five. Sampoong Department Store had restaurants above its shopping floors, expensive goods inside and customers going about their afternoon. Nothing about a normal purchase told them what was happening to the concrete overhead.

The upper floor was cracking. Parts of it were closed. Engineers were asked to inspect the damage. Yet the store continued to sell goods. Later accounts of court documents describe senior staff discussing evacuation and chairman Lee Joon refusing it because of the business the store would lose. Some executives left. The people shopping below did not receive a general evacuation order.

That decision would be examined after the disaster. First, though, there is a different question. Why was a large concrete building so close to failure before anyone entered it that morning?

The original construction scheme was altered to make a department store. A retail business wanted large open floors, where customers could move between displays without a forest of columns. But that space had a structural price. Some supporting columns were made smaller than the original design called for. The slabs above were expected to carry loads into a support system with less margin than it needed.

It is worth imagining one connection: a concrete floor meets a column below it. Instead of a beam carrying everything neatly across the ceiling, the floor itself has to transfer weight into that column. Under too much stress, the concrete around the connection can break. Engineers call this punching shear. The phrase is technical, but the picture is simple: the column can punch through the floor it was meant to support.

Not every slab in a building carries the same weight. At Sampoong, the fifth floor had originally been intended for a skating rink. It was turned into restaurants. Kitchens, fittings and heavier floor construction were added. A later engineering study calculated that the change raised the floor's permanent load by approximately thirty-five percent. That was weight resting on the structure before a single customer sat down.

Then there was machinery on the roof. Heavy cooling units were installed above the shopping levels. Neighbours complained about their noise. The equipment was moved to a different part of the roof, but accounts of the move say it was dragged across the concrete instead of being lifted over it by a crane.

That mattered for more than one reason. The units were already heavy. Moving them across a roof could damage the very slab that was supposed to hold them. The units also vibrated while operating. Later reporting linked the relocation to cracks that developed and worsened. The record does not allow a narrator to turn any one of these details into the sole cause. The failure came from an accumulation of design changes, construction weaknesses and loads.

By April of nineteen ninety-five, cracks had appeared near the upper level. The store still operated. A crack does not by itself tell a shopper when a building will collapse; buildings can develop damage for different reasons. But cracks growing around a heavily loaded floor demand investigation and, when a serious failure is suspected, keeping people out of danger. At Sampoong the response did not match the risk.

On the morning of June twenty-ninth, the cracking became harder to ignore. Staff saw damage spreading upstairs. Management shut sections of the fifth floor. The air-conditioning equipment was switched off after concerns about vibration. But switching off a motor does not remove its weight. Closing a restaurant does not make the reinforced concrete stronger. Customers kept shopping downstairs.

Engineers inspected the building. A later Korea Herald account of court records says an emergency board meeting began that afternoon and that some directors recommended evacuation. It also records chairman Lee Joon declining because of lost sales. Contemporary police reports attributed knowledge of the danger to company executives. These sources support a serious failure of management, without proving that every person working there shared the same knowledge or intention.

By late afternoon, the problem was no longer limited to cracks that could be marked and watched. The upper structure was deforming. Around six in the evening, part of the building gave way. One wing came down through the levels below, leaving broken concrete slabs stacked in the place where sales floors and work rooms had stood.

For some people the collapse was fatal immediately. Others were caught in spaces that the falling floors had not completely crushed. The final toll reached five hundred and two dead and nine hundred and thirty-seven injured. The numbers are the final figures; early reports during the search listed much smaller death counts because so many people were still missing.

The first challenge for rescuers was locating anyone alive. The second was reaching them without causing another collapse. A heavy slab might protect a small space beneath it. Shifting that slab to open a route could destroy that space. The search involved listening, cutting through barriers and working in a structure whose remaining parts were not stable.

On July first, roughly two days after the collapse, rescue teams reached a group of workers in a basement area. Contemporary reports described more than twenty people brought out after around fifty-two hours. A pipe delivered air, water and food to trapped people while crews worked through the concrete. That rescue proved the wreckage contained pockets in which survival was possible.

But a successful rescue on the second day tells you little about the tenth. Time was passing without food or reliable water for those not yet reached. Workers searched enormous amounts of debris, and the tally of people missing changed as bodies were recovered. Relatives waited, while rescuers had to decide which sounds and openings were worth pursuing.

On July ninth, they found another survivor: twenty-one-year-old Choi Myong-sok, a part-time shoe salesman. He had been trapped for more than nine days. He reported drinking rainwater that leaked into the wreckage and, when hungry, eating pieces of cardboard. Those details came from contemporary interviews, not from an imagined diary of what he thought.

His father and uncle had helped search the ruins. When Choi was found, they could finally hear from him rather than wait for a list of names. He was carried out after rescuers cut through concrete around his confined space. The discovery reopened a question that had seemed settled: if one person had survived this long, who else might still be beneath the slabs?

The search continued. Another young survivor was recovered later that week. By then, any claim that all remaining people must have died was impossible to sustain simply from the passing of days. Yet the rescue work itself remained dangerous, and finding a small survivable space did not mean a machine could immediately reach it.

Then came July fifteenth, sixteen days after the building had fallen. Searchers working through the wreckage heard a response. They stopped to investigate and opened a way into a trapped space. There they found a nineteen-year-old shop assistant named Park Seung-hyun. She had worked in the children's clothing department.

Park was brought out alive, weak but conscious. Contemporary hospital accounts describe her asking for water and for the date. She apparently believed she had been trapped for far fewer days. Doctors expressed surprise at her condition; reports disagreed about how many exact hours had passed and could not establish a simple explanation of how she endured so long. There is no reason to invent one for her.

One nurse recalled telling Park what day it was. It was July fifteenth. This small question placed her rescue in a reality she had not been able to measure from the darkness.

There was a second story developing beyond the rubble. Investigators were piecing together why the building had failed: undersized supports, altered loads, defective construction and concrete floor connections that were not able to withstand the stresses. That is different from blaming the disaster on the people who were in the store. Their presence did not create the structural defects. The safety decisions belonged to those responsible for the building.

The chairman of Sampoong and other people involved were convicted. Courts considered decisions made before and on the day of the collapse; later engineering work explained more precisely how the concrete failed. Accountability and explanation matter, but neither changes the fact that the building was kept open as the warning signs grew.

The rescue of Park was the last time someone came out of that wreckage alive. Its force as an ending is not that everyone survived: five hundred and two people did not. It is that on July fifteenth, after more than two weeks of searching, someone beneath the concrete could still ask an ordinary question.

What day is it? For Park, the answer marked the moment she was finally outside.
