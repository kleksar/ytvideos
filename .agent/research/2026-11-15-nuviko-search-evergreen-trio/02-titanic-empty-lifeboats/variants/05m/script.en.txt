Why did a lifeboat built to carry sixty-five people leave the Titanic with just twenty-seven? Who decided when to lower it? And if every lifeboat had been full, could everyone have survived?

The answer begins on a deck where the ship still looked enormous and the boats looked frighteningly small.

Titanic struck an iceberg at 11:40 on the night of April fourteenth, 1912. After midnight, crewmen began preparing the lifeboats. Stewards were waking passengers, helping them into lifejackets and directing them upwards. But the work on deck did not move at the same speed. At first, only a few passengers had reached the boats. The crew was arriving gradually too.

Then the first boats began to move.

At about twelve forty-five, lifeboat number seven was lowered on the starboard side. The British investigation later counted only twenty-seven occupants in a boat designed for sixty-five. Number six left with about twenty-eight, also against a capacity of sixty-five. A smaller emergency boat, number one, carried just twelve of the forty people it could hold.

Those numbers sound like a decision to abandon people. What actually happened was more complicated.

Early in the evacuation, the drop from Titanic's boat deck to the water was roughly sixty-five feet. Passengers were being asked to leave a brightly lit ocean liner and enter a wooden boat suspended over black water. Witnesses described people who would not board. Some women did not want to leave their husbands. Not everyone on deck understood how little time the ship had left.

But hesitation does not explain everything. The evacuation had not been properly rehearsed. Crew assignments were posted in lists, yet some of the men had not read them and did not know their boat. Officers were directing several stations while deckhands worked on covers, ropes and lowering gear. Escaping steam was so loud that Second Officer Charles Lightoller later said he had to give instructions with his hands.

There was another uncertainty. Some officers feared that a fully loaded lifeboat could buckle during lowering. The fear was later shown to be unfounded: other boats were successfully lowered with far more people aboard. Yet while the decision was being made, each boat needed to be brought down from a very high deck, and a cautious departure could look safer than waiting.

The crew even had a plan to collect more people after lowering. Some partly filled boats were instructed to wait near doors in the ship's side, where passengers might be taken aboard closer to the water. Those doors were never opened. The empty places that might have been filled there stayed empty.

Now the problem had changed. A boat already on the sea could not simply be pulled back up to the deck and reloaded. More passengers were reaching the boat stations, while the ship was settling lower and the opportunity to launch each remaining boat was shrinking.

Could the crew have done better? The inquiry said better organization could have saved more lives. It did not claim that every empty seat could necessarily have been filled. The exact gain is unknowable from the evidence.

And there was still a second failure, built into the ship before the iceberg appeared.

Titanic carried twenty boats: fourteen standard lifeboats, two emergency boats and four collapsible boats. Together their rated capacity was just eleven hundred and seventy-eight people. More than two thousand were aboard.

Even had all twenty boats launched perfectly full, there were nowhere near enough places for everyone at once. Worse, two collapsible boats were still being handled when the ship went down and floated away instead of being properly launched.

So the empty seats tell only half the story. The evacuation lost places that existed. The ship had never carried enough places in the first place.

The disaster changed requirements for lifeboat accommodation and crew preparation. But the lesson on that deck was already visible: an empty bench could be a lost chance, and a full boat still could not solve the shortage.
