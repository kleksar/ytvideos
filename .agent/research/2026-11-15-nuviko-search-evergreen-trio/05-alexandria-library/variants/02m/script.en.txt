Who burned the Library of Alexandria? Was it Julius Caesar, a Christian crowd, or the conquerors who arrived centuries later? If there was one famous disaster, why do the surviving sources disagree?

In Egypt, the Ptolemaic kings supported scholars who collected and copied papyrus texts. The library was connected to the Mouseion, a royal center of learning. Its strength depended on more than shelves: scholars needed funding, copies, and rulers willing to keep the work going.

That support could fail. Around 145 BC, political turmoil drove scholars out of Alexandria. The books did not all disappear that year, but the institution lost some of the people who sustained it.

Then Caesar fought in Alexandria in 48 BC. Ships near the harbor burned. Plutarch, writing long after the event, said the fire destroyed the great library. Cassius Dio instead described books burning in harbor warehouses. Those accounts do not establish whether the main collection went up in flames. Scholars still disagree about the scale of the loss.

In AD 391, a crowd destroyed the Serapeum temple, associated in some accounts with another collection. The temple's destruction is documented; which books remained there is disputed. And the story that seventh-century Muslim conquerors burned the library comes from writers centuries later. It is not reliable evidence of that event.

There is no securely identified final arsonist. Fires and violence damaged Alexandria, while royal support changed and papyrus needed repeated copying. Some knowledge survived elsewhere; we cannot count what vanished.

The library's lasting lesson is also its hardest: a collection can disappear before anyone knows which book was the last to be lost.
