Who burned the Library of Alexandria, and why do so many histories give a different name? If one fire destroyed the greatest collection of books in the ancient world, shouldn't we know when it happened? To understand the problem, begin in Alexandria, a city built where the Mediterranean met Egypt. In the third century before Christ, the Ptolemaic kings backed a remarkable scholarly institution beside their royal court. It was connected with the Mouseion, where scholars studied, debated, translated, and collected books on papyrus scrolls. The ambition was enormous: bring knowledge from across the Greek-speaking world into one place and copy it. But the word 'library' makes us picture a single building filled with shelves waiting for one disaster. The ancient evidence points to a more complicated institution, with collections, scholars, storage rooms, and changing royal support. That matters because you can burn a room without necessarily destroying every book or ending every scholarly activity. And you can lose a great collection without ever seeing a spectacular blaze on the night it disappears. Long before the best-known accusations, the library's political foundation was already less secure than it had been. Around 145 BC, a struggle within the royal family drove many scholars away from Alexandria. The institution's books did not all suddenly vanish, but royal support and the people who kept scholarship alive were changing. That is the first clue: a library is more than a warehouse of objects someone must guard. It needs money, trained people, new copies, and a reason for rulers to keep paying for them. Then comes the first famous suspect: Julius Caesar, fighting in Alexandria in 48 BC. During the struggle, a fire spread from ships near the harbor, and ancient accounts describe damage to books. Plutarch, writing more than a century later, says that this fire destroyed the great library. That is a genuine ancient claim, but it is not a surviving eyewitness inventory of everything lost. Another historian, Cassius Dio, speaks instead about books burned in storage buildings near the harbor. That difference leaves a very large question: were the main collection and the book stores the same place? We cannot settle it by drawing one enormous library building and setting the whole thing alight. Caesar's fire may have caused serious losses; the scale and location remain debated by historians. And there is evidence that intellectual activity associated with the Mouseion continued in Alexandria afterward. That does not prove the famous collection survived intact; it does undermine a neat, universally agreed ending in one night. Several centuries pass, and the story offers another possible ending, this time in AD 391. Christian authorities and their supporters destroyed the Serapeum, a major temple connected in some accounts with another book collection. The destruction of the temple is part of the record; what exactly remained of its books is much harder to establish. Some scholars regard this as a possible major loss of manuscripts, while others question how much library was still there. That distinction is frustrating, but it matters: evidence for a ruined temple is not an inventory of burned scrolls. Then a third story appears, placing the final destruction after the Arab conquest of Egypt in the seventh century. In the familiar version, a new ruler orders books destroyed because they are supposedly unnecessary beside sacred scripture. But the dramatic account was recorded centuries after the conquest, and historians treat it as highly unreliable. So the three famous culprits are not three equally solid reports describing the same clear event. We have a harbor fire, a later temple destruction, and a much later story whose evidence does not stand up. Meanwhile, there is a less theatrical way for books to disappear, one that happens even without an attacker. Papyrus wears out. If nobody copies a text, the last physical copy can decay or simply be lost. A royal court may pay scholars for generations, then change its priorities, dismiss people, or let collections scatter. Books can be moved, borrowed, damaged, recopied elsewhere, or slowly become impossible to trace in the surviving sources. None of these everyday processes tells us precisely how many scrolls Alexandria lost or when the last one disappeared. Even the enormous totals often quoted for the library are ancient estimates, not a catalogue we can check today. That is why a claim that one fire erased every work of ancient knowledge goes much further than our evidence. Knowledge survived in other places and copies, although an unknown amount of scholarship was almost certainly lost. The loss is real, yet turning it into a single dramatic night can hide what the sources actually say. If you ask which fire destroyed Alexandria's books, historians can point to documented fires and destruction in the city. If you ask who ended the Library of Alexandria, there is no one person and one date we can confidently name. The answer is a longer, less tidy history of political change, damage, uncertain collections, and the work of copying. And that changes the question the library leaves us with: not who struck the match, but what keeps knowledge alive.
