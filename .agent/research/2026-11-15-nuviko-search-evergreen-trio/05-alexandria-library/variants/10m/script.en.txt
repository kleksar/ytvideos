Who burned the Library of Alexandria, and why do so many histories give a different name? If one fire destroyed the greatest collection of books in the ancient world, shouldn't we know when it happened? To understand the problem, begin in Alexandria, a city built where the Mediterranean met Egypt. In the third century before Christ, the Ptolemaic kings backed a remarkable scholarly institution beside their royal court. It was connected with the Mouseion, where scholars studied, debated, translated, and collected books on papyrus scrolls. The ambition was enormous: bring knowledge from across the Greek-speaking world into one place and copy it.

A later writer, Galen, told of books found on arriving ships being copied for the Alexandrian collection. That is an ancient report, not an eyewitness camera recording the founding of the library. It nevertheless shows what made the project remarkable: finding one manuscript was only the beginning. Scholars had to compare versions, and scribes had to make new copies. One work could occupy several rolls, while a roll could be lost without the work disappearing everywhere. These small distinctions matter when a story later claims that all knowledge vanished in one fire.

 But the word 'library' makes us picture a single building filled with shelves waiting for one disaster. The ancient evidence points to a more complicated institution, with collections, scholars, storage rooms, and changing royal support. That matters because you can burn a room without necessarily destroying every book or ending every scholarly activity.

We cannot place the complete library on an accurate modern floor plan. Even the impressive ancient totals of scrolls are disputed estimates, not a surviving shelf-by-shelf register. A manuscript stored for copying beside the harbor might be different from a text being studied near the palace. When an ancient writer refers to books, he may be describing physical rolls, not a whole institution. This is why a vivid reconstruction of one vast reading hall can be misleading. It gives the fire a shape that the evidence cannot securely supply.

 And you can lose a great collection without ever seeing a spectacular blaze on the night it disappears. Long before the best-known accusations, the library's political foundation was already less secure than it had been. Around 145 BC, a struggle within the royal family drove many scholars away from Alexandria. The institution's books did not all suddenly vanish, but royal support and the people who kept scholarship alive were changing.

Ptolemy VIII's expulsion of intellectuals was a political act with a scholarly consequence. It disrupted people whose work made the collections useful. A reader can still enter a room of manuscripts after a scholar has left; what may disappear is the ongoing effort to interpret and preserve them. We do not know how many scrolls were affected or exactly what every scholar took away. That is the important limit. Here the problem is not a fire at all: a political decision changes the institution before any famous culprit appears.

 That is the first clue: a library is more than a warehouse of objects someone must guard. It needs money, trained people, new copies, and a reason for rulers to keep paying for them. Then comes the first famous suspect: Julius Caesar, fighting in Alexandria in 48 BC. During the struggle, a fire spread from ships near the harbor, and ancient accounts describe damage to books. Plutarch, writing more than a century later, says that this fire destroyed the great library. That is a genuine ancient claim, but it is not a surviving eyewitness inventory of everything lost. Another historian, Cassius Dio, speaks instead about books burned in storage buildings near the harbor.

Plutarch and Dio wrote many years after the fighting, not as officials counting books the following morning. The distinction in their descriptions is more than a translation curiosity. It changes what was damaged. One modern historian, Gjert Vestrheim, argues that Caesar's fire destroyed the Ptolemaic library and rejects the rival later destruction stories. Other scholars read the surviving evidence more cautiously. The disagreement cannot be solved by treating either author's short report as a complete inventory. Caesar's fire is a real event; the fate of the entire collection remains a separate question.

 That difference leaves a very large question: were the main collection and the book stores the same place? We cannot settle it by drawing one enormous library building and setting the whole thing alight. Caesar's fire may have caused serious losses; the scale and location remain debated by historians. And there is evidence that intellectual activity associated with the Mouseion continued in Alexandria afterward.

For example, the geographer Strabo described the Mouseion in the Roman period after Caesar. That offers a clue that the scholarly institution was still known, but it does not establish that the original collection survived intact. A library could have suffered major losses, acquired new texts, or continued in a different form. So the later presence of scholars is not an easy acquittal of Caesar, just as Plutarch's wording is not an eyewitness death certificate for every scroll. Both claims go beyond what their sources can prove.

 That does not prove the famous collection survived intact; it does undermine a neat, universally agreed ending in one night. Several centuries pass, and the story offers another possible ending, this time in AD 391. Christian authorities and their supporters destroyed the Serapeum, a major temple connected in some accounts with another book collection. The destruction of the temple is part of the record; what exactly remained of its books is much harder to establish. Some scholars regard this as a possible major loss of manuscripts, while others question how much library was still there.

The disagreement is substantial, not a matter of which date looks better on screen. Dirk Rohmann has argued that accounts connected with the Serapeum support the destruction of a library there. Vestrheim has questioned whether a distinct daughter library in that temple can be established at all. The temple's destruction in 391 is one event. Whether a functioning collection was still inside, and whether it descended from the earlier royal library, are separate historical claims. A picture of broken columns can establish neither what stood on the shelves nor what happened to the last surviving copies.

 That distinction is frustrating, but it matters: evidence for a ruined temple is not an inventory of burned scrolls. Then a third story appears, placing the final destruction after the Arab conquest of Egypt in the seventh century. In the familiar version, a new ruler orders books destroyed because they are supposedly unnecessary beside sacred scripture. But the dramatic account was recorded centuries after the conquest, and historians treat it as highly unreliable.

The later version has exactly the ingredients a memorable story needs: a powerful ruler, a simple order, and a spectacular method of destruction. What it lacks is contemporary documentation connecting those actions with an identifiable surviving Ptolemaic collection. It is especially important not to stage its attributed words as an authentic quotation from the conquest. Here the confidence level is different from the documented temple destruction or the harbor fire. These are not three equal eyewitness accounts from three different centuries.

 So the three famous culprits are not three equally solid reports describing the same clear event. We have a harbor fire, a later temple destruction, and a much later story whose evidence does not stand up. Meanwhile, there is a less theatrical way for books to disappear, one that happens even without an attacker. Papyrus wears out.

Imagine the difference between two otherwise identical texts. One has been copied in several cities. The other survives on a single damaged roll. A fire might destroy the first roll without erasing the work, while ordinary neglect might erase the second forever. This is an explanatory possibility, not a claim that we know the last owner or final day of a particular Alexandrian manuscript. A library needs people and resources to keep that chain of copies moving. Its buildings are only one part of the story.

 If nobody copies a text, the last physical copy can decay or simply be lost. A royal court may pay scholars for generations, then change its priorities, dismiss people, or let collections scatter. Books can be moved, borrowed, damaged, recopied elsewhere, or slowly become impossible to trace in the surviving sources. None of these everyday processes tells us precisely how many scrolls Alexandria lost or when the last one disappeared. Even the enormous totals often quoted for the library are ancient estimates, not a catalogue we can check today.

Later generations preserved a great deal of Greek and Egyptian learning in new copies and other centers. Many ancient works survive only in quotations or titles, and many others are lost. But we cannot assign each missing text to Caesar, the Serapeum, the conquest, or natural decay. Nor can we calculate how many inventions humanity would have gained if a specific building had survived. The scale of cultural loss may be immense without those precise numbers being knowable. The honest payoff is evidence ranked by strength, rather than a dramatic but untestable number.

 That is why a claim that one fire erased every work of ancient knowledge goes much further than our evidence. Knowledge survived in other places and copies, although an unknown amount of scholarship was almost certainly lost. The loss is real, yet turning it into a single dramatic night can hide what the sources actually say. If you ask which fire destroyed Alexandria's books, historians can point to documented fires and destruction in the city. If you ask who ended the Library of Alexandria, there is no one person and one date we can confidently name. The answer is a longer, less tidy history of political change, damage, uncertain collections, and the work of copying. And that changes the question the library leaves us with: not who struck the match, but what keeps knowledge alive.
