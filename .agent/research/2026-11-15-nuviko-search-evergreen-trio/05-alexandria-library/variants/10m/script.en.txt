Who burned the Library of Alexandria? Why does one ancient writer blame Julius Caesar, while another story points to a temple destroyed centuries later? And if the library vanished in Caesar's time, why did a Roman emperor subsequently send scribes to Alexandria to recover books? To answer those questions, we need to follow the people who collected the scrolls, the rulers who paid them, and the accounts that survived after the collection itself disappeared.

In the third century BC, Alexandria was the capital of the Ptolemies. Their palace complex stood near the Mediterranean, and their scholars had access to a city where ships brought people and manuscripts from distant places. The royal institution known as the Mouseion supported a community devoted to learning. Its associated library gave scholars an opportunity to compare texts that might otherwise have remained in separate towns or private collections. Bringing those texts together took money, but keeping them useful took skilled work.

Galen, a physician writing much later, tells a revealing story about the royal collection. Books arriving on ships were copied, and according to his account the originals went into the collection while the copies went back to their owners. We cannot independently follow a particular scroll from the ship to the library today. But the reported practice shows why collecting books was a process rather than a single grand opening: someone had to acquire, identify and reproduce each text. Several rolls could contain one work, and several copies could preserve that work in different places.

That system depended on the royal court. Around 145 BC, conflict within the Ptolemaic dynasty led Ptolemy VIII to drive scholars away from Alexandria. There is no surviving inventory of what happened to their books. The expulsion matters for a different reason: the people who could interpret and compare difficult texts were no longer guaranteed a place to work. A new ruler could interrupt generations of scholarship without setting a single scroll on fire.

Then a struggle for the Egyptian throne brought Caesar into the city in 48 BC. He had sided with Cleopatra against her brother Ptolemy XIII, and fighting broke out near the harbor. Caesar himself later described how he burned ships because his men could not defend them. His explanation concerns a military decision; that passage says nothing about a library. For the fate of the books, therefore, we have to move from the account of a participant to writers who lived much later.

Plutarch, writing more than a century afterward, says that the fire spread and destroyed the great library. That is the accusation that made Caesar the most famous suspect. But Cassius Dio, writing later still, describes books among the things lost around the docks and storage buildings. The Greek words in the two passages are not identical. In English translations they can look much more alike than they really are. Dio's reference to books does not securely identify the royal library building.

There is no reliable floor plan showing the exact location of every part of the collection. So neither the size of the harbor fire nor the number of books mentioned by later authors can tell us which rooms were lost. The historian Gjert Vestrheim argues that Caesar did destroy the Ptolemaic library. Other scholars interpret the evidence more cautiously. A serious loss in the harbor is plausible; the complete destruction of the royal collection remains disputed. What happened next in Alexandria gives us a different kind of clue.

The geographer Strabo describes the Mouseion in the Roman period. His account mentions a covered space for walking, a seating area and a building where scholars ate together. They shared property, and the official responsible for their institution was no longer appointed by the Ptolemaic kings but by the Roman ruler. Strabo does not give us a list of the library's surviving rolls. What he does describe is a scholarly community that still existed under a new government.

And there is an even more surprising piece of evidence from the first century AD. According to Suetonius, the emperor Domitian spent heavily on restoring libraries that had been destroyed by fire. He looked for replacement manuscripts in different places and sent people to Alexandria to copy and correct texts. The city that had supposedly lost its entire store of knowledge was now a destination for people trying to recover books lost elsewhere. That is a documented reversal in the ancient accounts, not proof that every original Ptolemaic manuscript survived.

The difference is important. Strabo gives evidence about an institution and its members. Suetonius gives evidence about the availability of manuscripts and expertise. Neither identifies which of the older library's books were still there. Perhaps some collections were rebuilt; perhaps others had never been damaged. We cannot choose between those possibilities simply because they would make the story tidier. For the next famous catastrophe, we have to leave the royal quarter and examine another site.

The Serapeum was a sanctuary dedicated to Serapis. Ancient and later accounts connect it with books, but it was not simply another name for the Mouseion. In AD 391, under the Christian Roman Empire, the sanctuary was destroyed. The fifth-century church historian Socrates describes the conflict surrounding the destruction, the role of the bishop Theophilus and imperial support for demolishing temples. His account is explicit about the religious conflict and the destruction of the site. It does not enumerate a collection of scrolls burned there.

Socrates does, however, name two people who left Alexandria amid the upheaval: the grammarians Helladius and Ammonius. He explains why he knows their names. In his youth, they had taught him in Constantinople. The record of another Alexandrian crisis therefore preserves a concrete human consequence, even when it tells us nothing certain about the contents of a library. Some scholars left the city, and at least these two carried their learning into another place. The account does not say that they carried particular books out of the Serapeum.

Was a library destroyed with the sanctuary? Dirk Rohmann reads the evidence as supporting the loss of a collection there, while Vestrheim questions whether a separate daughter library can be established at all. The temple's demolition is much better documented than the fate of its holdings. For anyone investigating the great royal library, there is an extra difficulty: even evidence that books were destroyed in the Serapeum would not by itself establish that they were the surviving original royal collection.

The third popular accusation belongs to a later political world. Muslim armies conquered Egypt in the seventh century. Centuries afterward, writers circulated a story in which a ruler ordered Alexandria's books destroyed on religious grounds. The story offers a neat ending, complete with a culprit and a reason. Yet the accounts are far removed from the conquest, and no contemporary record securely ties the alleged order to an identifiable surviving royal library. Historians generally regard that version as unreliable. It cannot fill the gap left by the earlier evidence.

So we can name the fire associated with Caesar, the destruction of the Serapeum, and the late legend. We cannot put all three on a timeline as established stages in the burning of one collection. And we have something the usual sequence of disasters misses: a later account of scribes traveling to Alexandria to recover books, and a church historian who learned from scholars who had left the city. Knowledge traveled across political borders and generations even when its institutions were disrupted.

Think of the practical problem from a copyist's point of view. If a work exists in several independent copies, a fire at one site does not necessarily erase it. If only one copy is left, it can disappear through damage or neglect without anyone recording the event. Papyrus deteriorates, and continued preservation requires fresh materials, time and readers who value the text. This explains how an institution's surviving books and the survival of individual works can follow different paths. It does not tell us how the last copy of any particular Alexandrian text was lost.

Many ancient writings survive only in fragments, quotations or titles. Others have disappeared altogether. We cannot say which missing work was lost in which Alexandrian disaster, and we cannot calculate the inventions that might have been made if a particular library had remained intact. The uncertainty does not make the losses trivial. It means their scale cannot be recovered from the dramatic image of a single burning building.

Who burned the Library of Alexandria, then? Caesar's own words establish a fire at the harbor, and later testimony connects it with lost books and possibly the royal library. The Serapeum really was destroyed, but its books and their relationship to the earlier collection are disputed. The conquest story does not have reliable contemporary support. No surviving source securely identifies one final destroyer of all the books people now associate with Alexandria.

Remember the scribes whom Domitian sent to Alexandria. Their task was to replace what another fire had taken away. The surviving record cannot tell us the names of every book they copied, or how many originals from the Ptolemies remained. It does give us a real answer to what kept some ancient knowledge alive after the fires: somebody still had a copy, and somebody else went to the trouble of making another.
