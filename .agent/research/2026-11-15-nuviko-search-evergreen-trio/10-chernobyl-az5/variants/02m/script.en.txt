Why could pressing the emergency shutdown button at Chernobyl make the reactor more dangerous? And what made its safety system behave that way?

In April 1986, engineers at reactor four were preparing a test. If the plant lost its external electricity, could a turbine that was slowing down keep cooling pumps powered until diesel generators started? The test concerned the electricity supply. The crew did not set out to test how much damage a reactor could survive.

But the reactor's power fell far below the planned level. Bringing it back up was difficult because xenon, a product of nuclear fission, was absorbing neutrons. Operators withdrew many control rods to maintain roughly two hundred megawatts of thermal power. The display could look steady while the margin for controlling the reactor was dangerously small. That margin was not clearly shown to them in real time.

Then there was the reactor's design. In these conditions, water turning into steam could increase the nuclear reaction, producing more heat and more steam. The emergency rods were meant to stop that reaction. So how could pushing them in make things worse?

Each rod combined a neutron-absorbing section with a graphite displacer. When some rods began descending from high positions, the graphite first displaced neutron-absorbing water near the bottom of the core. For a brief moment, that movement could add reactivity instead of removing it.

At about 1:23:40 a.m., somebody pressed AZ-5. Investigators have not established precisely why it was pressed at that instant. But in this unstable configuration, the rods' first movement could help trigger a rapid power surge. The absorbing sections did not take effect quickly enough. The reactor was destroyed in violent explosions.

People responding to the disaster faced radiation hazards, and nearby Pripyat was evacuated. Later investigations examined not just operator decisions, but concealed design weaknesses and inadequate safety information. Other RBMK reactors were modified.

AZ-5 was not a button that ordered an explosion. It was an emergency stop whose hardware could briefly increase the very reaction it was supposed to end. The real failure was that this possibility had been built into a safety system.
