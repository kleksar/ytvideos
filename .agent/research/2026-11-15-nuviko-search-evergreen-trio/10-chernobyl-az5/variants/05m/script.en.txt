Why could an emergency shutdown button make a nuclear reactor more dangerous? What did the operators at Chernobyl know before they pressed it, and what was happening inside the core that their instruments could not show them?

On the night of April twenty-sixth, nineteen eighty-six, reactor four at Chernobyl was preparing to shut down for maintenance. Before that, the crew had an electrical problem to test. If outside power failed, diesel generators would need time to start. Could a turbine, still spinning as it slowed, supply electricity to the water pumps during that gap? Nobody was supposed to test whether the reactor itself could survive a surge.

The test had already been delayed by the electricity grid's demand for power. When the team finally lowered reactor output, it dropped far below the level planned for the experiment. Around thirty megawatts thermal was a tiny part of its normal output. Bringing it back up was not as simple as turning a dial.

Inside the fuel, xenon-135 was absorbing neutrons. After the sharp reduction in power, its effect made it harder to sustain the chain reaction. Operators pulled out control rods to overcome that resistance. They got the reactor stable at roughly two hundred thermal megawatts. But stability on a power meter was not the same thing as a safe ability to control it.

Too many rods had been withdrawn. The remaining operating reactivity margin was below the required limit, and that margin was not conveniently displayed in real time. The people in the control room lacked a straightforward indication of how little room for control remained.

Now the real danger was hidden in two parts of the RBMK design. The first was water.

Water flowed past the fuel to carry heat away. It also absorbed some neutrons. Graphite blocks in the reactor slowed neutrons, helping the chain reaction continue. Under the conditions created that night, when water in a channel became steam, it absorbed fewer neutrons. That could increase reactor power. More power meant more heat; more heat could produce more steam. A small change could feed itself instead of calming down. This positive void effect was especially dangerous with the core already in an unfavorable low-power state.

The second part was meant to save the reactor: the emergency control rods.

Their job sounds simple. Push neutron absorbers into the core, and the chain reaction slows. But each of these rods also carried a graphite displacer. When a rod was pulled far up, there was water beneath that graphite section. As the rod started to descend, the graphite moved into the lower part of the channel before the neutron absorber reached it.

That mattered because the graphite displaced water which had been absorbing neutrons. For a moment, inserting a rod could raise reactivity near the bottom of the core. Only as the absorbing section advanced would the rod perform its intended shutdown function. The size of that initial effect depended on where the rods began and how the reactor's power was distributed.

By this point the test was underway. Steam had been cut off from a turbine so engineers could measure what its remaining rotation supplied to the pumps. At about 1:23:40 in the morning, the AZ-5 shutdown button was pressed. Why exactly then? The major post-accident inquiry could not establish the reason. A later account described it as a normal way to finish the test; the evidence does not justify portraying the press as a reaction to an already visible runaway.

The critical question is what happened next.

With many rods high above the core, their graphite displacers entered the lower regions first. Instead of immediately reducing all nuclear reactions, the movement could add reactivity there. Rising power also encouraged water to turn to steam, reinforcing the feedback that made the RBMK vulnerable. The absorbing portions of the rods were moving too slowly to regain control before an extremely rapid power excursion damaged the reactor.

Explosions destroyed the fourth reactor. They were not a nuclear-weapon detonation. The final seconds cannot be reconstructed with equal certainty in every detail, but investigators could identify the dangerous combination: a depleted control margin, powerful feedback from steam, and a shutdown system whose first movement could briefly increase the reaction.

Outside the control room, workers and firefighters faced severe radiation hazards, and the nearby city of Pripyat was evacuated. The damage would affect people and land far beyond the plant.

There had been a warning. The unusual positive effect of inserting RBMK rods had been noticed at another plant, Ignalina, in nineteen eighty-three. Intended restrictions on fully withdrawing rods were not effectively implemented before Chernobyl. The risk was not merely an unforeseeable coincidence discovered after the explosions.

Early official explanations emphasized operator violations. Later safety assessments put greater weight on the reactor's design, poor communication of hazards, and weaknesses in safety culture. The operating decisions still mattered; the hardware was not innocent either. Other RBMK reactors were modified, including changes to the control rods.

So why did AZ-5 make things worse? It did not send an instruction to explode. At that particular moment, a safety command moved graphite into places where it could briefly boost the reaction, while steam feedback magnified the rise. The real shock is not that someone pressed an emergency button. It is that the emergency system had been designed with a first movement that could work against its own purpose.
