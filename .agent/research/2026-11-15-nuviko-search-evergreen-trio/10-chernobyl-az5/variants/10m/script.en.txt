Why did pressing the emergency shutdown button at Chernobyl seem to make the reactor more dangerous? What happened inside the core in those seconds, and why was a shutdown system able to do the opposite of its job?

On April twenty-sixth, nineteen eighty-six, reactor four at Chernobyl was due for a routine maintenance shutdown. Before stopping it, engineers planned to repeat an electrical test. If the station lost its external power supply, could the remaining rotation of a turbine keep cooling pumps supplied until diesel generators took over?

They had tried a similar test the previous year. The electricity from the slowing turbine had fallen away too quickly, so new voltage regulators had been developed. There was a practical reason to try again. The problem was that the electrical team and the people responsible for nuclear safety had not coordinated the new test adequately. The work on the turbine could change conditions inside the reactor that supplied it.

First, the team needed to reduce the reactor's power, and the plan was to perform the test before the final shutdown. That process did not proceed cleanly: the electricity grid needed the plant's output, so the reduction was delayed. The plant remained at roughly half its thermal power for hours, and the team could resume lowering it only late that night. The delay changed which shift was working, while a complex test programme was already being carried out with poor coordination.

Then the reactor's power fell unexpectedly much lower than the test's intended level. At one point it was near thirty megawatts of thermal power, a tiny fraction of the reactor's normal output. Operators began trying to bring it back up, but the reactor did not respond the way a simple generator would. Inside the fuel, a substance called xenon-135 was absorbing neutrons, making the chain reaction harder to sustain. That xenon had been produced by earlier fission reactions; when power fell, the balance that usually consumed it changed. The more it absorbed neutrons, the harder it became to raise power again without changing the reactor's controls.

So control rods were withdrawn to overcome that effect and hold the reactor at a usable level. By around one o'clock, the power was stabilized at roughly two hundred megawatts thermal, still below the original plan.

On the power display, the number was steady again. Yet the operating reactivity margin, a measure related to the control rods remaining in the core, had fallen below the required minimum. The crew did not have a convenient continuous reading of that margin. Nor was its value included in the automatic protection system. The power meter could tell them how much heat the reactor was producing, but it could not tell them whether the safety system would stop the reaction reliably. That is the turning point in this test.

There were also changes in the circulation of cooling water. To see why, imagine a tall collection of fuel channels threaded through blocks of graphite. The graphite slowed neutrons, helping the nuclear reaction continue; the circulating water carried heat out of the fuel. That water also absorbed some neutrons, so it had a second role besides cooling.

Now comes the unusual feature: if liquid water in those channels turned into steam, fewer neutrons were absorbed by the water. In the operating conditions that developed that night, more steam could mean more nuclear reactions, more heat, and still more steam. Engineers call this a positive void coefficient, with 'void' referring to steam bubbles taking the place of liquid water. It is not accurate to say that steam always makes every reactor more powerful; this was a characteristic of that RBMK design. At low power and with the core in an unfavorable state, the effect could become especially dangerous.

Yet even that does not fully explain why the emergency shutdown itself could add to the problem. For that, look at the control rods, the long devices meant to absorb neutrons and slow the chain reaction. In many explanations of reactors, the story ends there: lower the absorber rods and power drops. But these RBMK rods had another piece attached to them, made of graphite. When a rod was pulled far out of the core, its graphite displacer occupied part of the channel, with water below it.

As the rod first moved down, that graphite could push out the water at the bottom of the channel. Because that water had absorbed neutrons, replacing it with graphite could briefly make reactions increase in that lower region. Only farther into the movement would the neutron-absorbing part of the rod make the intended overall reduction.

The effect depended on the starting positions of the rods and the distribution of power inside the core. Under different conditions the initial rise might not have led to a disaster. The full insertion was also relatively slow. By the time an emergency stop was needed, the protection system had to work in the actual state of the reactor, not the state assumed by a designer.

The test itself began shortly after one twenty-three in the morning, as steam was cut off from a turbine and it began slowing. The experimenters were watching what its remaining motion could do for the electrical supply to the cooling pumps. At about one twenty-three forty, the emergency shutdown command was given. Why that button was pressed at that exact moment is not conclusively established by the major post-accident investigations. One later account described it as part of ending the test; presenting it as a panicked response to a visible surge would go beyond what is known. What we can explain is the dangerous response the command could produce in the reactor's existing condition.

Many rods were high above the active region, and the graphite displacers entered parts of the fuel channels first. That introduced positive reactivity near the bottom of the core, just where the power distribution made the response especially hazardous. As power rose, water could boil into more steam, amplifying the positive feedback that was already a design vulnerability. The neutron-absorbing sections of the rods could not restore control before the rapidly escalating process damaged the core.

The reactor suffered a catastrophic power excursion and destructive explosions that tore open its structure. This was not an atomic-bomb detonation; the plant was destroyed through an accident involving reactor power, pressure, and violent mechanical failure. The precise sequence of the final destructive events is still not reconstructed with equal certainty in every detail. Investigators could establish that the shutdown design was capable of contributing to the surge. They could not establish every detail of how the final destruction unfolded.

At the plant, workers and firefighters faced severe radiation hazards while trying to respond. Beyond the plant lay Pripyat, where many station employees lived with their families. The reactor was now releasing radioactive material into the environment, and stopping the electrical test had become the least of anyone's concerns.

On April twenty-seventh, an announcement on the local radio told residents they would have to leave temporarily. Officials instructed them to prepare documents, essential belongings and food. Buses arrived to take people away. By the end of that afternoon tens of thousands of residents had been transported out of the town. The word temporary belonged to the announcement; the evacuation was followed by long-term resettlement. The consequences of the failure in the control room had reached the workers' own homes.

In nineteen eighty-six, the first international safety review placed considerable weight on human errors and violations of operating rules. But that was not the last report. More analytical work and evidence became available, including two reports from Soviet expert committees. In nineteen ninety-two the international advisory group published an update to its earlier assessment. It now gave much greater attention to shortcomings in the design, the information available to the operating personnel, and safety culture.

One of the earlier warning signs had come from the Ignalina nuclear power plant in nineteen eighty-three. The unusual response associated with inserting the rods had already been observed there. Restrictions intended to address this problem had not been effectively implemented before Chernobyl. The danger therefore did not emerge for the first time in the ruined reactor. There had been a chance to act on it earlier.

The later findings did not mean every decision that night was correct. They did change the question that investigators needed to ask: what had the operators been told about the safety limits of their own machine, and what had been done about the warning from another plant? After the accident the remaining RBMK reactors underwent safety modifications, including changes to the control rods. A protection system that could initially work against its purpose needed to be changed, rather than leaving the crew alone to compensate for its flaws.

The crew pressed a button meant to end the chain reaction. In the condition of the core that night, the first movement of its protection system could contribute to a surge that the system could no longer control. The defining failure was that the emergency stop could lose its protective function when it mattered most.
