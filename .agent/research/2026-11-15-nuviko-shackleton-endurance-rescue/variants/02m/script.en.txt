How could twenty-two men survive on an Antarctic island if no ship knew where to find them? And why did their six companions have to cross an ocean to ask for help?

Ernest Shackleton had planned to cross Antarctica. But before his expedition could land, sea ice trapped his ship, Endurance. After months of drifting, the ice crushed it. All twenty-eight men escaped onto the frozen sea.

They camped on a moving floe until it began to break. Then they launched three lifeboats and, six days later, reached Elephant Island.

They were on solid ground at last. But nobody lived there. There was no radio to call a ship, and ordinary shipping routes lay far away.

Shackleton chose the biggest lifeboat, James Caird. The carpenter strengthened it. Six men sailed roughly eight hundred miles toward South Georgia; Frank Wild stayed with the other twenty-two.

For sixteen days the small boat fought waves and ice. Navigator Frank Worsley could check the sun only four times. They found South Georgia, but landed on the wrong side of its mountains.

Shackleton and two companions crossed the mountains on foot and finally reached a whaling station. Help existed now. Reaching the stranded men was another matter.

Ice stopped three rescue attempts. On Elephant Island, the twenty-two waited beneath upturned boats, unable to know whether anyone was coming.

A fourth ship, the Chilean tug Yelcho under Captain Luis Pardo, reached them on August thirtieth, nineteen sixteen.

All twenty-two came aboard. With the six who had gone for help, every one of Endurance's twenty-eight men survived. Shackleton lost his ship and never completed the crossing. The rescue became the expedition's outcome.
