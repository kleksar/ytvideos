Why were twenty-two men left on an Antarctic island with no way to call a ship? How did six others cross eight hundred miles of ocean to seek help? And why did rescue still require four voyages?

In 1914, Ernest Shackleton had set out to cross Antarctica. His ship, Endurance, was meant to carry the crossing party to the coast.

But the pack ice closed around the wooden hull before anyone could land. For months the ship drifted with the ice, and the men could not free her.

Then the ice began crushing the ship. The crew abandoned her in October 1915, and in November Endurance sank beneath the floes.

The men recovered supplies and their three lifeboats. They built a camp on a slab of sea ice that kept moving beneath them.

There was no reliable route across the shifting ice to solid ground. They waited for enough open water to launch the boats.

In April 1916, their floe began breaking apart. All twenty-eight men climbed into three crowded boats and pushed off into freezing seas.

Six days later they reached Elephant Island. For the first time since December 1914, the men stood on solid ground.

The main problem had only just begun. Reaching land should have meant safety. But the island was uninhabited, far from shipping routes, and there was no way to call for help.

Shackleton decided to send a small party to South Georgia, where whaling stations had ships and people who could help.

Carpenter Harry McNish strengthened the largest lifeboat, James Caird, for an ocean crossing. The boat was only about seven metres long.

On April twenty-fourth, Shackleton sailed with five others. Frank Wild took charge of the twenty-two left on Elephant Island.

The six sailors fought enormous waves and ice building up on the boat. They chipped ice off its deck and bailed water to keep it afloat.

Navigator Frank Worsley needed glimpses of the sun to check their position. During the whole crossing, he managed only four useful sightings.

Between those rare observations, Worsley estimated the boat's position from its course and speed.

More than two weeks after leaving Elephant Island, they sighted South Georgia. But the weather stopped them landing immediately.

They finally made it ashore, far from the whaling stations. The ocean had been crossed, but help was still on the other side of mountains and glaciers.

Three men stayed by James Caird. Shackleton, Worsley and Tom Crean went into the island's mountainous interior on foot.

They had no established route across the mountains. After thirty-six hours of travel, they reached the whaling station at Stromness.

The men who had remained by the boat were also collected. Finally, the six from James Caird had reached safety.

But the rescue mission was not over. Twenty-two men remained on Elephant Island, with no news of whether their friends had survived.

Shackleton sailed back toward the island on a rescue ship. Pack ice blocked the way, and the ship had to turn back.

He found another ship and tried again. It too was stopped by ice before it could reach the waiting men.

A third attempt failed as well. The rescuers knew twenty-two men were stranded; the twenty-two had no way to know rescue had even begun.

On Elephant Island, the men lived beneath two overturned lifeboats, held above the rocks and covered with canvas.

They had been there for months. They did not know whether James Caird had reached South Georgia, or whether anyone would return.

Then Chile supplied a small steam tug named Yelcho, commanded by Captain Luis Pardo. Shackleton set out for the island a fourth time.

On August thirtieth, nineteen sixteen, Yelcho reached Elephant Island. The sea had finally opened a route through the ice.

A ship appeared on a horizon that the men had searched for four months. This time it came close enough to collect them.

All twenty-two men were taken aboard. Together with the six who had sailed for help, all twenty-eight members of Endurance's crew survived.

Shackleton never completed his crossing of Antarctica, and Endurance was gone. But the long escape of her twenty-eight men had finally ended.
