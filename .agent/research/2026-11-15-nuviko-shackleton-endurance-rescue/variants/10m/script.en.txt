How did twenty-eight men survive after their ship was crushed hundreds of miles from help? Why did reaching land leave twenty-two of them trapped? And how could a rescue take four attempts after their leader had already reached people who could send a ship?

In 1914, Ernest Shackleton set out to lead a crossing of Antarctica. His plan required his ship, Endurance, to land a party on the coast of the Weddell Sea. A second expedition on the opposite side would lay supply depots for the crossing. The crew of Endurance never reached the start of that walk.

Pack ice slowed their ship, then closed around it. By early 1915 Endurance was trapped. The ice carried the hull north while the men waited for a break that did not come. There was no road through the shifting floes and no open channel to sail through.

In October, pressure from the ice made the wooden ship impossible to occupy safely. The crew left her and moved their supplies onto the ice. On November twenty-first, nineteen fifteen, Endurance finally disappeared beneath the sea.

All twenty-eight were alive. But they had lost their ship, and the frozen surface holding their camp was itself travelling and changing. The original expedition had turned into an attempt to get home.

The men kept three lifeboats. For months they waited on the sea ice because dragging the boats across broken floes offered no dependable route to land. Then, on April ninth, nineteen sixteen, the floe began to split. They had to leave the camp and trust the lifeboats instead.

The next six days were spent in three crowded open boats. On April fifteenth they landed at Elephant Island. They stood on firm ground for the first time since December 1914.

It sounds like the end of a survival story. But Elephant Island was uninhabited. Ships did not normally pass it. The men had no working means of calling for rescue. Their new ground would not drift away, but waiting there could leave them forgotten.

That was the real turning point: surviving the sinking and finding land had solved neither the communication problem nor the need for a ship. Someone had to sail out and tell the world where the twenty-two would be waiting.

Shackleton chose South Georgia, an island with whaling stations. The distance was about eight hundred miles of open ocean. Their largest lifeboat, James Caird, was only around seven metres long. Carpenter Harry McNish worked to make the small craft more seaworthy for a journey it had never been built to make.

On April twenty-fourth, Shackleton left with five companions: Frank Worsley, Tom Crean, Harry McNish, Timothy McCarthy and John Vincent. Frank Wild took charge of the other twenty-two men on Elephant Island.

Now the expedition was divided. The six on the water had to find a small distant island. The twenty-two ashore could only keep their camp going and hope their friends would reach it. Neither group could send news to the other.

On James Caird, waves struck the hull and ice accumulated on the boat. The men had to break it away because the added weight threatened their ability to stay afloat. They also bailed out water. When they could eat, a small stove supplied hot meals in the cramped space.

Finding South Georgia was as important as keeping the boat afloat. Their navigator, Frank Worsley, needed the sun to check his position. Through the entire crossing he managed four useful observations. Between them he estimated where they were from the course they had steered and the distance he believed they had covered. The remaining uncertainty mattered: passing the island without seeing it could leave them without another place to land.

After sixteen days, on May eighth, they sighted South Georgia. But they did not simply step ashore. Severe weather made the coast dangerous. They finally landed on May tenth, after two more days of struggling to reach a small inlet.

The ocean crossing had worked. Yet the nearest people were still beyond mountains and glaciers on the other side of the island. The boat could not carry them around to the whaling stations.

They rested and reorganised on the coast for several days. Three would remain with James Caird. Shackleton chose Worsley and Crean to accompany him over the mountainous interior. They took food for the walk, but there was no established route through this terrain.

The three crossed snowfields, glaciers and mountain ranges. After thirty-six hours they reached the whaling station at Stromness on May twentieth. The three companions left by the lifeboat were subsequently collected.

For the first time since the ship was trapped, the men had reached a populated place with vessels that could help. All six who had sailed in James Caird were safe.

But that success did not tell Frank Wild and the others anything. On Elephant Island the twenty-two had made shelter from two overturned lifeboats raised above the rocky ground and covered with canvas. They could not know whether James Caird had found South Georgia, disappeared at sea or arrived somewhere too late to return.

Shackleton tried to get back almost immediately. A ship called Southern Sky departed with a volunteer crew. It approached Elephant Island, but an impenetrable barrier of pack ice stopped it around seventy miles from the waiting men. The ship was not equipped to break through. It turned back.

He sought another way. From the Falklands he asked for naval help, but the reply offered no ship until October. The men on Elephant Island had already been waiting for weeks, and he could not count on that timetable.

Instead he secured a second vessel, Instituto de Pesca No. 1, through contacts in South America. It sailed in June. Once again, sea ice prevented it from reaching the island. Knowing the location of the camp was not enough; the sea still had to allow a ship through.

A third vessel, the schooner Emma, was prepared for another attempt in July. It too met ice and failed. Three ships had gone out toward twenty-two stranded men. None had brought them home.

For those on Elephant Island, there was no sequence of news bulletins explaining why the ships had not arrived. They were still waiting under their makeshift roof. The six men who had left months earlier might have reached safety; the twenty-two could not confirm even that.

The fourth chance came from Chile. Its navy lent a small steam tug, Yelcho, commanded by Captain Luis Pardo. Unlike an icebreaker, Yelcho had no reinforced double hull; the ship also lacked a radio. Getting through the pack remained a risk, not a certainty.

Yelcho departed on August twenty-fifth. On August thirtieth, the sea finally offered an opening toward Elephant Island. In thick fog, the ship came close enough to search for the camp.

Then the fog lifted. From the island the waiting men could see that this vessel was coming to them, not turning away at the ice. Accounts of the rescue describe one of the surgeons, Alexander Macklin, trying to raise the camp's flag. It had frozen, so he held up his jumper instead.

All twenty-two men were taken aboard. After months of uncertainty, they could leave the shelter built from their own lifeboats. With the six who had crossed the ocean, every one of the twenty-eight men aboard Endurance had survived.

That number has an important boundary. The broader Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition included a separate Ross Sea party, which suffered three deaths while laying the supplies intended for Shackleton's crossing. It would be wrong to say everyone in the entire expedition survived.

Shackleton never completed the crossing he had planned. The ship was lost before his party could even land on the Antarctic mainland. But from the moment Endurance was trapped, each apparent solution had exposed a new problem: leave the crushed ship, leave the ice, escape the island, cross the ocean, cross the mountains, and finally bring a ship back through the pack.

The last task took four attempts. On August thirtieth, all twenty-two waiting men came home from Elephant Island alive. That is what the rescue achieved.
