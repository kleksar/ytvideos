Two Boeing 747s were on the same runway. One was still looking for its exit. The other had begun its takeoff roll. Neither crew could see the other through the fog.

The control tower had never given that second aircraft permission to take off. So why did its pilots believe they could? And how did two radio warnings disappear at the worst possible moment?

The answer begins nowhere near the runway. It begins with a bomb at a different airport.

On March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-seven, KLM Flight 4805 and Pan Am Flight 1736 were heading for Gran Canaria, in Spain's Canary Islands.

A bomb exploded inside the airport terminal. There was also a warning about another possible bomb. The airport closed, and aircraft that were supposed to land there were diverted.

Both jumbo jets landed at Los Rodeos, a smaller airport on nearby Tenerife. Other diverted aircraft crowded its parking areas.

Eventually, Gran Canaria reopened. Everyone wanted to get moving again. But on Tenerife, aircraft parked along the parallel taxiway were blocking the normal route to the runway.

The KLM plane had taken on additional fuel. The Pan Am crew was ready to leave but could not squeeze past it while it stood in the way.

A runway is normally where an aircraft accelerates for takeoff. That afternoon, controllers also had to use it as a taxi route.

First, KLM was told to taxi along the runway to its far end and turn around. Then Pan Am was instructed to follow, but leave through the third taxiway on its left.

If Pan Am left as instructed, the runway would become clear for the KLM takeoff. The plan depended on everyone knowing exactly where both aircraft were.

Then the fog thickened. From the tower, the controllers could no longer reliably see aircraft along the runway. The crews struggled to identify the exits in front of them.

The Pan Am pilots asked the tower to repeat which exit they needed. The answer came back: the third one. They counted the intersections as they rolled forward.

But the aircraft did not turn off at that third exit. It continued down the runway. Crucially, it never reported that the runway was clear.

At the opposite end, the KLM Boeing completed its one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turn. It was now pointing straight back toward the Pan Am aircraft, hidden in the mist.

Its captain was an experienced senior instructor. The crew had finished preparing for departure. But being ready to leave is not the same as having permission to leave.

Here is the distinction that mattered. A controller can clear a crew to fly a particular route after departure. That is different from clearing the aircraft to begin the takeoff run.

KLM's first officer asked for its air traffic control clearance. The tower gave instructions for the route ahead, including the altitude and direction to follow after takeoff.

That message told them where to fly. It did not tell them to take off. There was still another Boeing 747 somewhere ahead on the runway.

During the first officer's readback of those route instructions, the KLM captain began accelerating. He was starting a takeoff for which the tower had not issued clearance.

At the end of the readback came an unclear sentence. Depending on how it was heard, it sounded like the crew was at the takeoff position — or was already taking off.

The tower replied with a brief 'O.K.' and then told KLM to stand by for takeoff. The controller said another call would follow.

The Pan Am crew had heard enough of the exchange to worry. They transmitted an urgent clarification: they were still taxiing along the runway.

But that was exactly when the two radio transmissions overlapped. The tower's 'stand by' and Pan Am's warning reached the KLM cockpit together as a burst of interference.

The messages that could have revealed the mistake were not heard clearly. KLM kept accelerating into the fog.

A few seconds later, the tower asked Pan Am to report when it was clear of the runway. Pan Am replied that it would do so.

That exchange was audible in the KLM cockpit. It raised a question no one should have had to ask during a takeoff roll.

The KLM flight engineer asked whether Pan Am had already left the runway. The two pilots answered yes. The engines kept pushing their aircraft forward.

Pan Am had not left. Its crew was still searching for the exit when lights appeared through the grey air in front of them.

The pilots tried to turn their 747 toward the edge of the runway. Ahead of them, the KLM crew tried to lift its aircraft off the ground.

There was not enough room. The KLM aircraft struck the Pan Am jumbo jet and came down farther along the runway. Both aircraft caught fire.

Fire crews responding through the fog initially reached the wreckage of KLM without realizing another burning aircraft was nearby.

Nobody aboard KLM survived. Sixty-one people escaped from the Pan Am aircraft. The eventual death toll was five hundred and eighty-three.

This was the deadliest accident in aviation history. And the investigation had to answer an almost unbearable question: how could so many people die while both crews and the tower were communicating?

Investigators reconstructed the timeline using flight recorders, cockpit recordings and the tower's radio tapes. The crucial problem was not a mechanical failure in either aircraft.

The official Spanish findings identified the KLM takeoff without clearance as the fundamental cause. The Dutch investigation also stressed how the radio exchange had made the crew believe permission had been given.

The fog concealed the aircraft. Airport congestion put them on the same runway. A missed exit kept Pan Am there. Ambiguous wording and overlapping transmissions prevented the warning from getting through.

None of those conditions, by itself, was permission for KLM to depart. That final permission had never been issued.

The disaster exposed two weaknesses at once: crews and controllers could understand the same words differently, and a cockpit could continue acting on an assumption even after a colleague raised a doubt.

In the years that followed, aviation authorities strengthened standard radio phraseology. The word 'takeoff' became reserved for the actual takeoff clearance, rather than being used loosely in instructions about a future flight.

Airline training also placed growing emphasis on crew coordination and challenging an unsafe decision, regardless of who was sitting in the captain's seat.

Those changes did not make runways immune to accidents. But they addressed the specific kind of misunderstanding that turned Tenerife into a catastrophe.

The tower was talking. Pan Am was warning. The flight engineer was asking. Yet the KLM plane continued.

The difference that mattered was simple, and it is the answer to the question at the start: being cleared where to fly is never the same as being cleared to take off.
