How could two Boeing 747s collide on one runway while both crews were in contact with air traffic control? And why did a warning heard in the cockpit fail to stop the takeoff?

On March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-seven, a bomb at Gran Canaria airport diverted KLM Flight 4805 and Pan Am Flight 1736 to Tenerife. When Gran Canaria reopened, KLM was still refuelling, delaying Pan Am. Meanwhile, diverted planes blocked Tenerife's parallel taxiway, forcing both 747s to taxi on the runway.

KLM moved to the far end to turn around for takeoff. Pan Am followed, instructed to leave at the third exit on the left. Low clouds covered the airfield. From the tower, neither aircraft was clearly visible.

In KLM's cockpit, first officer Klaas Meurs interrupted the departure preparations: “Wait a minute, we don't have an ATC clearance.” Captain Jacob van Zanten told him to request it. Less than a minute later, KLM would be accelerating toward a runway still occupied by Pan Am.

The third exit turned sharply back toward the taxiway; the fourth was easier for a 747. Pan Am's pilots discussed which intersection the controller meant and asked for confirmation. The answer was the third. They passed it. The recording does not establish exactly how the difficult angle and counting uncertainty affected that decision. Pan Am remained on the runway.

At the opposite end, van Zanten had completed KLM's turn. He was also a senior flight instructor at the airline. His crew needed two distinct clearances: one describing the route to fly after takeoff, and another explicitly authorizing the takeoff run. The first did not grant the second.

The controller gave KLM its route and altitude. Meurs read back those instructions, ending with words that could mean the plane was at its takeoff position or already taking off. While he spoke, KLM released its brakes. Three seconds later, its engines began accelerating.

The tower's brief “O.K.” came only after KLM had started rolling. It had not triggered the takeoff. The controller then told KLM to wait for takeoff clearance, just as Pan Am transmitted that it was still taxiing on the runway.

The two radio calls overlapped on the same frequency. In KLM's cockpit, they produced a high-pitched interference noise. Neither warning came through clearly, and the jet kept accelerating.

But a later exchange was audible. The controller asked Pan Am to report when it had cleared the runway. Pan Am answered that it would. Flight engineer Willem Schreuder asked van Zanten whether Pan Am was clear. The captain did not hear him, so Schreuder repeated the question. Van Zanten answered that it was.

Thirteen seconds later, the aircraft collided.

Pan Am's crew spotted KLM about nine and a half seconds before impact. They increased power and tried to steer off the runway. KLM's pilots pulled up, trying to lift their jet over Pan Am. They could not. Both aircraft caught fire.

All 248 people aboard KLM died. On Pan Am, 335 died and 61 survived. The total was 583 deaths: the deadliest accident in aviation history.

The Spanish investigation named KLM's takeoff without clearance as the fundamental cause. Dutch investigators emphasized confused communications and surrounding conditions. The recordings expose the combined danger: the blocked taxiway put two jets on one runway, fog concealed them, overlapping calls obscured an instruction to wait, and a later question about whether the runway was clear did not stop the takeoff.

Investigators recommended less ambiguous radio language and a clearer distinction between route and takeoff clearances. Tenerife also became a major case in training crews to question and check a captain's assumptions; it did not single-handedly create that training.

The last warning was not lost in the static. The flight engineer asked if Pan Am had cleared the runway. The answer was wrong, and the takeoff continued.
