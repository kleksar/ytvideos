How did two Boeing 747s collide on the same runway while both crews were in contact with air traffic control? Why did one of them begin to take off without permission? And why did a warning from the other aircraft fail to stop it?

To answer those questions, we have to go back to March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-seven. Two enormous passenger jets were approaching Gran Canaria, a holiday island off the coast of Africa. Neither was supposed to land on Tenerife.

But a bomb had exploded inside Gran Canaria's airport terminal. With another explosion threatened, the airport closed. Incoming flights were diverted, including KLM Flight 4805 from Amsterdam and Pan Am Flight 1736, which had travelled from Los Angeles via New York.

Both aircraft landed at Los Rodeos on the neighbouring island. It was a much smaller airport. Now its apron was crowded with planes that were never meant to be there together.

Eventually Gran Canaria reopened. The Pan Am crew wanted to leave, but the KLM aircraft was blocking its way while taking on more fuel. By the time the two giants could move, other parked aircraft were obstructing the taxiway that normally ran beside the runway.

So the tower gave them an unusual, but permitted, route. They would use the runway itself to taxi: KLM first, Pan Am behind it. KLM would travel to the far end, turn around and prepare to depart in the opposite direction.

Pan Am had a different instruction. It was supposed to leave the runway at the third taxiway on the left. Once it was safely off the runway, KLM could receive permission to take off.

Then a bank of low cloud moved across the airport. The tower could no longer see the two aircraft properly. Neither crew could see the other. Every instruction, every report of a position, now mattered much more.

The third exit presented another problem. On the airport plan it turned sharply back toward the parallel taxiway. The fourth exit had a much easier angle for a Boeing 747.

Inside the Pan Am cockpit, the pilots discussed the turns they could make and whether they were counting the intersections correctly. They asked the tower to confirm the third exit. The answer came back: the third. One, two, three.

The crew still did not turn off at that exit. Exactly how much the difficult angle and the counting confusion influenced the decision remains uncertain. What matters for the next few minutes is certain: Pan Am was still on the runway, and it had not reported leaving it.

At the opposite end, the KLM crew had finished its turn. The aircraft was pointing back down the same runway. Its captain, Jacob van Zanten, was also a senior flight instructor at KLM, one of the airline's most respected pilots.

Then, at five forty-one past five, the first officer, Klaas Meurs, interrupted the preparations. His words are preserved in the cockpit recording: “Wait a minute, we don't have an ATC clearance.” The captain acknowledged this and told him to ask the tower.

This is the turning point. The crew had noticed that a required clearance was missing. Yet less than a minute later their Boeing would be accelerating down a runway that another 747 had not left. What changed between those two moments?

There were two different permissions involved, and mixing them up could be fatal. One told the pilots where to fly once they were airborne. The other gave them permission to start the takeoff run. They were not interchangeable.

At five fifty-three past five, the controller gave KLM the first kind: a route toward a radio beacon, with a heading and an altitude to follow after departure. The tower had not cleared the aircraft to take off.

Meurs began repeating those instructions over the radio, as pilots are trained to do. But his final words were ambiguous. They could sound like a report that the aircraft was at the takeoff position, or that it was already taking off.

At 17:06:11, while that exchange was still unfolding, the KLM aircraft released its brakes. Three seconds later its engines were accelerating. There had been no separate takeoff clearance.

Only after the takeoff run had begun did the tower answer with a brief “O.K.” The word was badly chosen, but it had not caused the captain to start rolling. The aircraft was already moving.

The controller went on to tell KLM to wait for takeoff clearance. At almost exactly the same moment, Pan Am transmitted that it was still taxiing along the runway.

Two people had supplied the information that could have stopped the takeoff: the tower had not given permission, and the other aircraft was still in the way. But their transmissions overlapped on the same frequency.

In the KLM cockpit, the overlapping signals produced a high-pitched interference noise for several seconds. Neither message came through with the clarity it needed. The jet continued to pick up speed.

Yet this was not a story in which every warning vanished. A few seconds later the tower asked Pan Am to report when it had cleared the runway. Pan Am replied that it would report when clear.

That exchange was audible on the KLM flight deck. To flight engineer Willem Schreuder, it raised an obvious question: if Pan Am would report when clear, perhaps it had not left the runway yet.

Schreuder asked the captain whether Pan Am was clear. Van Zanten did not catch the question, so the engineer repeated it. The captain answered emphatically that the other aircraft had left.

Investigators later considered whether the captain's authority made it harder for the others to challenge him again. The recording cannot tell us what anyone was thinking. It does tell us that the takeoff was not stopped.

Thirteen seconds after that answer, the aircraft would collide. Not thirteen seconds after a mechanical failure. Thirteen seconds after a question about the other plane had been raised and answered.

Down the runway, Pan Am had still not reached a safe exit. The cockpit crew could hear a growing engine roar, but visibility was so poor that the aircraft bearing down on them remained out of sight until the final moments.

Investigators calculated from the Pan Am recording that its crew spotted KLM about nine and a half seconds before impact. Captain Victor Grubbs and his crew tried to steer their enormous aircraft out of the other jet's path and increased engine power.

The KLM pilots also saw the obstruction. They tried to pull their Boeing into the air. Its nose rose, but the aircraft could not clear the Pan Am jet.

The KLM aircraft struck the Pan Am Boeing, then crashed farther along the runway. Both planes caught fire. By the time the crews could see the danger clearly, the remaining distance was too short.

All 248 people aboard KLM died. On Pan Am, 61 people survived; 335 died. In total, the collision killed 583 people, making it the deadliest accident in aviation history.

The investigation now had to explain how two undamaged aircraft, a functioning airport and working radios could produce such a disaster. It reconstructed the final minutes from cockpit recorders, radio tapes and the positions of the aircraft.

The official Spanish findings named the fundamental cause: KLM had begun taking off without clearance and continued after indications that the runway was occupied. Comments from the Dutch investigators put greater emphasis on the confusion in radio communications and surrounding conditions.

Those circumstances were real. A bomb had redirected the flights. Parking congestion had forced two 747s onto one runway. Fog had hidden them from each other. Pan Am had not taken the instructed exit. A crucial radio exchange had been obscured by interference.

But none of those events was permission to begin a takeoff. The practical lesson was not simply to blame the fog, one unlucky word, or one turn. It was to make every clearance unmistakable and every challenge inside the cockpit count.

The investigation recommended stricter, more exact radio language and a clearer separation between instructions about the route and actual takeoff clearance. The disaster also became an important example in the development of more open teamwork among airline crews.

Tenerife did not single-handedly invent crew resource management. But its recordings showed why a senior captain's assumption must be open to a question from a first officer or a flight engineer, and why the answer has to be checked rather than merely accepted.

At 17:05, the first officer noticed the missing route clearance. At 17:06, the tower was waiting to authorize takeoff. Pan Am was still on the runway. The flight engineer questioned whether it was clear. None of those facts was hidden from every participant.

The tragedy was that those pieces of information never stopped the aircraft. Two Boeing 747s collided not because no one had spoken, but because the words that mattered failed to change what was happening on the runway.
