How could two Boeing 747s collide on a runway while both crews were talking to air traffic control? Why did one aircraft begin taking off without permission? And why did an audible question about the other aircraft fail to stop it?

The answer begins away from the runway. On March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-seven, both jumbo jets were heading to Gran Canaria in Spain's Canary Islands. A bomb exploded in that airport's terminal. A threat of another bomb closed the airport to incoming flights. KLM Flight 4805 and Pan Am Flight 1736 landed instead at the smaller airport on Tenerife.

That diversion changed the ground layout. The apron filled with aircraft that had not planned to be there. Some stood where a departing aircraft would normally taxi beside the runway. When Gran Canaria reopened, there was a way to leave Tenerife, but not a normal route to reach the departure end.

Pan Am was ready to go, yet the KLM jet blocked its path. KLM had allowed passengers out during the delay and then began refuelling. According to the investigation, it took on more than fifty-five thousand litres. Pan Am had to remain behind it. The refuelling was not the cause of the crash, but it extended the delay during which the crowded airport's movements had to be reorganized.

With the parallel taxiway obstructed, controllers instructed the two huge aircraft to use the runway itself. KLM would travel toward the far end, turn around, and face back down the runway for takeoff. Pan Am would follow behind and leave at a connecting taxiway. Until Pan Am turned off, the runway could not safely be used for KLM's takeoff.

Then low clouds moved over the airport. The tower no longer had a clear view of either jet. The crews also lost the simple visual confirmation that the runway ahead or behind them was empty. Radio calls and the correct interpretation of taxiway intersections became crucial.

The controller directed Pan Am to leave at the third exit on the left. Its pilots repeated the instruction and discussed which intersections they were passing. The captain thought he had heard 'first' at one point; the first officer checked with the tower. The answer was unmistakable: the third.

But the shape of the airport made that instruction harder to carry out than it sounded. The third connection turned sharply back toward the parallel taxiway. A later exit would offer a much easier angle for a 747. The Pan Am crew discussed the turn and the count, then passed the intended intersection. The recordings cannot establish one exact reason for their failure to take it. They do establish that Pan Am remained on the runway.

Meanwhile KLM reached the far end and completed its one-hundred-and-eighty-degree turn. Captain Jacob van Zanten was a senior instructor at KLM; first officer Klaas Meurs was handling the radio. Inside the cockpit, their next recorded exchange matters more than a reconstruction of anyone's private thoughts.

Meurs interrupted the preparations: “Wait a minute, we don't have an ATC clearance.” Van Zanten acknowledged the missing clearance and told him to request it. The crew knew there was still a step to complete. Yet less than a minute later, their jet would be rolling down a runway occupied by Pan Am.

What exactly was missing? A flight might receive an air traffic clearance describing the route it should fly after departure: which beacon, what heading, what altitude. That was different from a takeoff clearance permitting the aircraft to start its run along the runway. Receiving the route could not make the runway clear or authorize acceleration.

The tower gave KLM its route toward a beacon, with heading and altitude instructions. These were directions for a flight that had left the ground. Meurs began reading them back to confirm what he had heard. His final wording was ambiguous: he might have been reporting that the aircraft was at the takeoff position, or that it was already taking off.

The cockpit recording fixes an important order. KLM released its brakes while Meurs was still speaking. Its engines began accelerating a few seconds later. Only then did the controller respond with a brief “O.K.” That word was confusing, but the aircraft had already begun its takeoff run before it was spoken.

The controller continued with the instruction to wait for takeoff clearance. At nearly the same instant, Pan Am transmitted that it was still taxiing down the runway. Either piece of information should have been incompatible with KLM continuing to accelerate. But both parties were transmitting on the same radio frequency at once.

Instead of two intelligible messages, the KLM cockpit heard several seconds of shrill interference. The instruction to wait and Pan Am's report did not arrive clearly. Radio communication had not failed completely; it had failed at the precise moment when those two transmissions overlapped.

The next exchange did come through. The controller told Pan Am to report when it had cleared the runway. Pan Am answered that it would report when clear. This was a useful clue even without seeing the other aircraft: the crew was speaking about leaving the runway in the future, not confirming that it had already left.

Flight engineer Willem Schreuder asked van Zanten whether Pan Am was clear. The captain did not hear the first attempt, so Schreuder asked again. Van Zanten answered emphatically that it was. His answer did not come from a fresh report that the runway had been vacated. It was an assurance inside a cockpit that was already accelerating.

The investigation raised the possibility that van Zanten's prestige made another challenge more difficult. But the recordings do not reveal what the other crew members felt or why they did not insist again. The supported fact is narrower and more important: the flight engineer raised the doubt, the captain dismissed it, and nobody stopped the takeoff.

There were about thirteen seconds from that answer to the collision. At the far end of the same strip of pavement, Pan Am was still taxiing in thick fog. The crew could not simply see the approaching jet at a distance and move out of its way.

Investigators calculated that Pan Am's crew saw KLM about nine and a half seconds before impact. They pushed for more engine power and tried to steer away from the aircraft coming at them. KLM's pilots also reacted, pulling up to try to clear the obstacle. The nose lifted, but the aircraft could not rise over the other 747.

KLM struck Pan Am and then fell farther along the runway. Both aircraft burned. The surrounding fog also complicated the immediate understanding of the scene: accounts of the emergency response describe rescuers initially reaching the KLM wreck while the damaged Pan Am aircraft was not yet clearly visible.

All 248 people aboard the KLM flight died. The Pan Am aircraft had 61 survivors and 335 fatalities. The toll reached 583 deaths, making Tenerife the deadliest accident in aviation history. Those numbers describe people aboard ordinary passenger flights that had only been diverted because their destination airport was temporarily closed.

Investigators had to reconstruct more than the final seconds. They examined the airport's unusual traffic arrangement, the missed exit, the worsening visibility, the separate clearances, the radio overlap and the cockpit exchanges. The question was how those events combined to permit an unauthorized takeoff to continue, not whether the airfield had been completely silent.

The Spanish investigation called KLM's takeoff without clearance the fundamental cause and identified the failure to stop after indications that Pan Am was still on the runway. Dutch investigators placed more emphasis on communications and the surrounding conditions. Neither description turns the third exit into proof of why Pan Am missed it, or the captain's possible time pressure into a proven private motive.

There was another detail in the cockpit and tower records: the runway centerline lighting was not working, and the Pan Am crew discussed the visibility needed for a safe departure. This did not grant KLM permission to take off. It shows that the crews were trying to manage a changing airport environment while still depending on exact radio instructions.

The resulting safety lessons were concrete. Route clearance and takeoff clearance needed unambiguous, separate wording. A message obscured by interference could not be treated as confirmation. Inside a cockpit, a question about a runway obstacle had to lead to a check, not merely a confident answer. Tenerife became an important case in crew coordination training, though it did not single-handedly invent that training.

Go back to the final minute. A first officer pointed out missing clearance. A controller instructed the crew to wait. Another aircraft reported it was still taxiing. And a flight engineer asked if that aircraft had left. The disaster did not happen because nobody had the relevant information. It happened when those warnings failed to stop an aircraft already in motion.
