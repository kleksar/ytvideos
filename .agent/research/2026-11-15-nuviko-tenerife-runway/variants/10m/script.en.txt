How could two Boeing 747s collide on a runway while both crews were talking to air traffic control? Why did a question heard inside one cockpit fail to stop the takeoff? And how did some of the people aboard Pan Am make it out alive?

The answer begins away from the runway. On March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-seven, both jumbo jets were heading to Gran Canaria in Spain's Canary Islands. A bomb exploded in that airport's terminal. A threat of another bomb closed the airport to incoming flights. KLM Flight 4805 and Pan Am Flight 1736 landed instead at the smaller airport on Tenerife.

Dorothy Kelly, a junior purser on the Pan Am flight, had expected to work at the back of the aircraft. Her colleague had asked her to take over announcements at the front. Kelly agreed. Many of the passengers were on their way to a cruise; while they waited at Tenerife, the cabin crew had little information to give them.

Outside, diverted aircraft filled the apron and obstructed the parallel taxiway. When Gran Canaria reopened, Pan Am still could not leave because KLM was refuelling in its path. The delay continued while controllers reorganized departures. It was one of the circumstances that brought the two aircraft together, not a sufficient explanation for the collision.

With the parallel taxiway obstructed, controllers instructed the two huge aircraft to use the runway itself. KLM would travel toward the far end, turn around, and face back down the runway for takeoff. Pan Am would follow behind and leave at a connecting taxiway. Until Pan Am turned off, the runway could not safely be used for KLM's takeoff.

Then low clouds moved over the airport. The tower no longer had a clear view of either jet. The crews also lost the simple visual confirmation that the runway ahead or behind them was empty. Radio calls and the correct interpretation of taxiway intersections became crucial.

At the far end of the runway, KLM completed its turn. Captain Jacob van Zanten was a senior flight instructor for the airline. His first officer, Klaas Meurs, interrupted the preparations. They still had not received their air traffic clearance. Van Zanten told him to request it.

What exactly was missing? A flight might receive an air traffic clearance describing the route it should fly after departure: which beacon, what heading, what altitude. That was different from a takeoff clearance permitting the aircraft to start its run along the runway. Receiving the route could not make the runway clear or authorize acceleration.

Meanwhile, Pan Am had been told to leave at the third exit on the left. Its pilots discussed the count and checked with the tower. They were told again to use the third. The angle of that connection was awkward for a 747, and the fourth exit was easier to negotiate. The recordings show that Pan Am passed the third; they do not establish a single reason why.

The tower gave KLM its route toward a beacon, with heading and altitude instructions. These were directions for a flight that had left the ground. Meurs began reading them back to confirm what he had heard. His final wording was ambiguous: he might have been reporting that the aircraft was at the takeoff position, or that it was already taking off.

The cockpit recording fixes an important order. KLM released its brakes while Meurs was still speaking. Its engines began accelerating a few seconds later. Only then did the controller respond with a brief “O.K.” That word was confusing, but the aircraft had already begun its takeoff run before it was spoken.

The controller continued with the instruction to wait for takeoff clearance. At nearly the same instant, Pan Am transmitted that it was still taxiing down the runway. Either piece of information should have been incompatible with KLM continuing to accelerate. But both parties were transmitting on the same radio frequency at once.

For several seconds the two transmissions interfered with each other. The crew of KLM could not clearly hear either the instruction to wait or Pan Am's report that it was still on the runway. The aircraft kept accelerating.

The next exchange did come through. The controller told Pan Am to report when it had cleared the runway. Pan Am answered that it would report when clear. This was a useful clue even without seeing the other aircraft: the crew was speaking about leaving the runway in the future, not confirming that it had already left.

Flight engineer Willem Schreuder asked van Zanten whether Pan Am was clear. The captain did not hear the first attempt, so Schreuder asked again. Van Zanten answered emphatically that it was. His answer did not come from a fresh report that the runway had been vacated. It was an assurance inside a cockpit that was already accelerating.

The Spanish investigation considered whether the captain's standing in the airline made a further challenge more difficult. The recording does not prove why the other crew members did not insist. It does show that a doubt had been voiced even after the radio interference and the captain's answer did not lead to a check.

There were about thirteen seconds from that answer to the collision. At the far end of the same strip of pavement, Pan Am was still taxiing in thick fog. The crew could not simply see the approaching jet at a distance and move out of its way.

Investigators calculated that Pan Am's crew saw KLM about nine and a half seconds before impact. They pushed for more engine power and tried to steer away from the aircraft coming at them. KLM's pilots also reacted, pulling up to try to clear the obstacle. The nose lifted, but the aircraft could not rise over the other 747.

The two aircraft collided and caught fire. All 248 people aboard KLM died. On Pan Am, 335 died and 61 survived. The final toll was 583. For the people who could still move inside Pan Am, the immediate problem was how to leave a badly damaged aircraft.

Kelly later recalled regaining consciousness in darkness. She saw a small point of light overhead, climbed toward it and emerged onto what remained of the upper deck. The usual emergency exits and slides were unusable in the area where she found herself. She and another person jumped down. Once outside, she began urging people to get away from the aircraft.

She noticed a man lying near the damaged aircraft. It was her captain, Victor Grubbs, who could not get away by himself. Kelly pulled him to safety despite her own head injury and broken arm. She went back again to assist others. Grubbs later credited her with saving his life.

Other crew members were helping too. First officer Robert Bragg had injured his ankle, but helped transfer injured people into vehicles. Flight attendants Suzanne Donovan and Joan Jackson freed themselves after part of the floor collapsed and then helped passengers reach openings in the aircraft. Carla Johnson guided passengers away from the wreckage.

Kelly accompanied two injured passengers in an airport van. In her later account, the first hospital was full, so they had to go to another. There she continued helping the nurses. She recalled that only after meeting other crew members at the hospital did she learn that another aircraft had struck Pan Am; she had initially suspected a bomb.

The Spanish investigation called KLM's takeoff without clearance the fundamental cause and identified the failure to stop after indications that Pan Am was still on the runway. Dutch investigators placed more emphasis on communications and the surrounding conditions. Neither description turns the third exit into proof of why Pan Am missed it, or the captain's possible time pressure into a proven private motive.

The investigators recommended clearer radio phraseology. Tenerife also became an important example for training crews to question an assumption and verify it, though it was not the sole origin of crew resource management. A flight's route and its permission to depart had to be communicated in unmistakably different terms.

On March twenty-seventh, nineteen seventy-eight, exactly a year after the accident, the United States Secretary of Transportation presented Kelly with the department's first Award for Heroism. Bragg, Donovan, Jackson and Johnson received FAA awards at the same ceremony. The question that failed to stop the takeoff is preserved in the cockpit record. The rescue that followed is preserved in the accounts of the people who made it home.
