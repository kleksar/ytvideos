How could rescuers bring twelve boys and their coach through a flooded cave when none of the children had ever learned to cave-dive?

And why could they not simply wait for the water to recede?

In June 2018, twelve boys and their football coach entered Tham Luang cave in northern Thailand.

Rain flooded the passages behind them. Outside, their families waited without knowing whether anyone was still alive.

Rescue divers pushed into the flooded tunnels. The current, tight passages and muddy water made every trip difficult.

On July second, British cave divers Rick Stanton and John Volanthen found the entire group on a rocky ledge.

They were alive. But finding them and bringing them home were two very different problems.

The way back included long underwater sections and narrow gaps where a diver had little room to turn.

Visibility was often terrible. A child who panicked and pulled off a breathing mask could drown before anyone could help.

None of the boys had cave-diving experience. The rescuers couldn't safely teach them that skill in the time available.

Waiting for the floodwater to recede might mean months in the cave. Oxygen levels inside were already falling.

Crews pumped out water and laid guide ropes. Divers also carried air cylinders along the route.

On July sixth, former Thai Navy SEAL Saman Kunan died while returning from a mission to place those supplies.

The cave divers proposed a startling answer: sedate the children so they would not panic in the flooded passages.

They turned to Richard Harris, an Australian anaesthetist who was also an experienced cave diver.

Harris was reluctant. An unconscious child cannot fix a leaking mask or tell a rescuer when something is wrong.

But leaving the group inside as the monsoon continued was dangerous too. The team prepared full-face breathing masks.

Harris would anaesthetize the children before they set off. Divers were trained to give extra doses if needed along the way.

Each child would have to keep breathing while a diver guided him around bends, rocks and submerged squeezes.

At some points the water gave way to muddy, partly dry chambers where other rescue workers could take over.

On July eighth, the rescuers began with four boys. Their journey out would test the entire plan.

The first child disappeared into the dark water with his diver. Others followed at intervals along the same route.

Beyond the flooded sections, teams carried the boys toward waiting medics and ambulances.

Then the first boy emerged alive. By that evening, all four had been brought out.

The team still couldn't rush back in. They needed to rest and replace air cylinders before starting again.

On July ninth, four more boys came out safely. Five people were still waiting inside.

On July tenth, the final four boys and their coach were brought through the passages.

All thirteen survived. The operation had brought them out over three days, after eighteen days trapped underground.
