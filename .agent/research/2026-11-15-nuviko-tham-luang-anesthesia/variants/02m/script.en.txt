How could thirteen people escape a flooded cave if twelve were children who had never cave-dived? And why was waiting for the rain to stop dangerous too?

In June 2018, twelve boys and their football coach went into Thailand's Tham Luang cave. Heavy rain flooded the way back. For nine days, nobody outside knew whether they were alive.

On July second, two British cave divers found all thirteen on a ledge. That was the good news. The route to the entrance still ran through narrow, muddy underwater passages. A child who panicked and pulled off a mask could drown.

The rescuers pumped water out and brought in air tanks. Yet oxygen inside was falling, and more monsoon rain was forecast. On July sixth, former Thai Navy SEAL Saman Kunan died on a supply mission. Even a trained diver could lose his life on that route.

Then came the decision that sounds backwards. Instead of teaching the boys to dive, the team would bring them out under anaesthesia. Australian doctor and cave diver Richard Harris prepared the children. Each wore a full-face breathing mask and was guided through the water by specialists.

The medicine prevented panic, but created another risk: an unconscious child could not report a leaking mask. The divers had to protect the airway and, when necessary, renew the anaesthesia along the route.

Four boys emerged alive on July eighth. Four more followed the next day. On July tenth, rescuers brought out the remaining four boys and their coach. All thirteen survived.

They did not escape by mastering cave diving in a few days. They survived because a vast rescue team made a perilous underwater journey possible for people who could not navigate it themselves.
