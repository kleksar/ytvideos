How do you bring thirteen people through a flooded cave when the children cannot cave-dive? Why not leave them safely on the ledge until the water falls? And what could possibly make putting them under anaesthesia the less dangerous choice?

In June 2018, twelve boys from a football team and their coach entered the Tham Luang cave system in northern Thailand. Monsoon rain flooded the passages behind them. Their bicycles were found outside, but their families had no way to know if the group had survived.

Cave divers searched where an ordinary rescue crew could not go. Some passages were so tight a swimmer could barely turn. Muddy water swallowed the light. Even with a guide line, the trip demanded specialized experience.

On July second, British divers Rick Stanton and John Volanthen reached a rocky ledge. All twelve boys and their coach were there, alive. Finding them ended the search; it did not solve the rescue.

To reach daylight, each child would have to travel through underwater bends and narrow gaps. None had the training to do this. A sudden movement could dislodge a mask, and a rescuer might not see the problem in the brown water. The adults could not simply carry a conscious, terrified novice as if crossing a shallow stream.

Waiting looked easier, but the cave could remain flooded for months. Oxygen in the boys' chamber was reported down to about fifteen percent, far below the usual level. Rescuers installed pumps, ropes and cylinders, while another wave of monsoon rain threatened to close their opportunity.

Then, on July sixth, former Thai Navy SEAL Saman Kunan died while returning from an air-supply mission. His death changed the meaning of every plan. This route was lethal even to someone accustomed to working underwater. How could thirteen inexperienced people survive it?

The cave-diving team proposed a method that sounded more dangerous than the journey itself: medically sedate the children before taking them into the water. They sought Richard Harris, an Australian anaesthetist who was also an experienced cave diver. He understood both the medicines and the passages where medicine would have to work.

Harris and fellow Australian diver Craig Challen examined the group. The idea was not to make the boys swim while sleepy. They would be anaesthetized, placed in full-face masks supplying oxygen, and moved through the flooded sections by divers who knew the route.

It solved one danger and created another. A frightened child could panic and tear off a mask; an unconscious child would not fight the equipment, but also could not say if it leaked. Every bend threatened the seal. The doctor could not travel beside each child throughout the journey.

The anaesthetic could wear off before they reached safety. Harris trained cave divers to administer further doses when needed. Those divers were now responsible not only for navigation and air but also for a patient who might stop breathing. On stretches where the water gave way to muddy ground, other rescue workers could carry the children onward.

On July eighth, the first four boys were prepared and taken into the flooded passages one after another. Rescuers outside could only wait to learn whether the method worked. The first boy reached the medical team alive. By evening, all four were out.

Yet the success did not mean the rest could follow immediately. The route needed fresh air cylinders and the divers needed recovery time. On July ninth they returned and brought out four more boys. The next day the remaining four boys and their coach followed.

All thirteen came out alive. In the medical area the breathing masks could finally be replaced and the children treated for cold and exhaustion. The answer to the opening question was not a miraculous shortcut: pumps, supplies, cave divers, medical preparation and constant attention to breathing made one dangerous decision work thirteen times.
