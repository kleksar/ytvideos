How do you bring twelve children and their coach out of a flooded cave when none of the boys has ever learned to cave-dive? Why can't you simply wait for the water to go down? And how do you get people through a tunnel where an experienced diver has already died?

The story began on June twenty-third, 2018, in northern Thailand. Twelve boys from a football team entered Tham Luang cave with their coach. During monsoon season, water can move through the limestone tunnels quickly. Rain flooded the route to the entrance while the group was still inside.

Their bicycles were left near the entrance. Families and search teams could tell where the group had gone, but they had no way of knowing where the children were or whether they had reached dry ground. Days passed with no contact.

In an open stretch of water, a diver can often head for the surface if something goes wrong. In a flooded cave, there may be solid rock overhead. Mud can hide the tunnel right in front of you. Rescuers laid guide lines so they could find their way by touch.

On July second, two British cave divers, Rick Stanton and John Volanthen, reached a ledge deep inside. Their lights found the entire group: twelve boys and their coach. All thirteen were alive. Their discovery ended the search, but placed an even harder question in front of the rescue teams.

How could anyone bring them back along that same route? The boys had no cave-diving training. Breathing from a mask was one skill; remaining calm in a dark, narrow, flooded passage while another diver guided you was another. Panic could mean a lost mask or a blocked passage, with almost no room to help.

One possibility was to wait. Rescuers sent food and medical assistance inward. But monsoon water might keep the cave flooded for months. Another possibility was to drill down from the surface. Locating a practicable route through the rock and moving thirteen people up it was not something anyone could promise in time.

Crews pumped huge amounts of water out of the cave, making more of the route accessible on foot. But some passages remained submerged. Meanwhile, officials reported that the oxygen level where the group was waiting had fallen to around fifteen percent, compared with about twenty-one percent in ordinary air.

To keep the route usable, rescue divers moved breathing cylinders into position. Every cylinder had to be carried through the very passages that made the evacuation so dangerous. The line of supplies was itself a risky expedition, repeated again and again.

In early July, former Thai Navy SEAL Saman Kunan lost his life on a mission to supply the route with breathing cylinders. He had volunteered for the rescue. Now the team faced the prospect of taking thirteen people along a route on which a trained diver had died.

The cave divers' proposal reversed the usual picture of a rescue. Instead of asking an untrained child to control every movement and breathing response, they would put the child under anaesthesia. A specialist would transport him through the flooded passages as a breathing patient, not as a new diver.

That plan needed a person who understood two unrelated kinds of danger: the confined underwater journey and medical anaesthesia. Richard Harris was an Australian anaesthetist and an experienced cave diver. He joined the rescue with fellow Australian diver Craig Challen, who was also a veterinarian.

They examined the group in the cave. The boys had received food, but the journey ahead would take hours. Under anaesthesia a patient can lose the ability to protect their own airway. In a hospital a doctor can reach the patient immediately. In the cave, rescuers would sometimes barely have room to move.

Yet without anaesthesia, the frightened child might reach for the mask or fight against the equipment. A full-face breathing mask was selected to cover both nose and mouth. The rescue masks used a positive-pressure supply of oxygen-rich gas, designed to help keep water out and air moving in.

A mask is not a guarantee. A loose seal in the wrong place could still become catastrophic. The children wore wetsuits and buoyancy equipment, and rescuers had to secure them for movement without making the tight passage impossible to negotiate. Equipment, body position and the guide line all mattered.

There was another clock inside the plan. The anaesthetic would not last for the entire journey, which could take around three hours. Harris trained cave divers who were not anaesthetists to give additional medication when required. That was a serious medical responsibility added to their diving responsibilities.

Divers would have to move through the submerged sections while watching their own air supply, the cave geometry and the patient's breathing. A patient could not tell them something was wrong. In dry or partly flooded chambers, other rescue workers were waiting to help move the children onward.

On July eighth, the first four boys were prepared. Each had to leave the relative safety of the ledge and enter the water unable to control the journey himself. The dive teams set off with intervals between them. Outside, there was no way to see how the plan was progressing inside the flooded rock.

Harris later recalled that one boy had difficulty breathing after receiving the anaesthetic. The doctor helped keep his airway open until his breathing improved. The episode made the medical risk concrete even before the child had completed the journey.

The rescue divers carried and guided the children through submerged bends. Where the passage opened onto mud and rock, teams transferred them onto stretchers and moved them toward the exit. They had to prevent the breathing apparatus from shifting while changing from swimming to carrying.

After the most difficult sections, the children reached a field medical team. The full-face equipment could be exchanged for medical oxygen masks. The first patients were cold and needed careful attention, a detail later described in medical reporting on the operation. The achievement was not simply crossing the cave. It was arriving alive and still breathing.

On the first evening, all four of the initial boys were out. That was the first convincing result for a method whose failure might have been fatal. But the divers did not immediately bring out the others. They needed rest, fresh cylinders and renewed supplies along the route.

On July ninth, another four boys reached the exit. Five people still waited on the ledge, including the coach who had entered the cave with the team. On July tenth, the divers went back for them.

All thirteen were brought out alive. They had spent eighteen days trapped underground. Yet some of the rescuers were still inside when the pumping equipment failed and the water began to rise. The people withdrawing from the cave now had to get themselves out.

The boys were taken to hospital to recover. Only after the medical team judged them ready did they learn that someone had died during the rescue. They gathered around a drawing of Saman Kunan, wrote messages on it and commemorated him with a minute of silence.

On July twenty-fifth, the football team took part in another ceremony. Eleven of the boys became Buddhist novices, with their coach also entering temple life. The families had promised to give thanks if the boys returned safely, and the ceremony honoured Saman. The twelfth boy was Christian and did not take part in the ordination. Saman's widow attended the ceremony.

In early August, the boys completed their stay at the temple. They placed robes before a large photograph of Saman and returned to their families. Their coach remained in the monkhood for longer. The team had entered the cave together, on an ordinary day in June. Now, they were saying goodbye to a man who had helped make their return possible.
