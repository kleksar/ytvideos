In October 1964, fifty-seven people escaped beneath the Berlin Wall through a secret tunnel.

On the second night, a border guard was killed. East Germany blamed the tunnel builders.

But records examined years later revealed that the fatal shots came from his own side.

How did a group of students free so many people, and why was the shooting covered up?

The Wall had divided Berlin since 1961, cutting families and friends off from one another.

Crossing from East Berlin to the West could lead to arrest or death.

Engineering student Joachim Neumann escaped in 1961, but his girlfriend Christa remained in the East.

He joined students digging tunnels because people like Christa had no safe way out.

An earlier tunnel was betrayed. Christa was arrested and sentenced to prison.

Joachim tried again, this time with a larger group working from an abandoned bakery.

The bakery stood in West Berlin, just beyond the heavily guarded border.

They dug down roughly twelve metres before turning toward the eastern side.

Then they cut a narrow passage, a hundred and forty-five metres beneath streets and barriers.

Old flour sacks carried the soil away, while the students worked for months.

At last the passage surfaced inside a disused outhouse in an East Berlin courtyard.

Now they had to lead people there without alerting guards stationed nearby.

On October third, their contacts quietly notified families waiting on the eastern side.

Visitors approached the apartment building and gave the code word: Tokyo.

Behind the building, guides led them into the outhouse and down a shaft.

The passage was so tight that escapees had to crawl on hands and knees.

At the other end, helpers raised them up into the West Berlin bakery.

One group after another reached freedom without crossing the Wall above ground.

Then Joachim received unexpected news: Christa had been released from prison early.

She knew nothing about the tunnel until Joachim arranged for word to reach her.

On the second night she came to the building, entered the passage and escaped.

Joachim and Christa were finally together again after years apart.

But the builders had intended to bring out more than a hundred people.

East German security officers had learned about the operation. The second night turned dangerous.

Plainclothes officers returned to the courtyard with armed border guards.

The remaining helpers rushed toward the tunnel as gunfire erupted in the darkness.

A twenty-one-year-old East German guard named Egon Schultz was hit and later died.

The helpers managed to escape, but the authorities discovered and closed the tunnel.

Fifty-seven people had crossed. Many others who were expected never got their chance.

East Germany publicly claimed that the escape helpers had murdered Schultz.

His death became part of a campaign against the people who dug the tunnel.

Yet investigators in East Germany had found evidence that contradicted the official story.

A helper's bullet had wounded Schultz, but the fatal shots came from another border soldier.

Those findings were kept secret, and the autopsy records were removed.

After the Wall fell in 1989, the shooting could be investigated openly again.

The evidence exposed the cover-up. It did not bring Schultz back.

Joachim and Christa later married. Christa and fifty-six others had escaped through the tunnel.

But Schultz's family had spent years hearing a false account of his death.
