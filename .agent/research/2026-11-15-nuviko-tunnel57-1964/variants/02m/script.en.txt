How could fifty-seven people cross the Berlin Wall without passing a checkpoint? And why did a border guard die when the escape route was discovered?

In 1964, a group of students worked from an abandoned bakery in West Berlin. Across the border, people they knew were waiting for a way out.

One builder, Joachim Neumann, had escaped in 1961. His girlfriend Christa had remained in the East. An earlier tunnel failed after an informer alerted the secret police; Christa was arrested.

Now the students dug a new route, about 145 metres long and roughly twelve metres below the streets. It reached an old outhouse in an East Berlin courtyard.

On October third, visitors approached the building, gave the code word Tokyo, and crawled to the bakery on the western side. The tunnel worked.

Christa, unexpectedly released from prison early, arrived the next night. She made it through. Joachim finally saw her in the West.

But security agents had found the entrance. They returned with armed border guards. During the escape helpers' retreat, twenty-one-year-old Egon Schultz was shot and died. The authorities closed the tunnel.

East Germany accused the helpers of murder. Records later showed a more disturbing sequence: a helper wounded Schultz first, but the fatal shots came accidentally from another East German guard. The state had concealed that finding.

Fifty-seven people reached freedom. Others never got their chance. Joachim and Christa were reunited; Schultz's family was left for years with an official account that was false.
