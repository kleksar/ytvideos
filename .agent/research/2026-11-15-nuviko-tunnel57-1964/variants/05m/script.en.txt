How did fifty-seven people get under the Berlin Wall while armed guards watched the streets above? What happened when two men came to the tunnel entrance pretending they wanted to escape? And why did East Germany blame the wrong man for a border guard's death?

In 1964, the Wall had divided Berlin for three years. Crossing without permission could mean prison or death. For families split between East and West, waiting for the border to open offered no certain end.

Joachim Neumann had crossed to West Berlin in 1961 using a borrowed Swiss passport. His girlfriend, Christa Gruhle, remained in the East. Joachim tried to help her through an earlier tunnel, but someone informed the Stasi. People waiting to escape were arrested. Christa went to prison.

Joachim joined another attempt. This time, the builders started beneath an abandoned bakery in West Berlin. For months, mostly students dug a passage about 145 metres long, roughly twelve metres below the streets. They carried the earth away in old flour sacks.

The tunnel ended in a disused outhouse behind an apartment building in East Berlin. That exit was the critical part of the plan. Instead of walking toward the guarded Wall, a person could enter an ordinary building, cross its courtyard and disappear underground.

But everyone would have to use the same door. One person followed or one stranger admitted inside could expose the entire operation.

On October third, helpers began guiding people to the building on Strelitzer Strasse. The password was Tokyo. Visitors crossed the courtyard, descended into the shaft and crawled through the narrow passage toward the bakery. On the other side, helpers pulled them out.

The first people reached West Berlin. Then more arrived. Each successful crossing created another reason to keep the entrance secret for the people still waiting.

During the escape, Joachim learned that Christa had unexpectedly been released from prison early. She knew nothing about the new tunnel until he managed to get a message to her.

On the second night, Christa came to the building, climbed down and crawled to the West. Joachim had tried once and lost her to prison. Now they were together again. But the helpers kept the tunnel open; they hoped to bring out more than a hundred people.

East German security agents had found out. Two officers in civilian clothes approached the entrance, claiming they wanted to escape. They said they needed to fetch another person and left.

They returned with armed border guards. The men at the entrance had not come for freedom. They had brought the people who could end everyone else's chance.

Among the guards was twenty-one-year-old Egon Schultz. Before military service, he had taught first-grade children in Rostock. The soldiers sent into the dark courtyard had not been told the full purpose of the Stasi operation.

Escape helper Reinhard Furrer noticed the armed men and ran to warn the others. The helpers hurried back toward the shaft. In the darkness, shots were fired.

One helper, Christian Zobel, shot Schultz and wounded him. Then another East German border guard fired into the courtyard and struck Schultz with bullets that proved fatal. Schultz died on the way to hospital. The helpers escaped, but the authorities discovered and closed the tunnel.

Fifty-seven people had crossed. Others who had been preparing to leave could no longer use that route.

East Germany announced that the escape helpers had murdered Schultz. His death became part of the state's public campaign against those who helped people flee.

There was a real shot from an escape helper. But East German investigators established that it was not the fatal one. The deadly rounds had come unintentionally from Schultz's fellow guard. The authorities kept that finding secret and removed the autopsy documents from the hospital records.

The escape helpers tried to challenge the account. In December, they sent balloons over the Wall carrying a letter addressed to Schultz's mother. They expressed sympathy and disputed the official explanation. Whether she ever received the letter is unknown.

After the Wall fell in 1989, investigators were able to examine the case again. The later inquiry confirmed who had fired the fatal shots. That did not bring Schultz back or make the gunfire at the tunnel harmless.

Joachim and Christa later married. Fifty-seven people had found a way through a border that split their lives. Schultz's family, meanwhile, had been given a story that concealed how he died. The tunnel carried people to safety; the records of that night took decades to bring into the open.
