How do you guide dozens of people under a guarded border without the soldiers above noticing? What happens when the secret police arrive at the very entrance? And who actually fired the shots that killed a young border guard?

In October 1964, the Berlin Wall had divided families for three years. Some people who crossed without permission were arrested; others were shot. A group of students in West Berlin had been digging because people close to them were trapped on the other side.

Their route began in an abandoned bakery. It went down roughly twelve metres, crossed beneath the border and surfaced in a disused outhouse behind an East Berlin apartment building. It was about 145 metres long.

That strange choice of exit mattered: visitors could walk into a courtyard without appearing to approach the Wall itself. The students had built a passage through a border they could not cross on the street.

One of them was Joachim Neumann. He had escaped from East Germany in 1961 with a borrowed Swiss passport. But his girlfriend, Christa Gruhle, stayed behind.

Joachim's first tunnel attempt ended badly. Someone informed the Stasi, the East German secret police. People waiting to escape were arrested, including Christa, who was sent to prison.

So the new tunnel was more than an engineering project to him. It offered a second chance, although he had no guarantee Christa would be free to use it.

Under the bakery, students worked for months. They carried excavated earth away in old flour sacks. The gallery was narrow enough that a person had to crawl. They could not simply announce a new route through the city.

On October third, 1964, the route opened. Helpers contacted people in East Berlin and directed them to a building on Strelitzer Strasse. At the door, the password was Tokyo.

Visitors went through the building, entered the courtyard and climbed down into the old outhouse. From there, they crawled toward the bakery. On the western side, hands helped pull them out.

Each arrival confirmed the plan could work. It also meant another person had to reach the same entrance without exposing everyone still waiting.

On the second day, Joachim received unexpected news. Christa had been released from prison early. She did not know about this tunnel until he arranged for a message to reach her.

That night she arrived at the building, entered the shaft and made the journey to West Berlin. The couple who had been separated by the Wall were together again.

The escape was not over. The builders had hoped to bring out more than a hundred people. But East German security officers had learned what was happening.

Two plainclothes agents approached the building pretending to be would-be escapees. They said they needed to fetch another person. Then they came back with armed border soldiers.

One helper, Reinhard Furrer, saw the danger in the courtyard and warned the others. The helpers hurried toward the shaft. Shots rang out in the darkness.

Twenty-one-year-old border guard Egon Schultz fell wounded. He died on the way to hospital. The helpers escaped, but the tunnel was discovered and shut down.

Fifty-seven people had crossed. Those still expecting a chance to escape would not get one through this tunnel. In East Germany, the next story was about who had killed Schultz.

The government said the escape helpers had murdered a border guard. His death was used publicly against them. What the authorities already knew, however, did not fit that accusation.

A helper had fired first and wounded Schultz. But the fatal rounds were fired unintentionally by another East German border guard. Investigators on the eastern side identified the difference and kept the finding secret.

The Stasi removed the autopsy documents. Only after the Wall came down could the circumstances be investigated openly again. That later investigation confirmed the fatal friendly fire.

Joachim and Christa later married. They were among the people whose lives changed when the students opened a route beneath Berlin. But Schultz's family had lived for years with a false account of his death.

The tunnel was named for its fifty-seven survivors. Its other legacy was a documented truth the state had tried to bury: the person blamed for the fatal shot was not the one who fired it.
