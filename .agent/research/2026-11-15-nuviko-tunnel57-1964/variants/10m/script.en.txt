How did students get fifty-seven people through the Berlin Wall? What gave the secret police a chance to stop the escape? And why was the family of a dead border guard still fighting over his death decades later?

In 1961, Berlin was divided by a wall. Families who had lived in the same city suddenly found that an ordinary visit could end in arrest. One engineering student, Joachim Neumann, was among the people separated from someone they loved.

That year, Joachim reached West Berlin using a Swiss student's passport. His girlfriend Christa Gruhle stayed in East Germany.

Joachim later recalled that he could not imitate a Swiss accent. So he avoided speaking. When the guards took his passport away to check it, he acted like a foreign visitor annoyed by the delay. Eventually they let him through.

Joachim reached safety, but he could not simply return for Christa. With the Wall in place, other students from the East faced the same separation. Some young West Berliners began digging passages to bring them out.

Most tunnel plans failed. The work could take months, and the police only needed to discover an entrance or an informer. Joachim joined one attempt, but someone reported it to the Stasi, East Germany's secret police.

The police arrested people waiting to escape, including Christa. She was sentenced to sixteen months in prison. Joachim would have to find another way to get her out of East Germany.

Joachim and other helpers began again. Their new starting point was a closed bakery on Bernauer Strasse in West Berlin. From the street, the building did not announce the huge task beneath it.

Under the bakery the workers dug down about eleven or twelve metres, depending on the account, before moving towards East Berlin. The passage eventually stretched about 145 metres. It was not a corridor people could walk through upright.

The work went on for months. The men slept in the bakery during long shifts and filled discarded flour sacks with soil. Every metre of progress underground meant more earth to deal with above.

There was also a problem they could not fully solve in advance: they were digging towards a neighbourhood they could not freely enter. They had to emerge somewhere useful without appearing in the open under a guard's eyes.

At last they surfaced inside a disused outhouse in the courtyard behind an apartment building on Strelitzer Strasse. An ordinary-looking entrance into a residential yard had become a hidden doorway under the Wall.

Now the builders needed to get people to the entrance without exposing it. The passage would be useful only as long as the authorities did not know where it was.

On October third, 1964, the helpers began directing people to the building. They used the code word Tokyo. Those who reached the right address entered the hallway and made their way into the courtyard.

Hans-Joachim Tilleman, one of the people who escaped, later remembered walking along the street as though he were going to visit friends. He could see border guards nearby. Once he had reached the building, he took off his shoes and went quietly into the courtyard.

Inside the outhouse, guides lowered the fugitives into a shaft. They had to crawl along the dark, narrow passage until they reached the bakery on the western side. At the exit, other helpers lifted them into safety.

People started arriving at the bakery. The builders had finally seen months of work produce the result they wanted.

The organisers were expecting more than a hundred people. The escape was far from over.

Then a letter reached Joachim. Christa was not due to leave prison until December, but she had been released early. She had written to tell him, unaware that another tunnel was ready.

Joachim managed to send word back. On the second night Christa reached the building, entered the passage and crawled to West Berlin.

Joachim and Christa were together again. Other families were still waiting for their chance. By then, informants had alerted East German state security to the escape operation.

Two Stasi officers in civilian clothes came to the building. The escape helpers mistook them for people who wanted to flee.

The men said they had another person to collect and left. But instead of returning with a refugee, they sought assistance from armed border guards. The soldiers were told to investigate suspicious people near the border.

One of those soldiers was twenty-one-year-old Egon Schultz. Just a year earlier he had been teaching first grade in Rostock. Military service interrupted his teaching career.

His former pupils still wrote to him. In his letters back, Schultz defended his work at the border, though in private correspondence he had complained about having to obey orders without thinking. That night the guards were not told the real purpose of the Stasi operation.

Reinhard Furrer, one of the escape helpers waiting on the eastern side, noticed the armed men too late. He ran to warn the others. The helpers moved towards the tunnel, trying to get back below ground before they were caught.

Gunfire broke out. One helper, Christian Zobel, fired a pistol and struck Schultz. The wounded soldier fell. In the darkness, a fellow East German guard also fired and hit him with the rounds that proved fatal.

Schultz died on the way to hospital. The helpers escaped through the tunnel, but the route was exposed and closed. The operation that had freed fifty-seven people was over; other people expecting an escape no longer had that passage.

East German news quickly accused West Berlin agents of murder. At the same time, investigations began on both sides of the Wall. The two sides did not have access to the same evidence.

The helpers admitted to West Berlin investigators that one of them had fired. On the eastern side, investigators already knew that the fatal injury came from a different gun.

East German officials classified that finding and removed the autopsy files from the hospital. An internal party assessment also criticised the Stasi officers' handling of the operation and the instructions given to the troops. None of this was reflected in the public accusation.

On the day of Schultz's funeral, the school where he had taught took his name. A plaque at the building where he died blamed the West. His parents attended commemorations of their son, held around that official account.

In December the escape helpers sent balloons across the Wall carrying a letter to Schultz's mother. They expressed sympathy and disputed the state's account. Whether she received it is unknown.

West Berlin prosecutors asked their eastern counterparts for help investigating the death. East Berlin refused to cooperate and demanded the extradition of the supposed killer.

In 1965 a soldier who had fled to the West reported that his superior had mentioned a guard killed by his own colleague. The explanation had later been withdrawn in front of the troops. The West Berlin case against the escape helpers closed that year; there was a fine for illegal possession of a weapon, but no murder conviction.

The Wall fell in 1989. In 1992 a new investigation opened. This time the earlier East German records could be examined.

The evidence confirmed that Zobel had wounded Schultz and another guard had killed him unintentionally. The case against that guard was dismissed in 1994. He had been acting under orders, and his claim of self-defence was accepted.

Schultz's mother nevertheless supported a new accusation against the escape helpers. After Furrer and Zobel had died, her lawyers pursued the remaining helpers. Prosecutors closed that case in 1999: they could not attribute Zobel's shot to the other helpers.

Joachim and Christa married. Their lives together had become possible because of the tunnel. For Schultz's family, the official story of his death had shaped decades of remembrance, even after the border was gone.

Eventually, some of the escape helpers and a friend put up a new plaque at the building. It remembers Egon Schultz as a victim of the Wall. Fifty-seven people had escaped through the tunnel; the new plaque also makes room for the man who did not survive its discovery.
