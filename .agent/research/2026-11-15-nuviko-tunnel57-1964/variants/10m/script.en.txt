How did a group of university students get fifty-seven people through a border that soldiers watched day and night? Why did the police discover the tunnel just as one builder was reunited with his girlfriend? And how did the government turn a soldier's accidental death into an accusation it knew was false?

The story begins in Berlin in 1961. A wall had divided neighbourhoods, railway routes and families. The concrete barrier was visible, but the new border reached into daily life: a journey that had once been ordinary could now end in arrest.

Joachim Neumann was a young engineering student in East Germany. In 1961, he used a borrowed Swiss passport to get through a checkpoint and reach West Berlin. His girlfriend Christa Gruhle was still on the eastern side.

In a later interview, Joachim remembered that the guards questioned his borrowed identity. He tried to behave like an impatient foreign visitor. This detail survives in his own recollection; it is not a transcript of the checkpoint conversation.

Joachim reached safety, but he could not simply return for Christa. With the Wall in place, other students from the East faced the same separation. Some young West Berliners began digging passages to bring them out.

Most tunnel plans failed. The work could take months, and the police only needed to discover an entrance or an informer. Joachim joined one attempt, but someone reported it to the Stasi, East Germany's secret police.

The police arrested people who had gathered to escape. Christa was among them and received a prison sentence. The first tunnel had not reunited the couple; it had placed her behind another locked door.

Joachim and other helpers began again. Their new starting point was a closed bakery on Bernauer Strasse in West Berlin. From the street, the building did not announce the huge task beneath it.

They had to cross a border on which watchtowers, barriers and armed patrols made a surface escape dangerous. Digging underneath it offered a different possibility, but only if the tunnel remained secret for every day of construction.

Under the bakery the workers dug down about eleven or twelve metres, depending on the account, before moving towards East Berlin. The passage eventually stretched about 145 metres. It was not a corridor people could walk through upright.

The students crawled, hauled earth and worked in a confined space for months. They filled old flour sacks with soil. That made the unwanted earth movable, but every sack still had to be handled without exposing the operation above ground.

There was also a problem they could not fully solve in advance: they were digging towards a neighbourhood they could not freely enter. They had to emerge somewhere useful without appearing in the open under a guard's eyes.

At last they surfaced inside a disused outhouse in the courtyard behind an apartment building on Strelitzer Strasse. An ordinary-looking entrance into a residential yard had become a hidden doorway under the Wall.

That solved the engineering problem. It left a harder human one. The people waiting in East Berlin had to be told when to come, how to behave and whom to trust. A single wrong visitor could expose the whole passage.

On October third, 1964, the helpers began directing people to the building. They used the code word Tokyo. Those who reached the right address entered the hallway and made their way into the courtyard.

One escapee later described walking past a nearby guard post while counting the house numbers. His account makes clear why the courtyard mattered: the danger was not only inside the tunnel. It began on the approach to the door.

Inside the outhouse, guides lowered the fugitives into a shaft. They had to crawl along the dark, narrow passage until they reached the bakery on the western side. At the exit, other helpers lifted them into safety.

The first people came through. Then more followed. The tunnel was doing something that months of work had only promised: turning a map line that families could not cross into a route they could use.

The organisers hoped to bring out more than a hundred people. But each successful crossing depended on the same doorway remaining unnoticed. They had created a route with extraordinary capacity and a single vulnerable entrance.

During the operation Joachim received news he had not expected. Christa had been released from prison ahead of schedule. She knew nothing about the new tunnel. Joachim arranged for a message to reach her in the East.

For him, the escape plan suddenly had a chance to achieve its most personal purpose. Christa went to the address on the second night, entered the shaft and crawled through to West Berlin.

Joachim and Christa were together again. The first failed escape had brought an arrest and prison. This one had brought her out. The students were still working to rescue others when the situation changed.

East German state security had learned of the operation. Two officers in civilian clothes approached the building, appearing to be people who wanted to escape. The helpers did not at first recognize them for what they were.

The men said they had another person to collect and left. But instead of returning with a refugee, they sought assistance from armed border guards. The soldiers were told to investigate suspicious people near the border.

One of those soldiers was Egon Schultz. He was twenty-one. Before military service, he had trained as a schoolteacher and taught children in Rostock. His life cannot be reduced to a figure in the escapees' story.

The border troops had not been told the full purpose of the Stasi operation. The agents knew they were moving against an escape network. The guards were sent into a dark courtyard to investigate what was described to them as suspicious activity.

Reinhard Furrer, one of the escape helpers waiting on the eastern side, noticed the armed men too late. He ran to warn the others. The helpers moved towards the tunnel, trying to get back below ground before they were caught.

Gunfire broke out. One helper, Christian Zobel, fired a pistol and struck Schultz. The wounded soldier fell. In the darkness, a fellow East German guard also fired and hit him with the rounds that proved fatal.

Schultz died on the way to hospital. The helpers escaped through the tunnel, but the route was exposed and closed. The operation that had freed fifty-seven people was over; other people expecting an escape no longer had that passage.

What happened next turned a fatal encounter into a long-running public lie. The East German government accused the escape helpers of murdering Schultz. News reports and public ceremonies presented him as a victim of hostile agents from the West.

There was a real shot fired by a helper, and the helpers did not deny that one of them had used a gun. But investigators on the eastern side found that the injury from that shot was not what killed Schultz.

The fatal bullets came unintentionally from another border soldier, firing under orders in the courtyard. Officials classified the findings and removed the autopsy documents from the hospital records. The public accusation remained.

For Schultz's family, the state did not merely provide incomplete information. It commemorated him through the account it chose to tell. His name was given to a school and a street; ceremonies tied his death to the official accusation.

The helpers did try to answer. In December 1964 they sent balloons over the Wall carrying a letter addressed to Schultz's mother. They offered sympathy and challenged the government's description of how her son had died.

But the helpers had no access to the concealed East German findings. The political argument continued while the forensic distinction between a nonfatal wound and the fatal shots remained hidden.

In West Berlin, prosecutors investigated the shooting. The case against the helpers was closed in 1965, although illegal possession of a weapon brought a fine. That was not the same as a full public accounting of the death.

The Berlin Wall fell in 1989. Only afterward did investigators have the opportunity to revisit the evidence without the same official secrecy. A case opened in 1992 examined the people involved, including the Stasi and border troops.

The later investigation confirmed the sequence: Zobel's bullet wounded Schultz, but another guard fired the fatal rounds. The fatal shot was unintended. The border soldier who fired it was not prosecuted for murder; the case was dismissed.

The discovery did not reverse the loss of a young teacher's life, nor did it make every action during the escape harmless. It established something narrower and essential: the government had publicly blamed people for a fatal act its own investigation attributed elsewhere.

Joachim and Christa later married. Their reunion was a direct result of the tunnel. Reinhard Furrer later became an astronaut. These are lives that continued beyond the two nights in Berlin, even as the investigation into Schultz's death remained unfinished for decades.

People remember Tunnel 57 because fifty-seven civilians crawled beneath the Wall into West Berlin. The other part of its history is what remained above ground: a dead border guard, a family given a false explanation, and records that finally contradicted the state's story.
