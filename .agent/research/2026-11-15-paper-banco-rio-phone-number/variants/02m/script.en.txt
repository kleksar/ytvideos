How did robbers disappear from a bank surrounded by police? And why did a phone number they gave to a television station help investigators find them?

On January 13, 2006, armed men entered Banco Río in Acassuso, near Buenos Aires. Twenty-three customers and employees were held hostage.

Police blocked the streets. A robber in a gray suit negotiated with them while his accomplices emptied safe-deposit boxes. Officers could watch the doors, but they could not see the passage prepared beneath the building.

The gang had connected the basement to a storm drain. They had even arranged boats to carry the stolen cash and jewelry along it.

When police finally entered, the hostages were alive and the robbers were gone. More than 140 boxes had been opened. Contemporary estimates put the loot at roughly nineteen million dollars.

But while buying time with police, the man in gray had also contacted a television newsroom. He wanted to speak on air and left a mobile number for the producers.

At the 2010 trial, communications analysts described a less direct trail. The number's SIM card was registered under a false name, but the handset identifier linked the device to other SIM cards. Those connections, alongside other evidence, helped investigators trace suspects and vehicles. Separately, the wife of one robber gave authorities information about him. Her reasons have been disputed; the tip still helped the investigation.

In 2010, four members were convicted after a trial. The man in gray later admitted his part and received a separate sentence. Most of the estimated haul was never recovered.

The escape had beaten the police cordon, but not the investigation. The people held inside the bank had faced real danger; the negotiator later disputed the familiar claim that every weapon was a toy.

One route led the robbers out through a drain. Another led police back through a number volunteered to television.
