A bank near Buenos Aires was surrounded by police. Twenty-three people were trapped inside. How did the robbers escape, and what eventually led investigators back to them?

On January 13, 2006, armed men entered Banco Río in Acassuso. One, wearing a gray suit, spoke to a police negotiator. His accomplices opened safe-deposit boxes while officers guarded the doors outside.

But the doors were the wrong place to watch. The gang had prepared a passage from the basement into a storm drain. Inflatable boats carried the stolen cash and jewelry through the drainage system, beyond the police cordon.

When officers entered the bank, they found the hostages alive. The robbers had vanished. Reports put the haul at millions of dollars, with much of it never recovered.

Then investigators followed a mistake made above ground. During the siege, the man in gray had called a television newsroom, hoping to speak on air. He left a mobile number. According to evidence presented at trial, analysts linked the phone's handset to other SIM cards and calls associated with suspects.

A separate lead came from a robber's wife, who gave investigators information. Accounts disagree about her motives, so the tip cannot be reduced to a neat story of revenge.

The evidence led to arrests and convictions. Four men were convicted in a 2010 trial; the gray-suited negotiator was sentenced separately.

This was never a harmless trick: twenty-three people had been held hostage, and a police negotiator later disputed claims that every gun was a toy. The gang escaped the police line through a drain. A phone number helped bring the investigation back to them.
