Police surrounded a bank near Buenos Aires. Twenty-three people were being held inside.

Officers watched the doors. The robbers had prepared another exit, and it wasn't a door.

The question was how they could vanish from a surrounded building—and why the police found them later.

It was Friday, January 13, 2006. Armed men entered a Banco Río branch in Acassuso.

They stopped customers and employees from leaving. Soon police had blocked the street outside.

One robber, wearing a gray suit, took charge of the conversation with a police negotiator.

While he kept officers talking, his accomplices were opening safe-deposit boxes.

The robbery had been planned long before that Friday. Its escape route was already waiting underground.

A passage from the bank's basement connected to a storm drain running beneath the road.

The gang had inflatable boats for moving through the drain. The police were stationed up above.

But the escape would only work if officers stayed focused on the hostage situation.

The people inside were forced to wait while the gang emptied boxes of cash and jewelry.

The man in gray even arranged a birthday song for one hostage, according to witness testimony.

The police negotiator later recalled one detail that worried him: an armed bank guard had been released with his weapon.

He suspected something unusual, but he had no view of the hole in the basement.

The gang took valuables from more than 140 safe-deposit boxes. The exact count varies between reports.

Then they went through the basement passage and into the storm-drain system.

Inflatable boats carried them and the loot along the drainage route, out of the cordon.

Police entered the bank and found the hostages alive. The robbers had already disappeared.

The operation had fooled the cordon. Contemporary estimates put the stolen valuables at roughly nineteen million dollars.

For a moment, it looked as though the gang had escaped without giving police a useful lead.

But during the siege, the man in the gray suit had also contacted a television newsroom.

He wanted to speak on air. To arrange it, he gave the newsroom a mobile number.

Investigators traced calls linked to that number and connected them with vehicles used in the robbery.

There was another lead. The wife of one robber went to the authorities with information about him.

Accounts later disagreed about her reasons. But her information helped investigators identify the men.

One suspect was detained weeks later. Others were identified and put on trial.

Four men were convicted at a 2010 trial. The man in gray later admitted his role and was sentenced too.

His combined prison term included convictions from other cases, not just the bank robbery.

Police recovered some cash, jewelry and vehicles. Most of the estimated haul remained unaccounted for.

The story is sometimes told like a harmless magic trick. But twenty-three people had been held hostage.

The police negotiator also disputed the popular claim that the robbers carried only toy guns.

The tunnel had taken the gang beyond the police line. A number given to a newsroom helped lead police back.
