How could a gang vanish from a bank surrounded by police? And how did a phone number the robbers gave to a television station help bring them to court? The people who had been inside the bank would have a very different story to tell when the case reached a judge.

On Friday, January 13, 2006, customers and employees were going about their day at a Banco Río branch in Acassuso, near Buenos Aires. Armed men took control of the building. Twenty-three people became hostages.

Police surrounded the bank. The officers had to establish contact with the men inside and try to get the captives out safely. A robber wearing a gray suit did much of the talking with the negotiator, Miguel Sileo.

While the police waited for a resolution, the bank's safe-deposit boxes were being emptied. Witnesses later recalled an extraordinary detail: the man in gray arranged a birthday song for one of the hostages. But people were still being held against their will, and the police could not assume they were safe.

When officers finally went into the bank, they found the hostages alive. They did not find the robbers.

There was a reason the men had disappeared without being seen at the doors. A hidden passage connected the bank to an underground drain. Investigators found physical evidence of the escape. More than a hundred and forty safe-deposit boxes had been opened, and one widely reported estimate put the loss at around nineteen million dollars. The exact value was uncertain.

The police now had to establish who had been involved. Finding the passage answered the first question, but it did not provide a list of names.

During the crisis, the man in gray had also contacted a television newsroom. He wanted to speak on air and had given the production staff a mobile phone number. The information was still available after the gang had gone.

At the trial, communications specialists explained that the number was registered under a false identity. Further investigation linked the device with other phone lines. These records helped the authorities develop leads alongside other evidence. There was also a separate source: the wife of one participant gave authorities information about him. Later accounts disagreed about her reasons.

Over the following months suspects were identified and detained. Four men were convicted in San Isidro in 2010. Their original sentences ranged from nine to fifteen years. The man in gray later admitted his part in a separate proceeding; his combined sentence also covered other offences.

The courtroom revealed another side of the robbery. Some retellings had claimed that the gang used only toy guns. The negotiator disputed that account, and the judges concluded that a functioning firearm had been used in the crime. They also considered the lasting harm to the victims when deciding the sentences.

Some former hostages broke down while giving evidence. Others had undergone therapy. Owners of the boxes described losing retirement savings and jewelry that had passed through generations of their families.

Years later, one family told a newspaper that they had learned about the theft on television while their father was seriously ill. They eventually recovered money through a legal process that took more than two years. Their father died before they could tell him about the recovery. The account does not establish that the robbery caused his death.

Much of the stolen property was never recovered. Compensation from the bank was a separate matter, pursued by victims in civil cases.

Television was part of the story long before a film was made about the robbery. A number given to a newsroom helped the investigation, and in at least one home the news was how a family learned its valuables were gone. The hidden passage explained how the gang left the bank. The evidence and the victims' testimony explained why leaving was not the end of the case.
