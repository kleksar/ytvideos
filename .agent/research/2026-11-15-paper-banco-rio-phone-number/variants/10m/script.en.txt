How could a gang disappear from a bank surrounded by police? How did a call to a television newsroom help the authorities put names to the crime? And why did the courtroom hear a very different version of the robbery from the one that would eventually reach the cinema?

On January 13, 2006, customers were visiting a branch of Banco Río in Acassuso, near Buenos Aires. Some had come to deal with their ordinary bank accounts. Others kept valuables in safe-deposit boxes. When armed men took control of the branch, twenty-three customers and employees became hostages.

Police surrounded the building and tried to establish contact with the people inside. Miguel Sileo, a police negotiator who later described the incident in interviews, spoke with a man wearing a gray suit. The important question for the officers at this point was whether they could get the captives out alive.

A customer later identified in newspaper coverage as Rodrigo had accompanied his wife to the bank. He had money and jewelry in the branch. Another customer, Ofelia, was away on vacation. She had two safe-deposit boxes there. Neither person's involvement in the case ended when the police eventually got into the building.

During the crisis, witnesses recalled a strange scene: the gray-suited robber arranged for a birthday song for a hostage. It is one of the details people still remember about the case. To those hearing about the robbery years later, it could sound like an episode from a comedy. But the police had to deal with actual captives, and a song gave them no assurance that the people inside were safe.

When officers finally entered the branch, they found the hostages alive. The men they had expected to arrest were missing. In a later interview, prosecutor Jorge Apolo remembered that the police briefly considered whether the criminals could have blended in with the captives. The people from the bank were separated while the authorities investigated what had happened.

Then came the discovery that made the robbery famous. There was a hidden passage between the bank and an underground storm drain. The men had left the building without going through the doors watched by police. The passage and other physical traces established an explanation for their disappearance.

The officers also found that more than a hundred and forty safe-deposit boxes had been opened. Money and jewelry had gone missing. The eventual estimate of the loss was around nineteen million US dollars, though an estimate of what was inside so many boxes could never describe every individual loss.

Finding the underground passage explained how the men had disappeared. It did not tell the police who they were. The search for the people behind the robbery continued after the captives had been released.

One of the leads had come from the men themselves. During the crisis, the gray-suited robber had contacted the television newsroom of Todo Noticias and supplied a mobile number so the staff could arrange a broadcast conversation. The number remained available to investigators after the robbery was over.

At the trial, communications specialists described how inquiries into the phone led to additional records. The original number was registered under a false identity, and further work linked the device to other phone lines. Those findings helped the investigation alongside other evidence. They were not a live map showing where every robber had gone.

There was also a witness who knew one of the suspects personally. Alicia Di Tullio, the former partner of Rubén de la Torre, gave information to the authorities. The reason she came forward became a subject of argument. She and De la Torre later gave different accounts of her motives.

By February 2010, Di Tullio was appearing in court. She described seeing her former partner with a large sum of money after the robbery. She also brought letters she said he had sent during his detention. The court added the letters to the evidence, and a prosecutor read passages from them aloud. De la Torre disputed her explanation for incriminating him. The testimony was no longer just a story told to the police; the judges could consider it together with the documents and other evidence.

The four men on trial were found guilty in May 2010. They were taken into custody while they waited for their sentences. For the prosecution, another question still mattered: what could the court establish about the danger to the people in the bank?

A popular account of the robbery held that the criminals had used only replica weapons. The judges reached a different conclusion. They accepted evidence that a functioning firearm had been used during the crime. The distinction mattered to the legal classification of the robbery. The judges could impose a more severe sentence than under the classification with which the case had originally reached trial.

When the court announced its sentences later that month, Rubén de la Torre received fifteen years, Fernando Araujo fourteen, José Julián Zalloecheverría ten, and Sebastián García Bolster nine. These were the original sentences of the four defendants, not a statement of how long each eventually stayed in prison.

The man in the gray suit, Mario Vitette Sellanes, admitted his involvement in separate proceedings later that year. His combined sentence also covered other offences. By the time the story was being told again in 2020, he was living in Uruguay and none of the other convicted robbers remained in prison.

The court had listened to more than investigators and defendants. Some people who had been held in the bank broke down when describing their experience years later. Others had needed therapy. Judge Alberto Ortolani described owners who had lost retirement funds and inherited jewelry. The lasting harm mattered to the court's decision on punishment.

Rodrigo recovered part of his stolen property some months after the robbery. Ofelia, who had interrupted her vacation, later said very little of her valuables had come back. Some jewelry that she received had been left behind in the branch itself. For her, recovering a few items did not mean that everything taken from the boxes was returned.

For owners of the boxes, another legal process lay ahead. Their claims against the bank could result in compensation even when the original objects could not be located. A lawyer who represented several of them later described a client who had sold a home and kept the proceeds in the bank while preparing to buy another. The loss had interrupted a plan already under way.

One family told a newspaper through that lawyer that they had learned about the theft from television. Their father was seriously ill in hospital. They eventually obtained financial recovery, more than two years after the robbery. He died before they could tell him about it. The family's account establishes that sequence; it does not establish that the robbery caused his death.

In January 2020, a movie about the robbery was about to arrive in Argentine cinemas. Some of the victims chose that moment to speak to a newspaper, while remaining anonymous. Another family described losing the certificates for artworks they had hoped to exhibit in Brazil, along with savings and family keepsakes. A payment could replace a sum of money. It could not necessarily bring back those objects or the opportunity for which they had been kept.

Most of the estimated stolen property was still missing when the film was released. Yet the story of the crime had traveled far beyond that branch of Banco Río. The television newsroom had preserved information that helped the police identify suspects. Television had also brought the news of the losses into people's homes. The film returned attention to the robbery, and the people who had lost their belongings used that occasion to tell their side of it.

The hidden passage answered the mystery of the empty bank. Phone evidence, a witness and the court answered the question of who was responsible. The family waiting more than two years for compensation shows what the dramatic escape left unresolved long after the police had gone home.
