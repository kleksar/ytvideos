Why did hundreds of police officers surround a bank for almost seven hours and never see the robbers leave? What had the gang built under the street? And how did a phone number given to a television producer help unravel an apparently perfect escape?

The first puzzle begins outside a bank in Acassuso, north of Buenos Aires, on Friday, January 13, 2006. Customers and staff were inside when armed men took control of the branch. Twenty-three people became hostages. Police sealed off the streets, positioned officers around the building and began negotiating.

To the officers outside, the immediate problem was the people inside. An attempt to rush the bank could put them in danger. The robber doing most of the talking wore a gray suit. His job in the operation was to keep those conversations going while others forced open safe-deposit boxes.

The people being held inside were not a theatrical detail. They were the reason police could not simply treat the building as an empty vault. Records of the case describe robbers using some captives as shields. Later accounts sometimes make the robbery sound like a harmless performance because the gang escaped without killing a hostage. That leaves out what the captives had to endure.

The plan had started long before this Friday. According to the later account of the investigation, two future participants were removed from the bank in November 2004 after security noticed them photographing its interior. The gang continued planning. The essential problem was not how to enter a bank. It was how to leave one after an alarm brought the police.

They chose a route the cordon could not cover from the street. Below the branch was a basement. Outside it ran a storm-water drain. A passage connected the two. What looked to police like a contained siege was also the upper floor of a hidden escape operation.

There was another problem with that route. Cash and jewelry taken from a bank weigh something, and a long stretch of drain is not an ordinary corridor. The group had inflatable boats. Investigators later described a small dam built to raise the water level enough to make those boats usable. The engineer associated with that part of the escape, Sebastián García Bolster, would eventually be among the men convicted.

Above ground, officers had no view of this preparation. They were watching entrances, trying to assess the armed men and talking about the release of hostages. A police negotiator later recalled the unusual decision to let an armed guard leave with his weapon. That is his recollection, rather than independent proof of every detail of the raid. His suspicion did not reveal the hidden passage in time.

The negotiations also had their own peculiar moments. Witness evidence described the man in gray arranging a birthday song for a hostage. This became one of the details that later made the heist famous. But its operational purpose is easier to understand without guessing what the robber was thinking: every minute spent talking was another minute the police remained focused on the bank's visible rooms.

Inside, the other men were emptying safe-deposit boxes. Reports differ slightly on the exact count; more than 140 were opened. They contained money and jewelry belonging to customers. Contemporary reports placed the value of the missing property at roughly nineteen million US dollars, an estimate rather than a verified inventory of every item.

Eventually the gang stopped giving police a useful conversation to follow. The officers went in and found the captives alive. The people they had come to arrest were no longer in the building.

In the basement, the answer appeared: an opening leading into the hidden passage. Investigators described a hole roughly fifty by thirty centimeters, a drop and then a tunnel that reached the large drain beneath the road. Reports of explosives and improvised devices left near the exit meant officers could not treat every object as harmless. The escape route was not merely an idea from a film. There was physical evidence of it.

The boats moved the loot along the drain. Investigators described the group emerging several kilometers away, beyond the line of officers. A white van had been positioned over a manhole. A hole in its floor allowed the bags to be loaded without an ordinary handoff on the street. The man later convicted as the driver, José Julián Zalloecheverría, was a part of that final transfer.

The escape depended on several different jobs: negotiating, opening the boxes, preparing the water route and moving the property away after the tunnel. It could succeed even while the police were doing exactly what a hostage crisis demanded above ground. An officer at the bank doors could not have seen a bag moving through a drain far below.

Investigators did not simply accept that the gang had vanished. In the following days, police searched the drainage system. Divers later found an outboard motor from one of the boats submerged in the water. It was a real trace of the route, but a motor alone did not supply the names of the people who had used it.

That is where the second part of the story begins. During the siege, the man in gray had contacted the television newsroom of Todo Noticias. He wanted to speak on air. To arrange the call, he gave the production staff a mobile phone number.

The number did not magically show police a live location. Communications specialists later testified that the SIM card behind it was registered under a false name. That line was not itself a straightforward log of robbery-day calls. But the handset had its own identifying number. Investigators checked whether the same device had used other SIM cards, uncovering additional lines to examine. Together with vehicle records and separate testimony, those connections helped identify suspects. A number volunteered for television had opened a trail, not solved the whole case on its own.

There was a separate source of information. The wife of one participant went to authorities with details about him. Later accounts disagree about her motive. It is enough to say her information became another lead; there is no need to invent a domestic argument or claim that one jealous act solved the entire case.

The police were now working with evidence of different kinds. The physical tunnel and boats established how the robbery had happened. The telephone records and personal information helped investigators establish who was involved. Those are different questions, and the discovery of one did not automatically answer the other.

By the time the case came to court, the seeming simplicity of the getaway had given way to a much larger inquiry. In May 2010, four men were convicted in San Isidro. Rubén de la Torre received fifteen years, Fernando Araujo fourteen, Zalloecheverría ten and García Bolster nine. The names and prison terms were reported at the time of the judgment.

The gray-suited negotiator, Mario Vitette Sellanes, admitted his role in a separate, abbreviated proceeding later that year. The sentence associated with him was a combined term that incorporated other previous cases. Treating the whole combined term as punishment solely for this robbery would misrepresent the record.

The question of the weapons remained important. Some popular retellings described the guns as toys. The negotiator disputed that simple version, and the trial dealt with armed robbery and unlawful detention, not merely a theatrical trick against a bank. Victims also reported consequences from being held captive and from losing property kept in the boxes.

Some money, jewelry, cars and other property were recovered. Most of the estimated haul remained missing. That fact explains the lasting fascination with the getaway, but it does not erase the convictions or the danger faced by the people trapped in the branch.

The plan solved a physical problem with extraordinary preparation: a police cordon watched the building, while the escape moved below it. Yet another part of the operation left evidence in public view. The man who kept officers talking also gave a television newsroom a number investigators could trace. The bank's hidden exit led outside the cordon; the phone trail led back toward the people who used it.
