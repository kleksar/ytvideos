A bank was surrounded. Police watched the entrances. Twenty-three people were held inside. Yet the people who had taken them hostage were able to disappear. Where could they go from a building ringed by officers? And why did an escape that seemed so carefully prepared still lead to convictions?

The answer begins outside Buenos Aires, in Acassuso, on Friday, January 13, 2006. Customers and bank employees were inside a Banco Río branch when armed men took control. Police closed off the area. To the people trapped in the building, the danger was immediate; they had no reason to know what the robbers had prepared beneath their feet.

One of the men wore a gray suit. He became the voice speaking to police negotiator Miguel Sileo. While the officers tried to secure the hostages' release, other members of the gang opened safe-deposit boxes. The negotiator could hear the man directing the conversation. He could not see what was happening beyond the public banking room.

That separation was the real design of the crime. The hostage standoff was visible from the street. The exit was hidden. In the basement, a prepared opening led into a passage and then a storm-water drain. The point was not to force a way through a line of armed police. It was to leave without ever crossing that line at street level.

Preparing an underground exit involved more than finding a pipe on a map. The passage had to connect to a drain large enough to use. The gang also needed to move the stolen valuables through it. Investigators later described a small opening, a purpose-built connection to the drainage system and inflatable boats waiting underground. A member of the group had even built a barrier to raise the water enough for the boats to float.

Above ground, Sileo faced a different problem. His task was to get people out alive. He later recalled that a bank security officer had been released with his weapon, an unusual decision that made him suspicious. But suspicion of an odd detail was not a view into the basement. The police still had to treat the threat to the hostages as real.

The men inside were not simply waiting for police to lose patience. The gray-suited negotiator continued talking as his accomplices emptied the boxes. Reports disagree on the precise count, but more than 140 safe-deposit boxes were opened. The valuables included cash and jewelry. Some contemporary accounts estimated the total haul at about nineteen million dollars; that is an estimate, not an audited inventory of every missing item.

The human scene inside the bank also resisted the neat version told later. According to testimony, the man in gray arranged a birthday song for a hostage. It is an unsettling detail, not evidence that the standoff was harmless. People were still being held against their will. Later claims that the guns were all toys were challenged by Sileo, who described weapons and threats as he understood them during the operation.

Then the robbery moved beneath the street. Valuables went through the basement opening and into the drainage system. Inflatable boats carried the load along the waterway. The gang's escape also depended on a vehicle waiting away from the bank; accounts of the investigation describe it as positioned to receive the bags near an access point to the drain.

The important geography is simple. Officers were around the bank. The drain extended past that cordon. The robbers did not have to win a confrontation at the front doors. By the time police entered the branch, they found the hostages alive, but the people who had held them were gone. The building was still surrounded. The gang was no longer in it.

For investigators, the escape route was only the beginning of another problem. A tunnel can explain how people left. It cannot, by itself, identify where they went, which people worked together or where the valuables were taken. Those questions required evidence outside the spectacular scene at the bank.

And during the standoff, a small piece of that evidence had already been handed to someone outside. The man in gray contacted the television news channel TN and wanted to speak live on air. The channel did not put him on television. According to communication specialists' testimony at the 2010 trial, he nevertheless left a mobile telephone number with the newsroom.

The newsroom passed the number to the police. At first glance, one telephone number might seem unhelpful: it was associated with a SIM card registered under a false identity, and the line itself was not a simple record of calls made throughout the heist. But a SIM card is not the whole phone. The handset has its own identifying number.

Investigators examined whether that same handset had used other SIM cards at different times. Trial testimony described several additional telephone lines appearing in that analysis. From there, analysts could study contacts and connections among people, times and vehicles. A phone number offered to a television producer became a route into a much larger call investigation. It did not, on its own, magically solve the case.

There was also a separate human lead. The wife of one of the robbers gave authorities information about her husband and the gang. Accounts of her reasons differ, and assigning a single private motive would turn disputed claims into fact. Her information mattered because it helped give the phone and vehicle evidence names and relationships to check.

Investigators did not have to rely on one theatrical slip. They examined communications, vehicles and property believed to have been bought with the proceeds. Part of the stolen cash and jewelry was recovered. Other valuables were never accounted for. One suspect was detained within weeks, and other arrests and legal proceedings followed as the investigation developed.

Fernando Araujo, described in contemporary reporting as one of the alleged planners, was eventually found hiding in a remote area of San Juan province. Early reporting described a tent, food supplies and a satellite navigation device. Those details belong to the investigation's unfolding, not to the moment of escape. They show how far the case had moved from the bank entrance watched by police.

By 2010, four defendants had been convicted in a trial over the robbery. The man in the gray suit, Luis Mario Vitette Sellanes, later acknowledged his role and received a sentence in a separate proceeding. His overall sentence also included other cases, so it would be misleading to describe all of it as the punishment for this single robbery.

The story's numbers still need care. The count of safe-deposit boxes differs among reports. Estimates of the missing haul are not identical. Claims about which weapons were genuine are contested. None of those uncertainties changes the central sequence: twenty-three hostages, a prepared passage to a storm drain, an escape beyond the cordon, and evidence that later linked suspects to the operation.

It is tempting to finish with the ingenious underground exit, as though that were the whole achievement. But the hostages did not experience a clever puzzle. Sileo's responsibility was to get them out alive, and they were alive when officers entered. The investigation then continued long after the television cameras had left the scene.

The gang built a route that made the police perimeter almost irrelevant. What they did not control was every trace left during the hours above ground. The number they volunteered to a newsroom, another person's testimony and ordinary evidence work helped undo an extraordinary escape. The most important exit was underground. One of the most important clues had been given away in a phone call.
