In May 1946, a screwdriver slipped in a Los Alamos laboratory. Someone had been holding two metal shells apart.

Inside was a small plutonium core. The room flashed blue, and the scientists were struck by a burst of radiation.

No atomic bomb went off. But the physicist at the table, Louis Slotin, would be dead nine days later.

Nine months earlier, another scientist had died using that same core. Why were people still doing these experiments by hand?

To understand the second accident, we need to find out what the Los Alamos scientists were measuring after the war.

They were studying when an assembly of plutonium could sustain a nuclear chain reaction, and when it would remain below that threshold.

Neutrons can escape the material. Other materials nearby can send some back, affecting how fast the reaction grows.

The measurements had a purpose. But a sudden increase could expose anyone nearby to deadly radiation before they even noticed it.

On August 21, 1945, a young physicist named Harry Daghlian was working late at the laboratory.

He was studying the core with dense blocks around it, watching the reading on a neutron counter as he worked.

The counter warned that the next movement would go too far. Daghlian began pulling the block away.

The block slipped and fell. The reaction surged, releasing an invisible burst of radiation.

Daghlian moved the material away and stopped the reaction. It lasted moments, but he had already been exposed.

He died twenty-five days later. The laboratory knew that a brief mistake could kill without any bomb going off.

Yet criticality work continued, and people still had to stand close to the experimental material.

The same core remained in use. Another experienced physicist, Louis Slotin, knew about Daghlian's death.

Slotin had helped assemble the first atomic test device. He was among the most experienced people in his field.

He wanted to return to other research. At Los Alamos, he was showing colleagues the procedure they would take over.

The equipment had changed, but a crucial part of the method had not: the scientist still worked inches from the hazard.

Slotin used a screwdriver to hold parts of the experiment apart, instead of a fixed safety spacer.

Other scientists watched. Years of experience did not make that small, movable tool any safer.

On May 21, 1946, the screwdriver slipped. The separated parts closed, and the reaction surged again.

Witnesses remembered a blue flash. Slotin immediately separated the parts, bringing the radiation burst to an end.

Once again, there had been no atomic explosion. The danger was a short burst of radiation that had reached the room.

The people nearby were also exposed, although their doses and later health outcomes were different.

Slotin became gravely ill. Doctors cared for him, but could not reverse the damage from the radiation.

He died on May 30, nine days after the accident. One small core had now been involved in two fatal accidents.

The core later earned the nickname 'Demon Core'. It was a memorable name, but there was no curse.

What mattered was a method that kept human hands beside a reaction that could change in an instant.

After the second death, Los Alamos stopped hands-on criticality experiments and moved the work to remotely controlled equipment.

Researchers could now work without standing beside the material. Safety no longer depended on one person's grip.

The original plutonium core was later melted down and its material reused. It wasn't detonated at Bikini, contrary to an old retelling.

Two different mistakes, nine months apart, had revealed the same problem. The first death had warned the scientists.

Only after the second did the laboratory change how people worked, moving the dangerous experiment away from their hands.
