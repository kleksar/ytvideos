How did the same small piece of plutonium fatally injure two scientists, nine months apart? And why did the first death not end the work that caused it?

At Los Alamos, researchers needed to learn when nuclear material could sustain a chain reaction. A measurement might help them handle it safely later. But the experiment itself placed people dangerously close to the material.

On August 21, 1945, physicist Harry Daghlian was working late. An instrument warned that his next movement would be unsafe. He pulled back, but a heavy block slipped and fell. The reaction surged. He moved the material away, yet the radiation had already reached him. He died twenty-five days later.

Safety procedures were tightened. The work did not end, and the central danger remained: a person still had to operate beside the experiment.

In May 1946, Louis Slotin was teaching colleagues how to perform another measurement with the same core. Slotin was experienced. He had helped assemble the device used in the first atomic test.

During the demonstration, a screwdriver slipped. The reaction surged; witnesses saw a blue flash. Slotin immediately separated the parts. No atomic bomb exploded, but the burst of radiation was enough to kill him nine days later.

The others in the room had also been exposed. The outcomes were different, but the second death exposed the flaw that experience and revised instructions had not removed.

Los Alamos stopped conducting these criticality experiments by hand. The dangerous material could be controlled from a distance.

The core was later melted down and reused. The change that mattered was not the nickname “Demon Core.” It was that the next researcher no longer had to keep a hand beside it.
