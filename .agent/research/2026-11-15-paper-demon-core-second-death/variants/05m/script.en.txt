How could the same piece of plutonium fatally expose two physicists in one laboratory? What did the laboratory change after the first death? And why did it take a second fatal accident to change the way the work was done?

At Los Alamos, researchers were measuring when nuclear material could sustain a chain reaction. The results mattered for handling and storing it safely. But the tests posed a dangerous contradiction: to measure the hazard, a person worked very close to it. A burst of radiation could be deadly even if the room remained intact.

On May 21, 1946, an experienced physicist was demonstrating one of these tests to colleagues. A screwdriver slipped. Witnesses saw a blue flash, and the scientist who stopped the reaction would die nine days later. His name was Louis Slotin. He already knew that this same core had killed someone before.

Nine months earlier, Harry Daghlian, a twenty-four-year-old researcher, had returned to the laboratory after dinner. The Nuclear Museum says he was working alone, contrary to the rules already in place. His experiment involved taking readings beside the plutonium core.

On August 21, 1945, a monitoring instrument warned him that his next movement would be unsafe. Daghlian tried to withdraw a heavy block. Instead, he dropped it. The nuclear reaction surged before he could move the material away.

Daghlian stopped the reaction, but that did not undo the radiation exposure. He was hospitalized and died on September 15, twenty-five days after the accident. The equipment had not exploded like a bomb; the fatal injury was caused by radiation.

His death changed something. Los Alamos tightened its safety procedures. There was also a shift toward work at the more isolated Pajarito site, where experimental activity began in April 1946. But work at a different site was not yet the same as operating every dangerous experiment from a safe distance.

That is the missing step in the story. Precautions could become stricter while some researchers still had to stand beside an experiment and trust that the next hand movement would be accurate.

Slotin knew the work exceptionally well. He had helped assemble the device for the Trinity test in 1945. Now he was preparing to leave that assignment and train successors, including Alvin Graves. The May demonstration was part of passing on expertise, not an attempt to make an atomic bomb explode.

The setup was different from Daghlian's. But Slotin was still close enough to the core that a slip could turn into a radiation accident before anybody in the room could get away.

When the screwdriver slipped, the reaction intensified almost immediately. Slotin separated the parts and brought it to an end. The laboratory was still standing. That visible calm concealed what had happened to the people inside it.

According to the National Park Service, Slotin noticed a sour taste and a burning sensation in his hand, then became ill as he left the building. The other people present had also been exposed, although they did not all receive the same dose or suffer the same outcome.

Slotin died on May 30, nine days after the demonstration. The two physicists had been involved in different experiments, with different immediate mistakes. Yet both accidents had left a human being close enough to receive a fatal burst.

This time the response went beyond tighter instructions. Los Alamos prohibited hands-on criticality experiments. The aim was to put physical separation between the researchers and the hazardous material, so a measurement no longer depended on someone's hand remaining steady beside it.

The change required equipment and facilities. A first dedicated building at Pajarito was constructed in 1947, and the laboratory's history places its first remote critical operation in 1948. The safe distance was built into the method over time; it did not appear overnight after Slotin died.

The core itself was later melted down and its plutonium reused. An older claim that this exact core was detonated at Bikini is contradicted by later archival research.

The nickname “Demon Core” makes the object sound like the cause. But the crucial difference between 1945 and the work that followed 1946 was not the name or even the material. After Daghlian, the rules were tightened. After Slotin, the people doing those measurements could finally be kept away from the danger.
