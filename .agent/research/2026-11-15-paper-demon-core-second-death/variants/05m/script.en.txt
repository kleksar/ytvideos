How could one laboratory lose two physicists to the very same piece of plutonium? Why did the first fatal accident not prevent the second? And what finally made the experiment safer?

In 1945, Los Alamos was learning how to work with material that could sustain a nuclear chain reaction. The scientists were not trying to detonate a bomb on their workbench. They needed measurements that would tell them when the material was approaching a dangerous threshold.

Here was the problem. A chain reaction could accelerate in a moment, exposing everyone close by to radiation. The room might remain standing. The people in it could still die.

In May 1946, a screwdriver slipped during one of those measurements. There was a blue flash. The physicist who stopped the reaction had only days left to live.

His name was Louis Slotin, and he already knew what had happened to Harry Daghlian.

Nine months earlier, on August 21, 1945, Daghlian was working late at the Los Alamos laboratory. He was a young researcher who had joined the Manhattan Project while still studying.

He placed dense blocks around a plutonium core and watched a neutron counter. The instrument warned that one further movement would be unsafe. Daghlian tried to withdraw the block.

Instead, it slipped.

The reaction surged. Daghlian moved material away from the core, stopping it, but the radiation had reached him. He died twenty-five days later.

The laboratory did respond. Safety rules were tightened after his death. But a change in rules was not the same as removing a person from the danger zone. Hands-on criticality measurements continued.

The same plutonium core remained available. It had originally been intended for another weapon, but the war had ended. Researchers were still using it to understand the behavior of nuclear material.

Slotin knew the work intimately. He had helped assemble the device for the Trinity test, the world's first atomic detonation. Now he was preparing to leave this work and train others to take over.

That is why colleagues were watching him on May 21, 1946. He was demonstrating a measurement, not attempting an explosion. The instruments and materials were different from Daghlian's experiment, but the danger lay in the same place: the operator's hands were still next to the core.

A screwdriver was being used to maintain separation between parts of the setup. During the demonstration it slipped, and the reaction suddenly intensified.

Witnesses reported a blue flash. Slotin separated the parts almost immediately. The reaction stopped.

For a moment, the laboratory might have looked as though little had happened. There was no mushroom cloud, no shattered building. But the burst had already delivered a severe dose of radiation.

Slotin felt ill quickly. He was taken for medical care, and his condition deteriorated. Other people present were exposed too, though their doses and outcomes differed. None of that could be read simply from the appearance of the intact room.

Slotin died on May 30, nine days after the accident. One core had now been involved in two fatal exposures. The men had made different immediate mistakes. Both had been working close enough for a sudden change in the reaction to reach them.

After this second death, Los Alamos ended manual criticality experiments. The laboratory moved toward equipment that could be operated remotely, with researchers separated from the material.

The work still needed to be done. The new answer was not to demand perfect hands or more courage, but to make a brief slip less likely to kill the person conducting the measurement.

The plutonium core was later melted down and its material reused. Contrary to an older account, that core was not detonated in the Bikini tests.

It became famous as the “Demon Core,” but a nickname cannot explain two accidents. The first death prompted tighter precautions. The second showed why distance, not simply better instructions, had to become part of the experiment itself.
