Why did two scientists die working with the same piece of plutonium? Los Alamos had already lost one of them when the second stepped up to the experiment. What, exactly, had the first accident failed to teach the laboratory? And what changed after the second death?

The object was small enough to sit on a laboratory workbench. It had been prepared during the last months of the Second World War. The war ended before it was used in another weapon, but the core stayed at Los Alamos.

Scientists still needed to understand when nuclear material might sustain a chain reaction. That knowledge mattered wherever such material was handled, stored or moved. The measurements were real work with a practical purpose.

Yet there was a contradiction at the heart of the method. To find a dangerous boundary, researchers were working close to it. An experiment could become much more reactive in an instant. The room would not necessarily explode. The people standing next to the table could still receive fatal radiation.

Now imagine the same object causing a second death after the first had led to new safety rules. In May 1946, that is exactly what happened.

The first victim was Harry Daghlian. He had studied physics at Purdue and joined the wartime laboratory in 1944. He had even helped prepare the plutonium for the first atomic test at Trinity.

On the night of August 21, 1945, Daghlian returned to his laboratory after dinner. According to the Nuclear Museum's biographical account, he conducted this work alone despite the rules that applied to such experiments.

His task was to measure how the surroundings affected the nuclear material. He watched a neutron counter as he worked with dense blocks beside the core. The instrument was giving him information that his eyes could not.

As the next movement approached a dangerous point, the counter gave a warning. Daghlian attempted to pull the block back.

It fell instead.

A rapid burst of radiation passed through the area. Daghlian removed material and stopped the reaction, but stopping it did not undo the exposure he had already received. The process also kept him beside the material during the response.

Nothing on the scale of an atomic bomb had exploded. That distinction mattered little to Daghlian's body. He was taken for treatment and became gravely ill.

On September 15, twenty-five days after the accident, Harry Daghlian died. He was twenty-four.

His death was not ignored. The laboratory tightened safety precautions. The Nuclear Museum records that the first accident did lead to stricter measures.

But the essential question remained: if the people doing these measurements had to remain at the workbench, could revised instructions really prevent the next sudden mistake?

The work continued because the measurements were needed. The object did not simply disappear from the laboratory. The same core was available when another physicist took up the work.

Louis Slotin was not a newcomer hoping to impress anyone. He had assembled the plutonium device for Trinity, the world's first atomic test. Few at the laboratory knew this kind of work better.

By the spring of 1946, he was preparing to leave these duties. The National Park Service records that he was training colleagues to take over. One of those men was Alvin Graves.

This detail changes the second accident. Slotin was not alone at a bench late at night. On May 21, 1946, others were present while he demonstrated a criticality measurement. They were there to learn work that would continue after he left.

The experiment was different from Daghlian's. The two men did not make the same movement with the same object. What the experiments shared was the reliance on someone working immediately beside potentially lethal material.

During Slotin's demonstration, a screwdriver used to maintain separation between parts of the equipment slipped. That is the account given by the National Park Service and the Nuclear Museum.

The reaction intensified almost instantly.

Observers described a flash of blue. Slotin immediately separated the parts and brought the reaction to an end. The lab remained intact.

It would have been a mistake to judge the seriousness of the accident by how the room looked. A nuclear chain reaction can produce a dangerous burst of radiation without blowing a building apart.

Slotin soon felt the effects and was taken to hospital. The National Park Service records that he experienced a sour taste and a burning sensation in his hand immediately after the accident, then became ill as he left the building. His condition continued to deteriorate.

The others in the room had been exposed as well. But exposure was not one uniform event shared equally by everyone there. Their locations and subsequent health outcomes differed. A headline about one victim cannot tell the whole story of everyone present.

The physicists and doctors had another problem: they needed to understand how much radiation had reached each person. Historical reports and later reconstructions would revise dose estimates over time. The absolute numbers are not necessary to see what the event had proved: a brief lapse could harm several people before anyone could physically escape.

Slotin died on May 30, nine days after the demonstration.

The first scientist had been working alone; the second had colleagues watching. One incident involved a falling block; the other, a slipped tool. The first brought stricter precautions, yet both left a researcher immediately beside a dangerous reaction.

The core later acquired the name “Demon Core.” Two deaths, linked to one object, invite a story about a curse. But a supernatural explanation would hide the question that matters: why did the possibility of a fatal exposure still depend on what a human hand did in the next instant?

There was another unsettling coincidence. The Nuclear Museum and an archival account at the Los Alamos Historical Society report that both accidents happened on the twenty-first day of a month, and both men died in the same hospital room. Those details are documented, but the calendar and the hospital room did not cause either accident.

A better explanation looks at the way the work was organized.

After Daghlian, stricter rules had been possible without eliminating manual operation. After Slotin, Los Alamos prohibited hands-on criticality experiments. The decision changed where the operator could stand, not simply how carefully the operator was told to work.

A remotely controlled laboratory did not mean that criticality research stopped. It meant the people doing it could be kept away from the hazardous material while instruments and equipment carried out the measurement.

And that change required buildings and machines, not just a new memo.

Work at the more isolated Pajarito site developed during this period. Historical records of the Los Alamos critical experiments facility describe a dedicated structure, known as a kiva, coming into use in 1947 for measurements that did not bring the material to criticality. Remote critical operations followed as the facility developed. The historical sequence matters: banning the old method was not the same thing as having every new machine ready the next morning.

The long-term solution was to make separation part of the process. An operator could receive readings at a distance instead of standing over the material and relying on a hand-held object as the last protection against a deadly mistake.

That was the real consequence of the second death. Not a promise that no accident would ever happen again, and not proof that the first death had produced no response. The lesson was narrower and more concrete: instructions had changed once; the physical relationship between worker and hazard had not changed enough.

The core itself did not survive as a single notorious object. Archival research by the Nuclear Museum explains that it was melted down and its plutonium recast or reused. An older popular account that it went off in a nuclear test at Bikini is incorrect.

There is a final reason this distinction matters. If the story is only about one unlucky object, the danger appears to end when the object disappears. But the two fatal incidents were about a repeatable method. The dangerous part was a person having to stand close enough for one slip to reach them.

Daghlian's death changed the rules. Slotin's death helped change the design of the work. And for the people who followed them into that laboratory, the most important difference was the distance between their hands and the experiment.
