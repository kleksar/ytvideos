A TV crew tried to move a cowboy mannequin in a California funhouse. Its arm snapped off, exposing human bone.

They called the police. What looked like a cheap prop was a real man's body.

The coroner found fluorescent paint, arsenic from old embalming, and a gunshot wound.

But who was he? The funhouse staff knew him only as a mannequin.

Then someone checked his mouth. There was a penny from 1924 and tickets from a crime museum.

The investigators traced that museum. A former owner's son knew exactly whose body it was.

His name was Elmer McCurdy. Police had shot him dead in Oklahoma in 1911.

So how did an outlaw end up hanging in a funhouse sixty-five years later?

When no family came to claim him, the undertaker kept his embalmed body on display.

Visitors paid five cents to see him, dropping the coins right into his mouth.

Five years later, two men turned up and said they were McCurdy's brothers.

The undertaker let them take him. But the men weren't relatives. They ran a traveling carnival.

McCurdy became a sideshow attraction, then passed through a series of exhibitions and owners.

By the time he reached the funhouse, he was treated as a wax figure. Someone had painted him orange.

The broken arm led investigators back through those exhibits. They checked his skull against an old photograph to confirm his identity.

In April 1977, McCurdy was finally buried in Oklahoma. He'd been dead for sixty-five years.
