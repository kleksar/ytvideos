How did a real man's body end up mistaken for a mannequin? Why did a coin and a museum ticket help identify him? And who kept moving him from one attraction to another?

In 1976, a television crew was working at a funhouse in Long Beach, California. A cowboy figure hung among the props. It looked old and badly painted, but nothing about it suggested the crew needed to call a coroner.

Someone tried to move the figure. An arm broke away, exposing human bone. Police were called. The supposed dummy was a preserved human body.

Examiners found layers of bright paint, traces of an old embalming chemical and evidence of a gunshot wound. It could not be identified by a label on the wall. The funhouse staff believed they had been displaying a mannequin.

Then investigators found something far more useful than the paint: a 1924 penny and admission tickets associated with a Museum of Crime, lodged in the dead man's mouth. The investigation could now follow an exhibition rather than guess at a face.

The museum trail led to the family of showman Louis Sonney. His son Dan recognized the body as Elmer McCurdy, an Oklahoma outlaw killed in 1911. But identifying him created a second question. How had a man who died sixty-five years earlier remained on public display?

McCurdy was not famous for a successful robbery. His last attempts at stealing from banks and trains went badly. In October 1911, after taking a small haul from the wrong train, he was tracked to a barn in Oklahoma. He died in a shootout with law officers.

His body was taken to an undertaker in Pawhuska. No family came to claim it. Preserved with an arsenic-based embalming fluid, the remains stayed unusually intact. Instead of disappearing into a grave, McCurdy became an exhibit. Visitors paid five cents to see the embalmed bandit, dropping the coins into his mouth.

That detail matters. When investigators found a much later penny in the same mouth, they were looking at a trace of the body's life as an attraction, not evidence of a theft committed in 1924.

In 1916, two men approached the undertaker claiming to be McCurdy's brothers. They obtained the body. The claim was false: they were the Patterson carnival promoters. The supposed family reunion was actually another transfer into show business.

Their traveling show advertised a dead outlaw who would never be captured alive. In 1922, the display passed to Louis Sonney, whose Museum of Crime presented McCurdy alongside wax figures of notorious criminals. People visiting those shows were not being shown an ordinary waxwork. The promoters knew a real corpse was part of the attraction.

Over the decades, the body moved between exhibitions, storage and film-related displays. Eventually, knowledge of who it was stopped traveling with it. In Long Beach, coated in brightly colored paint and hung inside a funhouse, it was treated as one more artificial figure. The exact moment that knowledge was lost is not established.

Dan Sonney's recognition did not close the case by itself. Forensic anthropologist Clyde Snow compared skull X-rays with an old photograph of McCurdy. The comparison supported the identification and tied the museum clues to a real man.

In April 1977, McCurdy was returned to Oklahoma and buried in Guthrie. The family claim of 1916 had kept him on the road. The broken arm in 1976 finally ended that journey.

The strangest part is not that a television crew found a body. It is that, for decades, an actual person had been advertised, sold as a spectacle and eventually forgotten as a person at all.
