How could a human body hang in a California funhouse without the staff knowing? Who first put that body on display, and how did it travel through more than half a century of entertainment? Finally, what piece of evidence brought a name back to the remains?

In 1976, a crew filming the television series The Six Million Dollar Man worked at The Pike, an amusement area in Long Beach. The funhouse had a cowboy figure hanging like an old fairground prop. The figure was weathered, brightly painted and apparently just another piece of the scenery.

A crew member went to adjust it. Part of an arm broke away. Under the painted surface was human bone. The crew had been handling a dead person.

Police took the remains for examination. The body was dry and shrunken. There were layers of paint and wax over it, traces of arsenic-based embalming, and signs of a gunshot wound. It was a real human body, but the examination did not immediately tell the police whose.

The discovery posed a different sort of problem from an ordinary identification. This body had passed through many hands. Some people had known it was genuine; others had eventually treated it like a manufactured figure. To understand why, the investigation would have to move backward, long before the television cameras arrived.

Elmer McCurdy was born in Maine in 1880. He worked at different jobs and spent time in the Army. By 1911 he was attempting robberies involving banks and trains. His plans repeatedly went wrong, and the results were far smaller than the money he had hoped to steal.

One train robbery that spring left much of the silver in a safe damaged by the explosive charge used to open it. Later that year a bank robbery brought a modest sum. In October, McCurdy and his companions targeted a train believing it carried a much richer shipment. They had selected the wrong train. The loot was only a little cash and whiskey.

These details matter for what happened after his death. McCurdy was not a celebrated mastermind whose fame naturally carried him onto a stage. The reputation later assigned to him was built by people advertising the body.

Law officers tracked him to a barn near Bartlesville, Oklahoma. A shootout followed. On October 7, 1911, McCurdy was dead. He was thirty-one. From this point onward, none of the decisions that gave him a career in entertainment were his.

The body went to an undertaking establishment in Pawhuska. No relative arrived to claim it. The undertaker embalmed it using an arsenic-based solution, a material used in that era. The treatment and subsequent drying helped preserve the remains for an unusually long time.

Instead of arranging a burial, the funeral establishment began exhibiting the body. McCurdy was dressed up and presented as an embalmed bandit. Visitors paid a nickel to see him. They were asked to put the coin through his open mouth.

The same mouth would later yield one of the clues that restored his name. But in 1911 the coins meant something simpler: the corpse had become a paying attraction.

Other showmen wanted it. In 1916, two men appeared claiming to be McCurdy's brothers. The undertaker released the remains to them. The men were not members of his family. They were James and Charles Patterson, carnival promoters who intended to put the body in their traveling show.

The Pattersons advertised him as the outlaw who would never be captured alive. That slogan turned a real death into a commercial character. Anyone seeing the exhibit could be told he was looking at a genuine preserved man. The scandal of the story was therefore not that every person over the next sixty-five years believed a dummy was real or a real body was a dummy. Knowledge changed as the displays changed.

In 1922 the body passed into the show business of Louis Sonney. His Museum of Crime traveled with figures and displays devoted to notorious outlaws. McCurdy was placed among wax representations of criminals, but unlike the wax figures, he was not an imitation.

The museum later appeared alongside the great transcontinental footrace of 1928. Spectators attending an exhausting cross-country sporting spectacle could encounter the dead outlaw as another attraction on the circuit. He had gone from an unclaimed body in Oklahoma to a traveling exhibit seen far beyond the place where he died.

In the 1930s, the preserved body was used to promote the exploitation film Narcotic. The exhibition told a different story about the dead man, presenting him as someone whose terrible fate illustrated drug abuse. That was promotional fiction, not a newly established finding about McCurdy's cause of death. Once the body was detached from its true history, it was easy for a new show to assign it a new identity.

Louis Sonney died in 1949. The body spent periods in storage. In the 1960s, it was connected with another low-budget production, She Freak, released in 1967. Later, the remains were transferred with a group of wax figures into the world of wax museums and haunted attractions.

Accounts describe damage and deterioration as the display was moved again. The precise route is not documented equally well at every step. We can identify several major owners and exhibitions without pretending to know every handoff, conversation or storage room.

Eventually it arrived at The Pike in Long Beach. In the funhouse it was painted in bright colors and hung among artificial figures. The staff who encountered it in 1976 understood it as a prop. That does not tell us precisely when or why the original information disappeared. It only shows the result: the real body had been absorbed into its own imitation.

Once the arm broke, however, a costume and a painted surface could no longer answer the investigators' questions. They examined the remains for indications of age and identity. Old embalming substances and the earlier gunshot injury pointed toward the past, but neither supplied a unique name.

Inside the mouth were a 1924 penny and ticket stubs associated with the Museum of Crime. The penny was not evidence that the man died in 1924; it was minted after his death. The tickets were more useful. They gave investigators a specific exhibition to investigate instead of an endless list of missing people.

The museum had belonged to Louis Sonney. His son Dan recognized the exhibit as the remains long known in his family's business as Elmer McCurdy. At last the detectives had a candidate identity and a plausible route connecting 1911 Oklahoma to the California funhouse.

But memory from an old showman was not the same as proof. Forensic anthropologist Clyde Snow compared X-rays of the skull with an old photograph of McCurdy. The anatomical match supported the identification. What began with a broken prop now had evidence from two directions: the museum trail and the human remains.

The two kinds of evidence answered different questions. The tickets explained how the body might have traveled. The skull comparison addressed whose body it actually was. Mixing them up would turn a clue into a conclusion it could not support alone.

Only after that identification could the larger history be reconstructed: an unsuccessful robber died in 1911, his corpse attracted customers at a funeral home, supposed brothers moved him into a carnival, and later shows kept turning him into whatever character suited their display.

The ending was not another entertainment contract. On April 22, 1977, the remains were buried at Summit View Cemetery in Guthrie, Oklahoma. People who had only known the story as a strange carnival mystery could now name the man whose body had spent decades on the road.

There are uncertain details in the accounts, from the exact day of the funhouse discovery to some of the intermediate changes of ownership. Those uncertainties do not alter the well-supported chain: unclaimed body, paid display, false family claim, long exhibition history, forensic rediscovery and burial.

The broken arm ended the journey by making it impossible to keep treating the figure as harmless scenery. The real resolution came when investigators followed a few small objects back through the shows and verified that there had always been a person behind the paint.
