How could a human body hang in a California funhouse without the staff knowing? Who first put that body on display, and how did it travel through more than half a century of entertainment? Finally, what piece of evidence brought a name back to the remains?

In 1976, a crew filming the television series The Six Million Dollar Man worked at The Pike, an amusement area in Long Beach. The funhouse had a cowboy figure hanging like an old fairground prop. The figure was weathered, brightly painted and apparently just another piece of the scenery.

A crew member went to adjust it. Part of an arm broke away. Under the painted surface was human bone. The crew had been handling a dead person.

Police took the remains for examination. The body was dry and shrunken. There were layers of paint and wax over it, traces of arsenic-based embalming, and signs of a gunshot wound. It was a real human body, but the examination did not immediately tell the police whose.

The people who had owned the body had left evidence on it. The police would eventually find that evidence, but the reason it was there goes back to a funeral home in Oklahoma.

Elmer McCurdy was born in Maine in 1880. He worked at different jobs and spent time in the Army. By 1911 he was attempting robberies involving banks and trains. His plans repeatedly went wrong, and the results were far smaller than the money he had hoped to steal.

One train robbery that spring left much of the silver in a safe damaged by the explosive charge used to open it. Later that year a bank robbery brought a modest sum. In October, McCurdy and his companions targeted a train believing it carried a much richer shipment. They had selected the wrong train. The loot was only a little cash and whiskey.

McCurdy had made little money as a robber. The people who would make money from his name entered the story after his death.

Law officers tracked him to a barn near Bartlesville, Oklahoma. A shootout followed. On October 7, 1911, McCurdy was dead. He was thirty-one. From this point onward, none of the decisions that gave him a career in entertainment were his.

The body went to Joseph Johnson's funeral home in Pawhuska. No relative came to collect it. Johnson used an arsenic-based embalming solution, common at the time. The preservation of the remains would become a clue when a medical examiner saw them decades later.

Instead of arranging a burial, the funeral establishment began exhibiting the body. McCurdy was dressed up and presented as an embalmed bandit. Visitors paid a nickel to see him. They were asked to put the coin through his open mouth.

The display attracted carnival operators. According to later accounts, Johnson repeatedly refused to sell the body. They could not buy it, but a claim from a relative might get it out of the funeral home.

In October 1916, two men appeared claiming to be McCurdy's brothers. They said they would arrange a burial in California. Johnson released the remains. But the men were carnival promoters James and Charles Patterson. The body went to Kansas rather than to the California burial they had described.

For the Pattersons the body was a genuine exhibit. They advertised him as an outlaw who had never been taken alive. Visitors could pay to see the actual man. The next owner would place him in a setting where most of the other criminals were made of wax.

In 1922 the body passed into the show business of Louis Sonney. His Museum of Crime traveled with figures and displays devoted to notorious outlaws. McCurdy was placed among wax representations of criminals, but unlike the wax figures, he was not an imitation.

In 1928 the museum's sideshow accompanied a race across the United States. McCurdy could be seen by crowds far from Oklahoma. Soon a filmmaker would use the same body to tell an entirely different story.

In the 1930s, the preserved body was used to promote the exploitation film Narcotic. The exhibition told a different story about the dead man, presenting him as someone whose terrible fate illustrated drug abuse. That was promotional fiction, not a newly established finding about McCurdy's cause of death. Once the body was detached from its true history, it was easy for a new show to assign it a new identity.

After Louis Sonney died in 1949, the body spent years in storage. His son Dan later lent it for a film project; it appeared in She Freak, released in 1967. Dan still knew what the exhibit was. That matters because the police would eventually need somebody who remembered it.

In 1968 the body was sold together with actual wax figures to the Hollywood Wax Museum. A newspaper report in 1977 described the transfer that way. The remains were eventually sold on to the amusement park in Long Beach. We do not know precisely when the knowledge of who he was stopped reaching new staff.

In Long Beach the figure was hanging among the funhouse props, covered with bright paint. The people working there took it for a mannequin. The police would need a way to connect the body to the old museum without knowing which museum to look for.

After the arm broke, the medical examination revealed signs of an earlier autopsy. The arsenic in the tissues was another indication that this was an old preserved body. Then a routine part of identifying unknown remains supplied a new lead.

During preparation for dental examination, investigators discovered a penny dated 1924 and ticket stubs in the mouth. The date on the coin would later fit the story of the traveling exhibit. But the tickets were useful immediately: they gave investigators the name of the Museum of Crime.

The museum had belonged to Louis Sonney. His son Dan recognized the exhibit as the remains long known in his family's business as Elmer McCurdy. At last the detectives had a candidate identity and a plausible route connecting 1911 Oklahoma to the California funhouse.

But memory from an old showman was not the same as proof. Forensic anthropologist Clyde Snow compared X-rays of the skull with an old photograph of McCurdy. The anatomical match supported the identification. What began with a broken prop now had evidence from two directions: the museum trail and the human remains.

The discovery made the news, and funeral homes offered to bury the man at their own expense. Officials waited to see whether relatives would claim the remains. After all those years of being passed from one business to another, simply finding his name was still not enough to get him into a grave.

This time, a man from Oklahoma came forward to arrange a burial. Fred Olds worked with the Los Angeles coroner, Thomas Noguchi, to bring McCurdy home. The resemblance between the skull and the historical photographs mattered for more than solving the mystery: the coroner needed a positive identification before releasing the body.

On April 13, 1977, Noguchi, Snow, Olds and funeral director Ralph McCalmont spoke at a news conference. A UPI report published the next day recorded the positive identification and the plan to bury McCurdy in Guthrie. Noguchi also announced further photographic work to document the features of the remains.

On April 22 a horse-drawn hearse carried McCurdy to Summit View Cemetery in Guthrie. Around three hundred people attended the funeral, according to later accounts. Newspaper photographer Claude Long recorded the burial. His photographs are now catalogued by the Oklahoma Historical Society.

In 1916 a promise of a funeral had sent McCurdy into a traveling carnival. In 1977 the people arranging his return actually carried out that promise. The name on the grave was Elmer McCurdy, the same name the medical examiners had recovered from a painted figure in a funhouse.
