And then an elevator fell seventy-five floors inside the Empire State Building. A twenty-year-old woman was still inside.

Her name was Betty Lou Oliver. And somehow, this was the second disaster she'd survived that morning.

New York, July 28, 1945. A US Army B-25 bomber was flying through thick fog, trying to reach an airport across the city. Its pilot had been warned that the top of the Empire State Building couldn't be seen.

He passed the Chrysler Building, turned the wrong way, and flew straight into the upper floors of the tallest building in the world.

The impact tore open the offices. Burning fuel spread through the building. One engine crashed through the far wall and landed on a roof across the street. The other went down an elevator shaft.

Fourteen people died. Betty was working as an elevator operator near the impact. The blast threw her from her car, and she suffered burns.

Rescuers reached her. They put her in another elevator to take her down for medical care.

But the crash had damaged its cables. The second car dropped all the way to the basement.

When rescuers reached the wreckage, Betty was alive. Her neck, back, and pelvis were broken, but she had survived a fall of roughly a thousand feet.

Two possible explanations have been suggested: air trapped in the shaft may have slowed the car, and broken cables piled beneath it may have softened the final impact. Nobody can measure exactly how much either factor helped.

Betty spent months recovering. Her fall became a world record nobody would want to break.

The flight had already turned the building into a fire scene. To get the injured elevator operator out, the rescuers put her in another lift for the journey downstairs.
