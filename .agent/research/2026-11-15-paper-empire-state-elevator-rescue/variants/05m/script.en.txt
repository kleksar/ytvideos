Why did an American bomber fly into the Empire State Building on a clear summer morning? Why did pieces of the same airplane start fires in different buildings? And how did a woman already injured in the crash survive a second disaster inside an elevator?

The first answer is that the morning wasn't clear at all.

On Saturday, July 28, 1945, New York was wrapped in fog. The war in Europe had ended, but the war in the Pacific was still going on. Army pilot William Smith was flying a B-25 with two other men aboard. This wasn't a bombing mission. He was trying to get his passengers to an airport.

Smith approached LaGuardia, then decided to continue toward Newark, across the city. The tower warned him that it couldn't see the top of the Empire State Building. He was an experienced combat pilot, but experience couldn't make the buildings appear through the cloud.

He passed the Chrysler Building and turned the wrong way. Ahead of him was the north wall of the tallest building on Earth.

The bomber hit near the seventy-ninth floor.

Fuel caught fire in the offices. The impact tore an enormous opening in the wall. One of the aircraft's engines kept going, passed through the building, and landed on a roof across the street. The other fell down an elevator shaft. Fire was now burning at the impact floors, in the building's lower levels, and across the street.

Firefighters had to reach the upper floors with elevator service badly disrupted. The city archive preserves the fire commissioner's report: crews brought the fires under control in nineteen minutes and extinguished them within forty. That was fast, but it didn't undo the losses. Three people in the plane and eleven people in the building were dead.

And above the street, one employee was still in desperate need of help.

Betty Lou Oliver was twenty. She operated an elevator near the top of the building. She was waiting in New York for her Navy husband to return from overseas, and she was close to finishing her job there.

The blast knocked her out of her elevator car. She suffered serious burns, but she was alive. First-aid workers reached her and chose another elevator to get her down to medical care. From their position high in the tower, that would normally have been the obvious route to the ground.

They couldn't see what the airplane had done to the equipment supporting that second car.

As it descended, damaged cables failed. The elevator dropped seventy-five floors, more than a thousand feet, into the basement. It fell without a functioning emergency brake.

When rescuers opened the twisted wreckage, they found Betty alive.

She had broken her neck, back, and pelvis. Her survival has two plausible explanations. Air pressure inside the relatively enclosed shaft may have resisted the falling car. And a heap of severed cables beneath it may have helped absorb the impact. Those are explanations, not a measured account of the forces acting on her body. The exact combination is unknown.

Betty spent four months in a hospital, followed by more recovery at home. Guinness World Records still recognizes her descent as the longest elevator fall anyone has survived.

Outside, investigators tried to reconstruct how the plane had reached the building in the first place. The pilot had been warned about visibility. His route toward Newark had taken him close to Manhattan's towers. The mistake didn't begin with the impact: it began while he was still trying to navigate through fog.

The building itself had not collapsed. By the following Monday, much of it had reopened for work. It is easy to look at that detail and think the catastrophe was surprisingly small. Fourteen families would have seen it differently.

And Betty's record isn't just an extraordinary survival story. It is also a reminder of how one accident can damage the very equipment people rely on to escape it.

Her first elevator had been hit by the crash. The second was meant to carry her to safety. It was the second that fell.
