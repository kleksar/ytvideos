How could a military plane strike the tallest building in the world without anyone stopping it? Why did one collision start fires in three different places? And what happened to the elevator operator who survived the impact, only to be sent down in another lift?

The last question has the strangest answer. But to understand it, we have to return to a city that could barely see its own skyline.

On July 28, 1945, Europe had been celebrating the end of its war for weeks. The Pacific war was still being fought. New York had soldiers arriving and leaving, civilians working, and a skyline dominated by a single tower: the Empire State Building.

It was Saturday. Many offices were less crowded than on a weekday. That fact would reduce the number of people exposed to what was coming, but it wouldn't prevent a tragedy.

There was thick fog over Manhattan. Some of the tallest buildings disappeared into it. On an ordinary day, a pilot navigating above New York could use the river, the streets, and the towers as unmistakable landmarks. That morning, the landmarks were exactly what he could not reliably see.

Lieutenant Colonel William Smith was flying an Army B-25. He had survived combat missions in Europe. Now he was carrying two other men on a transport flight from Massachusetts. There were no bombs to drop. The destination was an airport.

Smith approached LaGuardia Airport and contacted its tower. Contemporary city records preserve the exchange, although accounts differ on some of the exact radio timings. The important part is clear: his next intended stop was Newark, on the other side of New York, and the tower advised against continuing through poor visibility. Controllers warned that they couldn't even see the top of the Empire State Building.

Smith chose to proceed.

A decision to fly toward another airport isn't, by itself, a crash. Thousands of flights change their plans safely. The danger here was where the aircraft was going, how low it was, and what the pilot could actually see.

The B-25 came close to the Chrysler Building. Then, according to the reconstruction preserved in New York's municipal archive, Smith turned the wrong way. The next obstacle was not a distant mountain or an unseen radio mast. It was the north face of a skyscraper, filling the space ahead.

The bomber struck the upper offices of the Empire State Building, around the seventy-ninth floor. The world's tallest building had just been hit by an aircraft flying through the middle of New York.

From the street, the crash left a vast opening high in the wall. Inside, the force had ripped through offices occupied by people doing ordinary jobs. The aircraft carried fuel and oxygen tanks. Fires spread through the damaged floors. This was not a fire that had begun in a wastebasket and grown slowly. An aircraft had delivered burning wreckage directly into the building.

But the damage did not stay at the point of impact.

One engine tore through the far side of the tower, continued out across the street, and landed on the roof of a smaller building. It started another fire there. A different engine went down an elevator shaft inside the Empire State Building, carrying damage and fire toward the lower floors.

The city's fire department eventually reported three significant fire locations: high in the tower, across the street, and in the lower part of the tower itself. Firefighters could not treat this as one neat blaze with a single entrance and a single path upward.

Elevators that would normally move people rapidly through the building had been disrupted. Firefighters still had to reach floors hundreds of feet above the street, while people on the floors below and above the impact were exposed to heat and smoke. The commissioner later described people trapped on some of those floors and warned that panic would have made the situation much worse.

The alarm brought fire crews, ambulances, police, and Mayor Fiorello La Guardia. The fire department's later report recorded something remarkable amid the chaos: the blazes were brought under control in nineteen minutes and extinguished within forty. That was the result of an emergency response, not evidence that the accident had been harmless.

Three men in the aircraft died. Eleven more people in the building died. Fourteen people were gone after a flight that wasn't even a combat mission.

And there was another problem unfolding in the elevator system.

Betty Lou Oliver was twenty years old. She operated an elevator high in the building. She had taken the job while waiting for her husband, a Navy serviceman, to come home from overseas. She was near the end of that employment.

When the bomber hit, Betty was near the eightieth floor. The force threw her out of her stationary elevator car. She was badly burned. In a building filled with smoke, wreckage, and emergency crews, she had at least survived the initial collision.

First-aid workers reached her. Their immediate task was practical: move a seriously injured woman from a high floor to ground-level medical care. A lift was the normal way to travel that distance. So they placed her into a different elevator.

This is the terrible turn in the story. The people helping Betty had good reason to get her out quickly. But the equipment intended to take her to safety was part of the same damaged building.

The aircraft had struck elevators and their shafts. In the car now carrying Betty, the supporting cables and the cable connected to the automatic braking system had been damaged. As the elevator traveled downward, it lost its support.

The car fell seventy-five stories.

It is difficult to picture the distance until you imagine a normal elevator journey from a high office, past floor after floor, without slowing. Betty had already survived a plane entering the building. Now she was inside a metal car dropping more than a thousand feet toward the basement.

When rescuers finally reached the crushed elevator at the bottom, they found her alive.

She had suffered fractures to her neck, back, and pelvis. Those injuries were severe. Yet the event entered the records for a very different reason: no other person is documented to have survived a longer fall in an elevator.

How did she survive?

The tempting answer is that the shaft acted like a perfect air cushion. But that makes an uncertain explanation sound like a proven measurement. Two physical factors have been suggested. Air trapped inside the relatively enclosed shaft may have resisted the falling car. And several severed elevator cables had gathered below it, forming material that could have absorbed some of the impact.

We cannot reconstruct precisely how much speed was lost to air, how the cable pile deformed, or what forces reached Betty's body. The record establishes the fall and her survival; the complete mechanical explanation remains uncertain. It would be easy to invent a clever emergency brake or a last-second rescue. Neither is needed, and the documented braking cable had itself failed.

Betty was cut from the damaged car and taken to Bellevue Hospital. She remained in hospital for four months. Even when she could walk again, she needed further recovery. Eventually she returned to her family life and raised three children. Years later, she declined further interviews about the crash. She didn't owe the public an endless retelling of that day.

Outside the hospital, the city had to answer different questions.

How had a military aircraft come to fly so low through fog over Manhattan? The warning about visibility, the choice to continue toward Newark, and the turn near the Chrysler Building all became part of the explanation. It wasn't an attack, and the aircraft wasn't trying to strike the tower. It was a navigation disaster with consequences that spread far beyond the cockpit.

How safe was the building? The structural frame had not failed. The damage was devastating locally, but the tower remained standing. By the following Monday, much of the Empire State Building was back in business. Workers returned to a skyscraper that still had an enormous wound high in its side.

That reopening can sound like a triumph of engineering. It was. But it sits beside the dead and injured, not above them. Fourteen deaths cannot be cancelled out by a quick repair or a story about an extraordinary survivor.

And there was a question beyond the city: what happens when the negligence of someone working for the federal government injures people? The debate over government liability already existed before this crash. In 1946, Congress passed the Federal Tort Claims Act, opening a way to bring certain negligence claims against the United States. Histories of the disaster connect it to the demand for change, but it would be misleading to say this single crash created the entire law.

An aircraft had broken one building's wall, damaged its elevators, and forced rescuers to work in a structure they could no longer treat as normal. The impact was over in seconds. Its consequences reached across the street, down to the basement, into a hospital, and into years of recovery.

Today the building stands, and Betty Lou Oliver's fall remains a Guinness World Record. But the record only makes sense if we remember why she entered that second elevator.

The plane had already thrown her from her first car. The people who found her were trying to get her downstairs and out of danger. Their rescue took her into another lift. And that second lift was the one that fell.
