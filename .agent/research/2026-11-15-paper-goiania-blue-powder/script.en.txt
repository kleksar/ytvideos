How did a piece of scrap metal send more than a hundred thousand people to a stadium for radiation checks? And why did the answer arrive inside a plastic bag?

Goiânia, Brazil. September 1987. In a junkyard after dark, Devair Ferreira noticed a blue glow coming from a metal capsule.

He brought it into his house and showed it to relatives and neighbours. Some left carrying fragments smaller than grains of rice.

They did not know the material was radioactive. By the time the danger was identified, it had already travelled through several homes.

To understand how it got there, go back two years.

A radiotherapy clinic moved to new premises. It left behind a machine that had once delivered radiation to treat cancer.

The old building was partly demolished. But the radioactive source inside that machine had not been secured.

On September thirteenth, two men entered looking for scrap metal. They took part of the machine away in a wheelbarrow.

The steel looked valuable. Neither of them knew what was hidden inside the protective housing.

They tried to dismantle it. In the process, the capsule containing radioactive caesium chloride was broken open.

One of the men became ill. His nausea and other symptoms were first mistaken for something he had eaten.

The fragments eventually reached Devair's junkyard. That night he saw the glow and thought the powder might be worth something.

He invited other people to look. They could see the blue light in the darkness, but they could not see the radiation.

Within days, fragments were passed to friends and relatives. Some people rubbed the powder on their skin, like carnival glitter.

One man brought pieces to his family dinner table. His six-year-old daughter handled them while eating with her hands.

Those grains were not harmless decoration. They were pieces of a powerful radioactive source, small enough to spread onto hands, clothes and food.

The visible glow drew people closer. The invisible radiation, and radioactive material getting inside their bodies, was what injured them.

The danger did not stay at the scrapyard. Each fragment could carry contamination to a different home.

Then people began vomiting. Their skin became inflamed. Doctors saw patients who appeared to have food poisoning or allergic reactions.

There was no single address where everyone fell sick. Different families had carried pieces to different houses.

Devair's wife, Maria Gabriela, grew suspicious of the glowing material. Too many people around it had become ill.

On September twenty-eighth, she and a scrapyard worker collected the remains in a bag. They took it by bus to the local public health office.

She was trying to show officials the object she believed had caused the sickness. She did not know how dangerous it was to carry.

At the health office, the connection still had to be proved. The next day, a local physicist used a radiation detector to examine the object.

The instrument revealed the scale of the threat. This was not simply a stomach illness spreading through the neighbourhood.

It was a radiological emergency. The physicist urged people to leave the most dangerous areas, and specialists were called in.

Now investigators had to follow the fragments: from the clinic, to the scrapyard, to the people who had brought them home.

A few grains had ended up in places where families ate and slept.

Because caesium chloride dispersed easily once the capsule broke, removing the original metal object could not clean those rooms.

Crews identified contaminated sites, evacuated residents and began checking people who might have come into contact with the material.

The Olympic stadium became a screening centre. People arrived not knowing whether they were contaminated.

More than one hundred and twelve thousand people were checked. Two hundred and forty-nine were found to have radioactive contamination.

That distinction matters. The emergency alarmed a huge population; it did not mean everyone at the stadium had radiation sickness.

Twenty people needed hospital treatment. Some had been exposed so severely that cleaning their skin and clothes could not undo the injury.

Four people died. Among them were Maria Gabriela, who carried the bag to the authorities, and the six-year-old girl exposed at home.

Devair, the junkyard owner who first admired the glow, survived.

Seven houses were eventually demolished. Contaminated soil, furniture and other belongings had to be removed as radioactive waste.

The response was not a matter of lifting one dangerous machine out of a building. By then, pieces of its source had been scattered across homes.

Investigators traced the origin back to the disused clinic. A medical device that should have remained protected had been left accessible.

Under controlled conditions, that source had been used to treat cancer. In a ruined building, it became something people could carry home.

Maria Gabriela's suspicion could not reverse what had already happened. But the object she brought in gave authorities a direct clue.

The decisive evidence was not discovered by a scientist searching the abandoned clinic. It arrived at the health office in a plastic bag.
