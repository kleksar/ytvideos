How could a piece of scrap metal send more than a hundred thousand people to a stadium for radiation checks? Why did a blue glow travel through ordinary homes for days before anyone knew what it was? And what did a woman carry to a health office in a plastic bag?

Goiânia, Brazil. September eighteenth, 1987. Devair Ferreira, who ran a scrapyard, went into his garage and noticed a blue glow coming from a metal capsule.

It had arrived with some old medical equipment. Devair thought the glowing material was beautiful, perhaps even valuable. He took the capsule into his home.

Over the next few days, neighbours and relatives came to look. Eventually, some of them carried tiny fragments away. Pieces that seemed like curiosities were moving from one family to another.

On September twenty-fourth, a piece reached a dinner table. Ivo Ferreira had brought it home from his brother Devair's yard. His six-year-old daughter, Leide, handled it while eating with her hands.

There was enough radioactive material on that table to change the family's life. Leide would be one of four people to die. But nobody at dinner knew what the fragments contained.

To see how that capsule reached a child's plate, we have to go back to the building it came from.

At the end of 1985, a radiotherapy clinic in Goiânia moved to new premises. It took one radiation-treatment machine with it and left another behind.

That second machine contained a sealed caesium-137 source. Under proper control, it had been used to treat cancer. In the old premises, part of the building was demolished, leaving the machine accessible.

The licensing authority had not been told that the source was being left there. Nearly two years later, the metal casing still looked like something that could be sold.

Between September tenth and thirteenth, two men entered the site with simple tools. They pulled part of the machine free and carried it in a wheelbarrow to a house half a kilometre away.

The source was still inside its protective assembly. The men did not need to see any powder to be exposed to radiation. By September thirteenth, both were vomiting.

They assumed they had eaten something bad. One of them went to a doctor two days later with dizziness and a swollen hand. He was told he might be having an allergic reaction to food.

Meanwhile, the metal was still in the yard. On September eighteenth, one of the men worked on it with a screwdriver beneath a mango tree.

He punctured the thin window of the source capsule and scooped out some of its contents. At one point he thought the material might be gunpowder and tried to light it.

That same day, the dismantled assembly was sold to Devair's scrapyard. The dangerous part of a cancer-treatment machine had become a piece of scrap in an ordinary neighbourhood.

And there was something about it that made people want to get closer. In the dark, the source material appeared to glow blue.

The glow was real and was later observed during another controlled investigation. Its precise physical mechanism was still being studied in the original accident report. It was no indication that the material was safe.

On September twenty-first, a visitor used a screwdriver to remove more fragments. Some were only the size of grains of rice. They crumbled easily into powder.

Fragments went to the visitor's brother and to Devair's relatives. According to investigators, some people even dabbed the material onto their skin, as they might with carnival glitter.

That explains how one damaged capsule created several separate sites of contamination. The material could travel on skin and clothing; some of it could be swallowed along with food.

Three days later, Ivo took pieces home. The fragments were placed on the family table during a meal. His daughter Leide touched them and ate with her hands.

Investigators later estimated that she had swallowed a substantial amount of caesium-137. The very small size of the pieces made them easier to spread, not less dangerous.

The accident was already harming people who had never visited the clinic. Even so, no one had yet linked the different illnesses to an abandoned radiation machine.

Devair's wife, Maria Gabriela Ferreira, was among those who became sick. Between September twenty-first and twenty-third, she suffered vomiting and diarrhoea.

A hospital attributed her symptoms to something she had eaten and sent her home to rest. Her mother came to care for her and later returned to her own home carrying contamination with her.

Others had nausea, burns and painful skin changes. Some patients were treated as if they had food poisoning, allergies or tropical disease. Their symptoms seemed to come from different homes, not one workplace.

At Devair's yard, employees were still handling pieces of the machine to recover valuable lead. Two of those workers would also die from their exposure.

On September twenty-fifth, the remnants were sold on again, to another scrapyard. The metal had changed hands once more, and the people carrying it still did not know why their families were getting sick.

By September twenty-eighth, Maria was convinced the glowing material was responsible. She went with a scrapyard employee to recover the remaining pieces.

They put the remnants in a plastic bag and took a bus to the local public-health office. The employee carried the bag from the bus on his shoulder.

He would suffer a severe radiation burn there. Maria had set out to show officials the object that she believed was causing the illnesses; neither traveller had protective equipment.

At the office, a doctor put the bag on his desk. Later, concerned by it, he moved it outside to a chair in the courtyard.

The doctors examining the patients were beginning to put two clues together: unusual skin injuries and the metal object Maria had brought in. One suspected radiation damage.

They asked for a medical physicist. Walter Mendes Ferreira happened to be visiting Goiânia. On September twenty-ninth he borrowed a radiation meter from an agency that used it for geological measurements.

He switched it on before he even reached the health office. The needle immediately went to the end of its scale, whichever direction he pointed it.

Walter thought the instrument was faulty. He went back for a replacement.

The second meter gave the same warning. This time there was no reason to doubt it: a powerful source of radiation was nearby.

When Walter reached the health office, firefighters were considering taking the object away and throwing it into a river. He stopped them. Moving the source without containing it could spread the danger further.

He had the building evacuated. Then he took the meter to Devair's scrapyard, where it again registered readings beyond the instrument's scale over a wide area.

Now the officials could identify a danger they had been unable to see. But they still had to find all the fragments carried into houses over the previous days.

Authorities isolated contaminated places, evacuated nearby residents and called for specialist help. That evening, Goiânia's Olympic stadium was designated as a centre for checking people.

Some residents came because they might have handled the powder. Others came simply because areas of their neighbourhood had been cordoned off and they wanted to know whether they were safe.

Over the following weeks, 112,800 people were screened. Radioactive contamination was detected in 249 of them; for 120, it was found only on clothing or footwear.

Others had material inside their bodies. The IAEA's detailed report records forty-nine hospital admissions among the internally contaminated group, with twenty patients needing intensive medical care.

The numbers describe different things. The huge queue at the stadium was the scale of the search. The smaller groups were the people whose bodies or belongings had been contaminated.

By then the investigations had traced the source back through the scrapyards to the abandoned clinic. The metal had been recovered, but cleaning up the city's homes would take far more work.

Seven residences were demolished. Contaminated soil, household objects and building materials had to be handled as radioactive waste. One source had become a problem spread across several addresses.

On October twenty-third, both Maria Gabriela and Leide died. The same woman who had carried the warning to the public-health office did not live to see the response completed.

Two scrapyard workers, Israel Batista dos Santos and Admilson Alves de Souza, died later that month. Devair, who had first admired the blue glow, survived.

The source had once served a medical purpose. The disaster began because it remained accessible after the clinic moved, and because no one handling the scrap knew what they had found.

Maria's bag gave officials a way to identify the danger. By then, a family had eaten beside radioactive fragments, others had carried them between homes, and four people would not survive.

What sent 112,800 people to the stadium was not an explosion or a reactor accident. It was a neglected medical source, broken open and passed from person to person before anyone measured it.
