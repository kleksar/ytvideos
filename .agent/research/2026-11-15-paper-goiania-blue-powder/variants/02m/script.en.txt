On September twenty-fourth, 1987, in Goiânia, Brazil, a man brought home fragments from his brother's scrapyard. His six-year-old daughter, Leide, handled them while eating with her hands. They contained radioactive caesium-137.

The substance had once been used to treat cancer. When a clinic moved, it left a treatment machine behind. Two men took part of it for scrap metal. Both fell ill before the radioactive source was even opened. A doctor told one of them it might be a food allergy.

The damaged source was sold to Devair Ferreira, the owner of a scrapyard. In the dark, the material appeared to glow blue. Visitors took fragments away, and Devair's brother brought some to Leide's home.

Then Devair's wife, Maria Gabriela, became ill too. She suspected the metal, recovered what remained of the source with a scrapyard worker and took a bus to a public-health office. The worker later developed a severe radiation burn where he had carried the bag.

A doctor moved the bag outside. On September twenty-ninth, a physicist named Walter Mendes Ferreira switched on a radiation meter before reaching the office. The needle went off the scale. He went back for another instrument.

The second meter did exactly the same thing. The health office was evacuated. Now the authorities had to find where the fragments had gone.

More than 112,000 people were screened during the response. Contamination was found in 249. Seven houses were demolished. Maria, Leide and two scrapyard workers died.

An unsecured medical source had been opened and its contents shared among families before the danger was recognized. By the time doctors understood the cause, a medical device had become a public-health emergency.

How had a fragment of that source reached a child's table?
