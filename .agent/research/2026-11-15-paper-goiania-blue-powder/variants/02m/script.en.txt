In September 1987, six-year-old Leide sat down to eat in Goiânia, Brazil. On the table were fragments of a strange material. It had looked blue and luminous in the dark. Leide touched the pieces while eating with her hands.

They contained radioactive caesium-137. But her family had no idea.

The material came from a cancer-treatment machine abandoned when a clinic moved. Two men took part of the machine for scrap metal. Both became ill even before someone broke open the source.

The damaged capsule was sold to a scrapyard run by Devair Ferreira. Its blue glow attracted attention. Visitors took tiny fragments away, and the powder spread between homes. Leide's father carried some to the family table.

People began vomiting or developed painful skin injuries. Doctors initially suspected ordinary illnesses. Devair's wife, Maria Gabriela, became sick too. She suspected the metal and travelled to a public-health office with its remains in a plastic bag.

On September twenty-ninth, physicist Walter Mendes Ferreira approached with a radiation meter. The needle went off the scale. He thought the instrument was broken and fetched a second one.

It gave the same reading. The authorities finally knew what they were dealing with.

A stadium became a screening centre. Across the response, more than 112,000 people were checked; 249 were found to be contaminated. Seven houses were demolished. Four people died, including Maria and Leide.

The source had been recovered and identified. But before anyone thought to measure it, its fragments had already travelled from a scrapyard into an ordinary family meal.
