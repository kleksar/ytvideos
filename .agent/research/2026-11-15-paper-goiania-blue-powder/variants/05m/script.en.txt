How could an abandoned cancer-treatment machine end up on a family's dinner table? Why did a physicist think two radiation meters were broken? And what did a woman bring to a public-health office in a plastic bag?

Goiânia, Brazil, September 1987. Scrapyard owner Devair Ferreira noticed a blue glow coming from a metal capsule in his garage. He brought the object inside. Relatives and visitors came to look, and some took tiny fragments home.

On September twenty-fourth, Devair's brother Ivo carried some of that material to his family. His six-year-old daughter, Leide, touched it while eating with her hands. Nobody in the room knew that the particles contained radioactive caesium-137.

How had a medical source reached a dinner table?

Almost two years earlier, a radiotherapy clinic had moved premises and left a machine behind, with its sealed radioactive source unsecured. In September, two men removed part of that machine and carried it away in a wheelbarrow, hoping to recover metal.

Both became sick before the source was opened. One consulted a doctor and was told he might have a food allergy. Their illness did not look like a warning from the piece of metal in the yard.

On September eighteenth, a man punctured the capsule with a screwdriver. That day, the assembly was sold to Devair's scrapyard. In darkness, the material appeared to glow blue. Its precise glowing mechanism was not established in the original accident report; the colour certainly did not mean it was harmless.

A visitor later broke off rice-sized pieces. Some fragments crumbled, and some were passed among relatives. Investigators recorded that people even rubbed the substance on their skin like glitter. It spread across more than one house, on clothing and hands, and sometimes entered bodies with food.

Leide's meal was one of those exposures. Her father had no reason to think the material from his brother's yard was dangerous.

Meanwhile, Devair's wife, Maria Gabriela, was vomiting and suffering diarrhoea. A hospital attributed her condition to something she had eaten. Other people developed burns and skin injuries. Different households were getting sick, but the common object had not yet been identified.

Workers continued dismantling the assembly for its valuable lead. Its remnants were sold to yet another scrapyard. The contaminated material kept moving even as people sought medical help.

By September twenty-eighth, Maria suspected the source. She and a worker recovered the remaining pieces, put them in a plastic bag and took a bus to the local health office. The worker carried the bag on his shoulder and later suffered a severe radiation burn there.

A doctor moved the bag from his desk into the courtyard. The unusual injuries and this metal object finally raised a new possibility: radiation.

The next day, physicist Walter Mendes Ferreira borrowed a meter and switched it on before reaching the office. The needle went straight beyond its scale, no matter where he pointed it. He assumed the instrument was faulty and fetched another.

The replacement gave the same reading. Walter arrived to find firefighters considering taking the object away and throwing it into a river. He stopped them. The health office was evacuated, and measurements at Devair's scrapyard also went beyond the scale.

Now investigators faced a different problem. They knew the danger was real, but they did not know every home to which the powder had travelled.

Goiânia's Olympic stadium became a centre for examining residents. Over the response, 112,800 people were screened and contamination was found in 249. That does not mean everyone screened was ill; some contamination was found only on clothing or footwear.

The source was traced back through the scrapyards to the abandoned clinic. Seven homes were demolished and contaminated belongings had to be removed. Maria and Leide died, as did two scrapyard workers.

The questions had the same answer: a medical radiation source was left accessible, opened for scrap, and shared before anyone recognised it. Maria's bag helped officials identify the danger. It could not undo the exposure that had already reached the families.
