In August 1986, a lake in Cameroon killed more than 1,700 people in a single night.

The houses below it were still standing. There had been no fresh volcanic eruption.

So how could a lake kill people far beyond its shore? And how could anyone stop it from happening again?

To answer that, scientists had to look far beneath the surface of Lake Nyos.

Carbon dioxide was entering the lake from the ground beneath an old volcanic crater.

Under the pressure of deep water, much of that gas stayed dissolved, like gas inside a sealed bottle of soda.

The lake also had layers that rarely mixed all the way down. That let the gas accumulate near the bottom.

For years, the lake could look ordinary while storing more and more carbon dioxide.

On August 21, 1986, some of that deep water began rising. Scientists still disagree about what started the release.

As the water moved upward, the pressure dropped. Dissolved carbon dioxide began to form bubbles.

Those bubbles made the rising water lighter. It moved upward faster, pulling more gas-rich water along with it.

The process fed itself. In a short time, an enormous amount of carbon dioxide escaped from the lake.

There was no wall of fire to warn the villages. Carbon dioxide is invisible and has no smell.

Because it was denser than the surrounding air, the gas spread into the lower valleys.

People and livestock in its path lost access to breathable air. More than 1,700 people died.

And the danger did not disappear with the cloud. Deep inside the lake, carbon dioxide was still accumulating.

There had already been a deadly gas release at another Cameroonian lake, Monoun, two years earlier.

Now scientists faced a second question: how do you empty a lake of gas without setting off another release?

Their answer was to bring the gas-rich deep water to the surface on purpose, through a pipe.

They lowered the pipe into the deep water and started the flow. Then the lake itself did much of the work.

As water rose inside the pipe, bubbles formed and made the column lighter, helping pull more water upward.

The result was a fountain powered by the very gas the engineers wanted to remove.

Lake Nyos got its first operating degassing pipe in 2001. But one pipe was not enough.

New carbon dioxide kept entering from below, and the amount trapped in the deep water fell too slowly.

There was another worry: could deliberately stirring deep water destabilize the lake?

Researchers monitored the water. Their measurements found that controlled degassing had not disrupted the lake's layered structure.

More pipes were added in 2011 and 2012 to release the gas faster.

Later work suggested that a maintained pipe system could balance the new carbon dioxide entering the lake.

That is not a guarantee of permanent safety. The lake and the equipment still need monitoring.

But the engineers had changed the problem. Gas that once built up unseen could now escape through a controlled route.

The exact trigger of that night in 1986 remains uncertain. The pipe offers something the villages did not have then: a way to let the gas out before another sudden release.
