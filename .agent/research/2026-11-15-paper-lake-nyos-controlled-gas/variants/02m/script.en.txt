How could a quiet lake leave whole villages unable to breathe? And what could anyone build to stop it happening again?

In August 1986, Lake Nyos in Cameroon released a huge cloud of carbon dioxide. More than 1,700 people died. The houses were still standing. There had been no new volcanic eruption.

The danger had been gathering underwater.

Carbon dioxide entered the lake from below. At depth, the pressure kept much of it dissolved. And because the lake's layers rarely mixed completely, gas-rich water collected near the bottom.

On August 21, some deep water began to rise. The exact trigger remains uncertain. As the water climbed, the pressure dropped and bubbles formed. Bubbles made the rising column lighter, so it pulled up still more gas-rich water. That accelerating release sent invisible, heavier-than-air carbon dioxide into the valleys below.

Understanding the mechanism did not solve the problem. The lake was still receiving new gas.

So engineers tried something that sounds dangerous: bring deep water up deliberately.

In 2001, they installed a pipe reaching into the gas-rich layer. Once the flow was started, bubbles formed inside the pipe. The bubbly water lifted itself, carrying dissolved gas to the surface in a controlled fountain.

One pipe could not remove the gas quickly enough. More were installed in 2011 and 2012. Researchers checked that the intervention was not breaking down the lake's protective layering.

The original trigger in 1986 is still not certain, and the lake needs monitoring. But the answer to the second question is remarkable: the same gas that drove the disaster now helps power a way to reduce its risk.
