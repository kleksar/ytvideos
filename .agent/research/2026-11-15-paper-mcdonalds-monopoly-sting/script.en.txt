Why did the FBI send a film crew to interview a man who had just won a million dollars at McDonald's? How could someone decide who won a supposedly random game? And why did a court later overturn the convictions of four prize claimants?

In August 2001, Michael Hoover sat in his home with an enormous McDonald's check. He was there to tell a camera crew how he had found a winning Monopoly game piece.

The crew asked him to describe the discovery. Hoover said he'd bought a magazine to replace one lost at sea, and that the winning piece had been inside.

For a fast-food contest, it was an unusual story. But the camera crew had not come to produce an advertisement. Federal agents were recording his account as evidence.

The million-dollar prize was real. The problem was where the winning game piece had come from.

To understand why federal investigators were in that room, go back to the tiny stickers McDonald's customers had been peeling off their food.

During its Monopoly promotions, the company handed out game pieces on restaurant packaging. Most gave customers a free item or a minor prize. A few rare pieces offered much larger rewards.

The ordinary promise was simple: buy a meal, get a chance. Nobody opening a package could know whether the winning sticker was inside.

McDonald's used an outside company, Simon Marketing, to help run the promotion. That company was responsible for preparing and safeguarding some of the most valuable pieces before distribution.

Its security director was Jerome Jacobson. His job gave him access to the rare pieces that were supposed to be protected until they reached the public.

That was the weakness in the game. The person guarding the most valuable prizes could also remove them before customers ever had a chance to find them.

According to accounts assembled after the investigation, Jacobson began diverting winning pieces in 1989. One went to his stepbrother, who claimed a prize worth twenty-five thousand dollars.

A single relative could not keep collecting major jackpots without attracting attention. The scheme needed different people to appear as unrelated winners.

Jacobson passed pieces through a growing network of intermediaries. Recruiters found people willing to claim a prize, and some of the resulting money flowed back through the network.

The apparent winners were spread across different places. To ordinary customers, each jackpot could look like another lucky discovery.

Yet the rare piece had already been removed from the system that was meant to distribute it randomly. Buying another meal could not bring a customer closer to a sticker that was no longer available.

There was an important distinction among the people claiming prizes. Some understood that the game pieces had been diverted. Others were told much less about their origins.

That difference would matter years later. For the moment, the system kept producing winners with apparently separate stories.

Then, in 2000, an anonymous tip reached the FBI. It pointed toward suspicious prize claimants connected with someone known as Uncle Jerry.

Investigators followed the relationships among the winners and intermediaries. They began tracing the unusual pattern back toward Jacobson, the man charged with protecting the pieces.

But exposing one suspect was not the same as proving what each claimant knew. Agents needed evidence that could link a person to a particular prize and to the network behind it.

McDonald's cooperated with the investigation. The 2001 promotion continued while agents gathered evidence.

That brought the FBI to Michael Hoover's home. He was being filmed as a million-dollar winner, but the interview served another purpose.

The crew could record his explanation without announcing that he was being questioned by federal agents. His account could be compared with evidence gathered elsewhere.

The investigation was larger than that single filmed conversation. Agents studied the people who handled the pieces and the claimants who received them.

On August twenty-first, 2001, the Justice Department announced eight arrests, including Jacobson. At that point, the public learned that the contest's rare prizes had been redirected before they reached ordinary players.

Later accounts put the total value of money and prizes involved in the wider scheme at roughly twenty-four million dollars, although estimates made during the original arrests were lower.

Jacobson pleaded guilty. In 2003, he was sentenced to thirty-seven months in prison.

It might sound as though every person who collected one of those prizes had to be guilty as well. The courts did not reach that conclusion.

In 2004, a federal appeals court overturned four convictions. Prosecutors had not proved that those claimants knew their game pieces had been stolen.

Receiving a stolen winning piece and knowingly participating in its theft were not the same thing. The distinction mattered in court even though the diversion of the pieces was real.

The case left two very different groups of people: customers who thought they were playing for prizes, and claimants whose names appeared on winning tickets. Neither group necessarily knew how the rare pieces had been handled.

For years, some of the most valuable game pieces had been selected before a customer opened a package. What looked like extraordinary luck was sometimes the final step of an organised distribution scheme.

The hidden camera in Hoover's home helped investigators trace that network. But the deeper deception had happened long before anyone picked up a giant check: the chance to win had already been taken away from ordinary customers.
