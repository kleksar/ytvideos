In August 2001, a man held a giant McDonald's check for one million dollars in his living room.

A film crew was recording his good fortune. The two men behind the cameras were undercover FBI agents.

Why was the FBI filming a fast-food winner? To uncover a scheme that had stolen millions in Monopoly prizes.

For years, McDonald's had printed Monopoly game pieces on food packaging.

Most pieces brought free food or another ordinary prize. The rarest could win a million dollars.

Customers were meant to find those rare pieces by chance. One man responsible for protecting them knew exactly where they were.

McDonald's hired an outside marketing company, Simon Marketing, to manage the promotion.

Its security director was Jerome Jacobson. Part of his job was guarding the valuable winning pieces before distribution.

The envelopes were sealed. The security procedure existed to stop somebody from taking the prizes.

Jacobson was also one of the few people who could get near those envelopes.

In 1989, he passed a winning piece worth twenty-five thousand dollars to his stepbrother.

Later, valuable pieces moved through a growing network of associates, in return for money or a share of the winnings.

One person could not keep claiming the jackpots without drawing attention. The scheme needed new names.

Recruiters supplied people who would claim the pieces as if they'd found them in the game.

A claimant received the published prize, kept part, and sent some of the money back through the network.

The winners came from different places and seemed unrelated. The same small circle was quietly selecting them.

Jacobson later described opening sealed prize envelopes, swapping out rare pieces, and closing the envelopes again.

A customer peeling a sticker off a drink could never find a million-dollar piece that had already been diverted.

The scheme expanded. Some claimants knew what they were doing; others were never shown how their winning pieces had been obtained.

McDonald's itself was being cheated. Jacobson worked for its contractor, not as a McDonald's employee.

For years, the game kept running. Then, in 2000, the FBI received an anonymous tip.

The tip pointed to suspicious winners, linked to a man known as Uncle Jerry.

Agents examined records and traced connections back toward Jacobson.

They needed evidence without alerting everyone else in the network.

McDonald's agreed to let the 2001 promotion continue during the investigation.

One of the new million-dollar claimants was Michael Hoover. He agreed to be filmed.

He told the crew he had found the winning piece in a magazine bought to replace a copy lost at sea.

Hoover believed he was giving a promotional interview. The camera crew was gathering evidence.

The filming was one part of an investigation that had already linked multiple winners and middlemen.

On August twenty-first, 2001, the Justice Department announced eight arrests, including Jacobson.

Investigators eventually described a fraud involving roughly twenty-four million dollars in cash and prizes.

Jacobson pleaded guilty. In 2003, he received a prison sentence of thirty-seven months.

But the legal outcomes were not the same for everyone who claimed a prize.

In 2004, an appeals court overturned four convictions because prosecutors had not proved those claimants knew the pieces were stolen.

The stolen tickets were real. That didn't make every apparent winner a knowing participant in the theft.

And the ordinary customers spent years looking for rare pieces that had been secretly taken out of circulation.

The biggest deception was not the fake story of one lucky winner. It was who got to choose the winners in the first place.

That camera in Michael Hoover's living room wasn't there to celebrate a jackpot. It helped expose the people controlling it.
