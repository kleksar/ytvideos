Why did the FBI film a McDonald's Monopoly winner in his living room? And how could a random prize have been picked before anyone bought a meal?

In August 2001, Michael Hoover sat beside a giant million-dollar McDonald's check. A camera crew asked how he'd found the winning piece. Hoover gave an unusual account involving a replacement magazine after one had been lost at sea.

What he didn't know was that the crew was working with the FBI. Investigators wanted to know where the prize piece had really come from.

McDonald's customers collected Monopoly stickers on food packaging. Most brought small rewards. A few rare pieces promised huge prizes. Simon Marketing, the contractor handling part of the promotion, was supposed to keep those valuable pieces secure.

Its security director, Jerome Jacobson, had access to them. Investigative accounts say that from 1989 he diverted rare pieces, passing them first to a relative and later through intermediaries to people who appeared to be ordinary winners. Some claimants understood the scheme better than others.

By 2000, an anonymous tip had pointed the FBI toward a network linked to someone called Uncle Jerry. Agents traced connections between winners, and the staged interview with Hoover became part of their evidence-gathering.

On August 21, 2001, the Justice Department announced eight arrests. Jacobson later pleaded guilty and received a thirty-seven-month prison sentence.

But there was another ending. In 2004, an appeals court overturned four convictions because prosecutors hadn't proved those claimants knew the pieces were stolen.

The FBI's cameras helped expose a real diversion of prizes. They couldn't, by themselves, establish what every winner knew. For customers opening food packages, the essential betrayal came earlier: some rare prizes had been removed before anyone had a fair chance to find them.
