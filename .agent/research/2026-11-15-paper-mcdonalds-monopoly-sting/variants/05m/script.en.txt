Why did the FBI film a man who had just claimed a million dollars from McDonald's? How could the winners of a random game be chosen in advance? And why did four convictions fall apart after the theft was exposed?

In August 2001, Michael Hoover sat at home with an enormous McDonald's check. The camera crew asked him to explain his luck. Hoover said he had lost a magazine at sea, bought a replacement and discovered a winning Monopoly piece inside.

For years, people had been peeling little Monopoly stickers off their meals. Most were worth very little. A handful could make someone a millionaire. To give every customer a chance, the rare pieces had to reach the public without anyone picking their winners.

But the men and women recording Hoover's story were working with the FBI. Agents were investigating the company that was supposed to protect those prizes. Its security director had access to the winning pieces before any customer could find them.

McDonald's had hired Simon Marketing to run the promotion. Jerome Jacobson was responsible for distributing its most valuable game pieces. According to later accounts, he began diverting prizes in 1989, giving one early piece worth twenty-five thousand dollars to his stepbrother.

Jacobson eventually passed pieces through a network of recruiters. A person who received a piece could present it as a lucky find. Once the prize check arrived, some of the money went back through the intermediaries.

The authorities later linked Hoover's claim in Rhode Island to a recruiter named Andrew Glomb. Another million-dollar Monopoly claim, in Texas, was linked to different recruiters. Those were allegations at the time of the arrests, but they show why a list of winners alone was of little use.

People in different parts of the country could claim prizes that had come from the same insider. The restaurants could keep serving meals and awarding other prizes. Customers had no way of knowing that some of the rare pieces had never reached them.

In 2000, an anonymous tip led the FBI toward suspicious winners and a man known as Uncle Jerry. Investigators began following the connections between prize recipients and the people who had arranged their claims.

McDonald's cooperated. At the FBI's request, the 2001 game continued while agents gathered evidence. Hoover's interview was part of that investigation. Instead of revealing themselves as federal investigators, the crew let him explain where he said the piece had come from. The agents could then compare that account with the evidence linking his claim to the network.

On August twenty-first, the Justice Department announced eight arrests and alleged that more than thirteen million dollars in major prizes had been diverted. Later estimates put the wider scheme at about twenty-four million. Jacobson pleaded guilty and in 2003 received a thirty-seven-month prison sentence.

Yet the trial of four other defendants had exposed a problem. Prosecutors argued that people who obtained game pieces through others and claimed the prizes were part of the conspiracy, even if they did not know about Jacobson's theft.

The rules themselves were less clear. At trial, a McDonald's representative acknowledged that players could give their pieces to other people. Pieces were publicly traded online, including through the company's own website. Getting a sticker from somebody else did not prove that a person knew it had been stolen.

For weeks, the defense had argued that the prosecution needed to establish that knowledge. Five minutes before instructing the jury, the trial judge changed his position and told jurors that such knowledge was necessary. The prosecutor asked for a break. The judge refused. The jury convicted the four defendants.

In 2004, an appeals court concluded that the evidence had not established their participation in the single conspiracy they had been charged with. The government ultimately accepted that the four convictions should be set aside and acquittals entered.

The camera in Hoover's living room could record his explanation; it could not reveal what every other prize claimant knew. Jacobson's guilty plea, meanwhile, established his own role in the diversion.

For the customer peeling a Monopoly sticker off a cup, the real betrayal had happened much earlier. Someone had removed part of the chance they thought they were being offered, before their meal was ever served.
