Why did the FBI pretend to be a television crew visiting a McDonald's millionaire? How could someone choose the winner of a contest advertised as a matter of chance? And why, after the scheme was exposed, did four convictions fail in court?

In August 2001, Michael Hoover sat at home with a giant check for one million dollars. A crew was filming his story for what appeared to be a McDonald's promotion. Hoover described finding a winning Monopoly piece in a magazine he'd bought to replace one lost at sea.

It was a striking account. The cameras were recording more than a celebration. Federal investigators wanted Hoover's explanation preserved while they traced the source of the winning piece. The film crew was part of an FBI operation.

To understand the investigation, we need to look at the contest customers thought they were playing.

McDonald's had been running Monopoly promotions in which customers received game pieces with food. Some could be redeemed for ordinary prizes. The rarest pieces led to much more valuable winnings. The attraction was precisely that a meal could carry a tiny chance of a major reward.

A player could inspect the sticker on a package. They could not see the secure handling process that took place before that package reached the restaurant.

McDonald's relied on an outside contractor, Simon Marketing, for important parts of the promotion, including the handling of valuable game pieces. Within that company, the security director was Jerome Jacobson. His position placed him close to the rare pieces that needed the strongest protection.

This was supposed to be a safeguard. Instead, it gave one insider an opportunity that ordinary players never had.

If a rare game piece was taken before it went into circulation, no customer could discover it by chance. The advertised game could still operate, and small prizes could still be handed out, while a few of the most coveted winning pieces were directed elsewhere.

According to later investigative accounts, Jacobson began diverting winning pieces in 1989. One early piece went to his stepbrother, who claimed a twenty-five-thousand-dollar prize.

But a scheme involving major prizes faces an obvious problem: the same person cannot plausibly appear as the fortunate winner again and again.

The solution was not to keep winning personally. Jacobson passed pieces into a network. Intermediaries found people to present the prizes as their own, and some of the proceeds made their way back through the chain.

The outward result could resemble a series of independent lucky breaks. Winners appeared in different places, holding different prizes, telling separate stories.

The important fact was hidden upstream. Some of those winning pieces had not been distributed to customers through the game's ordinary process at all.

That also meant the people at the end of the chain did not necessarily share the same knowledge. One participant might understand that a rare piece had been removed from the promotion. Another might know only that an intermediary was offering an opportunity to collect a prize.

Those distinctions do not erase the diversion. They do change what prosecutors need to prove about each person.

While the network operated, customers continued to participate under the assumption that the rare prizes were available to be found. Some were still available. Others had been taken out before any honest customer could reach them.

In 2000, the FBI received an anonymous tip about suspicious winners and someone known as Uncle Jerry. The nickname became a lead, not a substitute for proof.

Investigators needed to connect prize claimants, the people who recruited them, and the insider with access to the pieces. A list of unusual winners by itself would not tell them how a winning sticker moved from a secure supply into a claimant's hands.

McDonald's cooperated with the inquiry. The 2001 promotion continued while investigators collected evidence. That allowed agents to examine how people actually claimed prizes rather than relying only on accounts after the fact.

One of those claimants was Hoover. He could describe a seemingly ordinary discovery, but his unusual account had to be checked against the evidence of where the piece came from.

So agents used the appearance of a congratulatory film shoot. They asked Hoover to tell his story for the cameras without identifying the interview as an FBI evidence-gathering operation.

The visible scene was a winner and a giant check. The investigative purpose was to record his account, then compare it with the links the agents were uncovering.

This was not the whole investigation. The case depended on tracing the movement of prized game pieces and the relationships among the people who handled and claimed them. A convincing story in one living room could not prove the entire network.

On August twenty-first, 2001, the Justice Department announced eight arrests, including Jacobson's. The news transformed a familiar fast-food promotion into a public account of prizes stolen from its distribution process.

The original announcement described the allegations as they stood then. Later retellings have estimated the wider money and prizes involved at roughly twenty-four million dollars. That retrospective figure should not be confused with the smaller sums reported at the time of the initial arrests.

For customers, the figure was not the central question. A promotion depends on a rule everyone can understand: if a piece is supposed to be randomly distributed, it must remain in that distribution until someone legitimately receives it.

The Jacobson case showed how the rule could be broken before a customer saw anything suspicious.

Jacobson pleaded guilty. In 2003, he was sentenced to thirty-seven months in prison. The central diversion of valuable game pieces was no longer merely an allegation about an unexplained pattern of winners.

But the legal outcomes did not end with Jacobson's sentence.

Other people had claimed prizes that passed through the network. Were they all knowingly involved in stealing the pieces? The court had to evaluate what each accused person actually knew, not simply whether a prize had come from a compromised source.

This is where the case produced a less obvious ending than the one promised by headlines about a rigged game.

In 2004, a federal appeals court overturned four convictions. Prosecutors had not established that those claimants knew the Monopoly pieces had been stolen.

The ruling did not declare the entire game honest, undo Jacobson's guilty plea, or prove that every claimant was innocent of every possible wrongdoing. It addressed a specific failure of proof for those convictions.

A stolen winning piece can reach someone who understands its origin. It can also reach a person without that knowledge. The fact of receiving it is not, by itself, proof of knowing participation in its theft.

That is why the same investigation could establish a genuine prize-diversion scheme while failing to sustain four particular convictions.

Return now to Hoover's living room. The large check was not a prop, and the advertised million-dollar prize was real. The hidden part of the scene was the identity and purpose of the people operating the cameras.

The recording helped investigators capture one claim in the chain. To judge the people in that chain, however, investigators and courts still needed evidence about access, movement of the pieces, and individual knowledge.

The case has two endings that belong together. Jacobson, the security director entrusted with protecting the rare pieces, was convicted after admitting his role. Four other convictions were set aside because a crucial fact about the claimants' knowledge had not been proved.

And for someone opening a Monopoly sticker at a restaurant, the deception happened before the contest could even feel random. Some of the prizes they hoped to find had already been sent to chosen hands.
