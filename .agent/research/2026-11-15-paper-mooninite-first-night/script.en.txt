Why did one small glowing sign close part of a Boston highway? Who knew it was an advertisement while police searched for explosives? And how did one of the men who installed it end up creating a light show for a Boston celebration six years later?

On January thirty-first, two thousand seven, a passenger noticed a black panel attached beneath an overpass near Sullivan Square.

The panel had batteries, exposed wires and rows of colored lights. To someone passing by, its purpose wasn't obvious.

The officers responding to the report didn't know who had installed it. They called in the bomb squad.

Police blocked part of Interstate Ninety-three. Trains on the Orange Line were delayed during the morning commute.

Then the bomb squad destroyed the board. The object they'd treated as a possible threat had an unexpected face.

Its lights formed a square-headed cartoon alien raising a rude finger. There had never been any explosives inside.

The board advertised a film based on the late-night cartoon Aqua Teen Hunger Force. Some viewers would have recognized it instantly.

Weeks earlier, two local artists had attached similar boards around Boston, Cambridge and Somerville.

They had hung there without causing an alarm. The same advertising campaign was running in other American cities, too.

But that morning, one report led to others. Police checked more bridges and streets, and they were dealing with separate suspicious calls as well.

It was a few years after September eleventh. Exposed wiring beside a busy road couldn't simply be dismissed when no one knew who put it there.

The people who ordered the signs, however, knew exactly what they were. Marketing firm Interference had hired Peter Berdovsky and Sean Stevens to install them.

Berdovsky even went to the overpass and filmed the police response. He recognized the display that officers were investigating.

But the agency had asked him to stay quiet while it dealt with the situation. The official explanation didn't arrive that morning.

Only late that afternoon did Turner Broadcasting, Cartoon Network's parent company, publicly accept responsibility for the campaign.

By then officers had searched more locations and disrupted transport. That evening, Berdovsky and Stevens were arrested.

Reporters asked the pair about the case the next day. The two refused to discuss it, talking instead about hairstyles from the nineteen-seventies.

The odd press conference drew attention, but the case continued. Both men later agreed to community service.

Turner and the marketing firm agreed to pay a combined two million dollars toward the emergency response.

Five days after the agreement, Cartoon Network's top executive resigned.

For Berdovsky, light remained his art. He kept working with electronic images and projections.

Six years later, First Night Boston was preparing its New Year's Eve celebration in Copley Square.

They wanted a large electronic light show. They chose Berdovsky to create it.

The artist who had been arrested over unauthorized illuminated boards now had an official invitation to light up the same city.

Reporters asked Boston's mayor, Thomas Menino, why Berdovsky was getting the job.

In two thousand seven, Menino had called the advertising stunt outrageous. This time he gave a different answer.

“We're a forgiving city,” he said.
