Boston police stopped trains and blocked a highway because of a small glowing sign.

It was January 31, 2007. A passenger spotted something attached beneath an overpass by Sullivan Square.

The black board had batteries, exposed wiring and rows of colored lights.

No one responding to the report knew who had put it there, or what it was meant to do.

The bomb squad arrived and treated the object as a possible explosive.

Police closed part of the highway. Trains on Boston's Orange Line were delayed.

Then the bomb squad destroyed the sign.

It wasn't a bomb. The lights formed a square-headed cartoon alien with one hand raised in a rude gesture.

Viewers of a late-night cartoon called Aqua Teen Hunger Force would have recognized the character.

The board was an advertisement for a film. It contained no explosives.

Artists had put up similar boards around Boston and nearby cities weeks earlier.

Nobody had raised an alarm about them until that morning.

Now one report was bringing in more reports, and police were checking other bridges and streets.

Officers were also fielding other suspicious calls that day, unrelated to the glowing characters.

In a city still alert to possible attacks after September 11, unexplained wiring beside a busy road wasn't something police would simply ignore.

But the people behind the advertisement already knew exactly what the signs were.

A marketing agency had hired two local artists, Peter Berdovsky and Sean Stevens, to put them up.

The same advertising campaign had placed boards in other American cities without causing a response like Boston's.

Berdovsky even went to the overpass and filmed part of the police response.

He recognized the sign, but the agency had asked him to keep quiet while it handled the situation.

Turner Broadcasting, the television company's parent, publicly took responsibility late that afternoon.

By then traffic had been disrupted, officers had searched more sites, and the two installers were under scrutiny.

Berdovsky and Stevens were arrested that evening.

The next day, reporters asked them questions about the case.

The pair refused to discuss it. They would only talk about hairstyles from the nineteen-seventies.

The press conference became a spectacle of its own. Their legal case didn't disappear.

Both installers later agreed to community service.

Turner and the marketing firm agreed to pay a total of two million dollars toward the cost of the response.

Days after that agreement, Cartoon Network's top executive resigned.

Six years later, Berdovsky was still making art using electronic images and light.

First Night Boston was preparing an official New Year's Eve celebration in Copley Square.

It needed a large electronic light show, and the organizers chose Berdovsky to create it.

The man whose unapproved glowing signs had brought out a bomb squad was now invited to light up a Boston square.

Reporters asked Boston's mayor about giving him the job.

He answered, “We're a forgiving city.”
