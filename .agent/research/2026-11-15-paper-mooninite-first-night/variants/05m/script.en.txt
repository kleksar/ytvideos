Why did a small glowing sign bring traffic to a halt in Boston? Who could have told police what it was? And how did an artist arrested over the sign end up with a chance to illuminate an entire Boston landmark?

On January thirty-first, two thousand seven, a passenger near Sullivan Square reported an unfamiliar electronic panel beneath an overpass. There were exposed wires, batteries and colored lights. Nobody responding to the report could identify the person who had put it there.

Police called the bomb squad. Traffic on Interstate Ninety-three was interrupted, and trains on the Orange Line were delayed during the morning rush.

The bomb squad destroyed the object. There was no explosive. The lights formed a square-headed cartoon alien making an obscene gesture.

Anyone who watched Aqua Teen Hunger Force might have recognized the character. The panel was advertising a film based on the late-night cartoon. And there were more of them.

Weeks earlier, a marketing company called Interference had given forty electronic signs to a local artist, Peter Berdovsky. He and Sean Stevens had put the signs up around Boston and neighboring cities. The campaign was running elsewhere in the United States without a similar emergency.

As news of the first report spread, Boston police received calls about other objects. They also had separate suspicious reports to investigate. Even after one board turned out to be harmless, officers still had to establish what they were finding elsewhere.

One person who could identify the advertisement was already there. Berdovsky went to the overpass and filmed the police response. According to later reporting, the agency that hired him asked him to keep quiet while it handled the situation.

Turner Broadcasting, the company behind Cartoon Network, publicly acknowledged the advertising campaign late that afternoon. By then, the response had already disrupted transport across the city. Berdovsky and Stevens were arrested that evening.

Outside court the following day, reporters tried to question the two artists about the incident. The men refused to discuss the case. Instead, they talked about hairstyles from the nineteen-seventies.

The criminal proceedings continued. The companies behind the campaign agreed to pay a combined two million dollars in connection with the emergency response. That was separate from what happened to the two artists.

Both men performed community service at a rehabilitation center in Boston. In May they stood in court and apologized, saying they had not expected the commotion. The charges were dropped.

Berdovsky continued to work with electronic images under the name Zebbler. Nearly seven years after the incident, Boston was preparing its First Night celebration to welcome two thousand fourteen. The organizers needed someone who could project an animated film onto the city's public library.

They offered the job to Berdovsky's studio. The project had fifty thousand dollars in private funding. Boston's arts director defended the choice by pointing to the artist's professional reputation since the incident.

The team had to make images fit the columns, windows and other features of the actual library. To prepare them, they built a three-dimensional replica of the building. This time, the lights would have a place chosen specifically for the artwork.

Composer Ben Cantil began work on the soundtrack while the pictures were still being developed. He made musical sections that could be adjusted as the animation took shape. The finished piece would tell the history of Boston.

The same mayor who had condemned the advertising campaign was now asked about the artist's return. Thomas Menino answered, “We're a forgiving city.”

And the commission went ahead. The studio documented the completed ten-minute projection on the library's facade. Cantil's college also reported that his music accompanied the countdown into the first minute of the new year.

The program ended with the familiar New Year's tune Auld Lang Syne. In two thousand seven, police had been called to remove Berdovsky's work from public view. As two thousand fourteen arrived, his work was part of Boston's public celebration.
