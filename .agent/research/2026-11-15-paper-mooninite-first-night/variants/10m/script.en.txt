Why did a glowing cartoon sign close a Boston highway? Who could have told the police what it was? And how did an artist arrested over that sign end up creating a New Year's show on an entire public building?

The story began on a weekday morning, January thirty-first, two thousand seven. A passenger near the Sullivan Square bus station saw something attached to a steel beam beneath Interstate Ninety-three.

There were batteries, exposed wires and a black panel dotted with colored lights. It looked like an electronic device, but nobody receiving the report could establish who had placed it there or why.

Authorities responded to that uncertainty. Northbound traffic on the highway was interrupted, and trains on the Orange Line were delayed during the morning rush. A bomb squad examined the object. About two hours after the original report, specialists destroyed it.

There was no explosive inside. It took time to identify the object, but its colored lights formed a square-headed cartoon alien making an obscene gesture.

The character came from Aqua Teen Hunger Force, a late-night animated show on Cartoon Network. A movie based on the series was coming out, and someone had decided to advertise it in the streets.

This wasn't the only board. Advertising firm Interference, working for Turner Broadcasting, had supplied forty electronic displays to local artist Peter Berdovsky, also known as Zebbler. He and a friend, Sean Stevens, attached the displays around Boston, Cambridge and Somerville weeks before the emergency.

Similar signs appeared in other American cities without a comparable emergency. The Boston installations had also gone largely unnoticed for weeks. That changed when reports of other objects began coming in.

Police started receiving reports of more suspicious objects. Officers were sent to bridges, intersections and other sites. A later police account noted separate threat reports arriving that day, unrelated to the signs. The first device had no explosive, but that didn't automatically identify every other object being reported.

In the years after September eleventh, an unidentified electronic object near a transport route could not simply be treated as a decoration. The police commissioner's later account describes a day of overlapping alerts, not a single cartoon sign that explained everything.

But someone did know exactly where those lights had come from. Berdovsky, the artist who installed the boards, went to the overpass while the emergency response was under way. He recognized the display and filmed what police were doing.

According to later reporting, the agency that hired Berdovsky asked him to keep quiet while it dealt with the situation. The public did not yet have an explanation from the people responsible for the campaign.

By late afternoon, Turner Broadcasting accepted responsibility for the displays. Its statement identified them as an outdoor advertising campaign for Aqua Teen Hunger Force and confirmed they were not explosive devices. That was the answer police had lacked in the morning.

But an official explanation could not rewind the disrupted transport or the hours spent examining reports. That evening, Berdovsky and Stevens were arrested. The next day they were accused in court of placing a hoax device and disorderly conduct.

Outside court, reporters waited for a serious explanation. Instead the artists insisted on discussing hairstyles from the nineteen-seventies. Their strange press conference spread widely online, making an already bizarre news story even stranger.

The jokes spread beyond the press conference. One street artist put reassuring stickers on ordinary objects such as parking meters. The city itself was dealing with the cost of the response.

City officials demanded accountability. Within days, Turner and Interference agreed to pay a combined two million dollars to agencies involved in the response and related security programs. That settlement was the companies' obligation, not a fine paid personally by each artist.

The fallout reached Cartoon Network's leadership. Its general manager, Jim Samples, resigned soon afterward, publicly recognizing that the episode had brought negative attention and expense to the company.

The installers had their own agreement with prosecutors. Both completed community service at Spaulding Rehabilitation Center. In May they returned to court, apologized and said they had not expected the reaction. The charges were dropped.

Berdovsky continued working as a video artist under the name Zebbler. He began collaborating with Ben Cantil, a musician who had graduated from Berklee in two thousand seven. Their joint performances combined electronic music and projection art.

Berdovsky's studio also took on larger projection projects. One technique became especially important to his work: making moving images fit the actual shape of the surface they would be projected onto. That experience would matter when Boston needed a New Year's centerpiece.

Nearly seven years after the scare, the organizers of Boston's annual First Night celebration approached Berdovsky's studio. They wanted an animated history of the city projected onto the Boston Public Library to welcome two thousand fourteen.

The proposed show was reported as a fifty-thousand-dollar project, funded by private donations. This was an organized public celebration, not an unauthorized installation. It had a defined venue, a production team and permission to use the building's facade.

The library's columns, windows and stonework presented a different challenge from a flat cinema screen. The studio built a three-dimensional replica of the building and used computer-aided design and machining in the preparation. A twelve-person team was working on the show in Somerville.

With video mapping, the artist can arrange a moving picture around the features of a real building. In this case the team planned an illusion in which parts of the library appeared to break apart, with the soundtrack changing as the bricks fell. The real building would remain untouched.

Reporters noticed that reversal. They asked why Boston would invite a man it had arrested in two thousand seven. The city's arts director defended the choice on the grounds of Berdovsky's subsequent reputation and experience as a video artist.

Berdovsky publicly described the invitation as a chance to give something back to Boston through his work.

Mayor Thomas Menino had called the earlier promotion outrageous. When he was questioned about the new event, his answer was considerably shorter than the long arguments that followed the bomb scare.

“We're a forgiving city,” he said.

The artists now had a deadline. Cantil was writing music for roughly a dozen scenes, but for much of the preparation he had only the outline of the story. He spent about a month developing musical themes and transitions that could be adapted once the pictures were finished.

The last images came together only a few days before New Year's Eve. Much of the remaining music had to be completed in the time that was left. Cantil recalled exporting audio until shortly before the soundcheck. The team brought in orchestral arrangements, harp and jazz piano alongside electronic sound.

Then the projection actually took place. The studio's account records the ten-minute presentation on the Copley Square facade, and Cantil's college reported that his soundtrack accompanied the countdown into two thousand fourteen. The music ended with a piano refrain of Auld Lang Syne.

The last piece of music marked the arrival of the new year. The artist whose light board had been removed during an emergency in two thousand seven had finally put an illuminated history of Boston on one of its best-known buildings.
