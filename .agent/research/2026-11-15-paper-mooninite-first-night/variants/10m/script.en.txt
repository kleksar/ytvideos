Why did a glowing cartoon sign close a Boston highway? Why didn't the people who installed it identify it when police brought in bomb squads? And how could an artist arrested over that sign later be invited to light up Boston's New Year's celebration?

The story began on a weekday morning, January thirty-first, two thousand seven. A passenger near the Sullivan Square bus station saw something attached to a steel beam beneath Interstate Ninety-three.

There were batteries, exposed wires and a black panel dotted with colored lights. It looked like an electronic device, but nobody receiving the report could establish who had placed it there or why.

Authorities responded to that uncertainty. Northbound traffic on the highway was interrupted, and trains on the Orange Line were delayed during the morning rush. A bomb squad examined the object. About two hours after the original report, specialists destroyed it.

The panel contained no explosives. Once its illuminated image was understood, its meaning was completely different from the threat officers had been investigating. The colored lights outlined a square-headed cartoon alien making an obscene gesture.

For a fan of Aqua Teen Hunger Force, a late-night animated television show, the character was familiar. The sign promoted a film based on that show. But the officers weren't watching a cartoon. They were responding to an unexplained object beneath a transport route.

This wasn't the only board. Advertising firm Interference, working for Turner Broadcasting, had supplied forty electronic displays to local artist Peter Berdovsky, also known as Zebbler. He and a friend, Sean Stevens, attached the displays around Boston, Cambridge and Somerville weeks before the emergency.

Similar signs appeared in other American cities. Most passed without much notice. In Boston, one commuter saw a battery-powered panel beneath a bridge, reported it, and triggered a response that rapidly went beyond a single location.

Police started receiving reports of more suspicious objects. Officers were sent to bridges, intersections and other sites. A later police account noted separate threat reports arriving that day, unrelated to the signs. The first device had no explosive, but that didn't automatically identify every other object being reported.

In two thousand seven, the attacks of September eleventh were still an important part of American security planning. Officials could not simply assume a device beside a road was harmless because it looked odd. Their critics would later ask why police failed to recognize the cartoon. Those are different questions.

The public was also receiving two kinds of information. People familiar with the television show saw a harmless advertising image. Emergency workers faced a board whose purpose and owner were not yet known. Recognizing a character and confirming that a device is safe are not the same task.

But someone did know exactly where those lights had come from. Berdovsky, the artist who installed the boards, went to the overpass while the emergency response was under way. He recognized the display and filmed what police were doing.

That creates the second puzzle. Why didn't this immediately end the uncertainty? According to the subsequent reporting, the agency responsible for the campaign asked him to remain quiet while it handled the situation. We don't know the contents of every conversation that day, so the sequence matters more than invented motives.

The company behind the campaign knew it was advertising. The officers on the street were investigating unknown objects. The explanation that could connect those two facts did not arrive publicly during the first response.

By late afternoon, Turner Broadcasting accepted responsibility for the displays. Its statement identified them as an outdoor advertising campaign for Aqua Teen Hunger Force and confirmed they were not explosive devices. That was the answer police had lacked in the morning.

But an official explanation could not rewind the disrupted transport or the hours spent examining reports. That evening, Berdovsky and Stevens were arrested. The next day they were accused in court of placing a hoax device and disorderly conduct.

Outside court, reporters waited for a serious explanation. Instead the artists insisted on discussing hairstyles from the nineteen-seventies. Their strange press conference spread widely online, making an already bizarre news story even stranger.

The spectacle also obscured the legal questions. A person who hangs an advertisement is not necessarily trying to frighten people. An advertisement can nevertheless cause real costs when placed without clear permission near infrastructure. The charges, the eventual dismissal and the company settlement must be kept separate.

Some residents and journalists mocked Boston's reaction. Others pointed out that an unidentified wired object cannot be dismissed safely just because it eventually turns out to be a cartoon. Authorities defended their decision to investigate; the public argued about whether the scale of the response was justified.

City officials demanded accountability. Within days, Turner and Interference agreed to pay a combined two million dollars to agencies involved in the response and related security programs. That settlement was the companies' obligation, not a fine paid personally by each artist.

The fallout reached Cartoon Network's leadership. Its general manager, Jim Samples, resigned soon afterward, publicly recognizing that the episode had brought negative attention and expense to the company.

For the two installers, the criminal case followed a different path. They apologized, completed community service at a Boston rehabilitation center, and in May the charges were dropped. An arrest had occurred, but there was no criminal conviction in the final outcome described by those reports.

Meanwhile the bigger argument about advertising continued. A campaign designed to attract attention by putting unexpected light boards around cities had attracted precisely the wrong audience: bomb squads. Politicians discussed stronger penalties for terror hoaxes, but a proposal is not the same as an enacted law.

If the story ended there, it would be a costly lesson in publicity. But Berdovsky was not only the man who had hung those panels. He was an artist whose medium was electronic light, and he kept making projected images after the scandal.

Under the name Zebbler, he developed work that used images and building surfaces rather than small boards beneath bridges. A projection can be made to fit the outline of a wall, window or facade. To achieve that, the artist needs to plan for the shape of the surface.

Six years later, Boston's annual First Night New Year's celebration was being prepared in Copley Square. The organizers planned a centerpiece for the festivities welcoming two thousand fourteen. They wanted moving images projected onto the Boston Public Library.

The proposed show was reported as a fifty-thousand-dollar project, funded by private donations. This was an organized public celebration, not an unauthorized installation. It had a defined venue, a production team and permission to use the building's facade.

Organizers selected Berdovsky's studio. His team planned to show recognizable scenes from Boston across the library's exterior. A contemporary local report described artists preparing a three-dimensional model of the building to line up the projected images with its architectural details.

That technique is called video mapping. The picture isn't merely enlarged onto a flat screen: the shapes of a building influence how the images are composed. The practical work helps explain why a skilled projection artist was hired for this event.

The artist had once watched officials remove an unidentified illuminated panel from beneath a highway. Now the organizers were preparing to give his images an entire public building, and a large audience, during a city celebration. The two jobs involved light, but their contexts were almost opposites.

Reporters noticed that reversal. They asked why Boston would invite a man it had arrested in two thousand seven. The city's arts director defended the choice on the grounds of Berdovsky's subsequent reputation and experience as a video artist.

Berdovsky described the invitation publicly as a chance to give something back to Boston through projection and light. That is his documented explanation. His private thoughts during the police response or the later commission are not known.

Mayor Thomas Menino had called the earlier promotion outrageous. When he was questioned about the new event, his answer was considerably shorter than the long arguments that followed the bomb scare.

“We're a forgiving city,” he said.

The same city, the same artist and the same medium had produced two very different responses. In the first case nobody could identify a wired panel by a highway, and police removed it. In the second, the organizers had invited the artist and planned the show in the open. The ending was not another emergency. It was a commission.
