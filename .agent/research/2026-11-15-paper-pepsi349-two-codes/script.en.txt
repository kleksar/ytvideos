A television presenter read out three digits: three, four, nine. Across the Philippines, people believed they'd just won a fortune.

Pepsi then told many of them their bottle caps were not winners. How could a winning number fail to win?

The answer involved a tiny security code. But the disagreement about that code would last for years.

In early 1992, Pepsi launched Number Fever to help sell soft drinks in the Philippines.

Buy a bottle, turn over its cap, and compare the printed number with the winning number announced on television.

A top prize of one million pesos made the nightly announcement a very big deal.

The campaign caught on. Pepsi extended it for five more weeks.

On May twenty-fifth, the number announced for the million-peso prize was three-four-nine.

Instead of a handful of winners, enormous numbers of people found three-four-nine beneath their caps.

Contemporary reports put the number of ordinary caps carrying those digits at around eight hundred thousand.

If all those caps qualified for the top prize, the bill would dwarf the contest's planned prize budget.

Customers came to collect. Pepsi rejected most of their claims, saying their caps carried the wrong security codes.

Each cap had a number and a separate code. The approved rules required a code matching the official winning list.

Pepsi had announced the number on television. Many customers believed that was all the promise required.

The company blamed a computer problem for the flood of three-four-nine caps. The precise failure was not independently established.

For the customer, this wasn't a debate about software. They held a cap that appeared to tell them they'd won.

Pepsi offered holders of the disputed caps five hundred pesos each, rather than the million-peso top prize.

Hundreds of thousands accepted the offer. Others joined protests, boycotts and lawsuits.

Many demonstrations were peaceful. There were also attacks on Pepsi trucks and facilities.

In one bomb attack directed at a Pepsi truck, a teacher and a young child were killed.

Other people died in the violence. A prize promotion had become a national controversy with human costs.

Pepsi defended the distinction between the televised number and the list of valid security codes.

Claimants argued that consumers understood the promise differently, and that the number printed on their caps should count.

Years passed as the lawsuits moved through courts. Some earlier rulings favored claimants; others did not.

In cases decided by the Philippine Supreme Court in 2006, the wrong security code defeated claims for the advertised jackpot.

That settled the legal claims in those decisions. It did not make the confusion or the violence disappear.

The television number had been real. The bottle caps had been real. But the promise people thought they heard was not what courts enforced.

Under the rules, winning required both the number and a matching code. On television, viewers heard the number.

That gap between a public announcement and the contest's fine detail carried the dispute into streets and courtrooms.

The smallest thing in the story was a code beneath a cap. Its consequences were anything but small.
