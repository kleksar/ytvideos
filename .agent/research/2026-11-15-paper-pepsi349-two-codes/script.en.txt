How could a bottle cap carrying the winning number leave its owner without the prize? Why were there hundreds of thousands of caps marked 349? And what did the courts finally decide about the promise Pepsi made on television?

The Philippines, May 1992. Families watched for the next number in Pepsi's Number Fever promotion. A cap might win a small cash prize, or much more.

The evening announcement gave three digits: three, four, nine. Across the country, people began checking the numbers hidden beneath their bottle caps.

Victoria Angelo, one of the people who later spoke to a newspaper, spread her collection of caps on a table. The prospect of a win had become a plan for her family's future.

But a number wasn't the only thing printed under the metal. There was also a security code. For many of the people holding 349, that second code was about to change everything.

In early 1992, Pepsi had launched Number Fever to sell soft drinks. A buyer could turn over the cap and compare its three-digit number with the winner announced in the media.

The top advertised prize could reach one million pesos. The promotion became popular enough that Pepsi extended it for another five weeks.

The winning combinations were chosen in advance, with corresponding security codes. The approved list was held under official controls so the company could verify claims.

On May twenty-fifth, Pepsi announced 349 as a winning number for the following day. People opened drawers, collected saved caps and went looking for three-four-nine.

The problem emerged almost immediately. Many caps displayed 349 and even large printed prize amounts, but their additional codes did not match the authorised list.

Contemporary reporting estimated that around eight hundred thousand ordinary caps had been printed with those same three digits. That was a count of caps, not eight hundred thousand proven prize winners.

For somebody holding the right three digits, the problem was hard to accept. The television had announced 349; now the company was saying that the number alone did not qualify.

The rules, however, described more than a three-digit match. A valid winning cap also needed the corresponding security code from the approved list.

In one later lawsuit, a claimant presented several 349 caps, including caps printed with a million-peso prize. The court record shows they carried a code the official list did not recognise.

Pepsi initially withdrew the announcement and substituted another number. It then publicised which security codes actually matched 349.

Those codes were kept separately from the televised three-digit announcement. The distinction was written into the rules, but many people had understood the promise differently.

Pepsi offered five hundred pesos for disputed 349 caps as a goodwill payment, rather than paying their printed million-peso amounts.

According to a later Philippine Supreme Court ruling, 486,170 holders accepted the offer. It cost Pepsi hundreds of millions of pesos.

Others refused. Some organised as the Ugnayan 349 association and went to court, arguing that the advertised number should entitle them to the prizes printed on their caps.

Meanwhile, public anger grew. There were demonstrations, boycotts and attacks on company vehicles. Peaceful protests and violent assaults were different events, and not every cap holder joined them.

In February 1993, someone threw an explosive at a Pepsi truck near Manila. It bounced away and killed a schoolteacher and a five-year-old girl standing nearby.

Neither victim had anything to do with deciding the promotion's rules. A dispute over small pieces of metal had now claimed lives.

The legal argument continued for years. Was the television announcement enough to create a right to payment, or did the printed security code remain a necessary condition?

The courts examined promotional posters, official lists, and the specific codes on individual caps. Some earlier decisions offered limited compensation; other claims were rejected.

In October 2006, the Philippine Supreme Court decided that the caps in the case before it did not qualify for the million-peso prize. Their security codes were not on the winning list.

The court did not find that three televised digits erased the code requirement approved for the promotion. That was the legal answer to the central dispute in that case.

For viewers, the answer still carries an uncomfortable distinction. People could hear the correct number on television and hold a cap with that number, yet have no valid winning claim.

Pepsi could explain the code. It could not undo the expectations raised by the announcement, the years of court battles, or the deaths during the violence.

Number Fever had asked people to trust three digits. When 349 appeared, the prize they thought those digits promised depended on a separate code that most viewers had never checked.
