How can a bottle cap show the winning number and still be worth almost nothing? Why did Pepsi offer five hundred pesos instead of a million? And who eventually settled the argument?

In 1992, families across the Philippines checked the numbers printed inside Pepsi bottle caps. Number Fever promised cash prizes, some as large as one million pesos.

On May twenty-fifth, the company announced the next winning number: three, four, nine. People rushed to claim prizes. Many caps carried those digits. Some even had a million-peso amount printed beside them.

Then Pepsi said those caps were not winners.

The missing detail was a second marking: a security code. Before the announcement, winning numbers had been paired with approved codes on a list kept under government supervision. The codes on many 349 caps were not there.

The number really had been announced. That made the refusal far harder to accept. Pepsi withdrew the announcement, later published the eligible codes, and offered five hundred pesos for disputed caps.

According to a later Supreme Court ruling, 486,170 people took that offer. Others went to court. Anger also spread beyond the claimants: during an attack on a Pepsi truck in 1993, an explosive killed a schoolteacher and a five-year-old girl nearby.

One group of claimants won a large award on appeal. In October 2006, the Philippine Supreme Court overturned it: the particular caps in that case lacked approved security codes. Other related cases reached the same legal distinction.

The television number was real. But under the rules upheld in those cases, three matching digits did not establish a winning claim without the correct code.
