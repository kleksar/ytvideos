Why did a Pepsi cap marked with a winning number fail to win the prize printed on it? And why did that dispute reach the Supreme Court?

In 1992, Victoria Angelo and her husband were raising five children near Manila. A contemporary report described how they collected bottle caps, hoping for a proper home and education for their children. Pepsi's Number Fever contest offered prizes of up to one million pesos.

On May twenty-fifth, television announced the next winning number: three, four, nine. Victoria believed her family's luck had changed. Across the Philippines, other people searched their saved caps and found the same digits.

Then Pepsi refused to pay many of them.

The number 349 really was a winner. But a cap also needed a security code from an approved list kept under government supervision. Many caps carrying 349—even some printed with a million-peso amount—had codes missing from that list.

Pepsi withdrew the announcement, then published the eligible codes. It offered five hundred pesos for disputed caps instead of their printed prizes. A later Supreme Court decision records that 486,170 people accepted. Others sued. The official rules required two matching markings; many viewers had understood the televised three-digit announcement as a complete promise.

Most protests were peaceful. Yet in 1993 an explosive thrown at a Pepsi truck killed a schoolteacher and a five-year-old girl nearby. Neither had designed the contest.

Years later, claimants in one lawsuit won an award on appeal. In October 2006, the Philippine Supreme Court reversed it: the specific caps before the court lacked approved codes. It did not rule on every cap in the country.

Victoria had hoped the number on her cap would change her family's future. The court's narrower answer was that 349 alone was insufficient under the approved rules. The second code determined whether a particular cap qualified.
