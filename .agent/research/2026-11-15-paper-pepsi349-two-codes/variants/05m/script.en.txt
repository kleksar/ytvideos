How could a bottle cap carrying the winning number leave its owner without the prize? What separated a winning 349 cap from an ordinary one? And what did the courts finally decide about the prize claims?

The Philippines, May 1992. Families watched for the next number in Pepsi's Number Fever promotion. A cap might win a small cash prize, or much more.

The evening announcement gave three digits: three, four, nine. Across the country, people began checking the numbers hidden beneath their bottle caps.

Victoria Angelo, who later spoke to a newspaper, had been collecting caps with her family. She hoped the winnings would pay for her five children's education and a proper home.

But a number wasn't the only thing printed under the metal. There was also a security code. For many of the people holding 349, that second code was about to change everything.

In early 1992, Pepsi had launched Number Fever to sell soft drinks. A buyer could turn over the cap and compare its three-digit number with the winner announced in the media.

The top advertised prize could reach one million pesos. The promotion became popular enough that Pepsi extended it for another five weeks.

The winning combinations were chosen in advance, with corresponding security codes. The approved list was held under official controls so the company could verify claims.

On May twenty-fifth, Pepsi announced 349 as the winning number for the following day. People arrived at redemption stations with matching caps.

The problem emerged almost immediately. Many caps displayed 349 and even large printed prize amounts, but their additional codes did not match the authorised list.

Contemporary reporting estimated that around eight hundred thousand ordinary caps had been printed with those same three digits. That was a count of caps, not eight hundred thousand proven prize winners.

For somebody holding the right three digits, the refusal was difficult to accept. The number had come from Pepsi's own announcement.

But the judges would later examine the additional code printed alongside the number. It had to match the approved list.

Pepe Pagdanganan, one of the eventual claimants, had seven 349 caps. Six displayed a million pesos apiece, and the seventh displayed a hundred thousand. The code on those seven caps was absent from the winning list.

Pepsi initially withdrew the announcement and substituted another number. On May twenty-eighth it published the approved codes for 349.

The new table listed prizes from one hundred to fifty thousand pesos. There was no million-peso entry for 349. A customer could hold a genuine cap printed with a million and still have no winning code.

Pepsi offered five hundred pesos for each disputed 349 cap presented by June twelfth. Customers who wanted the larger prizes had to decide whether to take that offer or pursue their claims.

According to a later Philippine Supreme Court ruling, 486,170 holders accepted the offer. It cost Pepsi hundreds of millions of pesos.

Others refused. Some organised as the Ugnayan 349 association and went to court, arguing that the advertised number should entitle them to the prizes printed on their caps.

Meanwhile, public anger grew. There were demonstrations, boycotts and attacks on company property. The people who demonstrated were not necessarily the people who committed violent acts.

During the unrest, a schoolteacher and a five-year-old girl were killed in an attack connected to the dispute. Both were bystanders.

The company now faced demands for payment in court as well as protests.

The legal argument continued for years. Was the television announcement enough to create a right to payment, or did the printed security code remain a necessary condition?

The courts examined promotional posters, official lists and the codes on the disputed caps. Pagdanganan and another claimant, Pepito Lumahan, initially received small awards from a trial court. On appeal, they were awarded millions.

In October 2006, the Philippine Supreme Court overturned that award. Their caps had no approved security code. The Court also cancelled the smaller awards, because the company's voluntary offer had expired.

For those two men, the final decision meant they received neither the printed prizes nor the smaller payments. The ruling concerned their particular claims.

Victoria's own legal outcome is not established by these records. But the answer to the puzzle of 349 was now clear: the televised number could be genuine while the code on a particular cap was not a winning one.
