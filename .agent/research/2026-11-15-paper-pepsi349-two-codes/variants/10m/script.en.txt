How could the number on a bottle cap match a prize announcement on television and still be worth nothing? And why did two men who won millions in a Philippine appeals court eventually lose even the few thousand pesos that Pepsi had once offered them?

In early 1992, Victoria Angelo and her husband Juanito lived near Manila with their five children. He took passengers around in a pedal-powered taxi. The family started buying Pepsi with meals and waiting for the evening results of a contest called Number Fever. A top prize of one million pesos was far beyond their ordinary earnings.

On May twenty-fifth, the number announced for the next day was three, four, nine. Victoria had caps with those digits. She later told a reporter that she had promised her children they could finish their education and the family could finally buy a proper house. All they seemed to need now was to collect the money.

But at redemption stations across the country, Pepsi rejected many of the 349 caps. Some bore the number and a million-peso prize, both printed by the company. Pepsi said they were not winners. To see how that could happen, we have to go back to the people who had arranged the contest.

Pepsi had run Number Fever in Latin America before introducing it in the Philippines. Its local bottler asked the Department of Trade and Industry for permission in January 1992. A Mexican consulting company selected sixty winning numbers and their matching codes from a pool of one thousand possible numbers. Pepsi sold several participating drinks, including 7-Up and Mirinda.

The winning list was kept at a bank. Opening the safe-deposit box required two keys: the department kept one and Pepsi kept the other. This arrangement was meant to prevent either side from changing the results on its own. When the contest was extended for five weeks, a second list went into another box under the same arrangement.

For customers, the daily routine was simpler. They listened for the three-digit number, checked their caps and took a match to a redemption station. Staff checked the cap before paying. The code was part of the promotion, but the number was the piece of information customers got with every new announcement.

When 349 appeared, it turned out that the same digits had been printed on both winning and ordinary caps. A newspaper report the following year estimated that about eight hundred thousand ordinary caps bore 349. This was an estimate of the number of caps in circulation, not a count of legally valid claims.

Pepsi initially cancelled the number for that day and announced 134 instead. But people had already heard 349. The company and the department opened the box containing the extended contest's list, and on May twenty-eighth Pepsi published the valid codes for 349. There really were winning caps with that number.

The newly published table contained prizes of one hundred, one thousand, ten thousand and fifty thousand pesos. None of those entries was for a million. Now a person holding a genuine bottle cap with a million printed beside 349 could compare it with the table and discover that the code on the cap was missing.

Pepsi offered a separate payment of five hundred pesos for each disputed cap, provided it was presented by June twelfth. That left customers with a choice: take a fraction of the sum printed on their caps or continue to demand the advertised prize. A later court judgment recorded that 486,170 holders took the offer. It put the company's total expense at about 240 million pesos.

Two customers who refused were Pepe Pagdanganan and Pepito Lumahan. Pagdanganan had seven caps from bottles of 7-Up and Mirinda. Six displayed a million pesos apiece and the last displayed one hundred thousand. Lumahan had two caps worth a printed total of 1.1 million. The caps in their case carried a code that the published table did not recognise.

On July ninth, after the five-hundred-peso offer had expired, Pagdanganan demanded the printed prizes. Pepsi refused. He and Lumahan filed a joint lawsuit in December. Another group, whose members had joined an association of 349 claimants, had filed its own complaint a month earlier after its written demands went unanswered.

By then the dispute extended far beyond those two court files. There were demonstrations, boycotts and attacks on company property. In February 1993, a teacher and a five-year-old girl were killed in an attack connected to the unrest. They were bystanders. Their deaths cannot be treated as the actions or responsibility of everyone who took part in a protest.

By July of that year, Pepsi was reporting hundreds of civil suits and thousands of fraud complaints. Those were allegations, not verdicts. For people who believed the TV announcement had promised them money, the courts offered another chance. But the judges would have to decide what weight to give the separate code.

In 2000, the trial court hearing Pagdanganan and Lumahan's case refused to award the printed prizes. It did, however, order payments of 3,500 pesos to Pagdanganan and one thousand to Lumahan. Those figures came from multiplying their seven and two caps by the original five-hundred-peso offer. They appealed.

In the meantime, the association's lawsuit had produced a different kind of result. The trial judge also found that the caps did not qualify for the prizes, but awarded compensation for moral harm. In 2001, an appeals court increased that compensation from ten thousand to thirty thousand pesos per person and added legal fees. Even without a winning cap, the claimants appeared to have gained something.

Then, in February 2004, the appeals court in Pagdanganan and Lumahan's case went further. It treated the code as a condition improperly added to the advertised terms and ordered Pepsi to pay the two men five million and 1.2 million pesos, respectively. These were the sums in the judgment, not a fresh count of the numbers printed on their caps. The two men now had a multimillion-peso court award.

But before that victory, other holders of 349 caps had already taken their cases all the way to the Supreme Court. Their arguments had been rejected. In one decision in January 2004, the Court had even removed a lower court's award of five hundred pesos because the company's voluntary offer had expired.

Pepsi relied on those earlier rulings when it challenged the new award. Pagdanganan and Lumahan argued that their claim was based on a breach of contract and differed from the previous cases. The Supreme Court considered that the crucial facts and the disputed condition were substantially the same. It would apply the rule it had already established.

In October 2006, the Supreme Court overturned the multimillion-peso award. It restored the dismissal of the original claim. Then it went one step further: it also cancelled the 3,500 and one thousand pesos that the trial court had awarded. The offer had long expired. In that lawsuit, neither man was entitled to the prize or the goodwill payment.

In June 2007, the association's claimants also lost their damages awards when the Supreme Court applied the earlier rulings. Their case was separate from the two men's claim. The result concerned the particular caps and claims that reached those courts, not the legal status of every cap ever printed with 349.

The surviving records do not establish Victoria Angelo's individual legal outcome. But we know what she hoped to buy when the number was announced. And we know what happened to the two men who rejected the smaller payment and took their claim through the courts: after almost fourteen years of litigation, their seven and two caps brought them nothing under the final judgment.
