How could Pepsi announce a winning number and then refuse to pay people holding it? Why were so many caps printed with the same digits? And how could some claimants win millions in one courtroom, only to lose the case years later?

In May 1992, a number appeared on Philippine television: three, four, nine. For people who had been saving bottle caps, it was the sort of announcement they had waited for. Many found 349 inside their caps. Some caps also carried printed amounts of one million pesos.

A later newspaper report described Victoria Angelo spreading her collection of caps across a table and thinking about what a win could mean for her family. She was one claimant among many. The announcement had turned an ordinary piece of packaging into something people wanted to redeem.

The contemporary account described Angelo as a mother of five. Her husband, Juanito, earned a modest living taking passengers around in a three-wheeled cab. The family had begun buying Pepsi often and watching the evening results. She spoke of education for her children and of having a proper home. Those are the wishes recorded by a journalist in 1993, not thoughts we can invent for every family. Nor did their hopes make the disputed cap automatically valid. But they explain why people took an ordinary television announcement so seriously.

At a collection point, however, a matching number did not guarantee payment. Pepsi began rejecting caps that carried the announced digits. To understand why, we have to look at a second line of printing that television viewers had not been asked to remember.

Earlier that year, the company had introduced Number Fever, a promotion for Pepsi and other soft drinks it bottled in the Philippines. Buyers checked a three-digit number beneath a cap and compared it with the number announced in the media. The amounts printed on different prize caps ranged from smaller sums to a million pesos.

Pepsi's local operation had sought approval from the Department of Trade and Industry in January. A consulting firm selected the winning combinations in advance, together with matching security codes. During the original promotion, sixty winning numbers were chosen from a thousand possible three-digit combinations.

The organisers tried to protect that list from manipulation. The official numbers and codes were deposited in a bank safe-deposit box. One key belonged to the government department, the other to Pepsi. Neither party was supposed to control the list alone.

When the promotion succeeded, Pepsi extended it by five weeks. A second set of twenty-five numbers and their security codes was selected. That extended list, too, went into a locked box. The people watching the daily announcements mainly heard a number. The verification system behind the counter was more complicated.

On May twenty-fifth, Pepsi announced 349 as the winning number for the following day. This was not simply a false number accidentally read on air. The court records confirm that 349 belonged to the authorised selection. The problem was that enormous numbers of caps bearing 349 also had codes that were not on the approved list.

People arrived at redemption stations with caps they believed entitled them to the printed prizes. Some disputed caps carried the code L-2560-FQ; another disputed code was L-3560-FQ. For many claimants, those tiny letters were not the part of the promotion they remembered. They had watched the number being announced.

In one later case, Pepe Pagdanganan produced seven 349 caps: six with a million-peso amount and one marked one hundred thousand pesos. All seven carried a code that the authorised winning list did not include. He was asking Pepsi to honour the amounts physically printed beneath the metal.

Pepsi, meanwhile, had its own problem. The rules and the controlled list distinguished winning from non-winning codes, but the public announcement had made the three digits seem decisive. A contemporary report estimated roughly eight hundred thousand ordinary caps with 349. That was an estimate of printed caps, not a verified count of people owed prizes.

The company first withdrew 349 and substituted another announced number. On May twenty-eighth it published the list of security codes that actually matched winning 349 caps. The distinction was unusually striking: the official table included smaller approved prizes, while some disputed caps visibly carried a million-peso amount. A cap could display the announced number and a large printed prize yet fail both tests of eligibility.

The official table reproduced in a later Supreme Court decision made the discrepancy clearer. It listed approved 349 codes for prizes of fifty thousand, ten thousand, one thousand and one hundred pesos. It did not turn every 349 cap with a printed million into a million-peso winner. For a customer who had been told to listen for the winning number, this second verification step was crucial. For Pepsi and the government department overseeing the promotion, it was the test that distinguished one cap from another. Both groups were looking at the same three digits, but they were not using the same test.

The public dispute did not end with the new table. Pepsi offered five hundred pesos for each disputed 349 cap as a goodwill payment. This was not an admission that the printed million-peso amount was payable. It was a separate offer to people whose caps would not be honoured as winners.

A later Supreme Court judgment recorded that 486,170 holders accepted. It put the total cost at about 240 million pesos. Yet those who refused were not all making the same calculation. Some believed the television announcement should bind the company. Some joined a claimants' association, Ugnayan 349, and sent demands before filing lawsuits.

The case had also left the counter and entered the streets. There were protests and boycotts. There were attacks on company vehicles, too. It matters to keep those events separate: holding a disputed cap or joining a peaceful demonstration did not make someone responsible for violence.

A July 1993 report recorded the scale of litigation using figures attributed to Pepsi: at least twenty-two thousand people were behind hundreds of civil lawsuits, and thousands of criminal complaints alleging fraud had also been filed. Those numbers describe claims made at that moment, not proven winners or convictions. The dispute had multiplied into different courts with different questions. Some people sought the prize amounts printed under the caps; others sought compensation for the way the promotion had unfolded. That difference would matter when lower courts tried to decide what, if anything, Pepsi still owed.

In February 1993, an explosive thrown toward a Pepsi truck near Manila bounced away and killed a schoolteacher and a five-year-old girl standing nearby. They were bystanders, not people who had drafted the promotion or decided which codes counted. The identity and motives of the attacker are not established by the records used for this script.

While that damage could not be reversed in court, the prize claims kept moving through the legal system. One group sued in November 1992. In 1996, a trial court found their caps were not winning caps but still awarded each plaintiff ten thousand pesos for moral damages. An appellate court later increased those damages to thirty thousand per person and added attorney's fees.

A separate case took another route. Pagdanganan and another claimant filed suit in December 1992. In 2000, the trial court rejected their demand for the printed prizes and awarded only the smaller goodwill amounts. In 2004, however, an appellate court reversed the result and ordered Pepsi to pay five million pesos to one claimant and 1.2 million to the other.

For a time, then, two versions of the same dispute existed in legal decisions. One said the correct security code controlled the award. Another treated the television number as sufficient, arguing that a code requirement had been improperly added to the advertised terms. That was no longer a simple question about a misprinted cap. It was about which promise the law would enforce.

The 2004 appellate reasoning mattered because it treated the security code as an additional condition that had not been clearly expressed in the advertised rules. The Supreme Court approached the problem differently: other final rulings involving the same unauthorised codes and the same promotional terms had already established the rule. Lawyers call the duty to follow those controlling decisions precedent. What looked like a new victory in one court could therefore disappear when a higher court applied the settled rule to another claimant. This is why the televised announcement and the court's final answer cannot be collapsed into a single headline.

In October 2006, the Philippine Supreme Court reversed the multimillion-peso appellate award. It held that the claimants' particular codes did not appear on the winning list and that earlier Supreme Court rulings on similar 349 claims governed the case. A number alone was not enough under the terms it applied.

In June 2007, the Court resolved the other appeal as well. It set aside the moral-damages awards to the association claimants, again relying on final decisions about 349 caps with unauthorised codes. These were decisions about particular claims and legal records, not proof that every person's experience or understanding had been identical.

So the three questions now have answers. The publicly announced number really was 349. Many ordinary caps had the digits but not the corresponding security code. Courts in these cases ultimately enforced the code requirement, even after lower courts had sometimes awarded substantial sums.

Pepsi could publish the correct codes and make goodwill payments; neither action could restore the lives lost in an attack or give everyone the same understanding of what they had heard. The number on television was only one part of the promised prize. The second part had been printed much smaller.
