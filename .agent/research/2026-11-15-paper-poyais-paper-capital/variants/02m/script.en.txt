How did a printed map persuade families to cross the Atlantic for a city that wasn't there? And what happened to the man who sold them the journey?

In the early 1820s, a Scottish former soldier named Gregor MacGregor promoted Poyais, supposedly a thriving new country on Central America's Caribbean coast.

There was a real stretch of land. A Miskito ruler had granted MacGregor rights there. But the capital, St Joseph, and the developed state described to British buyers were not waiting for settlers.

MacGregor's promoters produced maps, land certificates and a guide describing fertile farms and a bustling town. Investors also bought bonds. Some emigrants exchanged their pounds for Poyais currency.

In 1822 and 1823, two ships brought roughly 250 people to the coast. They found no promised capital or facilities. They tried to build shelters, but illness, exposure and food shortages became deadly.

A vessel from British Honduras helped evacuate survivors in May 1823. The rescue came too late for many; people continued dying after reaching Belize. At least 180 of those first settlers died.

You might expect MacGregor's career to end there. Yet some survivors publicly defended him. He promoted Poyais again in France, was arrested and stood trial. In 1826 a French court acquitted him. He later offered Poyais securities in Britain again.

The coast had never been imaginary. The functioning country sold on paper was. And the people who crossed an ocean expecting a home paid the price.
