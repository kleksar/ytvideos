How can a map show a capital city, a bank and a government — yet the families who sail there find none of them? What made those papers believable? And after people died, why could the man behind them still sell Poyais?

In Britain in the early 1820s, Gregor MacGregor offered two ways to join a new country on the Caribbean coast of Central America. Investors could buy its bonds. Families looking for a new home could buy land and sail there.

MacGregor was a Scottish former soldier who really had fought in South America. He styled himself the Cazique, or ruler, of Poyais. And there was a genuine piece of paper behind the claim: in 1820, a Miskito ruler had granted him rights to land along that coast.

The land was real and inhabited. The grant did not establish the prosperous independent state MacGregor advertised. But buyers in London couldn't inspect that coast. They could inspect a printed guide, maps marked with a capital called St Joseph, land certificates, and even Poyais currency.

Those documents made a distant place feel ready for ordinary life. People paid for plots. Some exchanged pounds before leaving. Others bought bonds supposedly backed by the revenues of a functioning government.

One of the families was Ann Bagster's. She later told a British court that her husband, a mason, had paid eight pounds for forty acres. Ann travelled with him and their six children. They had committed far more than the price of a certificate.

The first ship left London in 1822. Another followed with more emigrants in early 1823. Roughly 250 people travelled on those two voyages. MacGregor was not among them.

When the passengers reached the Black River coast, the capital on their maps was nowhere to be found. No town was prepared to receive them. The families had crossed an ocean expecting to move into a settlement; now they had to make shelter themselves.

Ann later said that when her family arrived in March, there was no town and no roof waiting for them. Local Indigenous people helped the settlers obtain food and build huts at first, but that assistance could not replace the promised infrastructure. The number of people needing supplies grew as sickness spread.

A rescue schooner from British Honduras reached the settlement in May 1823. Evacuation brought survivors to Belize. It did not mean the danger was over. Many arrived already ill, and more died there.

Ann lost her husband and two children after they reached Belize. In a reported court hearing in Britain in January 1824, she described those deaths and said she was still suffering from fever. This was testimony about what happened to her family, not a scene invented to make the story dramatic.

Across the first two voyages, at least 180 of roughly 250 emigrants died. A later ship found the earlier camp abandoned and went to Belize instead; its passengers were not part of that first death toll.

News of the disaster reached Britain. The Miskito ruler publicly repudiated MacGregor's land concession in 1823. Yet not every survivor blamed MacGregor. Some defended him in signed statements and blamed other organisers. Those statements exist; their authors' private reasons remain uncertain.

MacGregor went on promoting Poyais in France. French authorities arrested him in 1825. In 1826 he was tried and acquitted. That decision did not establish that the promised capital had existed, and he later offered Poyais-related securities again in Britain.

So the answer was never that someone had invented an entire coastline. The coastline, its people and an initial land grant were real. What the printed documents sold was a functioning country ready to receive families.

Ann Bagster could return to Britain and tell a court what she had found. Her husband and two of her children could not. The maps had offered a city; the ships had delivered them to a shore without it.
