What happened when emigrants arrived on a Caribbean coast and couldn't find the capital printed on their maps? How had they been persuaded to buy land in a country with its own money? And why did the seller continue after so many people died there?

In Britain in the early 1820s, those answers were still in the future. Gregor MacGregor, a Scottish soldier with genuine experience in South America, was offering people a chance to invest or move abroad.

He claimed to rule a country called Poyais. Its attractive promises appeared on maps and official-looking papers. Some families believed the documents enough to cross an ocean.

Then ships reached the advertised shore. The passengers looked for the town and the officials who were meant to welcome them. They found neither.

Back in Britain, he called himself the Cazique, or ruler, of a new state called Poyais.

He had obtained a grant of land from a Miskito ruler on Central America's Caribbean coast. The coastline was real. That concession did not establish the independent government or developed city described to buyers.

But the thriving country he described to customers, complete with a prosperous capital, did not.

In London, though, there was plenty to examine before handing over your savings.

MacGregor's promoters had maps, formal-looking papers, and symbols of their supposed nation.

A printed guide promised fertile land, valuable resources, and a capital called St Joseph.

It described public buildings and the comforts of a settled town. These were promises on paper.

Families weren't just buying an investment. Some planned to start their lives there.

Land certificates went on sale. MacGregor also raised money by issuing bonds in the name of Poyais.

British buyers paid real money for property they had never seen.

Some emigrants even exchanged their pounds for Poyais currency before they sailed.

One ship left London late in 1822. Another followed from Scotland early the next year.

Roughly 250 emigrants took those voyages. MacGregor himself did not sail with them.

When the passengers finally reached the Mosquito Coast, the capital they had been shown on maps was missing.

There was no developed port, bustling market, or government ready to welcome them.

They looked for the town. Then the settlers tried to make shelter for themselves.

They had prepared to move into a settlement, not to create one on an unfamiliar coast.

Food, exposure, and disease soon became deadly problems. The paperwork offered no protection.

In May 1823, a vessel from British Honduras found the colonists and helped take survivors to Belize.

Rescue did not end the suffering. Many were already ill, and deaths continued after the evacuation.

Of the roughly 250 who had sailed on those first two ships, at least 180 died.

The survivors could describe exactly what they had found where the printed map showed a city. A later ship with more emigrants reached an abandoned camp and diverted to Belize. Those passengers must not be added to the death count from the first two voyages.

Yet several of them publicly defended MacGregor, blaming other people involved in the expedition.

Their words are documented. Their private reasons for saying them are not something we can know.

MacGregor had gone to Paris. There, he set about promoting Poyais to new investors.

French officials became suspicious of the supposed state, and MacGregor was arrested in 1825.

He stood trial the following year. The court acquitted him.

The verdict didn't create the missing city. Nor did it stop the Poyais scheme.

Back in Britain, MacGregor continued promoting Poyais bonds and land claims.

Years later he returned to Venezuela, where his earlier military service was officially recognised.

He received a military pension and died there in 1845, without a conviction for Poyais.

So what did those families really buy? The land and the Caribbean coast were real.

The established government, the busy capital, and the new life promised by the documents were not waiting for them.

On the map, there was a city. When the ships arrived, there was only a shore.
