How could documents persuade families to sail for a city that wasn't there? Why did a later ship reach an abandoned camp? And would a court convict the man selling those promises, or could he offer them again?

To answer those questions, we have to follow two journeys: the one made by the documents in Britain and the one made by real passengers across the Atlantic.

In the early 1820s, Britain was buying bonds issued by newly independent states in Latin America. Investors hoped that distant countries would offer returns they could not get at home. Across the ocean, the old Spanish colonial order was changing fast. A new name on a bond certificate did not automatically sound absurd.

Gregor MacGregor understood that market. He was a Scottish soldier with genuine military experience in South America. He came back to Britain claiming to rule a place called Poyais, on the Caribbean shore of Central America. He used the title Cazique, presenting himself as the head of a state rather than simply a man selling land.

Here is the detail that makes this story more complicated than an invented island on a map. In 1820, George Frederic Augustus, a Miskito ruler, really did grant MacGregor land rights on the coast. The territory and its inhabitants existed. But a land concession was not proof that MacGregor possessed the sovereignty, government, prosperous towns and public services he later advertised. Historical accounts also differ over how to describe his motives. We don't have to settle what he privately believed to see the gap between the sales pitch and the conditions on the ground.

In Britain, MacGregor's supporters made that gap difficult to notice. Maps gave Poyais borders and place names. A printed guide, issued under the name Thomas Strangeways, described the landscape and a capital called St Joseph. Land certificates turned plots on a distant coast into things a buyer could hold. The words and drawings looked much more solid than a promise made across a table.

For a buyer, there were two quite different offers. You could purchase land and hope to build a new life. Or you could buy debt issued in the name of the Poyais government. The second depended on the same assumption as the first: that a functioning government and economy existed to support the promises.

In October 1822, a Poyais loan was marketed in London. Its nominal value was £200,000. The securities promised interest backed by the supposed government's future revenues. These are financial terms, not evidence that the money or interest was ever fully paid. A surviving bond certificate dated 1823 shows just how tangible the paper side of the operation became.

Meanwhile, the sales campaign reached people who wanted more than an investment. Some were prepared to leave Britain and settle on the new coast. The guide spoke of fertile land and a developed capital; land documents made the plan feel administrative, almost routine. Some emigrants exchanged British pounds for Poyais currency before departure. Their money was real, but the economy described to them was not ready to receive them.

The first ship, the Honduras Packet, left London in September 1822 and reached the Black River coast that November. Its passengers searched for the town they expected. They found no functioning capital, no organised reception, and none of the infrastructure that the promotional material had led them to anticipate.

What should people do when they've crossed an ocean but the settlement they're meant to join cannot be found? They could not simply walk back to London. The emigrants made camp and tried to organise themselves. And the absence of a town did not immediately tell them whether a delayed official, a wrong landing point, or the entire story was to blame.

A second ship, the Kennersley Castle, arrived in early 1823 with more emigrants. Together the first two voyages brought roughly 250 people. More passengers meant more people needing food, shelter and medical help. But the basic problem had not changed: no established settlement was ready for them.

On the coast, this was no longer a dispute about a bond's price. Illness and exposure were affecting people living in improvised conditions. A real coastal region had been portrayed as a completed society that could receive families arriving from overseas. That false promise changed what they packed, what help they expected and what they needed to survive.

In May 1823, a vessel from British Honduras encountered the settlers. Officials urged evacuation, and the survivors were brought toward Belize. Some had already died. Others were too sick to travel easily. Evacuation was not a clean final scene in which everyone stepped ashore and recovered. More deaths followed after the voyage to Belize.

Of the roughly 250 people who came on those first two ships, at least 180 died, according to the historical accounts used here. The figure is a minimum, not an exact count of deaths at one place or on one day. Fewer than fifty ultimately made it back to Britain.

But here is a second twist: the rescue did not immediately stop every ship. A later vessel, the Skene, carried additional emigrants toward the same supposed country. Its captain discovered that the earlier encampment had been abandoned and went instead to Belize. These later passengers should not be silently added to the death count for the first two voyages. The distinction matters because different ships reached different outcomes.

By then, reports were moving in the opposite direction across the Atlantic. British papers began reporting the failure of the settlement. In September 1823, a public statement by the Miskito ruler said the land grant to MacGregor had been cancelled. That was a political act about the actual territory, not proof that the promised city had ever stood there.

The consequences reached investors too. Historical research documents steep losses in the trading prices of Poyais securities during 1823. People who had bought the paper as a financial opportunity now had to reckon with reports from a place where families had struggled to stay alive.

You might think the survivors' accounts would settle everyone's opinion of the seller. Yet some survivors publicly defended MacGregor and blamed people responsible for the expedition instead. Their statements are part of the historical record. Why each person took that position is not something we can reliably reconstruct from a brief declaration. Nor does defending MacGregor make the advertised capital real.

MacGregor moved on to France. There he attempted to interest new buyers in Poyais. French authorities became suspicious. He was arrested in 1825, then tried in 1826. The court acquitted him.

That verdict is often made to sound like a final verdict on the whole country. It wasn't. A court acquittal does not build the missing town, refund the families or prove the advertising accurate. It tells us that the prosecution did not obtain a conviction against MacGregor in that proceeding. He went on promoting Poyais-related claims and bonds.

And the story doesn't end with a prison door because there wasn't one for MacGregor in this case. He eventually returned to Venezuela, where his earlier military service was officially recognised. He received a pension and died there in 1845.

The most important distinction is also the hardest to capture in a headline. Poyais was not an empty spot invented without reference to any people or land. The coastal territory was real and already inhabited. What buyers were promised was a capital, government and working society ready for their arrival. Those were the parts that failed the test when ships came.

The printed map could turn a distant claim into a city with a name. The loan could turn that city into a marketable investment. And a land certificate could persuade a family to leave home. But when the passengers needed a roof, medicine and a functioning town, the documents could provide none of them.

That is why the ending belongs to the emigrants rather than to the cleverness of the scheme. They arrived expecting to start a new life. Many never got the chance. And after the reports, rescue ships and trial, the man who sold the promise could still offer it again.
