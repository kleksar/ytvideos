How did families end up sailing across the Atlantic for a town that wasn't there? Why did ships keep coming after the settlement failed? And what happened when the man who sold those promises took a newspaper to court?

In the early 1820s, Britain was buying bonds issued by newly independent states in Latin America. Investors hoped that distant countries would offer returns they could not get at home. Across the ocean, the old Spanish colonial order was changing fast. A new name on a bond certificate did not automatically sound absurd.

Gregor MacGregor understood that market. He was a Scottish soldier with genuine military experience in South America. He came back to Britain claiming to rule a place called Poyais, on the Caribbean shore of Central America. He used the title Cazique, presenting himself as the head of a state rather than simply a man selling land.

In 1820, George Frederic Augustus, a Miskito ruler, had granted MacGregor land rights on the coast. So there really was land, and people already lived there. But the concession did not establish the independent government, capital and public services that MacGregor advertised. Historians disagree about his intentions; the conditions the emigrants encountered are a separate question.

In Britain, MacGregor's supporters made that gap difficult to notice. Maps gave Poyais borders and place names. A printed guide, issued under the name Thomas Strangeways, described the landscape and a capital called St Joseph. Land certificates turned plots on a distant coast into things a buyer could hold. The words and drawings looked much more solid than a promise made across a table.

In October 1822, a Poyais loan was marketed in London. Its nominal value was £200,000. The securities promised interest backed by the supposed government's future revenues. These are financial terms, not evidence that the money or interest was ever fully paid. A surviving bond certificate dated 1823 shows just how tangible the paper side of the operation became.

A family did not have to understand the bond market to take part in Poyais. Ann Bagster ran a shop while her husband worked as a mason. He bought forty acres for eight pounds. According to her later court testimony, they took their six children across the Atlantic in January 1823. Some emigrants also exchanged their British money for Poyais currency before leaving.

The first ship, the Honduras Packet, left London in September 1822 and reached the Black River coast that November. Its passengers searched for the town they expected. They found no functioning capital, no organised reception, and none of the infrastructure that the promotional material had led them to anticipate.

The passengers had to make camp. Carib and Paya people initially supplied food and helped build huts, according to historical research. Their help mattered, but there was no prepared town with the services that had been advertised in Britain.

The Kennersley Castle brought another group of emigrants in early 1823. Ann's account places her family's arrival in March. The two ships had brought roughly 250 people to a place without the facilities they expected. Ann later recalled that her family lay ill without anyone to bring them drinking water.

In May 1823, a vessel from British Honduras encountered the settlers. Officials urged evacuation, and the survivors were brought toward Belize. Some had already died. Others were too sick to travel easily. Evacuation was not a clean final scene in which everyone stepped ashore and recovered. More deaths followed after the voyage to Belize.

People in Belize brought food and helped Ann's family, as she later acknowledged. But her husband and two children died of fever within four weeks of their arrival there. Ann eventually returned to Britain aboard Captain Wilson's ship; she was still suffering from fever when she gave evidence the following January.

Of the roughly 250 people who came on those first two ships, at least 180 died, according to the historical accounts used here. The figure is a minimum, not an exact count of deaths at one place or on one day. Fewer than fifty ultimately made it back to Britain.

The rescue did not immediately stop the voyages. A later ship carrying emigrants discovered that the earlier camp had been abandoned and went to Belize instead. Its passengers are not included in the estimate of deaths among the first two groups. One voyage ended at a deserted camp rather than another attempt to establish a settlement.

By then, reports were moving in the opposite direction across the Atlantic. British papers began reporting the failure of the settlement. In September 1823, a public statement by the Miskito ruler said the land grant to MacGregor had been cancelled. That was a political act about the actual territory, not proof that the promised city had ever stood there.

The consequences reached investors too. Historical research documents steep losses in the trading prices of Poyais securities during 1823. People who had bought the paper as a financial opportunity now had to reckon with reports from a place where families had struggled to stay alive.

Some survivors publicly defended MacGregor and blamed other organisers. Their statements survive, though we cannot know each signer's private reasons. There was another way for MacGregor to dispute the reports: he could take the publishers to court.

In January 1824, a London court heard his libel action against the owners of the Morning Herald. The paper had reported accusations that MacGregor had misled emigrants and taken their money. His claim sought two thousand pounds in damages. The newspaper's defence argued both that it had accurately reported a public proceeding and that the underlying allegations were true.

The newspaper called witnesses. One was Ann Bagster. The man who had sold her family a new home was now the plaintiff, and she was giving evidence for the defendants. Her account of buying the land, finding no town and losing three members of her family appeared in The Examiner two days after the hearing.

The judge distinguished the evidence of suffering from the specific allegation that MacGregor had fraudulently exchanged Poyais notes for money. The latter had not been established to his satisfaction. The jury found for the newspaper on the accuracy of its report, but found for MacGregor on the pleas asserting the truth of the charges. The damages awarded to him: one shilling. It was a mixed result in a civil libel case, not a criminal acquittal.

MacGregor continued promoting Poyais in France. He was arrested there in 1825 and tried in 1826. This time he faced a criminal proceeding, and he was acquitted. The earlier British ruling and the French verdict were two different legal events. Neither establishes that a functioning capital awaited the emigrants.

He subsequently offered Poyais-related securities in Britain again. Eventually he returned to Venezuela, where his earlier military service was recognised. He received a pension and died there in 1845.

A map and a bond could be sold in Britain; a land certificate could send a family across an ocean. Ann Bagster's testimony was another kind of document. It recorded what was left of the promise when her family actually reached the coast. Her family had paid for a place to live and she returned to Britain without her husband and two of her children.
