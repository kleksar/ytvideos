In a Texas cancer center, a radiation machine fired. The detector immediately went off the scale.

There was no patient in the room. A physicist was trying to reproduce an accident.

Two people had already received devastating doses from a machine its maker called safe.

After hours of failed attempts, the clue appeared: how fast the operator typed.

The machine was called Therac-25. Doctors used it to aim radiation at tumors.

It could deliver electrons directly, or produce X-rays using a different arrangement inside.

That distinction mattered. A high-energy beam required the right hardware in its path.

The operator chose the treatment on a computer terminal and checked what the screen displayed.

In March 1986, a man in Tyler was treated for a tumor on his back.

During treatment, he described a sensation like hot coffee being poured on his skin.

The machine displayed an obscure message: Malfunction 54. It did not explain the danger.

The patient had received far more radiation than intended. The consequences were devastating.

Engineers from the manufacturer inspected the device but could not reproduce an overdose.

Their answer was that the machine could not produce the exposure the clinic feared.

With no cause demonstrated, the equipment went back into service.

On April 11, another Tyler patient was injured during treatment for skin cancer.

He would die on May 1. The first patient also later died of his injuries.

Now medical physicist Fritz Hager and a technician investigated the controls themselves.

They tried repeating the treatment setup after hours, but the error kept disappearing.

The operator could change a treatment entry without starting the entire setup again.

One quick correction changed the selection on screen from X-rays to electrons.

But inside, the machine had not finished changing what the beam would strike.

The display said one thing, and the hardware was still in another state.

A very powerful beam could then fire without the arrangement needed for safe delivery.

The sequence was so timing-sensitive that slower typing could fail to reproduce it.

They ran the test again, with measuring equipment in place of a patient.

The instrument shot off its scale. It was recording a dose far beyond treatment levels.

A specialist from another cancer center helped confirm the result with different equipment.

One reconstructed exposure lasted roughly a tenth of a second. The dose was enormous.

Now the hospital had evidence the maker's earlier assurance could not explain away.

But one hospital knowing the trick would not protect patients elsewhere.

Hager began telephoning other clinics that used the same machine.

Physicists in several places followed the sequence and reported the same failure.

At one clinic, the result finally explained a patient injury they had already seen.

This was not simply an operator pressing the wrong button. A displayed correction could leave the hardware unsafe.

Older machines had physical safety devices that could interrupt an unsafe setup.

Therac-25 relied much more heavily on software to detect and prevent dangerous conditions.

Investigators later documented six massive overdoses from 1985 to early 1987.

Not all six came from the same bug. Other flaws could reach dangerous states too.

That is why fixing a single key or one timing error was not enough.

After further incidents, authorities pushed to stop routine use until stronger safeguards were installed.

Physical protections and software changes became part of the corrective work.

The people already injured could not be made whole by those repairs.

But Hager's repeatable test turned a disputed warning into proof others could check.

And it showed why a reassuring screen meant nothing if the machine beneath it disagreed.
