How could a radiation machine say a cancer treatment was safe when it had just hurt the patient? And why did it happen again?

In March 1986, a man arrived at a clinic in Tyler, Texas, for treatment with a Therac-25. The operator entered his settings. When the beam started, the machine stopped with a vague message: “Malfunction 54.”

The patient had received a dangerous overdose. Yet engineers could not reproduce the failure, and the machine was returned to service.

Three weeks later, another man was injured. The physicist at the clinic, Fritz Hager, needed a test that would work without putting another patient at risk.

He stayed with the operator after hours. First they tried entering the settings slowly. Nothing unusual happened. Then they repeated a small correction quickly: changing the selected treatment from X-rays to electrons.

That made the difference. The computer screen showed the new choice, but part of the machine had not finished adjusting. A powerful beam could fire in the wrong physical configuration. With the treatment table empty, Hager's radiation meter went off the scale.

A specialist checked the finding with another instrument. Hager told physicists at other clinics how to reproduce it. They found the same fault; an earlier unexplained injury in Georgia now made more sense.

But investigators eventually documented six overdoses, caused by more than one failure. The solution required software changes and independent physical safeguards.

The second Tyler patient died that May; the first also died later. A repeatable test could not undo their injuries. It showed what a reassuring screen had concealed—and gave other clinics a way to test the danger before another patient lay on the table.
