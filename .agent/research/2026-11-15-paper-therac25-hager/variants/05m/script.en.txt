How could a cancer-treatment machine injure a patient while its screen said it was ready? Why was the same machine used again after the first warning? And how could anyone prove what was wrong?

In 1986, a clinic in Tyler, Texas, used a machine called Therac-25 to aim radiation at tumors.

One night, physicist Fritz Hager tested it with nobody on the treatment table. His radiation meter went straight off the scale.

He was trying to explain two injuries. The first had happened on March 21, when a man came for treatment of a tumor on his back.

The man later described a sensation like hot coffee being poured onto his skin. The treatment had gone badly wrong.

The machine stopped with a message: “Malfunction 54.” It did not tell the operator that a massive overdose might already have occurred.

Engineers examined the machine but could not make it repeat the accident. The manufacturer disputed that it could deliver the dose the clinic suspected.

The machine returned to service. On April 11, another patient was injured during treatment for skin cancer on his face.

That second patient died on May 1. The first man also died later. Now the clinic had two tragedies and still no reproducible explanation.

Hager and the technician stayed after hours to investigate. They entered the treatment settings over and over, but the machine kept behaving normally.

The clue was in an ordinary correction on the operator's screen. Therac-25 offered two treatment modes: electrons and X-rays.

The X-ray mode required a different internal arrangement, including a metal target in the beam's path. The computer was supposed to coordinate that hardware.

The operator had entered X-rays, moved back through the form, and quickly changed the choice to electrons. On screen, the correction appeared complete.

But the internal control had not fully caught up. What the display showed and what the machine was preparing to do were no longer the same.

The speed mattered. Slow down, and the error could disappear. Make the correction quickly enough, and a dangerous state could slip through.

A powerful beam could then fire without the protective arrangement needed for that treatment. The screen alone could not reveal it.

That was the sequence Hager finally reproduced in the empty room. The measuring instrument could not display the enormous output.

A specialist from another cancer center checked it with different measuring equipment. One reproduced pulse lasted about a tenth of a second.

Now the clinic had more than a suspicion: a repeatable test that others could perform before treating anyone.

Hager telephoned other clinics that used Therac-25 and explained the sequence. Physicists at several sites tried it for themselves.

They reproduced the failure. In one Georgia clinic, it helped explain an earlier patient injury that had never made sense.

Why could the device reach that state at all? Earlier machines had physical safety interlocks. Therac-25 relied much more heavily on software.

And the fast-entry fault was not the whole story. Investigators documented six massive overdoses from 1985 to early 1987, involving more than one kind of defect.

After further accidents, authorities required stronger safeguards. The response involved software changes and physical protections, not just one keyboard fix.

The Tyler clinic stopped using its Therac-25. That did not undo what happened to either patient.

But Hager had given other physicists a specific test: which settings to change, how quickly to change them, and what the instrument would show with the room empty.
