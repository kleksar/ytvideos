How could a machine designed to treat cancer deliver a devastating overdose while its console looked almost normal? Why did hospitals keep encountering injuries that engineers called impossible? And what finally allowed someone to prove that the machine itself was at fault?

In 1986, radiation treatment depended on precision. A beam was aimed at a tumor; healthy tissue around it had to be protected. The Therac-25 offered two ways of delivering that beam from a single machine. In one mode, electrons treated tissue nearer the surface. In another, a high-energy beam struck a metal target to produce X-rays for deeper treatment.

Those modes needed different arrangements of parts inside the treatment head. The operator selected the treatment on a terminal, and the computer controlled the equipment. A normal-looking screen did not, by itself, demonstrate that every moving part had reached the right position.

At the East Texas Cancer Center in Tyler, this distinction became catastrophic. On March 21, a man came for another session following surgery for a tumor on his back. The operator had entered the treatment parameters, corrected a mode selection, and started the beam.

The machine stopped with a brief fault, “Malfunction 54.” The display did not explain that the patient might have received a huge dose. The man reported a burning sensation; later, his injuries made it clear that something serious had happened.

Engineers examined the machine. They checked for problems, but they could not reproduce the dangerous event on demand. According to the later account of clinic physicist Fritz Hager, the manufacturer maintained that the equipment could not have delivered the suspected overdose. Without a demonstrated mechanism, the machine was returned to clinical service.

On April 11, the same clinic was treating another man, this time for skin cancer on his face. Another treatment produced the same troubling malfunction. That patient died on May 1; the first man died months later. The clinic now had two patients injured, a recurring error message, and still no test that consistently made the problem happen.

Hager could not simply assume that the console was telling the truth. After hours, he worked with the operator and an empty treatment room. They entered treatment settings over and over. Ordinary attempts made the machine behave correctly. The missing condition was not an exotic command. It was the speed of a correction made during routine data entry.

The operator could enter X-ray mode, move back through the form, and change it to electron mode. If the correction happened slowly, the internal control had time to catch up. Performed quickly enough, the screen could reflect the new selection while an internal operation still belonged to the earlier one.

To a person at the console, the entries appeared verified. Inside the machine, the timing of the software and hardware no longer matched. Under that unsafe condition, the accelerator could deliver a powerful beam without the protective configuration appropriate for the chosen treatment.

The first decisive result did not require a new patient. Hager repeated the fast correction and measured the output with the treatment room empty. His radiation instrument went beyond its range. Another specialist used different equipment to check the finding; a dangerous pulse could last only a fraction of a second.

That was a turning point, because a failure that occurs once can be dismissed or misunderstood. Hager now had a sequence another physicist could try: enter one mode, move back, change it rapidly, and measure what happened without anybody on the table. He contacted other Therac-25 centers. Other physicists reproduced the fault, according to his later account.

At a clinic in Georgia, a patient had already been severely injured during treatment in 1985. The cause had been disputed. The repeated test gave investigators a way to understand at least one earlier incident that had seemed inexplicable.

Yet the history of Therac-25 did not begin in Texas. In June 1985, a patient in Marietta, Georgia, suffered a serious radiation injury after treatment. The clinic physicist raised the possibility of a machine failure, but the cause was not established at the time.

In July, another patient was injured at a clinic in Hamilton, Ontario. The machine displayed a treatment pause and appeared to show that no dose had been given. The operator attempted the treatment again, following what had become an ordinary response to some warnings. Investigators considered a fault in how the moving treatment head was positioned, but could not confidently reproduce the underlying event.

Independent experts in Canada recommended a separate physical way to verify that position. Changes were made, yet the full independent protection they recommended was not then installed. That mattered later, because a warning on a terminal and a check performed by the same controlling software could fail together.

At a hospital in Yakima, Washington, a patient developed an unusual striped skin injury after treatment late in 1985. The staff investigated other explanations. A later letter from the manufacturer argued that the damage could not have been caused by a malfunction or operator error. Only after a further accident did the clinic reconsider that earlier injury as an overdose.

The second Yakima accident happened in January 1987. It exposed another problem, different from the rapid keyboard correction in Tyler. A software counter used during machine setup could roll over and briefly make an unsafe condition appear acceptable. Treating all six incidents as the result of one fast typist would miss that separate defect.

The common weakness was broader. Earlier machines in the family had physical interlocks that could block some dangerous states even if software behaved incorrectly. Therac-25 relied much more on the computer for those decisions. Investigators later found that related software errors had existed in an earlier model, but its hardware protections had prevented the same harm.

This was also an information problem. The clinics did not initially have a complete picture of one another's injuries. Warning messages could look like routine interruptions, and some fault conditions let an operator try again. A physicist at one hospital was struggling to understand what a patient at another hospital had already experienced.

The American regulator eventually required a more substantial corrective response. Changes included new software and independent hardware protections, such as a circuit to stop the beam after a single dangerous pulse and a separate way to monitor the position of the treatment head. The manufacturer did not solve every part of the crisis by removing one keyboard shortcut.

The larger accident investigation, published by Nancy Leveson and Clark Turner in 1993, traced six known massive overdoses between June 1985 and January 1987. They described not just faulty code, but failures in system design, testing, communication and the response to evidence from patients and clinicians. The available records do not support reducing all the accidents to one cause.

Back in Tyler, Hager's test changed what the staff could demonstrate. An apparently ready console had hidden a dangerous mismatch, and the empty room made that mismatch measurable. By sending the sequence to other clinics, he turned one center's unexplained injuries into a warning that could be checked elsewhere.

The deaths could not be reversed. The lasting result was practical: a safety claim was no longer enough. The equipment had to be tested in the state it could actually reach, including when a skilled operator changed a setting quickly—and protected by something independent of the screen that said it was ready.
