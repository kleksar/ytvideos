Seven people took a familiar painkiller and died. The killer was never identified. But that crime changed a small detail you can still find under a medicine cap. September 1982, near Chicago. Twelve-year-old Mary Kellerman took Extra-Strength Tylenol for a cold. Within hours, she was dead. In another suburb, Adam Janus also died after taking the same medicine.

At Adam's home, his brother Stanley and Stanley's wife Theresa took capsules from a bottle there. They died too. Three deaths in one family gave investigators a crucial connection. Elsewhere, the deaths looked unrelated. The families lived in different parts of the Chicago area. The common clue turned out to be something bought in ordinary shops: Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules.

Laboratory tests found cyanide in some of those capsules. Someone had poisoned medicine people trusted. Nothing on an ordinary box told a buyer what had happened inside. Back then, many medicine containers could be opened and closed without an obvious sign of interference. The product was sold far beyond the affected neighborhoods. How many bottles might still be dangerous?

Officials did not know. They had to warn people before they knew who was responsible. Police cars drove through Chicago neighborhoods telling residents not to take the capsules. Drugstores pulled them from their shelves. Families checked what they had already bought. Johnson & Johnson eventually recalled about thirty-one million bottles of Tylenol.

Investigators concentrated on possible tampering after the medicine had left the factory. But the exact place where the poison entered the packages was never established. Then someone sent the manufacturer an extortion letter demanding one million dollars. The man who wrote it was convicted of extortion. He was never charged with the seven murders.

A disturbing letter was not evidence enough to show who had poisoned the capsules. The police could keep searching. People buying medicine needed protection immediately. On November fifth, 1982, the FDA issued rules for tamper-resistant packaging of most nonprescription medicines. The idea was practical: opening a package should leave something a customer can see.

Nobody was promising that poisoning could be made impossible. The goal was to make an earlier opening harder to hide. Tylenol came back with three barriers: a glued carton, a plastic band around the cap, and foil underneath. Each layer added a visible sign to check before anyone swallowed a capsule.

A plastic band could show that someone had disturbed the cap area. The foil was another warning. Once broken, it would not look untouched to the next customer. A child-resistant cap has a different purpose: making the bottle harder for young children to open. A tamper-evident seal is about a different question: has somebody opened this before me?

The seal is not a guarantee that everything inside is safe. It is a warning system. The new packaging approach spread across American nonprescription medicine shelves. The investigation did not produce the same neat ending. No one was convicted of the poisonings. In September 2026, authorities reported a possible new lead arising from an Idaho cold case.

Chicago detectives said they would examine any connection. The murders remained unsolved. So the most familiar result of this case is not a name on a courtroom door. It is the plastic ring, the glued flap, and the foil you peel away before taking medicine. Those barriers do not make every product safe. They help reveal when a package was opened before you got it.

And that is why the tiny warning matters: do not use if the seal is broken.
