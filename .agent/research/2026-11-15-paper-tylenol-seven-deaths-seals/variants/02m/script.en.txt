Why does a bottle of medicine have foil under its cap? And how did seven unsolved murders lead to that ordinary piece of packaging?

In September 1982, twelve-year-old Mary Kellerman died near Chicago after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol. Adam Janus died in another suburb. Then his brother Stanley and Stanley's wife Theresa took capsules from a bottle at Adam's home. They died too.

A local nurse, Helen Jensen, found the bottle and its store receipt. Six capsules were missing. Investigators were facing deaths that first seemed unrelated, but laboratory testing exposed the connection: cyanide had been put into Tylenol capsules.

Nobody knew how many bottles were dangerous. Police warned residents, stores removed the product, and Johnson & Johnson recalled about thirty-one million bottles. Investigators looked into tampering after the medicine left the factory, but the precise location and the person responsible were not established.

Finding a murderer and protecting the next customer had become two different problems. On November fifth, the FDA published rules requiring visible evidence of opening on most over-the-counter medicine packaging.

Tylenol returned with a glued carton, a plastic band around the cap and foil across the bottle opening. A child-resistant cap makes access harder for children. Those other barriers answer a separate question: has someone already opened this?

The killer was not convicted. The packaging solution could not guarantee safety either. It gave buyers something concrete to check before taking medicine: an intact seal, and a warning not to use the product if that seal is broken.
