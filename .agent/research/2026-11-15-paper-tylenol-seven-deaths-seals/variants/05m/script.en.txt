Why does a medicine bottle have a seal beneath its cap? Why are there sometimes several separate barriers? And what can a safety measure accomplish when the person who made it necessary is still unknown?

A family in suburban Chicago was about to provide an answer no one wanted. In September 1982, Adam Janus died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol. His relatives gathered at his home. Adam's brother Stanley and Stanley's wife Theresa took capsules from a bottle there. They also died. Three deaths in one family were suddenly part of a far larger question.

Elsewhere in the Chicago area, twelve-year-old Mary Kellerman had died after taking Tylenol. Other deaths were being investigated in separate neighborhoods. No family had bought a medicine bottle expecting to become part of a criminal investigation. The connection was not obvious from the addresses.

Helen Jensen, a local public-health nurse, checked the Janus home. She found a Tylenol bottle and the receipt for its purchase. Six capsules were missing. Her suspicion that the medicine linked the deaths was initially disputed, according to Jensen's later account. But samples were tested, and the terrible common denominator emerged: capsules containing cyanide.

That discovery solved one question and created a much bigger one. The investigators had found a poisoned product, not the person who poisoned it. How many other packages might be on shelves or already inside homes? And would an ordinary buyer notice that a capsule had been altered?

Police cars warned people in Chicago neighborhoods not to take the medicine. Retailers pulled it. Johnson & Johnson recalled around thirty-one million bottles of Tylenol. Investigators examined whether somebody had tampered with capsules after the medicine left the manufacturer. The exact point of interference was never established. The immediate danger could not wait for a courtroom verdict.

An extortion letter added another lead. It demanded one million dollars from the company. Its author, James Lewis, was convicted of extortion, but not of the seven murders. The letter did not provide the missing proof that he had poisoned the capsules. The criminal investigation continued without a confirmed killer.

So a separate group had to answer a more practical question. If it was impossible to know who would next interfere with a bottle, could packaging make the interference harder to hide?

On November fifth, 1982, the FDA issued rules for tamper-resistant packaging covering most nonprescription medicines. The standard was not that no package could ever be opened. It was that breaking into it should leave evidence visible to a customer. Manufacturers could choose among different protective designs; a warning on the label would identify the feature to inspect.

When Tylenol returned, its new package had three different physical barriers. The outer carton was glued shut. A plastic band covered the area around the cap. Beneath that cap was a foil seal. Those parts did different jobs at different points in a customer's journey, from shelf to bottle opening. Removing them left traces that were harder to mistake for an untouched product.

That was also why a child-resistant cap was not enough. Such a cap is designed to make opening difficult for young children. It can be closed again. A tamper-evident barrier serves a different purpose: to give the next person a visible indication that somebody got inside first. Neither device makes the contents automatically safe.

In 1983, federal law made tampering with packaged consumer products a crime. The investigation into the original seven deaths still did not yield a murder conviction. New leads have appeared, including one reported by Idaho investigators in 2026, but a possible connection is not proof of guilt.

Look at the warning printed beside a seal. It does not say the manufacturer has identified every danger. It tells the customer exactly what to check. Seven murders remained unresolved, yet the response changed a decision millions of people make before swallowing a tablet: whether an apparently ordinary package has already been opened.
