Why can a bottle of medicine have a glued box, a plastic band and a foil seal all at once? How did investigators connect deaths in different Chicago suburbs to the same product? And how could anyone design a useful safety response before they knew who the killer was?

The answer begins on September twenty-ninth, 1982. Twelve-year-old Mary Kellerman was at home in Elk Grove Village with a cold. She had taken Extra-Strength Tylenol. She collapsed and died that morning. Her family had no reason to know that another household, a few miles away, was moving toward the same catastrophe.

In Arlington Heights, four-year-old Kasia Janus accompanied her father Adam to a Jewel grocery store. Decades later she remembered him buying flowers for her mother and a bottle of Tylenol. Adam, a twenty-seven-year-old postal worker, fell ill at home and died. His relatives came to comfort the family. Adam's younger brother Stanley and Stanley's wife Theresa had recently married. Both took capsules from that same bottle. Both died too. An ordinary shopping trip had left one family with three deaths and an urgent question.

But noticing that connection was itself a struggle. Helen Jensen, a local public-health nurse, spoke to the family and checked the house. She found the medicine bottle, counted six missing capsules, and located a receipt showing a recent purchase. Jensen later recalled that her suspicion of Tylenol was initially met with disbelief. She kept insisting that the medicine be examined. A bottle, a receipt, and a count of missing pills were mundane objects, but together they pointed toward evidence investigators could test.

Elsewhere near Chicago, other deaths first looked unrelated. Mary Reiner, twenty-seven, had given birth to her fourth child about a week earlier. Thirty-one-year-old Mary McFarland became ill at work after taking Tylenol. Paula Prince, a thirty-five-year-old flight attendant, was found dead at home. None had shared the Janus family's bottle. Mary Kellerman, three Januses and those three women made seven victims. When laboratory tests revealed cyanide in the capsules, investigators finally had evidence linking the scattered cases.

The poison was not an accidental side effect of acetaminophen. It was a separate, deadly substance placed in capsules. Someone had altered medicine people expected to work normally. The product itself was a clue, but it was also a risk that could still be sitting in someone's bathroom.

That uncertainty changed the job facing the authorities. Police could investigate murders at their own pace only if nobody else was about to take another contaminated capsule. They had to warn people before they had a suspect, a motive, or a complete map of the affected bottles. Police vehicles drove through neighborhoods broadcasting warnings. Drugstores removed the medicine from their shelves. Families were told to check what they already had at home.

Johnson & Johnson, the company behind Tylenol, recalled about thirty-one million bottles. That enormous number was not a count of poisoned bottles; it showed the breadth of the response when investigators could not confidently bound the danger. The company faced the destruction or replacement of ordinary stock while the police continued searching. A public-health decision had to be made under uncertainty, rather than waiting until every criminal question had been answered.

Investigators examined the possibility that capsules had been tampered with after they left the manufacturer. But the precise place and method of interference could not be established in a way that identified the killer. It is tempting to imagine one person walking through a particular store aisle and opening a particular bottle. The evidence does not license us to recreate that scene as an established fact.

Then a letter arrived demanding one million dollars from Johnson & Johnson. Its writer, James Lewis, became an important figure in the investigation. He was convicted of extortion and served a prison sentence. But he was not convicted of, or charged with, the seven murders. The existence of a threatening letter did not resolve where the poisoned capsules came from. A headline about a suspect and a court finding of murder are different things.

Now consider the customer's situation. Before 1982, many over-the-counter medicine packages could be opened and closed without leaving a clear warning to the next buyer. A shopper had no laboratory and no way to see cyanide inside a capsule. Investigators were trying to discover who had crossed that boundary. Packaging designers and regulators had to change the boundary itself.

The crucial idea was not to make every container impossible to open. That would also make medicine impossible to use. It was to make a prior opening visible. An intact barrier could give the buyer an observable fact. A broken or missing one could tell that buyer to stop, without having to understand who interfered or why.

On November fifth, only weeks after the Chicago deaths, the United States Food and Drug Administration published tamper-resistant packaging requirements for most over-the-counter medicines. The regulations allowed different engineering solutions, provided the packaging had an indicator or barrier that could reasonably be expected to show visible evidence of tampering. Labels also had to point customers toward the protective feature. Not every category of product was covered in exactly the same way.

Tylenol's return to stores in November offered a concrete example. The company introduced a package with three seals. The flaps of the cardboard carton were glued down. A plastic band covered the neck of the bottle near the cap. And foil sealed the opening beneath that cap. A purchaser could encounter one barrier while picking up the box, another while removing the cap, and a third before reaching the capsules.

These were not three magical guarantees stacked on top of each other. They were three chances for evidence of interference to be noticed. If a carton had been opened, a band cut, or a foil layer lifted, an ordinary person now had something physical to inspect. The warning was specific: do not use the medicine when a protective seal has been broken. The design still depended on people looking at it, and it did not turn a sealed product into a promise of perfect safety.

There is another distinction that is easy to miss when every part of the bottle looks like safety packaging. A child-resistant closure is meant to make opening more difficult for young children, and it can be closed again. A tamper-evident feature answers a question about the product's history: does it look as though someone accessed this package before the current buyer? One protects against a particular kind of accidental access. The other provides a visible warning about possible interference. They are not interchangeable.

The change also continued after the first Tylenol redesign. In 1983, the Federal Anti-Tampering Act made interference with packaged consumer products a federal crime. And the FDA later refined its packaging rules: for certain two-piece hard gelatin capsule products, the 1989 amendment required at least two tamper-resistant features unless the capsules themselves used suitable tamper-resistant technology. The goal of those later specifications was to address weaknesses found in available packaging designs, not to claim that tampering had been eliminated.

What happened to the murder case while those protections became familiar? It did not reach the ending that detective stories usually offer. No one was convicted of the seven Chicago poisonings. James Lewis, whose extortion conviction made him a prominent name in the case, died in 2023. His conviction does not establish that he committed the murders.

Decades later, the consequences were still visible in that first family. Kasia Janus, the girl who had gone shopping with Adam, spoke publicly about losing her father, uncle and aunt. She described the milestones her father missed and the guilt she felt about having accompanied him to the store. Shopping itself became difficult: she examined packages for signs of opening. Family, friends and therapy helped her heal. That was her later account of life after the murders, not a new clue to the killer's identity.

Kasia no longer closely follows the search for the killer. A conviction could answer a question for investigators; it could not bring her father back. The new packaging could not undo the deaths either. But it could give the next customer an opportunity that the Janus family did not have: visible evidence that someone had already opened the package.

The case leaves us with two different outcomes. The criminal question — who poisoned the capsules? — remains open. The engineering question — how can a shopper recognize that someone has opened a package? — received a practical answer. The next time foil resists your finger under a medicine cap, that small obstacle is doing a job: giving you a chance to notice a broken boundary before taking what is inside.
