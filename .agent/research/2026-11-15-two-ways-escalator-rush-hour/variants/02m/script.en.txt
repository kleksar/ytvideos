Should I leave one side of the escalator free for walkers, or use both sides for standing?

I'm at a busy station, and people are waiting just to step onto the escalator. I want to get more of that queue moving in two minutes. Getting one person to the top first is a different race.

Reports on a London Underground trial at Holborn give me two average throughput figures. I'll use them as fixed boarding capacities in a drawing of the station. I haven't counted people stepping on at Holborn myself. My four hundred waiting commuters are imaginary.

In version A, I keep the walking lane. Some people climb faster, but they leave gaps between them. At a modeled capacity of one hundred and fifteen per minute, two hundred and thirty people board in two minutes. One hundred and seventy are still waiting.

I bring all four hundred back to the start. In version B, everyone stands, two abreast. At one hundred and fifty-one per minute, three hundred and two board. Ninety-eight remain. I've got seventy-two more people onto the same staircase without speeding up the steps.

London's trial found a throughput advantage on busy, long escalators. My calculation doesn't tell me when each passenger reaches the top. And if I arrive alone with no queue, walking, where permitted, may still get me upstairs sooner.

Even at Holborn, the experiment did not become a permanent rule across London. Staff had to persuade real people to stand on both sides.

So for a packed queue like mine, B wins on boarding. When the station is quiet, I'd choose the quickest permitted route for myself.

Then another train pulls in. Its passengers fill the entrance, and I'm back to the same choice.
