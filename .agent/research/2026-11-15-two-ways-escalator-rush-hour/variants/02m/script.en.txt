At the bottom of a crowded escalator, I have two choices. Do I leave a lane for people who walk, or fill both sides with people standing?

I want to get everyone through the entrance. Not just get the fastest person to the top.

So I draw the same four hundred commuters twice. Each version gets exactly two minutes. For boarding speed, I use two rates reported after an actual trial at London's Holborn station. This is my calculation, not footage of a new experiment.

In version A, people stand on the right and walk on the left. Some walkers move up faster than the standers. But they leave bigger gaps between themselves, and fewer people step onto the escalator. At the reported rate of one hundred and fifteen people per minute, only two hundred and thirty board. One hundred and seventy are still waiting.

I reset the crowd. In version B, nobody walks: both sides fill with standing passengers. That looks less urgent. Yet at one hundred and fifty-one people per minute, three hundred and two board. Ninety-eight remain. That's seventy-two extra people onto the escalator, from the identical starting queue.

London measured greater throughput on long, heavily crowded escalators. I haven't measured anyone's full journey to the street. And if I arrive when there's no queue, walking may still get me upstairs sooner.

There's another catch. The two-sided standing rule didn't become London's permanent habit. People were reluctant to change.

So when I return at rush hour, I don't just look at how fast the steps move. I look at how many people are waiting.

And there's the escalator again, packed at the bottom. Which side will I choose?
