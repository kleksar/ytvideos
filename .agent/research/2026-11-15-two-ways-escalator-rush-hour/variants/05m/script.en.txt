Which escalator rule actually moves a rush-hour crowd faster? Why can the person walking up reach the top first while the queue behind them moves more slowly? And what did London learn when it asked passengers to stand on both sides?

I reach the bottom of an escalator as a trainload of commuters pours toward the exit. The familiar arrangement is right side for standing, left side for walking. If I am in a hurry, the walking lane looks obvious. If I am watching the queue build behind me, I have a different problem: how many people can step onto those moving stairs every minute?

Those are two different measures. One is my own trip upstairs. The other is the crowd's boarding rate.

The change in perspective matters most on a long, crowded escalator. Walking seems faster because an individual can pass people who stand. But a walking lane doesn't necessarily fill each moving step. Some people are unwilling to walk all the way up, and those who do may leave space around themselves. Meanwhile, a tightly spaced standing line can use both sides.

London's Holborn station offered a real test. After an initial trial in 2015, Transport for London ran a longer standing-on-both-sides experiment in 2016. Staff, signs and floor markings asked passengers to break a deeply familiar habit. The reported results showed that, in very busy conditions, the unusual arrangement could let more passengers board.

I want to see what that difference would mean to one queue. I choose four hundred waiting commuters and give both rules two minutes. My starting crowd is imaginary. The boarding rates are published comparisons from the Holborn trial, not measurements from a simulation I physically ran.

First, I keep the walking lane. The reported rate is one hundred and fifteen people boarding per minute. After one minute, one hundred and fifteen have stepped onto the escalator. After two, two hundred and thirty have boarded. I still have one hundred and seventy people at the foot of the stairs.

Then I reset the entire scene. The same four hundred people wait in the same place. This time everyone stands two abreast. The comparison rate is one hundred and fifty-one boarding per minute. In two minutes, three hundred and two board and ninety-eight remain below.

That's seventy-two more people boarding in the same modeled interval. The second rule increases modeled boarding throughput by roughly thirty-one percent. I haven't made anyone walk faster; I've filled more of the available moving staircase.

Notice what I did not measure. Boarding doesn't mean reaching the top within those two minutes. Nor does this simple model capture a train arriving midway, a passenger with luggage, a stopped escalator, or changing preferences. It holds each reported boarding rate constant and assumes the queue stays available. That is why I call it a model.

It also doesn't prove that every commuter wins. Imagine arriving alone when there is no queue at all. I can walk up permitted steps faster than I can stand on them. Leaving a walking side might make sense for my individual trip. A lightly used, short escalator may not gain anything from filling two standing lanes. Holborn's result was conditional, not a rule for every station.

There is a second twist in the real story. In April 2016, some riders ignored instructions to stand on both sides. Staff had to encourage the new pattern. By March 2017, Transport for London said it had no plan to extend the scheme more widely. Reports describe maintenance ending the extended trial early; they do not establish that the boarding figures had been disproved.

So I have two answers, depending on what I'm trying to improve. To move a heavily congested queue onto a long escalator, the standing-only arrangement had a measurable advantage at Holborn. To get one unqueued person upstairs quickly, walking can still be faster.

Next time I reach the bottom, I'll look at the queue and the station's rules before I choose a side. The escalator doesn't change speed; the bottleneck around it does.
