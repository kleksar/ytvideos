I promised to take six family photos before dinner. There are only five relatives, but the list has me calling the same people in, sending them away, and calling them back. Do I respect the order I was given, or rearrange it?

I lay all six requests on the table. A picture of Mom and Dad. Grandma with the two kids. All five together. Grandma by herself. The kids by themselves. And Grandma with Mom and Dad. Whatever order I choose, all six pictures must be taken. I cannot solve the problem by leaving someone out.

I also need a fair way to count. The posing area starts empty, and it must end empty. When one person crosses its edge in either direction, I count one move. A child entering is one. Both parents leaving are two. Nobody gets a free exit just because I stop filming. This is an invented counting exercise, not a measured photo session or a prediction of minutes.

First I obey the written list. Mom and Dad step in: two moves. I wave them out and bring Grandma and the kids over: five more, seven so far. For the whole-family photo, Mom and Dad come back. Nine. I take the picture, then ask almost everyone to leave for Grandma's portrait. Thirteen. Grandma steps out and the two kids come in. Sixteen.

And now I notice the last card. It calls for Grandma with my parents. I have just sent all three away from that combination. The kids go out; Grandma and both parents return. The counter reaches twenty-one. When the last three leave, it reaches twenty-four.

I have six finished photos, and the camera never moved. Most of the extra work happened between shots, because the list kept separating groups I would need together again.

For the second attempt, I reset the room. Same family, same six pictures, same empty start and finish. The only thing I change is the sequence. I start with Grandma alone. One move. Mom and Dad join her for their picture together: three total. Grandma steps aside, leaving the parents for their own shot: four.

Now I need the whole family. Grandma returns and the kids join her, bringing the count to seven. My parents step out for Grandma and the kids: nine. Grandma leaves for the kids' picture: ten. Finally the two kids leave: twelve.

Twelve against twenty-four. I did not have to speed up the camera, skip a photo, or ask anyone to run. I just kept useful people in place between pictures. For these exact requests, twelve is the minimum: checking all 720 possible orders gives the same minimum, although several different orders can reach it.

Then I look at who did the moving. My parents each crossed twice in the second order. Each child crossed twice. Grandma crossed four times. She was first, but I still made her leave and come back. The shortest total route is not automatically the kindest route for every person.

So I add a constraint: let Grandma enter once, take every photo that needs her, and leave once. I try parents, Grandma with parents, all five, Grandma with kids, Grandma alone, then the kids alone. I count two, three, five, seven, nine, twelve, and finally fourteen when the kids leave.

Fourteen moves is two more than the absolute minimum, but Grandma only crossed twice. I have not changed the six requests. I have changed what I care about. If keeping an older relative comfortable matters more than two crossings, the second-best total may be the better family decision.

This is where my neat model stops. In a real room, walking distance, posing time, who is ready, access needs and the light might matter more than the number of entrances. And if someone hands me a different list of groupings, these totals no longer apply.

I finish with the same six photographs in every case. Twenty-four moves following this particular list. Twelve when I minimize total crossings. Fourteen when I keep Grandma from returning. The useful trick is to group photographs by who is already there, then check whose effort I am minimizing. At the next gathering, someone hands me a new list. Before moving the tripod, I look at who can stay in place—and who might need a break.
