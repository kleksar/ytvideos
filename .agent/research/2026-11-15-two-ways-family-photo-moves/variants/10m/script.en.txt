I have six family photographs to take before dinner, five relatives waiting beside a single tripod, and a sheet of paper that looks perfectly harmless. The first request is Mom and Dad. The second is Grandma with the kids. The third is everybody. By the time I reach the sixth, I have asked the same people to come back several times.

So I stop before taking the first photograph. What exactly am I trying to save? Is it best to follow the list, to have everyone walk as little as possible, or to keep one family member from being called back repeatedly? I can test those choices without pretending I have measured a real family gathering.

I copy the six requested groupings onto separate cards. Parents: Mom and Dad. Grandma and the two kids. The full group of five. Grandma alone. The kids alone. And Grandma with both parents. Those are the only six pictures in my experiment. If I change the subjects, I am no longer comparing the same job.

I mark one rectangle on the floor as the posing area. Everyone begins outside it. When I am finished, everyone must leave again. Each time a person crosses the rectangle's edge, I mark one move. Two parents entering together count as two moves, not one group movement. If someone stays there between two pictures, I add zero. I am not counting smiles, shutter clicks, or time spent walking across the room.

Why bother including the final exit? Because leaving it out can make the last photo look artificially cheap. I want the same starting and ending state for every order. The six photos are fixed, and so are the rules. Only their order changes.

I try method A: the list exactly as it was handed to me. Mom and Dad step in for their portrait. My counter reads two. They both leave; Grandma and the two kids enter. That change costs five, so I am at seven before the second photograph is taken. I call Mom and Dad back for the full-family shot, and the counter reaches nine.

Then the list asks for Grandma alone. Four people step away at once: both parents and both kids. The counter moves from nine to thirteen. Next I want the kids together, so Grandma steps out as they step in. I reach sixteen. Five photographs are done. Surely the last will be simple.

It isn't. The final request is Grandma with Mom and Dad. That means the kids step out, and all three adults I need step back in. Five more crossings take me to twenty-one. The last picture is taken. Grandma and my parents step away, and the counter stops at twenty-four.

I set the camera down and look at the pictures. None of them is wrong. The problem is the journey between them. My parents each crossed the boundary six times. Grandma crossed four times; so did each child. For five people and six photos, I have manufactured twenty-four entrances and exits simply by respecting a bad order.

Method B starts from a full reset. Every relative is outside again. The six requested groupings have not changed, and I still count the last departure. I rearrange the cards on the table instead of asking people to rearrange themselves over and over.

I start with Grandma alone. One move. Mom and Dad step in beside her, so their group of three costs just two more moves. I take that picture and have Grandma step aside. My parents are already correctly placed for their portrait. Four moves so far, three photographs complete.

For the full-family picture, Grandma returns and both kids join the parents. The counter becomes seven. I take the shot. Then the parents step aside and I photograph Grandma with the kids. Nine. Grandma leaves and the kids have their own photograph. Ten. Finally the children step away, and I reach twelve.

I still took all six photos. Nobody appeared twice in a single frame, and no requested family member disappeared. I changed the order, not the assignment. The first list required twenty-four boundary crossings. This new sequence requires twelve. The count is halved within the model.

But one efficient-looking order does not prove it is the best. With six different picture cards there are six choices for the first, five for the next, and so on. That's 720 possible orders. I can have a short calculation check each one: start with an empty set of people; between cards, add one for each person who changes between outside and inside; then count the final exit.

The result matters. Twelve really is the smallest total for this exact set of requests. And my Grandma-first order is not unique. Eight orders reach twelve. Some start with the parents, some with the kids. Grandma first was a surprise in my chosen example, not a law of family photography.

Now comes the part I missed while watching the total. I write the moves beside each person's name. In the original list, Mom and Dad each crossed six times. Grandma and the children each crossed four. In my twelve-move solution, Mom, Dad and both kids crossed only twice each. Grandma still crossed four times: in for her portrait, out for the parents' picture, back for the whole family, and out again before the kids' solo shot.

So the minimum total distributes the work unevenly. If someone finds moving between groups tiring, I cannot call a twelve-move result automatically considerate. It is a mathematical win on the metric I chose. It does not measure comfort.

I put a second rule on the table: Grandma should not have to leave the posing area and then return. Once she enters, I want every picture involving her to happen before she exits. I keep all the original photo requests. I am changing the objective, not editing out a difficult picture.

I try a third sequence. My parents pose first. Two moves. Grandma joins them: three. I bring in both children and take the full-family photo: five. My parents leave, and I photograph Grandma with the kids: seven. The children step out for Grandma's portrait: nine. Grandma then leaves, and the children come back for their own picture: twelve. They leave at the end: fourteen.

Notice what changed. Grandma entered once and left once. Mom and Dad each moved twice, while each child moved four times. The total is fourteen rather than twelve. I have traded two extra group movements for two fewer crossings by Grandma. If I specifically want Grandma to remain for all her photographs, checking the possible orders shows fourteen is the best total under that added restriction.

The third sequence also exposes something a big green 'winner' label would hide. If the children are the ones who struggle to move, I might choose differently again. If I counted distance instead of crossings, an entrance from the far side of a large hall could matter more than two steps beside the backdrop. And if a relative arrives late, the best sequence can change while I am shooting.

I have to be careful about calling any of this a time saving. A person crossing the line might need a second, or the entire family might spend longer arranging themselves for one photo. A grandmother who stays in the frame may still have to turn, move a chair or wait for everyone else. My arithmetic does not contain those costs, so it cannot say that twelve moves saves half the session.

There is one practical rule I can take away without pretending that the model is reality. Before asking anyone to pose, I can lay out the requested groups and try to keep shared people together from one shot to the next. I can also ask whose movement I want to reduce. That is more honest than telling every family to start with Grandma or always shoot the biggest group first.

I finish with six photographs on the table. With the order I received: twenty-four moves. With the fewest total boundary crossings: twelve. With Grandma entering and leaving only once: fourteen. The 'best' answer depends on whether I care about total shuffling or one person's effort. Next time, the groupings and the people may be different. At the next family gathering, someone hands me a new list. Before I arrange the cards, I look at who actually has to move.
