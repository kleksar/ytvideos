Why did I park closer to the shop and still arrive half a minute later? When should I drive past the first empty space? And does the answer change when I have to carry my shopping back?

I can test all three questions without pretending that I timed a real supermarket. I built a tiny, imaginary car park: one straight approach lane, a separate return lane, and a shop door at the end. From the entry point to the door is two hundred metres. The parked cars stay where they are for each comparison.

I have two choices. A means I stop at the first empty bay. B means I drive all the way to the door, loop into the return lane and park in the nearest available bay I encounter coming back. Both runs start in exactly the same place with the same spaces empty.

My first measure is the time until I reach the door, not the distance to my parked car. I set my driving speed to five metres per second and my walking speed to one point two five. These are assumptions in a small model, not observed supermarket averages. I also ignore traffic, finding the bay, and the actual parking manoeuvre. I want to see what the choice alone does.

Here is the first layout. The first free bay is a hundred and fifty metres from the door. Another free bay is a hundred and twenty metres from it. Everything closer is occupied.

I try A. I reach the first vacancy after driving fifty metres. That's ten seconds. From there I walk a hundred and fifty metres. That's two minutes. I touch the shop door at two minutes ten.

I reset the lot. Same car, same vacancies, same clock. This time I follow B.

I pass the first bay. I pass the second. I reach the door, turn onto the return lane, and come back to the vacancy a hundred and twenty metres away. I've driven three hundred and twenty metres in total: sixty-four seconds. I have a shorter walk, ninety-six seconds. Total: two minutes forty.

I have parked thirty metres closer and arrived thirty seconds later.

Where did the time go? A drove fifty metres. B drove three hundred and twenty. That's two hundred and seventy more metres behind the wheel, or fifty-four extra seconds. Walking thirty metres less saved me just twenty-four seconds. Fifty-four minus twenty-four: thirty seconds lost. The closer spot was real. So was the detour.

Now the interesting part. I leave that first vacancy alone, still a hundred and fifty metres from the door. I move only the second free space. Instead of a hundred and twenty metres out, it's now forty metres from the door.

I choose A again: still two minutes ten. B now drives two hundred and forty metres, walks forty, and reaches the door in eighty seconds. B wins by fifty seconds. The rules did not change. Only the location of one vacant bay did.

So where does the result flip? With these exact speeds and this simple return lane, if the first bay is a hundred and fifty metres away, B needs to find its closer bay less than ninety metres from the door to beat A. At ninety metres it's a tie. At a hundred and twenty, B loses. At forty, B wins. This isn't a magic ninety-metre rule for real car parks. Change the speeds, the lane or the first vacancy, and the boundary moves.

But I don't usually shop just to reach the door. I have to return to the car. Suppose I walk back with bags at one metre per second. I add that return walk to the same runs, without changing either car route.

For the hundred-and-twenty-metre vacancy, A now takes four minutes forty to reach the door and return to the car. B also takes four minutes forty. The extra walking back has erased B's earlier loss. If the closer vacancy is at forty metres, the full round trip becomes four minutes forty for A and just two minutes for B.

That changes what I mean by fastest. A parking spot is not just a point on a map. It changes how much of my entire visit I spend walking. If I only care about arriving at the door, I get one comparison. If I care about getting there and back with shopping, I must count both walks before announcing a winner.

I still cannot look at an unknown parking lot and predict every vacancy. Nor does my little straight lane represent traffic, garages, disabled spaces, changing occupancy or road rules. It proves only the arithmetic of the conditions I chose.

So I will not tell myself to always grab the first vacancy, or always head for the entrance. I will ask two better questions: how far will I have to drive to improve my space, and how much walking will that improvement really save?
