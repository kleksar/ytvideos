Who opened the Berlin Wall on the night of November 9, 1989? And why did the guards at the first checkpoint have no order to let anyone through?

In East Berlin, a government spokesman named Günter Schabowski was reading a new travel announcement at a televised press conference. East Germans had spent months demanding change, while many others had left the country through neighboring states.

A reporter asked when the new rules would take effect. Schabowski checked his papers and answered, in German, “immediately, without delay.”

There was a problem. The rules were supposed to be announced the following day, and even then travel involved paperwork. But the television news carried a far simpler message: the border was open.

People went straight to the crossings. At Bornholmer Straße, the guards had received no matching instructions. Their job was to control a border that thousands now believed had disappeared.

Around nine twenty that evening, they let some people pass. The guards marked their identity papers in a way that could stop them returning. It was a desperate attempt to reduce the crowd, not the opening people had come to claim.

More people arrived. The checkpoint commander, Harald Jäger, tried to get direction from above. None came that resolved the crisis. By about eleven thirty, he and the guards stopped the checks and raised the barrier.

People walked across the bridge into West Berlin. Around twenty thousand crossed there in the next hour, and other checkpoints opened later that night.

One spoken answer had helped bring them to the gates. But the wall did not open because a spokesman said a word. It opened because thousands acted on that announcement, and the people holding the barrier could no longer enforce it.

The next morning, the concrete was still standing. Its power to keep Berlin divided was broken.
