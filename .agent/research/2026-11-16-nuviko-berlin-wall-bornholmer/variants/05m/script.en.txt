How can a border guarded for twenty-eight years become useless in a single evening? Who made the decision to open it when the government hadn't told its guards to do so? And was the fall of the Berlin Wall really one official's mistake?

On November 9, 1989, Berlin was still two cities separated by a fortified border. People in the eastern half could not simply walk over to the western half. At the Bornholmer Straße crossing, guards checked documents before anyone could leave.

But East Germany was changing faster than its leaders could control. Protesters had filled the streets. Other citizens were leaving through neighboring countries. The government tried to stop the exodus by writing new travel rules. A draft released three days earlier had drawn more criticism, so officials revised it.

That evening, Günter Schabowski, a senior party spokesman, sat before international reporters and television cameras. He had been given the revised announcement, but the plan was to publicize the new rules the next morning.

Near the end, he read the part about private travel. People could apply without giving the old explanations for why they wanted to leave. A reporter asked the obvious question: when?

Schabowski looked at the paper. “Immediately, without delay,” he said.

The difference between the written plan and that answer was enormous. The reform was not a command to throw open every barrier that minute. It still involved applications and visas. Yet the answer was broadcast live, and the West German evening news soon led with the message that East Germany was opening the border.

Now the story moves away from the conference table. The people watching television had no reason to know which instructions had reached the guards. They went to the checkpoints to use the freedom they had just heard about.

At Bornholmer Straße the guards saw the crowd growing, but their orders had not changed. They still had to check people trying to cross. Nobody had given them a working procedure for the announcement on television. The commander of passport control, Harald Jäger, sought guidance as the pressure mounted.

At around 9:20 p.m., the checkpoint began letting some of the most insistent people leave. There was a catch. The guards stamped their identity documents invalid, in effect preventing those people from returning to the East. It was a limited release meant to relieve the crowd, not a decision to allow everyone to travel freely.

It failed almost immediately as a way of controlling the situation. People kept coming. The news had offered a general promise, not a small exception for a handful of travelers. The guards could not keep explaining a distinction that the gathering crowd did not accept.

More calls did not produce an instruction that solved the problem. And although the wall still stood, the control system behind it was breaking down in public, in front of the people it was meant to keep apart.

By about 11:30, Jäger and the guards at Bornholmer Straße stopped the checks and opened the barrier without an order authorizing that full opening. People crossed the Bösebrücke bridge into West Berlin. Within an hour, roughly twenty thousand had passed through that crossing.

Other Berlin checkpoints opened later the same night. Strangers embraced. Families who had lived on opposite sides of the barrier could finally travel across it. The scenes of people on the wall became the images the world remembers.

But those pictures can hide what happened first. Schabowski had not personally opened a gate. Jäger had not organized a revolution. The crowd had not arrived because it knew the exact wording of an internal regulation.

Each acted on different information. The party had a planned announcement. Television had a headline. The citizens had a promise. The guards had no matching order.

And the citizens did more than watch. By going to the border and refusing to treat the old procedure as the final answer, they forced the question that officials had postponed: who would stop them now?

There was no bloody clash that night at Bornholmer Straße. The wall's physical removal took much longer, and German reunification came in October 1990. But on November 9, the border lost its power to divide the city.

It happened between a live answer before seven in the evening and a raised barrier four and a half hours later.
