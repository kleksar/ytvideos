A black Mercedes rolled out of a military cargo plane in Uganda. The men inside were pretending to be the president's guards.

It was July 1976. More than a hundred people were being held at Entebbe Airport after their Air France flight had been hijacked. The captors threatened to kill them unless prisoners were released.

Israel considered negotiations. It also began planning a rescue thousands of kilometres from home. But how could soldiers reach the hostages before the gunmen realized what was happening?

The answer began with the passengers who had already been freed. They described the old airport terminal and the guards. Planners also obtained drawings of the building.

Four Hercules transport planes flew toward Uganda at night. One carried a Mercedes and two escort vehicles, meant to resemble the convoy of Ugandan ruler Idi Amin.

When the first plane landed, the cars drove toward the terminal. The disguise did not hold. Shots were fired near the building, and the commandos rushed inside.

The fighting was brief. Soldiers brought the surviving hostages out to the aircraft. A hundred and two people were rescued.

But three hostages died in the assault, and the commander, Yonatan Netanyahu, was killed. A fourth hostage, Dora Bloch, had been taken to a hospital before the raid. She was later murdered by Ugandan agents.

The French captain, Michel Bacos, returned with the passengers. He and his crew had refused an earlier chance to leave while others were still captive.

The planes escaped with those they could save. No single trick made that possible. Intelligence, long-range flying, the decision to keep talking, and a dangerous ground assault all had to come together.

And to reach the terminal before the guards sounded the alarm, the first thing the soldiers unloaded wasn't a weapon. It was a black Mercedes.
