How do you rescue more than a hundred hostages from an airport four thousand kilometres away? What happens if your planes can get there but cannot get home? And why would a hostage rescue begin with a black Mercedes?

On June 27, 1976, an Air France flight was travelling from Tel Aviv to Paris. During its stop in Athens, four hijackers boarded. Once airborne, they seized control of the plane. Captain Michel Bacos was forced to fly first to Libya for fuel, then to Entebbe Airport in Uganda.

Uganda was ruled by Idi Amin. His soldiers helped guard the captives, now held in the airport's old terminal. The hijackers demanded the release of prisoners held in several countries. They set a deadline, threatening to kill the hostages.

Some passengers were freed over the following days. Most of those still detained were Israeli or Jewish passengers, along with the French crew. Bacos and his colleagues had been offered a way out. They chose to stay.

In Israel, ministers argued over whether anyone could be rescued so far from home. A raid would need to surprise armed captors inside a building surrounded by Ugandan troops. And the aircraft would still need fuel to escape.

Then the people who had been released supplied something the planners desperately needed: a description of where the hostages were held. Israel also obtained plans of the terminal. A building that had been little more than a distant address became somewhere a rescue team could prepare to enter.

Negotiations continued. The captors extended their deadline. That bought time, but nobody knew whether the military plan would work.

One proposal involved approaching from Lake Victoria. The plan eventually chosen was riskier in a different way: land large Hercules transport aircraft at Entebbe after dark, drive troops to the old terminal and get the passengers out before the defenders could react.

The planes could reach Uganda. Flying away was the problem. They could not count on refuelling at Entebbe, and an emergency stop in neighbouring Kenya needed coordination. The rescue force planned for those possibilities while the political decision remained unsettled.

The assault team also had to cross part of the airport. Its solution was a car, not a new weapon. Idi Amin was known to travel in a Mercedes with escorts. The soldiers loaded a black Mercedes and two Land Rovers into the lead transport plane and prepared to resemble his motorcade.

On the night of July 3, the first Hercules landed at Entebbe. The vehicles rolled out and headed toward the old terminal. But the disguise did not last. There was a confrontation with Ugandan guards and gunfire began.

Exactly who fired first, and whether the cover was blown by a mistake, remain disputed by participants. The outcome is not: surprise was slipping away, so the commandos ran toward the terminal.

Inside, hostages were told to stay down as the rescue force fought the gunmen. Outside, other troops dealt with the airport's armed defenders. The central assault took minutes. The passengers who could move were hurried to the transports.

One hundred and two people were freed. Three hostages died during the raid. The assault commander, Yonatan Netanyahu, was fatally wounded. Another hostage, Dora Bloch, had been taken to a hospital before the rescue and was later murdered by Ugandan agents. A successful extraction did not mean everyone had survived.

The aircraft carried the rescued people out via Kenya and on to Israel. Bacos was among them, with the passengers he and his crew had refused to abandon.

For years, the raid was often told as a story about a daring deception and a rapid assault. Documents released for its fiftieth anniversary in 2026 made the political story clearer: officials negotiated while preparing a military option, rather than choosing one simple course from the start.

And there was a final argument. Uganda and African leaders condemned the breach of Ugandan sovereignty. Israel defended the operation as a rescue of people held under armed guard. The disagreement did not disappear when the hostages came home.

So what brought the aircraft back? Not the car alone. The released passengers, the building plans, the extra time from negotiations, the pilots, the assault team and the route out all mattered. The Mercedes got the commandos moving across the airport. It could not guarantee that every person would return.
