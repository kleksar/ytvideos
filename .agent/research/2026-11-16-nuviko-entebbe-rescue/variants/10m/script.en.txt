How could a military force rescue hostages from an airport thousands of kilometres outside its own country? How could it land heavy aircraft without warning the gunmen? And what would those aircraft do if they could not find enough fuel to fly home?

The usual version of the Entebbe rescue begins with a black Mercedes and a sudden assault. But the car only makes sense after a week in which a hijacked aircraft, an airport building, an international argument and a dangerous fuel calculation became parts of the same problem.

On Sunday, June 27, 1976, an Air France aircraft departed Tel Aviv for Paris, with a scheduled stop in Athens. Four hijackers boarded during that stop. Shortly after takeoff, they seized the flight and forced Captain Michel Bacos to change course. The aircraft landed in Benghazi, Libya, to refuel, then continued across Africa to Entebbe Airport in Uganda.

The hijackers belonged to Palestinian and West German militant groups. They demanded the release of prisoners held by Israel and several other countries. They set a deadline and threatened to kill the captives if the demands were not met. Their hostages were ordinary passengers, including people travelling to family events and holidays, and the crew who had taken them aboard.

Uganda was ruled by Idi Amin, a dictator whose troops supported the hijackers. When the passengers were moved into the airport's disused old terminal, Ugandan soldiers guarded the building. A second, newer terminal stood elsewhere at the airport. That difference would later matter to anyone trying to land a plane and find the hostages in darkness.

In the terminal, the captors separated many Israeli and Jewish passengers from the others. Some captives were released in batches. The French crew could have gone, too. Bacos declined to leave while his remaining passengers were still captive, and his crew stayed with him. The people who remained had no way to summon their own rescue.

Those released passengers, however, carried information. After returning to safety, they were questioned about the layout of the terminal, the rooms and the guards. Israeli planners also obtained building plans, because an Israeli firm had worked on the old terminal. These were not magic answers. A door shown on a drawing still had to be reached under armed guard. But they changed the planning question from where the hostages might be to how a team could enter the building.

The new deadline was approaching. Israel's leaders were under pressure from hostage families and were weighing negotiations against military options. Their earlier experience offered no easy rule. Hostages had been released through deals in some cases; other rescue attempts had ended with civilians dead.

In 2026, previously classified Israeli records made the decision-making process more visible. Officials discussed talking to the captors while a rescue force was being prepared. Negotiation was not simply the opposite of action. The discussions helped secure extra time, while commanders tried to decide whether a real military option existed at all.

That was the first big obstacle. Entebbe lay roughly four thousand kilometres from Israel. Parachuting troops into the area, or approaching from nearby Lake Victoria, were among the possibilities studied. Neither solved the central problem as convincingly as landing transport aircraft at the airport itself. The eventual plan was to use Hercules cargo planes at night, unload troops and vehicles, reach the old terminal and move the hostages back to the aircraft.

The planes had the range to reach Uganda. They did not have a comfortable margin for the return. Fuel at Entebbe could be unavailable or unusable during a firefight. Kenya, to the east, offered a possible place to land, refuel and care for casualties, but the planners could not assume every detail of that arrangement would work. Equipment and contingency plans had to travel with the rescue force. The operation could succeed inside the building and still fail if the planes could not leave.

There was also the airport itself. Heavy transport planes are difficult to hide. Even after one landed, the assault team had to cover the distance between runway and old terminal before the hijackers reacted. By then the deadline, the released prisoners and the building plans would be irrelevant if the guards saw an approaching convoy as an attack.

So the soldiers loaded an unusual piece of equipment. Idi Amin sometimes arrived at the airport in a Mercedes limousine with accompanying vehicles. The assault team brought a black Mercedes and two Land Rovers, intending to pass as his entourage long enough to reach the terminal. This was a disguise intended to buy seconds, not a guarantee of safe passage.

As preparations continued, pilots rehearsed and units planned separate tasks: strike the room holding the hostages, deal with armed defenders elsewhere at the airport and keep the aircraft ready for departure. According to later operational accounts, the first Hercules departed while the political approval process was still under way; final authorization came before the attack. The men in the planes were not following a settled plan that had been ready for weeks. They were heading toward an operation built in days.

At night, on July 3 going into July 4 in Uganda, the first Hercules approached Entebbe. The transport landed, and the vehicles drove out. Somewhere ahead lay the old terminal, with passengers who had already been held for nearly a week. The plan depended on the convoy getting close enough that the defenders would not have time to organize.

That deception broke down near Ugandan guards. Gunfire began. Participants have disagreed ever since about whether the guards recognized the car, who fired first and whether an unnecessary shot destroyed the surprise. Those accounts cannot all be treated as proven at once. What is clear is that the team could no longer rely on quietly driving to the door. The assault had to move immediately.

The commandos ran into the old terminal and fought the hijackers while other troops confronted Ugandan soldiers around the airfield. Amid the gunfire, the passengers were ordered to get down. The soldiers then hurried the surviving hostages toward the waiting transports. An operation designed to be over before the captors could react was no longer quiet, but the force managed to take control of the hostage area.

The operation rescued one hundred and two people. That figure matters. So do the deaths behind it: three hostages were killed during the assault, and the commander of the assault unit, Yonatan Netanyahu, was fatally wounded. Accounts of particular shots and responsibility are not all consistent, which is one reason to resist the neat version in which every decision during the raid went exactly as planned.

One passenger was not even at the terminal when the rescuers arrived. Dora Bloch had been transferred to a hospital in Kampala. The commandos could not extract her from the old airport building because she was not there. After the raid, Ugandan agents murdered her. That fourth hostage death is often lost when the story ends with aircraft taking off and people celebrating their return.

The rescue planes flew via Kenya and continued home. Among those carried out was Michel Bacos, the captain who had remained with his passengers after others were released. The flight that began as a hijacking had ended not with one group of prisoners exchanged for another, but with a risky military extraction and the loss of lives on both sides.

Back in 1976, the operation also produced a diplomatic dispute. Uganda and representatives of African countries condemned the violation of Ugandan sovereignty. Israel argued that it had acted to protect captives held with the support of Amin's forces. The disagreement was debated internationally; the survival of many hostages did not settle every legal or political question.

Fifty years later, the release of more government files showed just how many decisions had to line up. The captors' deadline had to be pushed back. Released passengers had to be heard. Building information had to be acquired. Aircraft had to reach the airport and still have a route out. The ground team needed speed even after the disguise failed. None of these steps made the outcome inevitable.

So the answer to the opening questions is not simply that a special unit was brave or that an old car fooled some guards. The rescue happened because intelligence, negotiations, aircraft, timing and an assault came together in a very narrow window. It brought a hundred and two people home. It did not bring everyone home. And the black Mercedes that crossed the runway was only one fragile part of that much larger plan.
