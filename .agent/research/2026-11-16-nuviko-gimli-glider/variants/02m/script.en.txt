How could a passenger jet run out of fuel after the crew checked the amount twice? And how did an old airfield become the only place it could land?

In July 1983, sixty-nine people were aboard an Air Canada Boeing 767 flying across Canada. The fuel gauges were blank. Ground staff measured the fuel manually before departure, and the crew repeated the check at Ottawa.

Both checks gave the wrong answer.

The new aircraft measured fuel weight in kilograms. But the calculation used a factor that converted litres into pounds. The result looked like enough fuel, even though the tanks held roughly half what the crew believed.

Hours later, a low-pressure warning appeared. Captain Robert Pearson turned toward Winnipeg. Then one engine stopped. The second followed. Most of the electronic displays went dark.

Without engine power, the Boeing was a glider. Pearson actually had experience flying gliders, but this one weighed many tonnes and could make only one landing attempt.

First officer Maurice Quintal helped find a closer option: Gimli, a former military airfield. As the runway approached, the aircraft was too high. Pearson turned it sideways through the air to lose height, then straightened it for touchdown.

The nose landing gear failed to lock. It folded on the runway, scraping the aircraft to a stop.

That failure mattered. Part of the old runway was being used for drag racing, with families camped beyond the track. The jet stopped before reaching them. Everyone aboard survived.

Investigators found more than a wrong number. The gauges had failed, procedures had been bypassed, and nobody had clearly owned the fuel calculation.

The crew had checked twice. What saved the passengers was what happened after those checks failed.
