One Christmas tree travels from Canada to Boston every winter. The reason begins with a ship burning in a harbour.

December sixth, 1917. Halifax, a Canadian port, was crowded with wartime shipping. A Norwegian vessel, Imo, collided with the French ship Mont-Blanc.

Mont-Blanc was carrying explosives for the First World War. The collision started a fire. Its crew abandoned ship, but the burning vessel drifted toward a pier.

People watched the smoke from the shore. About twenty minutes after the collision, the cargo exploded.

Almost two thousand people died. Thousands more were injured. Homes, streets and hospitals were wrecked, and winter was already there.

Neighbours and Canadian rescue teams began helping immediately. Meanwhile, Boston organized a train carrying doctors, nurses and supplies.

That train couldn't simply race straight through. A blizzard delayed it. The Boston team finally reached Halifax on December eighth, two days after the explosion.

They distributed food, water and medical supplies, and relieved local medical workers who had barely stopped since the disaster. Boston's help continued during the rebuilding.

In 1918, Halifax sent Boston a Christmas tree to say thank you. The gift did not become an unbroken annual tradition straight away. It was revived in 1971.

The medical train has long stopped running. The tree hasn't. Nova Scotia still sends one each year, not because Boston needs a Christmas tree, but because Halifax remembers who came to help.
