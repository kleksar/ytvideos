Why does Boston receive a Christmas tree from Canada every winter? What happened in a harbour more than a century ago that made one city owe another a lasting thank-you? And why did that thank-you almost disappear before it became a tradition?

In December 1917, Halifax was one of Canada's busiest wartime ports. Troops and cargo moved through its narrow harbour on the way across the Atlantic. On the morning of December sixth, two ships were trying to pass through the same stretch of water.

One was Imo, a Norwegian vessel heading out to collect relief supplies. The other was Mont-Blanc, a French ship entering with explosives for the war. They collided in the Narrows. A fire broke out aboard Mont-Blanc.

The crew knew what was in their holds. They abandoned ship. But the fire kept burning as Mont-Blanc drifted toward the waterfront, close to the homes and railway yards of Halifax. From shore, the smoke drew spectators. Most had no way to know what that ship was carrying.

The fire burned for roughly twenty minutes. Then, shortly after nine in the morning, the explosives detonated.

The blast shattered the north end of the city and sent a wave across the harbour. Nearly two thousand people died, and another nine thousand were injured. Some were hurt far from the waterfront by windows breaking under the force of the explosion. Thousands had nowhere to sleep.

One railway dispatcher, Vincent Coleman, had received a warning about the cargo. Before the explosion, he stayed to send a message along the line, telling stations to stop approaching trains. He died near his telegraph. Historians still debate whether his warning stopped one particular passenger train; the wider warning and railway response are better documented.

The problem facing the survivors now was not just the wreckage. Hospitals and shelters filled up. The explosion had damaged transport and communication links. A winter storm was approaching.

Help began locally. Soldiers, sailors, neighbours and nearby towns started bringing out survivors and treating the wounded. Six relief trains from Nova Scotia and New Brunswick arrived on the day of the blast. They carried people and supplies into a city whose railway yards had been torn apart.

Then word reached Boston, in the American state of Massachusetts. Within hours, the city had organized a train with doctors, nurses, food and medical supplies. It left that night.

But a blizzard delayed the journey. The Boston relief party did not reach Halifax until the morning of December eighth. By then, local medical workers had spent two days handling the emergency. The visitors helped distribute supplies and took over some of the exhausting work.

The help did not end when the first train emptied. Relief continued as people tried to replace homes and rebuild streets. The explosion had destroyed more than sixteen hundred houses. The devastated Richmond neighbourhood would eventually be redesigned and rebuilt.

The disaster also changed medical care. Many people suffered serious eye injuries. The scale of those injuries helped spur the creation of a national organization for Canadians who were blind. Treatment of burned children influenced later surgical practice. None of that undid the loss. It shows how long the effects lasted after the harbour was cleared.

In 1918, Halifax sent a Christmas tree to Boston. It was an answer to the people who had come north with help, not a bill to be repaid.

But there is a part of the story people often miss. The gift stopped being a regular event. It was revived in 1971. Since then, Nova Scotia has continued to send a Christmas tree to Boston each year.

Today the tree stands on Boston Common. Anyone can admire it without knowing the ships' names or the story of the train. But the reason it crosses the border is the same: when Halifax needed help, Boston sent it.

The train arrived through a blizzard. The tree now arrives as a thank-you.
