Why does an enormous Christmas tree travel from Nova Scotia to Boston every December? Why did a railway message from a dying dispatcher matter to the rescue of a city? And how did a disaster in Canada become a bond that people in an American city still celebrate more than a century later?

The answer begins in Halifax, in the last years of the First World War. The Canadian city had one of the Atlantic's most important harbours. Soldiers, weapons and food moved between railway cars and ships. Before a convoy could sail toward Europe, it might first need to gather in the broad basin behind a narrow entrance to the harbour.

That narrow passage mattered on the morning of December sixth, 1917. Two ships were moving in opposite directions. Imo, a Norwegian vessel on a mission to collect relief supplies for Belgium, was headed outward. Mont-Blanc, a French ship filled with wartime explosives, was heading inward.

They collided. The damage started a fire on Mont-Blanc.

This was not an ordinary cargo ship fire. The crew knew their vessel carried explosives and abandoned it. But Mont-Blanc kept drifting. For people along the waterfront, the visible facts were a collision, smoke and a ship moving toward the piers. The dangerous cargo was not obvious to the crowds who gathered to watch.

The fire continued for roughly twenty minutes. As it drifted toward Pier Six, the ship brought its cargo closer to homes, businesses and railway yards. Some naval officers understood the danger. Most of the people looking at the burning vessel did not.

Vincent Coleman worked at a railway station close to the harbour. A naval warning had reached him: the burning ship was loaded with ammunition and might explode. Coleman could leave. Instead he returned to his telegraph key to warn stations that trains approaching Halifax had to be stopped.

The message went out along the railway. And that is where a familiar heroic story needs one correction. It is often said Coleman definitely stopped an approaching passenger train carrying about three hundred people. The museum's account says the evidence about that exact train is conflicting. The warning unquestionably spread along the line and helped alert railway workers. That documented effect is important enough without claiming a rescue the surviving records cannot prove.

Coleman did not survive. Shortly after nine in the morning, Mont-Blanc exploded. The railway station near the waterfront was destroyed.

The blast was equivalent to roughly three thousand tons of TNT. The ship itself and Pier Six vanished into the destruction. Buildings collapsed. A wave struck the shores of Halifax and Dartmouth. Glass became a weapon in homes far from the harbour. Nearly two thousand people died, and approximately nine thousand were injured.

A disaster of that scale cannot be told with a number alone. The Richmond district near the harbour was ruined. Families lost not just walls and roofs but the streets and workplaces around them. The Mi'kmaw community of Turtle Grove was also devastated. The provincial museum records that two children of community elder Jerry Lonecloud died in the explosion.

Meanwhile, the people who could still move had to begin finding the injured. Roads, docks, railway connections and telephone and telegraph lines were damaged. The explosion had destroyed parts of the very system that would normally bring help.

Yet the first rescuers did not arrive from a distant city. Neighbours, soldiers, sailors, and nearby communities were already working. On the same day, six relief trains came from elsewhere in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick, carrying firefighters, doctors, nurses and supplies. Their arrival mattered before anyone from Boston could possibly reach Halifax.

The medical emergency was enormous. Hospitals were overwhelmed. Many people had been wounded by fragments of glass. Children needed treatment for severe burns. Halifax was not simply waiting for a dramatic train from America; its residents and local helpers were trying to keep an emergency response alive with what they had.

One doctor, Clement Ligoure, had already been forced to establish his own medical practice after being denied privileges at local hospitals because he was Black. After the explosion, he treated injured people from the city's north end and continued working for weeks. His story is part of the rescue as surely as the trains. A city's response depended on people who were not always given an equal place in that city.

The emergency also moved far beyond Halifax. Word crossed the border to Boston and the state of Massachusetts. Within hours, people there organized doctors, nurses, food and other medical supplies into a relief train. It left on the night of December sixth.

There was one more obstacle. On December seventh a blizzard hit. The journey north slowed. A medical team that wanted to arrive quickly had to push through winter conditions while Halifax's own workers kept treating victims.

The Boston train finally reached Halifax on the morning of December eighth. Two days had passed since the explosion. The visitors distributed food, water and medical supplies, and helped relieve exhausted local staff. For those already providing care, the help meant someone else could take a shift. That practical detail explains why the arrival mattered.

And the city still needed more than a medical delivery. Around sixteen hundred homes had been destroyed. Thousands of people were homeless. Shelters, replacement furniture and longer-term building work had to follow the first emergency. Relief committees in Canada and Massachusetts continued helping during reconstruction.

The devastated working-class district of Richmond was not simply put back exactly as it had been. Planners drew up new streets and housing. The Hydrostone development used fire-resistant building materials and was designed to provide hundreds of homes. Nova Scotia's archives preserve plans, photographs and construction records of the reconstruction. They document a city being physically rebuilt, not just a single impressive act of charity.

Medical consequences also lasted. The high number of eye injuries helped spur the creation of a national organization for blind Canadians. Doctors' work on children with burns contributed to changes in pediatric care. Those developments should never be offered as a good side of the explosion. They were responses to injuries people should never have suffered.

So where does the Christmas tree enter this story? Not in 1917, while the rescue trains were still operating. In 1918, after the immediate emergency, Halifax sent a tree to Boston as a gesture of thanks. The meaning was straightforward. When the city had been devastated, Boston had helped.

But the yearly procession that people recognize today did not begin without interruption. The gift fell out of regular practice. In 1971 it was revived, and from then on Nova Scotia continued sending a Christmas tree to Boston each year.

That gap matters. The tradition was not simply an automatic routine repeated from the first Christmas after the blast. A later generation deliberately brought it back. By then, the people who had been inside the burning harbour, in Coleman's railway station, or on the first medical trains were becoming part of history. The people sending the trees were choosing to remember them.

There is another distinction worth keeping. Boston did not rescue Halifax by itself. Local residents, Canadian provinces, ships in the harbour and other countries also gave essential help. The tree honours a real American contribution without erasing everyone else's work.

Today, the gift is a tree on Boston Common, far from the narrow strip of water where two ships struck each other. People gathering to see it may never have heard of Mont-Blanc, Vincent Coleman or the doctors who reached Halifax through the snow. The lights are a celebration, but the reason for the journey is older than the celebration itself.

A train once crossed the border because a city was hurt. Now, every winter, a tree crosses it because that help has not been forgotten.
