How can a satellite drop radioactive metal across Canada? And who pays when the wreckage belongs to another country?

Early on January 24, 1978, two Canadian police officers saw a red fireball breaking apart above the northern wilderness. It wasn't a meteor.

It was Kosmos 954, a Soviet satellite used to watch ships from orbit. Its instruments needed electrical power, supplied by a small nuclear reactor. A malfunction prevented the reactor from being moved into a safer disposal orbit. Now the whole spacecraft was falling.

Canadian officials asked Moscow what had come down and where it might be dangerous. They hadn't received timely warning. The Soviet ambassador suggested any contamination would be limited.

Canada couldn't simply accept that assurance. The United States offered help, and search crews began Operation Morning Light. They checked the city of Yellowknife first and found no radiation there. Farther out, detectors picked up radioactive pieces on frozen ground and lake ice.

This wasn't a nuclear bomb going off. It was reactor material scattered by a spacecraft breaking apart. Some fragments were dangerous enough to require special handling.

The search carried on through the year. Canadian authorities later estimated they had recovered only about one tenth of one percent of the satellite's power source. That figure did not mean the entire search area was equally contaminated.

Then came a second search, through international law. Canada claimed roughly six million Canadian dollars in costs. In 1981, the Soviet Union agreed to pay three million.

The satellite had fallen in minutes. Finding its pieces and settling the bill took years.
