How does a Soviet spy satellite become a radioactive search operation in Canada? Why did the people on the ground learn so little about the machine that was coming down? And how did a crash in the sky end with a bill sent across the Cold War?

On January 24, 1978, two Canadian police officers watched a bright red object break into pieces above the Northwest Territories. To anyone looking up, it could have resembled a spectacular meteor.

But this object had a name: Kosmos 954. The Soviet Union had launched it four months earlier to help locate ships at sea. Tracking distant targets from orbit required electrical power, and the spacecraft carried a nuclear reactor to provide it.

That reactor wasn't a nuclear bomb. But it still contained radioactive material that was not supposed to fall into a populated country. A malfunction prevented the reactor from being sent to a safer disposal orbit when the satellite failed.

Canada now had two very different problems. Something dangerous might be scattered over its northern territory. And the country that built it wasn't providing a timely, complete explanation of what to look for.

On the day of the fall, Canadian diplomats questioned the Soviet ambassador. He said the satellite had been expected to re-enter and suggested that any contamination would be small and local. Canadian officials kept asking for more precise information.

They couldn't wait for the diplomatic answer. Within minutes of the re-entry, the United States offered technical assistance. Canada accepted. The joint search was named Operation Morning Light.

The first concern was Yellowknife, a northern city close to the region of the fall. Specialists arrived with protective equipment and radiation detectors. Their checks found no radiation in the city. That was reassuring, but it didn't answer what had happened across the lakes, forests and frozen ground beyond it.

Aircraft searched a broad corridor. A specially equipped U-2 aircraft also checked for signs of an airborne radioactive cloud along the satellite's path; that check found none. Searchers still had to examine the ground.

On January 25, an airborne detector identified radioactive material far to the northeast of Yellowknife. Another hit came on the ice of Great Slave Lake. Each detection changed the problem from a possible hazard into a real cleanup.

Then an unexpected clue arrived. On January 28, six men at a remote cabin reported metal projecting from a river. Those pieces were identified as part of the satellite. The wilderness itself was providing the evidence.

At first, search teams found fragments associated with the reactor. Later they began detecting tiny pieces of radioactive fuel. The idea that the entire reactor would harmlessly disappear during re-entry could no longer be taken at face value.

Canada's formal claim said that some recovered fragments had potentially lethal radioactivity. Specialists handled them carefully and took material to a nuclear laboratory for testing. The point of the operation was to find dangerous pieces, not to declare the entire north uninhabitable.

Search and cleanup work continued in two phases, through October. The Canadian government describes a vast affected search region of 124,000 square kilometres. Its later estimate was that only about 0.1 percent of the satellite's power source had been recovered. That is an estimate of material recovered, not a map showing equally high radiation everywhere.

The final obstacle was money. Canada's total response cost almost fourteen million Canadian dollars. It formally claimed about six million from the Soviet Union, restricting the claim to particular costs attributable to the incident.

Canada invoked a 1972 international convention: a country that launches a space object is responsible for damage it causes on the Earth's surface. This was not a trial. It became a negotiation.

In April 1981, the two governments signed a settlement for three million Canadian dollars, about half the amount claimed.

The satellite had not produced a nuclear explosion. Yet it had forced people to search an enormous landscape, measure unfamiliar debris, demand answers from another government, and negotiate a bill. The wreckage came down in one morning. Its consequences lasted for years.
