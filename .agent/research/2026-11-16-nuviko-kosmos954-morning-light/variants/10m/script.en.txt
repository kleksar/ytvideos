What happens when a nuclear reactor falls out of orbit over another country? How do you look for radioactive debris across a wilderness larger than many countries? And when the search is over, who receives the bill?

On a January morning in 1978, two Canadian police officers saw a red fireball crossing the dark sky near Yellowknife. Pieces separated from it as it fell. The sight looked like a meteor breaking apart. The explanation, however, would lead from a frozen river to a diplomatic meeting in Moscow.

Four months earlier, on September 18, 1977, the Soviet Union had launched Kosmos 954. It was a military reconnaissance satellite designed to help locate ships at sea. This was the Cold War. Finding vessels at great distances had strategic value, and a radar satellite needed more electrical power than a small battery could supply for such a mission.

Kosmos 954 carried a compact nuclear reactor. That doesn't mean it carried a nuclear weapon, or that a nuclear explosion was inevitable. A reactor makes heat and power through controlled nuclear reactions. But its fuel is radioactive, and handling that material on Earth is quite different from leaving it in space.

The intended safety arrangement was to move the reactor into a higher disposal orbit when the spacecraft could no longer operate. Instead, a malfunction prevented that separation. The satellite's path became unstable, and by January its return to the atmosphere was expected. Where its material would land, and how much would survive the heat of re-entry, were much harder questions.

Early on January 24, observers at Hawaii's Haleakala observatory tracked the object, according to a Northwest Territories historical account. Farther north, the police officers watched glowing fragments fall toward Canada. The satellite broke up over an immense region of lakes, forests and remote settlements.

The first serious question was not whether the machine existed. Canada knew a Soviet spacecraft was descending. The question was what radioactive material might now be on Canadian ground. A fragment could look like ordinary metal while carrying radiation a person could neither see nor smell.

Within minutes, the United States offered Canada technical assistance. Canadian officials accepted. But another conversation was already becoming strained. They asked the Soviet ambassador why Canada had not been warned in time about the likely re-entry near its territory, and urgently requested details about the reactor.

The ambassador said Moscow had expected the re-entry and had considered the Aleutian Islands a possible area for debris. According to Canada's later formal claim, he described any contamination as likely to be minor and local. He also offered Soviet specialists to help. Canadian officials responded that they first needed precise information about what had come down.

That uncertainty changed how the search had to work. Officials could not draw a small circle around a crash site, mark it safe, and leave. Pieces had scattered along a long path. They had to locate them, test them, and decide which ones could threaten people who might encounter them.

The Canadian and American effort received a name: Operation Morning Light. The first checks addressed the obvious concern, Yellowknife. A team of nuclear accident specialists arrived with detectors and protective clothing. The Northwest Territories historical account says their appearance alarmed residents. Tests in the city did not find radiation.

That was a genuine piece of good news. It was not a declaration that the entire wilderness was safe. A city measurement says something about that city. It cannot tell you what lies hundreds of kilometres away on an ice-covered lake.

An American U-2, equipped for sampling, checked the satellite's path for a radioactive cloud. According to the same regional historical account, that flight also found no radiation. The searchers then concentrated on the ground, where physical fragments could remain even without a detectable cloud overhead.

Aircraft flew across a roughly fifty-kilometre-wide corridor stretching for hundreds of kilometres. Crews used radiation detectors to pick out locations worth inspecting. The preliminary aerial search covered about forty thousand square kilometres. Even that number was only part of the larger region officials eventually had to consider.

On January 25, a detector registered radioactive material roughly three hundred kilometres northeast of Yellowknife. Another signal came from the ice of Great Slave Lake. The search was no longer just precautionary. It had identified debris that required handling.

Then ordinary people supplied another lead. On January 28, six men staying at a cabin by the Thelon River reported metal protruding from the river. The fragments were identified as pieces of the satellite. A search designed around aircraft and instruments still depended on observations made from the ground.

Imagine the practical problem without inventing a dramatic encounter. A detector gives a reading, but a team still has to reach the location. A metal fragment must be approached and measured before it can be collected. Snow, ice, enormous distances and limited access turn each positive reading into a separate operation. The records describe a coordinated effort, not a single spectacular recovery.

At first, searchers found radioactive parts associated with the reactor. After more than a month, the regional account describes much smaller pieces of the reactor's fuel appearing farther south. That mattered because Moscow had argued that the reactor was designed to break up completely during re-entry. The material actually found on the ground showed that a simple assurance about burning up was not enough.

Canada's formal claim later said all but two of the recovered fragments were radioactive, and some were dangerously so. The fragments went to the Whiteshell nuclear research establishment in Manitoba for examination. Researchers needed to identify what they had found, while cleanup crews needed reliable measurements to decide what to remove.

The numbers require care. Government of Canada material describes an affected search region of around one hundred twenty-four thousand square kilometres. It also reports a later estimate that only about one tenth of one percent of the satellite's power source was recovered. Neither statement means that every square kilometre was equally contaminated, or that the missing material remained in one intact piece waiting to be found.

By this point, the operation had revealed a second kind of risk: missing information. Canada had asked Moscow for details on January 24 and repeated its requests. In its formal statement, Canada said satisfactory information arrived late, with notes in March and May. Crews were already searching while the governments exchanged explanations about a machine designed and launched elsewhere.

The Soviet government eventually acknowledged that the debris identified in Canada came from Kosmos 954. Meanwhile, Canadian and American teams continued work through a second phase that lasted into October. The mission had expanded from checking a possible crash site to detecting, removing and studying dangerous fragments scattered across several northern regions.

There is a temptation to end the story here, with the last helicopter landing and the last measuring instrument being packed away. But the next conflict had begun before the fieldwork finished. Who should bear the cost of protecting Canadian territory from a satellite launched by another country?

Canada had spent nearly fourteen million Canadian dollars in the overall response. That did not become the exact sum demanded from Moscow. In its formal claim, Canada separated ordinary government expenditure from particular costs incurred because of the debris. The amount it claimed was just over six million Canadian dollars.

The legal argument rested in part on a treaty from 1972, the Convention on International Liability for Damage Caused by Space Objects. Its rule for damage on the Earth's surface puts responsibility on the launching state. Canada did not have to portray the satellite as an act of war to argue that the country which launched it should compensate the country affected by the fall.

That claim contained a second important distinction. Canada wasn't sending a bill for a fictional city destroyed by a nuclear blast. There had been no nuclear explosion. It was seeking compensation for identifying, removing and testing radioactive debris, and for the work needed to reduce possible harm.

Diplomats negotiated for years. The Soviet Union did not simply accept every Canadian number. The final agreement was signed in Moscow on April 2, 1981. It stated that the USSR would pay three million Canadian dollars in full and final settlement: roughly half of what Canada had formally claimed.

The agreement did not magically gather the missing fragments. It also was not a court ruling proving that every precaution had been adequate. It was an agreement between two governments about the consequences of an event that had crossed a border without anyone on the ground being able to stop it.

The accident contributed to wider discussions about nuclear power in space. In November 1978, the United Nations authorized a working group to consider the safety of nuclear-powered satellites. In Canada, the incident also became an important lesson in coordinating responses to radiological emergencies.

For the police officers watching the fireball, the event began in the sky and ended out of sight. For the search crews, it began with readings on snow and ice. For the governments, it ended with a signed payment agreement three years later.

Kosmos 954 never produced the nuclear explosion that a sensational version of this story might imply. Its real consequences are more revealing. An object designed to operate above national borders broke apart inside one country's territory. Thousands of kilometres of search routes, a disputed set of costs and a formal international settlement followed.

A satellite can fall in minutes. Responsibility for what comes down with it can remain long after the fireball is gone.
