How did a fisherman help find a missing hydrogen bomb? And why wasn't finding it the end of the search?

In January 1966, an American bomber collided with a tanker aircraft while refuelling above southern Spain. Seven crew members died. Four hydrogen bombs fell near the village of Palomares.

None produced a nuclear explosion. But the conventional explosives in two of them burst on impact, scattering radioactive material across the ground.

Search teams found three bombs on land. The fourth had vanished.

Francisco Simó Orts had been fishing offshore. He reported seeing something descend toward the sea beneath a parachute. At first, American officials thought he might have spotted a falling airman. But every member of both crews had already been accounted for.

He may have seen the missing bomb.

The Navy brought ships and a tiny research submarine called Alvin to the Mediterranean. Even with the fisherman's clue, the seabed was vast, steep and difficult to see.

In March, Alvin located the bomb deep underwater. An attempt to recover it failed, and the weapon slipped farther down the slope.

It had to be found again.

An unmanned vehicle called CURV was sent down to help raise it. CURV became tangled in the parachute lines. Rather than abandon both, the Navy hauled the vehicle and the weapon up together.

On April 7, after roughly eighty days, the fourth bomb reached the deck of an American ship.

But Palomares had two different problems. The lost weapon had been recovered from the sea. Radioactive contamination from the other bombs remained on land, and the argument over its cleanup would outlast the recovery by decades.
