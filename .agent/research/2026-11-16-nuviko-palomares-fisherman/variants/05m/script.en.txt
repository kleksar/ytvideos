How do you search for a hydrogen bomb when the only person who knows where it went is a fisherman? What happens when you finally find it, but your equipment cannot bring it back? And what does success mean if the village beneath the crash is still contaminated?

On January 17, 1966, a United States B-52 bomber was refuelling over southern Spain. It collided with the tanker aircraft. Both aircraft broke apart, seven people died, and four American hydrogen bombs fell around the coastal village of Palomares.

The weapons did not produce nuclear explosions. But two struck the ground hard enough for their conventional explosive components to detonate. That scattered radioactive material over nearby land. The other problem was simpler to describe and harder to solve: one of the four bombs could not be found.

Three were recovered on land. Soldiers kept looking for the fourth.

Offshore, a fisherman named Francisco Simó Orts had noticed something falling toward the water beneath a parachute. He thought it might be an airman.

Officials checked that account against the crews of the two aircraft. Everyone was accounted for. The object on the parachute could have been the bomb.

That turned a search of fields and scrub into a search of the Mediterranean seabed.

The Navy assembled a fleet, divers and underwater equipment. Among the machines was Alvin, a small research submarine still early in its working life. Alvin could carry people down where ordinary surface search teams could not look. It could not, however, make the seabed flat, clear or easy to map.

The fisherman could give the search teams an approximate location. He could not give them a precise underwater coordinate.

Days became weeks. The submarine moved along steep underwater slopes, its crew trying to identify objects through limited visibility. A pale shape on the seabed could be a rock, debris or the missing weapon. The search had to be repeated and refined.

In March, Alvin finally found the bomb. It lay near a steep slope, partly concealed by its parachute.

Now the mission changed from finding an object to lifting a heavy and dangerous one without losing it again.

An improvised attempt to catch and raise the weapon failed. The bomb slipped farther down the slope. The Navy had discovered its target, but no longer had it securely in reach.

Alvin went back. By early April the weapon had been located again, deeper than before.

This time the team brought in an unmanned, cable-controlled vehicle called CURV. The plan was to attach a line and recover the bomb. Instead, CURV became tangled in the parachute lines. The machine intended to free the weapon was itself trapped beside it.

The officers faced an awkward choice: abandon the attempt, or use the accidental tangle as a connection.

They raised CURV and the bomb together, then brought the weapon aboard the USS Petrel on April 7, about eighty days after the collision.

The most difficult object in the search was finally back in American hands. Yet this was not the end of the Palomares story.

The bomb under the sea had remained intact. On land, the two bombs whose conventional charges had burst had spread radioactive contamination. Workers carried out cleanup, but some affected ground remained. In 2026, sixty years after the crash, Spanish authorities were still managing restricted and monitored areas and discussing how to remove remaining contaminated soil.

So the recovery had two very different endings. A fisherman's observation helped bring a lost nuclear weapon out of deep water. The accident's legacy in the village did not come up with it.
