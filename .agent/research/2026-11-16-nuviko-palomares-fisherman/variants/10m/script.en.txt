How could a fisherman identify a hydrogen bomb that the United States military could not find? Why did a successful deep-sea discovery nearly turn into a second disappearance? And why, sixty years later, was the same Spanish village still asking what should happen to contaminated ground?

The first part of this story ended with a bomb on the deck of a ship. The second part is still not fully settled.

On January 17, 1966, an American B-52 bomber was refuelling from a KC-135 tanker over southern Spain. This was the Cold War. The bomber was carrying four thermonuclear weapons. During the refuelling manoeuvre the aircraft collided. Both broke apart, and seven crew members died.

The wreckage came down around Palomares, a farming and fishing community near the Mediterranean. So did the four bombs.

There was no nuclear detonation. That distinction matters: a hydrogen bomb did not explode as a nuclear weapon over the village. But the ordinary explosive components in two bombs detonated when they hit the ground. Radioactive material was scattered over fields and nearby land.

For the people searching the wreckage, the situation divided into two jobs. They had to locate every weapon, and they had to deal with the contamination already on the ground.

Three bombs were recovered on land. The fourth was missing.

That sounds like a simple counting problem, until you look at the terrain. Aircraft fragments were spread over an area of countryside near the sea. The bomb might have been hidden among the debris, carried farther by its parachute, or fallen into the water. Searching the land did not reveal it.

An account from a fisherman changed the direction of the operation.

Francisco Simó Orts had been fishing offshore when he saw something coming down toward the Mediterranean under a parachute. He believed at first that it might be a member of the aircrew. That was a reasonable interpretation of what he could see from his boat, but the Americans already knew what had happened to the men aboard both aircraft.

The missing object on the parachute might not have been a man at all.

It might have been the fourth hydrogen bomb.

The clue narrowed the search. It did not solve it. The fisherman could describe where he had been and roughly what he had seen, not put a marker on an object hundreds of metres beneath moving water.

The Navy brought ships, divers and specialised equipment to the Spanish coast. Among them was a submersible called Alvin. It had been built for oceanographic research, not for recovering military weapons from a steep underwater slope.

That difference would become important.

A ship at the surface can measure its position. A small vehicle working far below still has to establish where it is in relation to an uneven seabed. A clue that is useful on a chart can leave an enormous area to examine underwater. Visibility was limited. Slopes could carry an object down into deeper water.

Alvin's crew made dive after dive. From the surface, the search could seem like a long succession of ships moving through the same patch of sea. Below, each dive meant trying to distinguish one unfamiliar object from rocks and wreckage, then finding a way back to it.

The searchers were also working with a young technology. Alvin could place human observers at depths that ordinary divers could not inspect for long. But the team later acknowledged that the machine lacked the sort of practical underwater navigation system such a search demanded.

Their eventual success would become part of the reason to improve it.

In March, after weeks of work, Alvin found what the crews had been hunting. The bomb lay on the seabed, associated with its parachute, near a steep slope.

That was the discovery everyone had been waiting for. It was not the end of the mission.

The weapon was too large and awkward to retrieve casually. A recovery attempt used an improvised hook to catch it. The hook failed, and the bomb slipped farther down the slope.

Imagine the problem without imagining a dramatic countdown that nobody recorded. A heavy object has been found in deep water. The team tries to secure it. The connection fails. The seabed falls away. Suddenly the one position they had worked so hard to establish is no longer reliable.

They had to search again.

Alvin returned to the area. Its crew also had to take care around the parachute: lines and fabric could catch a submersible as readily as they could catch a lifting hook. By early April, the searchers had located the bomb again, at a greater depth.

The Navy had recovered the location twice, but still needed a dependable way to recover the weapon itself.

This is where another vehicle entered the story: CURV, an unmanned underwater machine controlled by cable from a ship.

Its task was more mechanical than Alvin's. Instead of sending people down to identify a shape, operators could use the vehicle to connect lifting equipment to the bomb. They were trying to convert a sighting into a firm attachment.

The attempt did not go according to that plan.

CURV became tangled in the lines of the bomb's parachute. The recovery device was now attached to the thing it was supposed to free, but not in the way anyone had intended. A normal rescue of the machine risked losing the weapon yet again.

The officers chose to take advantage of the accidental connection. CURV and the bomb would be lifted together.

Once the assembly had been brought into shallower water, divers were able to secure cables. On April 7 the weapon was brought onto the USS Petrel.

The fourth bomb had spent roughly eighty days in the sea.

That final lift is an ending with everything a documentary seems to need: the witness's clue, a long search, a discovery that failed, a second discovery, a machine getting caught and an unexpected way to use that mistake.

But if we end the story there, we leave out what the residents of Palomares were left with.

Remember that two other bombs had broken open on land. Their conventional charges had scattered plutonium-bearing material. The sea search did not remove those particles from the fields.

Workers cleaned up affected areas after the accident. Contaminated soil was removed and some of it shipped to the United States. That was a substantial operation, but it did not produce a permanently settled account of what remained.

The public picture was more reassuring than the long-term problem. In 1966 a Spanish minister and an American ambassador took a widely photographed swim to show confidence in the local coast. A photograph of people swimming, however, cannot establish that every contaminated patch of land has been cleaned.

Decades later, Spanish institutions were still monitoring the area. Some parcels had restrictions on their use. Scientists tracked residual material and the potential for it to be disturbed.

By the sixtieth anniversary in January 2026, Spain's EFE news agency was reporting roughly fifty thousand cubic metres of contaminated soil still awaiting a final solution. That is a volume estimate from the contemporary reporting, not a claim that every part of Palomares is unsafe.

The head of Spain's CIEMAT research agency also gave an important qualification. Its monitoring programme estimated annual public exposure at under one tenth of a millisievert. The agency did not report disease it could directly attribute to the remaining contamination. It said the older cleanup plan was partly obsolete and that Spain lacked an approved repository for the radioactive waste that a new operation would produce.

Those statements can coexist. Officials may assess current exposure as low, and still need to decide what to do with material that is not supposed to remain there indefinitely. Residents and environmental organisations have continued to call for removal. Different parties disagree about urgency and responsibility.

This is the part of the accident that cannot be solved by pointing to the recovered bomb in a museum photograph. The problem is no longer finding a single metal object. It is identifying, managing, transporting and agreeing where to store contaminated soil, across changing governments and decades of negotiations.

The fishermen and the submarine crews were solving a question with a location: where did the fourth bomb go? Once they found it, they could try a different tool, change the lifting plan and bring it to the surface.

The people dealing with the land face a different question: where should the remaining contaminated material go, and who will complete that work?

One of the Cold War's most extraordinary searches took about eighty days. Sixty years later, the bomb was long gone from the seabed, but Palomares was still living with the unfinished part of the accident.
