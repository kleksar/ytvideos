How can a dam kill a town without breaking? And what does a mountain have to do with it?

In the Italian Alps, the Vajont dam was an extraordinary wall of concrete. It stood more than two hundred and sixty metres above the valley floor. Downstream lay Longarone, a town near the Piave River. Above the reservoir rose Mount Toc.

The wall was built to withstand water. But the land beside the water had a different problem.

Geologists identified an old landslide inside Mount Toc. Cracks appeared. In November 1960, part of the slope slipped into the reservoir. The operators monitored movement and changed the water level. They also built a tunnel so water could pass if another slide blocked the lake.

Those precautions did not establish that the town below was safe. The most dangerous question was how much rock could move, and how quickly.

On the evening of October ninth, 1963, the mountain answered.

Around eleven at night, roughly two hundred and seventy million cubic metres of rock moved into the lake in less than a minute. Water surged up both sides of the reservoir. Part of it went over the dam and rushed down the narrow gorge.

Longarone and nearby communities were devastated. Around nineteen hundred people died.

The concrete arch remained standing.

That was the terrible contradiction. The dam had not burst. It had held back the lake as designed, but it could not hold back a wave driven over its crest by a collapsing mountain.

Afterward, investigators examined the warnings, the monitoring, and the decisions made while the slope was moving. Italy opened inquiries and criminal proceedings; the trial records are now preserved as a major historical archive.

The Vajont disaster was not simply a story of weak concrete. The wall survived. The people below it did not.
