A wave of molasses raced through Boston faster than people could run.

It was January 1919. On a crowded street near the harbor, a steel tank had just burst. More than two million gallons of thick, dark syrup poured into the neighborhood.

The liquid struck buildings, damaged the elevated railway and swept people off their feet. A nearby firehouse was hit. Molasses sounds slow when it comes out of a jar. This was an enormous moving mass, and estimates put its first surge at roughly thirty-five miles an hour.

People rushed to help. Police officers, sailors and medical workers pulled survivors from the wreckage. But as the spilled molasses cooled in Boston's winter air, it became harder to move through. Getting someone free was not like washing syrup from a kitchen floor.

Twenty-one people died. About a hundred and fifty were injured.

There was another reason this disaster was so disturbing. The tank had warned the neighborhood for years.

Built in 1915 to store molasses for industry, it leaked through its seams. Children reportedly collected the drips in pails. Instead of fixing the underlying weakness, the company had the tank painted brown, making the leaks less visible.

After the collapse, its owner argued that saboteurs might have attacked it. An investigation and years of lawsuits pointed elsewhere. A court-appointed auditor concluded that the company had been negligent. The tank's steel walls were too thin for the load they had to hold.

This wasn't a freak accident caused by an unusually sticky substance. It was a deadly failure of a structure that people already knew was leaking.
