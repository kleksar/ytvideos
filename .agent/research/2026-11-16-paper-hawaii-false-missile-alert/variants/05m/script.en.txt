How could an entire American state be warned of a missile attack when there was no missile? And if officials discovered the error almost immediately, why couldn't they take the warning back?

On the morning of January 13, 2018, Hawaii was already familiar with the possibility of a missile threat. Then, at 8:07, an official emergency warning appeared on phones across the islands. It told people to seek immediate shelter. The last line said this was not a drill.

It was a message designed to be believed. It arrived through the same emergency channels people relied on for real danger, and it appeared on television too. News reports from that morning show people seeking cover in bathrooms and garages. Some families called relatives. Others tried to find out how much time they might have.

Behind the warning was a practice exercise that had gone badly wrong.

That morning, the midnight crew at Hawaii's emergency management agency was handing over to the day crew. The outgoing supervisor decided to run an unannounced missile drill during the changeover. That was supposed to prepare operators for an especially difficult moment.

But the supervisors themselves misunderstood who would be taking part. The incoming supervisor thought the exercise involved the outgoing shift. So the people about to receive the practice call weren't being supervised as intended.

At 8:05, a recorded message was played to the day-shift warning officers. It began by announcing an exercise and ended the same way. In between, however, the recording contained the wording used for a real missile warning — including the claim that it was not a drill.

Most of the warning officers understood it was training. The person at the sending terminal said something different afterwards: according to their written account, they heard the real-warning wording and believed an attack was happening. Federal investigators never interviewed that officer directly, so they could not fully verify the account.

Now comes the second failure. A live alert and a test alert could be created through the same software interface. The officer selected the live template, and a confirmation window asked whether they wanted to send the alert. It did not force the sender to check the complete warning again. No second officer had to approve it.

At 8:07, the system sent the message.

One minute later, the officer saw the warning on a personal phone. By 8:09, Hawaii's governor had been told it was a mistake. At 8:12, the agency canceled the alert internally. But cancellation inside the system was not the same as sending an unmistakable correction to everyone who had received it.

That third failure turned a short-lived internal mistake into a public ordeal.

The agency had a prepared template for telling the state about an incoming missile. It had no equally ready template for announcing that the original warning was false. The officials who knew the truth couldn't simply press a matching button and make the first message disappear.

At 8:20, the agency posted on Facebook and Twitter that no missile was heading toward Hawaii. That helped people who saw or heard about those posts. It didn't reach everyone who had received the official alert, and a social-media post did not carry the same authority as the original emergency message on a locked phone.

Authorities still had to agree on and prepare a new broadcast correction. At 8:31, an agency official began creating the corrective messages. The public finally received the follow-up emergency alert at 8:45.

Thirty-eight minutes had passed. The warning had been false for all thirty-eight.

The investigation changed the explanation that many people first heard. This wasn't safely described as somebody simply hitting the wrong button. The drill used contradictory words, supervision failed, one person could send a statewide warning, the testing and live systems weren't clearly separated, and the retraction procedure was missing.

Hawaii responded with requirements for two credentialed officers to validate alerts, advance notice to supervisors for drills, and a prepared false-alert correction.

There was no incoming missile that morning. But the public had been asked to trust a message that the issuing agency could not quickly undo. The most revealing part of the story is not the mistake at 8:07. It's the absence of a simple answer at 8:08: there is no missile.
