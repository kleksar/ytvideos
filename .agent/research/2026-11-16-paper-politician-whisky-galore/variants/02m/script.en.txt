A ship carrying 264,000 bottles of whisky ran aground beside a Scottish island.

It was February 1941. Britain was at war, supplies were rationed, and the cargo ship SS Politician had barely begun its Atlantic voyage when it struck a sandbank near Eriskay.

The crew got safely ashore. Then the islanders learned what was in the hold: thousands of cases of whisky, intended for export.

Fishing boats began making trips out to the wreck. Bottles came back hidden among ordinary cargo and found their way into homes and sheds.

To many islanders, this was salvage: goods rescued from a ship that could no longer sail. To the customs officers, it was something else. The whisky had not been released from duty, and taking it without permission was illegal.

So the real contest began on land. Officers searched homes. Islanders concealed bottles. Some were prosecuted and sent to prison.

There was another twist. The ship also carried Jamaican banknotes, and some of those notes made their way into circulation. Now the authorities were searching for more than whisky.

Finally, officials had the damaged wreck blown up to stop further raids. The islanders could not go back for another load.

But the story escaped them. A novel turned it into a comedy, and a famous film followed. Decades later, divers recovered bottles that survived beneath the water. One ended up in a Scottish maritime museum.
