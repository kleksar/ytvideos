Why did a British customs officer want a shipwreck blown up after its crew had already been rescued? Why did people on a Scottish island see themselves as rescuers while the government treated some of them as criminals? And what was hidden on that ship besides whisky?

The story begins in wartime Britain, with a cargo vessel called SS Politician.

On February third, 1941, the ship left Liverpool for a voyage across the Atlantic, with Jamaica and New Orleans among its destinations. It was carrying everyday goods: vehicles, medicines, tobacco, and supplies intended for sale overseas. In wartime, that cargo mattered. So did the ship, and the sailors responsible for getting it to port.

Among the ordinary freight was an extraordinary quantity of whisky. Around twenty-two thousand cases had been loaded from bonded warehouses in Scotland. More than a quarter of a million bottles were travelling away from the country where they had been made.

Just two days after sailing, bad weather pushed the vessel toward the Outer Hebrides, a string of islands off Scotland's western coast. On the morning of February fifth, the Politician struck a submerged sandbank near Eriskay.

Water entered the ship. She was stranded, and her crew needed to get off. Islanders helped bring the sailors to safety. That was a real rescue, and an uncomplicated one: people were in danger on a damaged vessel, and their neighbours on shore helped them out.

But the rescue brought the islanders close to the holds of the Politician. Soon they learned just how much whisky was on board.

Eriskay was a small community living through wartime restrictions. Supplies were scarce, and ordinary goods were not always easy to obtain. An immense cargo of whisky was suddenly sitting in a wreck just offshore.

Fishing boats went out to the ship. Bottles came ashore in quantities that officials would later try to calculate, though precise estimates vary. They were carried into homes, concealed in buildings, and hidden in places where a search might not find them.

Some island accounts describe whisky being brought back in baskets, with ponies used to move it along the shore. The important distinction is not how clever any particular hiding place was. It is that local families and a government department were now giving the same act two completely different names.

The islanders called it salvage. A ship had gone aground. Valuable goods were exposed to the sea. Why leave them there?

Customs officers did not regard the cargo as abandoned property. The whisky had been placed aboard as commercial freight and had not been released from customs control. A stranded vessel did not erase its ownership or the taxes attached to it.

That distinction changed everything. What had looked like a remarkable stroke of luck now meant searches, confiscations and criminal cases.

Local customs officer Charles McColl pressed the investigation. Houses were raided; bottles were seized. Some islanders were taken to court. Several were convicted and given short prison sentences.

Imagine how strange that sequence must have looked from either side of the dispute. The island had first helped sailors escape the wreck. The same wreck then supplied goods that brought police and customs officers into the community. For the authorities, failing to enforce the rules would have meant allowing valuable untaxed cargo to disappear. For residents who understood the removal as salvage, punishment seemed a bitter response to something they believed they could legitimately do.

And the whisky was not the only cargo that had found an unexpected route ashore.

The ship was carrying Jamaican banknotes. These were not souvenirs: they were currency meant for a destination across the ocean. Some escaped the wreck and appeared in circulation. That created a separate problem. A bottle could be hidden, drunk or sold. A banknote could travel far from the coast, change hands repeatedly and make its origin harder to trace.

An account preserved by the Eriskay heritage community says some of those notes were initially treated as almost worthless and even used to light fires. That detail belongs to island recollection rather than a verified inventory of each note. What is established is that the cash was part of the cargo, and that banknotes from the wreck later appeared outside the island.

The government faced another challenge. Even after the first searches, the damaged ship still lay in the water. There were official efforts to salvage what remained, but its presence meant people might keep coming back. Remove one haul, and there could be another.

Officials eventually obtained permission to destroy the remaining wreck with explosives. The idea was not to punish the sailors or to make the wreck safe for a voyage. The ship was already lost. It was to prevent further unauthorized recovery of cargo.

It is an almost absurd image: a vessel survives its original accident long enough to be deliberately blown apart by the authorities who want to stop people removing the whisky.

But the consequences of the disaster did not fit into a single funny ending. People had been prosecuted. Families had been searched. For those who saw themselves as salvors rather than thieves, official action was remembered as injustice. The wreck had delivered a short-lived bounty, then brought lasting arguments about the difference between possession and ownership.

Now comes the part of the story that often gets confused with what really happened.

In 1947, writer Compton Mackenzie published the novel Whisky Galore. In 1949 it became a popular British film comedy. The screen story has invented characters, comic situations and fictional islands. It is not an eyewitness record of who carried which bottle or what the real customs officer said. It took the unusual circumstances of the Politician and made a story that audiences could recognise: a small community finding itself in a battle of wits with official authority.

That film helped keep the ship's name alive. Decades later, when people told the story, the comic version could be easier to remember than a list of court cases. Yet the actual island and actual wreck remained.

In 1987, diver George Currie recovered a bottle from the Politician. This was not a prop produced for a film or a replica made for tourists. It was an object retrieved from the real ship, decades after it ran aground. The whisky inside was not regarded as safe to drink, but its age and origin made it historically interesting.

The Scottish Maritime Museum later acquired that bottle. Its display also included material connected to the diver who found it. The bottle had survived the wreck, the authorities' attempts to stop salvage, and many years underwater.

That alone would be a fitting ending. But the artifacts kept resurfacing in public life. In September 2026, a local news report described another auction of bottles linked to the Politician. By that point the cargo had become more valuable as evidence of the story than it ever could have been as an ordinary drink.

There is an irony here, but it is a real one. In 1941, officials tried to stop islanders taking bottles off the wreck because the cargo still belonged to someone. Decades later, museums and collectors sought surviving bottles precisely because of the forbidden trips, the courtroom arguments and the ship that was destroyed.

The wider legend is often told as a story of lucky islanders and angry officials. That misses the rescue at the beginning and the cost to people who faced criminal charges. It also misses the second cargo: banknotes intended for Jamaica that instead helped turn a local shipwreck into a much larger investigation.

The central question was never whether a bottle could float ashore. It was whether a shipwreck changed the rules for what happened to its contents. The two sides answered differently, and the conflict ended with arrests, prosecutions and the destruction of the remaining vessel.

But nobody could destroy the tale. The sailors were saved. Some islanders were punished. The wreck was blown apart. The novel and film made it famous. And, years later, a bottle from the actual ship sat in a museum — a small piece of glass outlasting the argument over who had the right to take it.
