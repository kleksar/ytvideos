In 1974, a ship that claimed to be mining the Pacific was trying to steal a Soviet submarine.

The submarine, K-129, had vanished six years earlier. It carried nuclear missiles. Soviet ships searched for it, then gave up. American intelligence found the wreck more than three miles down.

Recovering it could reveal how Soviet weapons and submarine systems worked. But no one had ever lifted such a massive object from that depth. And if Moscow saw the operation, the mission might end before it began.

So the CIA asked billionaire Howard Hughes to provide a cover story. His company would supposedly mine valuable lumps of metal from the seabed.

A special ship, the Hughes Glomar Explorer, was built around a hole in its own hull. Engineers hid a giant metal claw beneath the water, then lowered it on sections of steel pipe.

On July fourth, 1974, the ship reached the wreck. Nearby Soviet vessels watched the supposed mining expedition.

The claw closed around part of K-129. Slowly, the ship began lifting it. Then, high above the seabed, the submarine section broke. A large part fell back into the darkness.

The remaining section came aboard. It contained the bodies of six Soviet sailors, who received a burial at sea. What else the Americans learned remains partly secret.

The CIA planned another attempt. But documents stolen from a Hughes office helped journalists expose the operation in 1975. The second recovery was canceled.

The United States had built an extraordinary machine and recovered only part of its target. The cover story had worked at sea; it failed on land.

And when Soviet ships looked at the Glomar Explorer during that first mission, what were they actually watching?
