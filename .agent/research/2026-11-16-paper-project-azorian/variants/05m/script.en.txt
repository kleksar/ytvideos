How do you steal a submarine that belongs to a rival superpower when it lies more than three miles underwater? How do you hide a ship big enough to lift it? And what happens when the submarine breaks on the way up?

In 1968, the Soviet submarine K-129 left the Kamchatka Peninsula for a patrol in the Pacific. It carried nuclear-armed missiles. Then contact was lost. The Soviet Union searched, failed to find the wreck, and ended its effort.

American intelligence located the submarine on the ocean floor, far northwest of Hawaii. It was too deep for an ordinary salvage operation. But it offered something Washington badly wanted: a chance to examine a rival's missile systems, equipment, and possibly its secret communications.

The problem was not simply finding a crane strong enough. Nobody had recovered anything of this size from such a depth. A ship would have to hold its position in ocean currents while lowering heavy equipment through miles of water. And the Soviet Union could not be allowed to understand what it was watching.

This is where an apparently civilian mining ship becomes the center of a Cold War intelligence operation.

The CIA approached billionaire Howard Hughes. His name would help explain an unusual vessel supposedly collecting manganese nodules—small mineral-rich lumps lying on the deep seabed. The cover story was public. The real purpose was not.

Engineers built the Hughes Glomar Explorer around that deception. From a distance it resembled an experimental industrial vessel, with a drilling-style tower. Inside, it had a docking well called a moon pool. Doors beneath the ship could open to the sea, allowing equipment to come and go without being lifted visibly onto the deck.

The heart of the system was a giant gripping device, nicknamed Clementine. It was built under cover, then transferred into the ship from a submerged barge. The idea was simple to describe and extraordinarily difficult to perform: lower a claw around a sunken section of submarine, close its jaws, lift the weight, and bring it through the ship's hidden opening.

The crew could not lower a three-mile cable and hope for the best. The ship used a long column made from steel pipe sections. It added those sections as the claw descended, then removed them during the lift. Every step depended on the ship staying aligned with a wreck lying far below, where no one on deck could simply look down and see the jaws close.

On July fourth, 1974, the Glomar Explorer arrived over the wreck. Soviet vessels came near enough to observe the strange expedition. To them, it appeared to be a mining project. The true test was taking place beneath the waterline.

The claw reached the submarine. It took hold. And the Glomar Explorer started hauling its secret cargo upward.

The system had survived years of design and construction. It had reached the exact place where American intelligence said the wreck lay. It had grasped its target under Soviet observation. But success still depended on the submarine remaining in one piece.

Partway through the lift, the recovered section broke apart. A large part dropped back to the ocean floor. The remaining portion stayed in the gripping device and came aboard.

That is the central result of Project Azorian: neither the complete theft of a Soviet submarine nor the total failure often implied by a dramatic retelling. The ship recovered part of the wreck. Public accounts do not establish everything the United States learned from that material, and not every intelligence result has been released.

The recovered remains included six Soviet submariners. They were buried at sea with military honors, and the ceremony was filmed. In 1992, the film was given to Russian president Boris Yeltsin.

For the CIA, there was still a practical option: return and attempt to recover the section left behind. Planners began preparing another mission. At sea, the bizarre cover story had survived contact with Soviet ships.

On land, a completely different chain of events was destroying it. Before the first voyage, thieves had broken into an office connected with Hughes and taken documents tying the company to the CIA. The effort to find those papers brought investigators into the story. Rumors reached reporters.

In February 1975, the Los Angeles Times connected the supposed mining ship with a submarine recovery. In March, television reporting spread the revelation. The American government neither confirmed nor denied the reports—an approach later associated with the name Glomar.

Once the Soviets understood what the vessel was for, another quiet visit to the wreck was no longer realistic. The second recovery was canceled.

Think about the two separate tests the CIA faced. One was physical: could a giant claw travel down through the ocean, catch a submarine and lift it? Partly, yes. The other was political: could an enormous purpose-built ship make its mission invisible? At sea, for long enough, yes. After the documents surfaced, no.

And that is why a deep-sea mining ship became famous not for the metal it collected, but for the submarine it was never supposed to be seen retrieving.
