How could the United States steal a Soviet nuclear-armed submarine without entering Soviet waters? Why did it need a ship that looked like a mining platform, a giant mechanical claw, and a hidden opening in the hull? And how could a burglary in California ruin a mission thousands of miles away?

In 1968, a Soviet submarine named K-129 left the Kamchatka Peninsula on a patrol into the North Pacific. It carried nuclear-armed ballistic missiles. Then the submarine disappeared with its crew. The cause of its sinking remains disputed; several explanations have been offered, but none should be treated as established in this story.

Soviet forces searched the Pacific and ultimately abandoned the search. American intelligence, however, located the wreck, far northwest of Hawaii and roughly sixteen and a half thousand feet below the ocean surface.

That depth changes everything. It is more than three miles. The pressure is crushing, the sea is dark, and a salvage vessel on the surface cannot simply send divers down to connect a tow rope. Any machine lowered to the wreck would have to travel through kilometres of water while the ship above it held position.

The prize was enormous. A lost Soviet missile submarine could offer clues about missile design, onboard communications, mechanical systems, and how Soviet submarines were built. American officials hoped to study an adversary's equipment in ways impossible from a photograph or intercepted radio transmission. Whether the operation would actually yield its most valuable secrets was another question.

And there was a second problem, at least as difficult as the depth: this was Soviet military property. Washington could not openly announce that it intended to bring a rival country's armed submarine onto an American ship.

To make the attempted theft possible, the CIA would need to disguise an industrial project on a scale that would be impossible to hide in an ordinary shipyard.

Engineers considered the challenge for years. Some proposed ways of making the wreck buoyant from below. Ultimately the plan settled on something that sounded less like an intelligence operation than an enormous arcade machine: a claw would close around a section of the submarine and lift it, piece by piece, all the way to the surface.

But the claw was only the end of the system. First, someone had to construct a ship large enough to handle it. Then the ship had to remain positioned above a target miles down. And every conspicuous piece of equipment had to fit the public story.

The CIA reached out to Howard Hughes, the billionaire associated with aircraft and ambitious engineering projects. Hughes agreed to lend his name to a deep-sea mining enterprise. Its stated target was manganese nodules, mineral-rich objects scattered across parts of the ocean floor.

Mining these nodules was a plausible explanation for an unusual, expensive vessel fitted with deep-water machinery. People could ask why it had an industrial tower, why it needed heavy equipment, or why it spent weeks over one patch of ocean. The public answer was ready before those questions were asked.

The ship was called the Hughes Glomar Explorer. It carried structures resembling those of an offshore drilling vessel. Its most important space, however, lay inside the hull.

Engineers created a central docking well known as a moon pool. Instead of lifting the submarine over the side where a passing ship could see it, the Glomar could open doors beneath itself and haul the object into a sheltered internal space. The ship would then close the opening.

The capture device, nicknamed Clementine, looked like an immense metal claw. Building it openly next to the vessel would have raised questions the cover story could not easily answer. So it was assembled inside a separate enclosed barge, HMB-1. The barge was submerged beneath the Glomar Explorer, which took the claw aboard through its hidden well.

Think about that transfer. To conceal the machine designed to steal a submarine, engineers first had to move it into the ship without showing it above the water.

The equipment also had to reach the wreck accurately. A cable alone could not provide the control required for this operation. The Glomar used a system of heavy steel pipes, joined into a long column. The crew added sections as the claw descended. During the lift they would remove them in reverse order.

That meant the ship's position mattered constantly. The surface vessel could move in ocean currents; the submarine could not. An error at the top would travel all the way down through miles of pipe. Meanwhile, any Soviet ship that came nearby would see what appeared to be unusual mining equipment working for a wealthy businessman.

The Glomar Explorer was built in the early 1970s and tested before the recovery. It was so wide that, according to later historical accounts, it could not pass through the Panama Canal on its journey to the Pacific. The vessel went the long way around South America.

Those detours did not alter the central plan. In the summer of 1974, the ship sailed toward the known location of K-129. The operation had taken six years from the submarine's disappearance to the attempt to raise it.

On July fourth, the Glomar Explorer arrived at the recovery area.

Soviet vessels approached during the operation. The American ship was huge and its activity was plainly visible. Its real purpose was not. The men aboard could not make the Soviet observers disappear. They could only continue to behave like a crew carrying out a complex ocean-mining project.

While the watchers saw a ship with drilling equipment, the claw was descending through the water toward a wreck they had not found.

Eventually the gripping device reached the submarine section and closed around it. The crew began reversing the process: raising the pipe column, removing steel sections, lifting the captured object bit by bit. This was not a fast extraction. Each stage depended on the load surviving the next.

At this point the engineering plan had done something unprecedented. The secret vessel had reached the right place, lowered an enormous machine through more than three miles of water, and caught part of a Soviet submarine. The remaining distance upward was suddenly the only thing separating an extraordinary operation from the cargo inside the ship.

Then the load broke.

During the ascent, part of the captured submarine section fell away and plunged back to the seabed. Different published accounts disagree on precisely how high the section was and on the mechanical details of the break. The established outcome is narrower and clear: a substantial part was lost, but the claw kept hold of another part.

That remaining section reached the Glomar Explorer.

The CIA had not recovered everything it wanted. Public accounts also do not establish everything it did recover or learn. Some items discussed in popular retellings are possibilities, not independently confirmed finds. The safest conclusion is the most interesting one: the mission had partly succeeded at an almost impossible physical task while failing to bring up the entire intended target.

The human result should not disappear behind the machinery. The recovered section contained the bodies of six Soviet sailors. The Americans conducted a formal burial at sea with military honors and filmed it. In 1992, the film was provided to Russian president Boris Yeltsin.

For the submarine's crew, there was no rescue to celebrate. Their loss had happened years before the Glomar arrived. The ceremony marked the difference between recovering military technology and recovering the remains of men who had died aboard it.

But the American planners had not yet finished with the wreck. A second mission was considered to recover the portion left behind. The special ship still existed, and its equipment had already proved that a partial lift was possible.

The obstacle that stopped the next attempt began not in the Pacific, but in Los Angeles.

In June 1974, before the Glomar had departed for the wreck, burglars entered an office associated with Hughes' business organization. Among the stolen documents was material connecting Hughes to the CIA and the vessel. Investigators became involved. The search for the documents drew attention to a relationship that was supposed to remain hidden.

The press began piecing together the story. CIA director William Colby personally asked journalists who had learned about the operation not to publish it. The silence did not last.

On February seventh, 1975, the Los Angeles Times reported connections between Hughes' vessel, the intelligence agency, and the submarine. A month later, Jack Anderson discussed the operation on national television. An enormous ship that had been capable of working under Soviet observation without exposing its mission was being identified in American news reports.

The White House would neither confirm nor deny the allegations. That phrasing became associated with the name Glomar, and later entered the language of government responses to sensitive information requests.

For the CIA, this was not merely embarrassing publicity. The Soviet Union could now connect the vessel with the wreck. By mid-1975, a Soviet ship was assigned to watch the recovery site. The possibility of returning quietly for the missing section had evaporated. The White House canceled further recovery efforts.

The project left behind several different results. It showed that an enormous submerged object could be caught and partially raised from extraordinary depth. It recovered some of the lost submarine and six sailors' remains. Its intelligence yield is still not completely public. And a burglary helped expose a naval operation that nearby Soviet ships had failed to identify while it was happening.

The remarkable failure was not that the Glomar Explorer looked suspicious. It was that a believable mining disguise lasted long enough for the secret lift, while stolen office papers later brought the whole story into the open.

A submarine vanished in the Pacific. A mining ship went looking for minerals. A claw came back holding something else. And the second voyage never happened—because by then the world knew what the ship was really built to retrieve.
