A brand-new warship was leaving Stockholm. Twenty minutes later, it was on the bottom of the harbour.

This was Vasa, a Swedish ship built to carry heavy cannon into war in 1628. There was no enemy fleet waiting for it. Its first danger was a breeze.

Before the voyage, sailors had run from one side of the upper deck to the other to test the ship's balance. The ship rolled so much that the test was stopped. Yet the sailing went ahead.

Crowds gathered on the shore. Four sails were raised. The lower gunports were open, ready for a ceremonial salute.

The first gust made the ship lean. It straightened again. Then a stronger gust pushed it over farther.

Water poured through the open gunports. Those holes were meant for firing cannon, not for taking in the sea. With too much weight high above the water and too little stability below, Vasa could not recover.

People jumped overboard. Most escaped, but at least thirty died. The pride of the Swedish fleet sank within sight of the city, after sailing barely a kilometre.

What makes this more than a story about a bad launch is what happened later.

For over three centuries, the ship lay in dark mud. In 1956 it was found again. Divers worked beneath its hull, pulling steel cables through tunnels in the seabed. In 1961, Vasa rose to the surface.

Today, the original ship stands in a museum. The failed voyage left a remarkably complete window into the lives and work of people from four centuries ago.
