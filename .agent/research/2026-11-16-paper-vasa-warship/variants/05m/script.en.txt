How could a new warship sink before it had even left its home city? Why did its crew have a warning before the voyage, and why did that warning not stop it? And how did a disaster in 1628 leave behind a ship we can still walk around today?

Sweden was fighting for power around the Baltic Sea. King Gustav II Adolf had ordered a large, heavily armed warship called Vasa. Its hull was built in Stockholm, and its sides carried two levels of gunports. The ship was supposed to sail out, join the fleet and carry soldiers toward the war.

Instead, its first voyage would last about twenty minutes.

The ship was impressive from shore. It carried sixty-four guns and elaborate carved decorations. But a warship has to do more than look powerful. It must stand upright when the wind pushes on its sails.

Before sailing, a group of men tested Vasa by running across the upper deck from one side to the other. This was a simple way to make the hull roll and see whether it would right itself. The motion became so alarming that the test was stopped.

The problem had been visible while the ship was still tied up.

On August tenth, 1628, crowds lined Stockholm's waterfront. Vasa eased away from the harbour below the royal castle. There were sailors and officers aboard, along with other passengers who would not be making the whole voyage. Four sails went up. The gunports were open for a salute.

For a short time, the new warship moved as planned.

Then a gust of wind filled the sails. Vasa leaned to one side. It came upright again. A stronger gust followed, and this time the ship heeled farther.

The lowest row of gunports was now close to the water. As the deck tilted, water rushed through those open holes into the lower gun deck. The extra water made an already unstable ship harder to recover. It leaned farther, took on more water, and began to sink.

People tried to get clear. Most survived, but at least thirty did not. Vasa went down in full view of the city, after a journey of barely a kilometre.

So why had a vessel built for battle been defeated by an ordinary gust?

The problem was not that the crew forgot how to sail. The shape and weight of the ship left it dangerously top-heavy. It was too tall and narrow for the balance it needed. The heavy structure and equipment high above the water could not be compensated for by enough weight low in the hull. When the sails pulled sideways, there was too little restoring force to bring it back upright.

The open gunports turned that weakness into a rapid flooding disaster. Closing them might have changed when water entered, but it would not have made the ship well balanced. And saying simply that the king wanted more cannon does not explain the whole physical failure.

The next day, officials began investigating. They questioned the captain and others, and the surviving records discuss the ship's instability. Yet the voyage could not be undone, and Vasa stayed on the bottom.

That might have been the last chapter. But the wreck was preserved beneath the harbour, in mud and relatively sheltered water.

In 1956, a searcher named Anders Franzén found traces of a large wooden ship. Divers confirmed that an old warship was still there, with much of its structure standing. The new question was whether anyone could raise it without tearing it apart.

Divers worked beneath the hull, cutting six tunnels through the mud. Steel cables went through those tunnels and up to floating pontoons. By controlling the water inside the pontoons, the salvage team could lift the heavy ship a little, move it toward shallower water and set it down again.

They repeated that difficult process in stages. Then came months of plugging leaks and making the old hull watertight enough for the final lift.

On April twenty-fourth, 1961, people gathered on Stockholm's waterfront once more. This time they were waiting for the ship to come up, not sail away. At three minutes past nine, the first timbers rose above the surface.

The warship was back after 333 years.

Archaeologists found tools, clothing and personal belongings inside. Vasa became a museum ship, rather than the fighting ship it was built to be.

The answer to the first question is stark: the warning test had shown instability, and the next gust exposed the same flaw in front of a crowd. The open gunports made the loss sudden. The answer to the last question is stranger: what failed in twenty minutes survived underwater long enough to show us a whole lost working world.

Its first journey ended at the bottom of Stockholm harbour. Its second took more than three centuries.
