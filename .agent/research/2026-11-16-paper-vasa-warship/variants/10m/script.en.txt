Why was a new warship allowed to sail after a test showed it might capsize? How did it sink within sight of Stockholm in just twenty minutes? And when people finally brought it up 333 years later, why was it still in danger?

The ship was Vasa. In August 1628, it was supposed to be an answer to Sweden's enemies, not a disaster in its own harbour.

The Swedish king, Gustav II Adolf, was fighting for control of the Baltic. A large warship could carry troops, protect shipping and fire heavy guns from more than one deck. Vasa was built in Stockholm, where carpenters, blacksmiths, sailmakers and carvers worked to turn oak timber into a floating weapon.

There was one problem they could not decorate away.

Vasa had a tall hull with two rows of gunports, a powerful battery of sixty-four guns, heavy masts, rigging and an elaborate carved stern. It looked imposing beside the waterfront. But much of its structure stood high above the water, and the underwater part of the hull was relatively narrow.

Imagine a heavy object balanced high on a narrow base. The slightest shove matters much more than it would if the weight were lower. A ship also has ballast to help it right itself. Vasa did not have enough stability for its shape and weight.

That was not a secret discovered only after the sinking.

While the ship was still at the pier, Admiral Klas Fleming watched men run across the upper deck from one side to the other. They were testing how far the vessel would lean under a shifting load. Vasa rolled so badly that the test was stopped.

The ship was still expected to sail.

The king was away with the army, and the vessel was already late. But the surviving record does not establish one decisive reason the departure was not postponed. What we can say is narrower: a test was stopped because the ship rolled dangerously, and the ship sailed anyway.

On Sunday, August tenth, people packed Stockholm's shores to watch. Small boats moved around the new warship. There were crew members aboard, and other passengers who were only going part of the way.

Four of Vasa's sails were raised. The lower gunports were open for a salute, with cannon pointing out over the water.

The vessel moved away from the castle and into the harbour. For a short while it was doing precisely what its builders intended.

Then a gust filled the sails.

Vasa leaned. The sailors and spectators saw the hull tilt, but the ship came upright again. That first recovery is important. For a moment the danger looked temporary.

The next gust was stronger. Vasa heeled farther, until the lower gunports were close enough to the water for waves to enter.

The ship had holes cut into its sides because it was built to fight. Now those same openings let the harbour pour into the gun deck.

Water was heavy. As it spread inside, the extra weight pulled the leaning ship farther over. More water came through the ports. A failure of balance became a failure the crew could not reverse by trimming a sail.

The great warship began to sink.

Captain Söfring Hansson ordered the sails eased and the gunports closed, but there was scarcely time. He himself was dragged below the surface by the sinking ship before reaching air again. Vice Admiral Erik Jönsson went below to check the guns; a ladder collapsed as he tried to escape, and he was pulled from the water badly hurt.

Small boats raced to pick up people who had jumped overboard. Others reached for the masts still sticking above the water. About thirty crew members and guests died, most trapped below decks. Among the dead was Hans Jonsson, a former captain who had remained aboard for the first voyage.

The whole first voyage had lasted about twenty minutes. Vasa had travelled barely a kilometre.

The king's new weapon was on the bottom, with much of its armament still aboard. There had been no battle and no enemy broadside.

The disaster did not end when the survivors reached shore. The next day authorities began an inquiry. The officers said they had done their duty. The builders pointed to dimensions the king had approved. The original designer, Henrik Hybertsson, had already died.

The investigators could describe the fault: there was too little hull below the water to support the heavy structure above. The gunports turned that instability into rapid flooding. But the inquiry never established a person to punish, and no one was punished.

Nor did the salvage teams of the seventeenth century manage to raise the vessel. Divers later recovered much of its valuable bronze armament, working from a diving bell, while the hull stayed in the mud.

Then came a decision that might have ended the story very differently. In 1920 two brothers sought permission to blast old wrecks in this area of the harbour to retrieve dark oak for furniture. The authorities refused. The application did not identify Vasa as a proven individual target, but the ban helped keep the wrecks of this stretch intact.

Three centuries after the disaster, Vasa was still there. The challenge now was to find a way to bring up the whole ship, not just its guns.

Anders Franzén had spent years searching records and dragging a homemade coring tool along the harbour bottom. At first he searched the wrong side of the shipping channel. New archival clues brought him across to the other shore.

On August twenty-fifth, 1956, the tool returned with dark waterlogged oak. A second sample suggested something larger than stray driftwood. Franzén persuaded the navy to investigate.

Diver Per-Edvin Fälting descended through nearly opaque water. By touch he found a vertical wooden wall with one row of square openings, then another above it. A two-gundeck warship had survived below the modern city. The evidence pointed to Vasa.

Finding it was not the same as raising it.

The old oak had spent centuries soaked in water. The hull was heavy with mud, and no one could know from the surface whether it would hold together when pulled free. A wrong lift could break what time had preserved.

Franzén won support from the navy, museum authorities and a commercial salvage company. Engineers rejected schemes that would be difficult to control and chose to lift the weight from below.

From 1957 to 1959 divers cut six tunnels beneath the hull. Steel cables passed through them to two pontoons, Oden and Frigg. The pontoons could be flooded to lower them, then pumped out so their rising buoyancy tightened the cables and pulled the wreck upward.

On August twentieth, 1959, Vasa finally broke free of the seabed. But the ship did not break the surface. It was still submerged and loaded with mud. The team moved it into shallower water in eighteen separate lifting stages. Each move put less water above the wreck and offered another chance to inspect it.

Near shelter, the crew closed gunports, plugged holes left by rusted fasteners and reinforced fragile parts. They had to make the old vessel watertight enough to float without its entire weight hanging from the cables.

This was the opposite of the first voyage. The ship had been lost in minutes because a dangerous change happened too fast to stop. The recovery was designed so each dangerous change could be stopped and checked before the next lift.

On April twenty-fourth, 1961, spectators stood along Stockholm's waterfront once again. This time radio and television cameras were there too.

At three minutes past nine, a dark piece of timber broke the surface. More frames emerged. Then carved figures and the outline of the great wooden hull.

Vasa had returned after 333 years.

But sunlight introduced a new danger. Archaeologists stepped into a hull still loaded with mud, water and objects. The weight inside was estimated at more than a thousand tonnes. Pumps worked for ten days to drain the water. The team feared the old structure could buckle before they had emptied it; some of the ballast had to be hauled out through the hatch.

Now came the reason to save the entire ship. Sailors' chests stood near the gun decks. Small coins remained in pockets; tools and shoes lay where their owners had left them. In a locker, excavators found the remains of six sails still folded together as the sailmaker had delivered them in 1627. Human remains reminded the team that around thirty people had died aboard. At least one can be identified in the written record: former captain Hans Jonsson.

More than thirty thousand objects were recovered during the first excavation months. The challenge was so large that the team searched Stockholm scrap dealers for old bathtubs to hold the wet finds until they could be treated.

And the wood itself was not safe merely because the ship floated. If centuries of soaked oak were allowed to dry suddenly, it could crack and shrink. Conservators sprayed the ship with a water-soluble wax called polyethylene glycol for seventeen years, from 1962 to 1979, then dried it gradually for years more.

The ship now stands in Stockholm's Vasa Museum, but preserving it remains an active engineering problem. Researchers monitor movement and weakening in its timber, and a new support structure is being installed. Raising the hull was the spectacular moment. Keeping it intact is the longer responsibility.

The warning before Vasa's first voyage was real. So was the choice to sail. After twenty minutes, that choice had cost lives. The inquiry did not punish anyone.

Three centuries later, Franzén and the salvage team made a different choice: to recover the entire hull instead of stripping it for timber or guns. It took careful lifts, a desperate excavation and decades of conservation.

Vasa is still being protected from collapse. And that is what makes its second voyage worth telling: the ship intended to carry cannon into battle has instead carried the belongings and traces of its people into our time.
