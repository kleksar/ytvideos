Why did Sweden send a warship out when a test had already shown it could roll dangerously? How did a breeze sink that ship before it left Stockholm? And why, more than three centuries later, did people work for years to bring the same wreck back?

The ship was Vasa. In August 1628, it was supposed to be an answer to Sweden's enemies, not a disaster in its own harbour.

The Swedish king, Gustav II Adolf, was fighting for control of the Baltic. A large warship could carry troops, protect shipping and fire heavy guns from more than one deck. Vasa was built in Stockholm, where carpenters, blacksmiths, sailmakers and carvers worked to turn oak timber into a floating weapon.

There was one problem they could not decorate away.

Vasa had a tall hull with two rows of gunports, a powerful battery of sixty-four guns, heavy masts, rigging and an elaborate carved stern. It looked imposing beside the waterfront. But much of its structure stood high above the water, and the underwater part of the hull was relatively narrow.

Imagine a heavy object balanced high on a narrow base. The slightest shove matters much more than it would if the weight were lower. A ship also has ballast to help it right itself. Vasa did not have enough stability for its shape and weight.

That was not a secret discovered only after the sinking.

While the ship was still at the pier, Admiral Klas Fleming watched men run across the upper deck from one side to the other. They were testing how far the vessel would lean under a shifting load. Vasa rolled so badly that the test was stopped.

The ship was still expected to sail.

The king was away with the army. The new vessel was overdue, and no one on the quay could simply make it a different ship. There is no need to invent a conversation in which someone orders a doomed voyage. The documented facts are disturbing enough: the test showed the danger, the test stopped, and the launch went ahead.

On Sunday, August tenth, people packed Stockholm's shores to watch. Small boats moved around the new warship. There were crew members aboard, and other passengers who were only going part of the way.

Four of Vasa's sails were raised. The lower gunports were open for a salute, with cannon pointing out over the water.

The vessel moved away from the castle and into the harbour. For a short while it was doing precisely what its builders intended.

Then a gust filled the sails.

Vasa leaned. The sailors and spectators saw the hull tilt, but the ship came upright again. That first recovery is important. For a moment the danger looked temporary.

The next gust was stronger. Vasa heeled farther, until the lower gunports were close enough to the water for waves to enter.

The ship had holes cut into its sides because it was built to fight. Now those same openings let the harbour pour into the gun deck.

Water was heavy. As it spread inside, the extra weight pulled the leaning ship farther over. More water came through the ports. A failure of balance became a failure the crew could not reverse by trimming a sail.

The great warship began to sink.

People jumped into the water and tried to reach safety. Most survived, but around thirty died. Some could not get out of the vessel as it flooded.

The whole first voyage had lasted about twenty minutes. Vasa had travelled barely a kilometre.

The king's new weapon was on the bottom, with much of its armament still aboard. There had been no battle and no enemy broadside.

The disaster raised an uncomfortable question: who was responsible?

The day after the sinking, authorities began questioning the captain and others. Records from later hearings describe a ship that was too heavy above the water, with masts, yards, sails and guns. There was not enough effective ballast below.

Those words describe the underlying problem more precisely than the familiar joke about a king who wanted too many cannon. A demand for firepower was part of the context. But the physical failure came from the relationship between hull form, high weight and righting ability.

Even with its gunports closed, Vasa would still have been a badly balanced vessel. With them open, the first deep heel became a flood.

A ship intended to defend a kingdom had failed a very ordinary task: staying upright in the wind.

People tried to recover it soon after the accident, but the whole hull remained underwater. Divers in the seventeenth century later salvaged many of its valuable bronze cannon. The ship itself slipped from practical attention.

It lay in the mud beneath Stockholm harbour for more than three hundred years.

In 1956, a determined researcher named Anders Franzén was searching for old shipwrecks in the harbour. A small core of dark oak from the bottom helped lead him to a large wooden hull.

Navy divers went down into water so cloudy that they had to feel their way along the wreck. They found two rows of gunports. Even part of a mast was still standing. The structure was not a loose field of timbers; it was recognisably Vasa.

Finding it was not the same as raising it.

The old oak had spent centuries soaked in water. The hull was heavy with mud, and no one could know from the surface whether it would hold together when pulled free. A wrong lift could break what time had preserved.

Franzén gathered support from the navy, the museum authorities and a commercial salvage company. There were imaginative proposals for lifting the wreck, including filling it with floating objects or freezing it. The engineers chose a method they knew how to control.

Between 1957 and 1959, divers dug six tunnels beneath the hull. They used high-pressure water and dredging equipment to cut through the mud without simply dragging cables around fragile projecting wood.

Through the tunnels went heavy steel cables. Above the wreck waited two floating pontoons, Oden and Frigg. The cables formed a supporting cradle under the ship.

The lifting method was clever because it could be repeated.

The pontoons were partly filled with water and the cables tightened. Then pumps drove water out of the pontoons. As the pontoons became more buoyant, they pulled upward on the cables and the ship.

On August twentieth, 1959, Vasa broke free of the mud.

It was an astonishing first success, but it was not the moment the world finally saw the ship. The hull was still underwater. It was lifted, moved toward shallower water, and set down again.

Then the salvagers repeated it. Again and again. Eighteen stages took the ship to a sheltered spot where divers could continue working.

There, the task changed from lifting a wreck to preparing an ancient wooden container to float. Rusted fasteners had left holes. Open gunports were covered, leaks plugged, and parts of the bow and stern made watertight. Steel rods helped support the structure. Divers cleared heavy debris from inside.

The salvage itself had become a long experiment in patience. Unlike the first voyage, progress was made in small, deliberate moves.

There was still a risk: the old hull might hold together in the mud yet fail as it rose into air. The rescue team had to keep that uncertainty in view while continuing the lift.

On April twenty-fourth, 1961, spectators stood along Stockholm's waterfront once again. This time radio and television cameras were there too.

At three minutes past nine, a dark piece of timber broke the surface. More frames emerged. Then carved figures and the outline of the great wooden hull.

Vasa had returned after 333 years.

But the second journey was not over. The ship still held enormous quantities of water and mud. Pumps worked to empty it. Archaeologists entered a wet, unstable interior and recorded objects as they came out.

They found chests, shoes, tools, barrels, gun carriages, coins and clothing. Some sails had been left carefully folded before the departure and were still bundled together. These were not the possessions of heroes in a painting. They were the working things of people who expected to go to sea and come back.

More than thirty thousand objects came out during the first excavation months. Later dives recovered thousands more pieces from around the hull.

There was one more enemy to face. Water had helped preserve the timber underwater. If the old wood simply dried out, it could shrink and crack. Conservators had to treat the ship and its objects for years, turning the spectacular moment of recovery into a long, careful preservation effort.

Today Vasa stands inside a purpose-built museum in Stockholm. Visitors can see the carved stern, gunports and scale of the ship, while the recovered belongings tell smaller stories about those who built and sailed it.

So did the test warn the officers? Yes: the running test showed the ship was unstable and was stopped. Did the gust alone sink it? No: the gust exposed a top-heavy design, and open gunports let water turn a dangerous heel into a rapid loss.

The last answer took 333 years. The same hull that could not survive its first twenty minutes at sea remained intact enough for divers, engineers and conservators to return it to daylight.

The warship never fired its guns in battle. Its unintended second voyage carried something else out of the harbour: the ordinary lives that its first voyage had left behind.
