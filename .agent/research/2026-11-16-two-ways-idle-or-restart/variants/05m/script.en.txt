When I'm parked waiting for a pickup, I can hear the engine ticking away. It feels harmless. Turning it off feels like extra work, and restarting it must use some fuel. So is leaving it on actually cheaper? And if it isn't, is the difference worth bothering about?

I want to compare two choices, not tell a story about a test I never performed. In option A I leave a warmed-up gasoline engine idling. In option B I switch it off and restart it once. I use one safe parking space and the same ninety-second wait. Before the answer, which would you choose?

Here's what I put into my model. The engine uses three-tenths of a US gallon per hour while idling. That's a hypothetical car within a published range for passenger vehicles, not an exact reading from my dashboard. For the restart, I use the fuel equivalent of ten seconds of idling. US energy researchers have found that stopping and restarting often uses less fuel than idling for more than about ten seconds. That is a useful guide, not a promise about every car in every temperature.

Option A burns fuel for ninety seconds. That comes to seven and a half thousandths of a gallon, roughly twenty-eight millilitres.

Option B uses nothing while the engine is off. In this model, restarting costs about three millilitres. I save approximately twenty-five millilitres by picking B.

That's the first reveal. A whole ninety-second wait, and the difference would barely cover the bottom of a small medicine cup.

I imagined repeating this identical stop a hundred times. Option A uses about two point eight four litres across those waits. Option B uses around three-tenths of a litre for the assumed restarts. The saving is about two and a half litres. At three dollars fifty per gallon, that is about two dollars thirty-three in total.

If someone promised me enormous savings from a single school pickup, that arithmetic would be a reason to question them. Small amounts add up, but the number of stops matters.

Now I can change the wait without secretly changing the rules. What if I stop for only five seconds? With my ten-second restart equivalent, option A wins. I would burn less by staying on. At twenty seconds, B saves roughly three millilitres. At five minutes, B saves around ninety-one. The longer I stand still, the more time option A spends burning fuel to go nowhere.

Notice what I have not measured. I haven't measured starting wear, battery health, emissions or cabin comfort in an actual vehicle. Those aren't extra points I can award to my favourite option. Research on short stops suggests the fuel benefit can exceed typical starting-system costs in suitable conditions. But a car with a struggling battery, unusual engine, heavy electrical load or a need to keep the cabin comfortable is not my bare-bones model.

There's another problem with a neat ten-second rule: people hear it and apply it everywhere. My comparison takes place while safely parked. The US Department of Energy recommends switching off at predictable stops, explicitly excepting traffic. That's different from manually killing the engine at a live junction or in a moving queue. An automatic stop-start system designed for a vehicle is also not the same as me operating the ignition by hand every few seconds.

I also have to be careful about the word cleaner. Burning less gasoline normally means less carbon dioxide from that fuel. But a restart can change other tailpipe pollutants, and studies of newer direct-injection engines have found particulate-emission trade-offs under some conditions. My fuel calculation can't settle all of air quality with one number.

So the winner is B for the ninety-second parked wait under the announced assumptions. The benefit is real in the model, but modest per stop. A very brief stop could reverse the fuel result, and real traffic changes the safety decision entirely.

The useful question isn't whether I'm a good driver because I press a button. It's whether I know how long I'll be safely parked and whether the engine needs to stay on. On my next pickup, I start by noticing the sound I used to ignore: a parked engine spending fuel without moving an inch.
