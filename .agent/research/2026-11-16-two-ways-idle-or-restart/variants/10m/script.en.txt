If I leave my car running while I wait, am I wasting money? If I switch it off and restart, am I just moving the cost somewhere else? And why can a perfectly sensible fuel-saving rule become the wrong thing to do in traffic?

I park in a marked pickup space and imagine a ninety-second wait. The car is warm, the engine is gasoline-powered, and I'm not using the air conditioner. I have two choices. A: leave the engine idling. B: turn it off and restart once when I'm ready to leave. Choose one now. I want to find the result from the same starting conditions, not from a convenient story about a car I never actually tested.

The first thing that matters is what happens while nothing appears to happen. At idle, my car isn't moving, but its engine is still overcoming friction, powering accessories and keeping itself turning. Fuel enters the cylinders anyway. A parked minute is not a fuel-free minute.

To make that visible I assign the hypothetical car a rate of three-tenths of a US gallon per hour. That's within a published passenger-car range of roughly two-tenths to five-tenths. I am choosing a model parameter, not reading a live dashboard. If my actual engine has a different rate, the answer in millilitres will change.

Now I need a fair price for restarting. Starting an engine does use fuel. Some people picture a giant gulp that wipes out anything saved while the engine was off. But warm-restart tests have found a crossover on the order of ten seconds of idling. I will use a ten-second equivalent as an explicit assumption for this car. It's an approximation, not a measurement that applies to every vehicle.

For option A, ninety seconds times three-tenths of a gallon per hour works out to seven and a half thousandths of a gallon. In a smaller unit that's about twenty-eight millilitres. Imagine that amount in a measuring cup.

Option B burns no gasoline during the off period. The assumed restart costs about three millilitres. With both options returning to a running engine, I don't get to charge B for an extra drive that A doesn't make. The same car leaves the same space at the same moment. The difference is approximately twenty-five millilitres.

So B wins the fuel contest for this ninety-second stop. I could end here. But if I did, the number would sound more important than it is, and I would miss the part that actually changes how I make the choice.

I repeat that one wait a hundred times on paper. A has burned approximately two point eight four litres in total. B has spent about three-tenths of a litre on the assumed restarts. I save roughly two and a half litres. If gasoline is three dollars fifty per gallon, that adds up to about two dollars and thirty-three cents.

That's not a secret trick for slashing a weekly fuel bill. It's a small, repeatable saving. And it's a good reminder that a percentage can sound dramatic when the original amount is tiny.

Here's the first complication. My ninety-second assumption made B comfortable. Suppose I pull over for five seconds instead. Keeping the same engine and restart rule, A burns the equivalent of five seconds while B spends ten seconds' worth to restart. This time A wins on fuel. At twenty seconds, B wins again, but by only about three millilitres. At five full minutes, the saving for B grows to around ninety-one millilitres. This isn't three different experiments claiming to be one. It's a sensitivity check: I changed the wait length, announced the change, and kept everything else fixed.

It also shows me why predicting the stop matters. If I think a queue will sit for two minutes but it inches forward every few seconds, my simple one-restart model has failed. B would need more starts than I budgeted. The result cannot be recycled unchanged for a queue that moves unpredictably. A smooth car-specific automatic stop-start system is not the same thing as manually turning my ignition off every time the brake lights ahead flicker.

Now let me test a different hidden assumption: the idle fuel rate. Published passenger-car estimates span roughly point two to point five gallons per hour. Keeping the same ten-second-equivalent restart rule, a hundred ninety-second stops could save about one point seven to four point two litres instead of my middle estimate of two and a half. This is a model range, not a measured confidence interval. An actual restart cost may not rise in lockstep with idle consumption. To learn about my car, I'd need real fuel-rate observations and reliable restart data, not just change the number in a script.

You might ask why I bother with a few millilitres at all. Because the original question wasn't whether I could finance a holiday with them. It was whether the engine continues consuming fuel when standing still, and whether the restart automatically cancels the benefit. In the defined warm, parked case, the answer is clear. It doesn't.

But I don't want to turn a fuel calculation into careless maintenance advice. Starting draws electrical energy from the battery, uses a starter or another starting system, and creates mechanical work. Researchers have considered those costs. An Argonne-linked assessment found that, in general, drivers can save money by switching off during suitable short stops, even when starting-system wear is considered. That doesn't mean every old starter, weak battery or modified car can be treated as new. Nor does it mean I should change a vehicle's designed automatic system without reading its manual.

Now I reach an even less obvious distinction: less gasoline is not identical to less of every pollutant. When the engine burns less fuel, the carbon dioxide associated with that fuel generally goes down. Warm restarts are not the same as cold starts, because the emissions-control catalyst may remain hot during a short pause. Still, research on some direct-injection cars has found restart-related increases in particular particle emissions even while fuel use improves. Different engine technology, temperatures and pollution measurements can give different trade-offs. My two-column fuel calculation measures fuel. It does not measure all exhaust chemistry.

The same is true of comfort. On a mild day I can wait with the engine off and no cabin cooling. On a hot day with passengers depending on air conditioning, that condition may not hold. In very cold weather I might need defrosting or heating. Some vehicles can run electric accessories while the engine is stopped; others cannot do so in the same way. The moment I add these demands, I must either update the model honestly or say I don't know the result.

And then comes the most important correction. The US Department of Energy advises shutting off a personal vehicle at predictable stops longer than about ten seconds, except in traffic. Why is that exception there? Because a predictable, safely parked wait is a different driving situation from a junction or a queue that may advance at any moment. Turning a running engine off manually in active traffic can affect readiness and safety. I cannot use my twenty-five-millilitre result as an instruction to do that. On the road I follow traffic rules and the vehicle's designed systems, not a simplified animation.

Think about the two scenes that can look almost identical on screen. In the first, I've parked in a designated area and know I have time to wait. I can plan one restart. In the second, I'm one car length from a green light and have no idea when I will move. The cars look stationary in both pictures. But the action available to me, and the cost of not being ready, are different. That is why I don't declare a single winner for every moment a speedometer reads zero.

If I were to make a real follow-up experiment, I would log actual stop durations, idle fuel rate, restart fuel use, temperatures and cabin loads. I'd compare safe parking events of similar length, not cherry-pick the longest wait for B or count the wrong number of starts. I'd also separate fuel use from battery wear and emissions measurements. Without those observations, the honest label on my chart is MODEL, not ROAD TEST.

I finish with a winner, and a boundary. For my identical ninety-second parked stops, B, switching off once, saves roughly twenty-five millilitres each time. At a hundred such stops, that is around two and a half litres, not a life-changing sum. A can use less fuel if the stop is shorter than the assumed restart crossover. Real cars can differ. And manually switching off in moving traffic isn't the recommendation.

The thing I take into my next pickup isn't a magic number. It's a question I can actually answer: am I safely parked for a predictable wait, or am I still part of moving traffic? Once I've answered that, I can make the choice without pretending that every stationary car is in the same situation.
