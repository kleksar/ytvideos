Can two people leave home sooner if their only bathroom sits empty for seven minutes? And if we both do exactly the same amount of work, where could three extra minutes possibly come from?

At seven thirty-seven, I have twenty-three minutes before I need to walk out with Sam. There is one bathroom. Two people need showers and the sink. Neither of us can be in it together. But we have separate places to get dressed.

I can keep the bathroom occupied until all bathroom jobs are done, or I can break up those jobs. The first choice looks efficient. No empty room. No waiting for a second turn. The second choice looks wrong: somebody is always stepping out before finishing.

We are going to race both schedules against eight o'clock. This is a deliberately simple time model, not footage of an actual morning or a claim about everyone's routine.

First, the jobs. My shower takes four minutes. Brushing my teeth takes three. Dressing in the bedroom takes ten. Shoes at the door take one. Sam takes five to shower, two at the sink, ten to dress, and one for shoes. We each have our own clothes and shoes ready. No travel time, no surprise delays, and no way to make a job shorter by rushing. We start together. One person at a time in the bathroom. Dressing and shoes happen outside.

Let's try A. I take the bathroom at seven thirty-seven. Shower, then brush. Seven minutes later, at seven forty-four, I leave. Sam enters immediately. Five minutes in the shower, two at the sink. The bathroom is fully occupied until seven fifty-one. That is fourteen busy minutes, with no gaps.

It feels like we used every available second. At seven forty-four I am already dressing, and by seven fifty-five my shoes are on. I'm ready to leave. But Sam did not start dressing until seven fifty-one. Ten minutes later, it is eight oh-one. One more minute for shoes. Sam is ready at eight oh-two.

Our shared departure is eight oh-two. We are two minutes late, despite the bathroom never being idle while it was needed.

Now I reset the exact same clock and the exact same tasks. In B, I shower first but do not brush yet. At seven forty-one I step out and start dressing. Sam takes the shower from seven forty-one to seven forty-six.

What happens next looks like a scheduling mistake. Sam leaves the bathroom to get dressed, even though Sam has not brushed. I am still dressing. For five whole minutes, nobody is at the sink. The bathroom is empty.

At seven fifty-one, ten minutes after I started dressing, I return and brush until seven fifty-four. Then I put on my shoes; I'm ready at seven fifty-five. Sam has been dressing since seven forty-six and finishes at seven fifty-six. So the bathroom stands empty for another two minutes, and Sam finally brushes from seven fifty-six to seven fifty-eight. One minute for shoes.

Both ready at seven fifty-nine. One minute before the deadline.

Let me put those two endings side by side. In A, I was ready at seven fifty-five and Sam at eight oh-two. In B, I was still ready at seven fifty-five, but Sam was done at seven fifty-nine. We didn't speed up a shower, shorten anyone's dressing, or quietly give Sam a head start. We changed which jobs occupied the same piece of real time.

The surprising part is that both schedules use the bathroom for exactly fourteen minutes: four plus three for me, five plus two for Sam. In A those minutes are packed together. In B they are separated by seven empty minutes. Yet the finish moves three minutes earlier.

The bathroom clock isn't the clock that decides when we leave. The last person to finish decides. A perfectly busy room can still leave somebody with ten minutes of dressing after everyone else is done. An idle room can let that ten minutes run alongside another person's job.

Does that mean B always wins? No. Here is a useful counterexample. Suppose every extra visit to the bathroom requires three minutes to find toiletries, move things around, and reset the room. That is a new assumption, not a fact about bathrooms. Under B, the two return visits cost six extra minutes of real work. With the same order, I finish brushing at twenty minutes, and Sam finishes brushing at twenty-five. Shoes push our shared finish to twenty-six minutes, one minute worse than A's twenty-five.

I would not choose B without knowing whether those return visits are genuinely easy. Nor would I tell anyone that a sink should be left vacant when somebody needs it. The empty intervals helped only because we were both dressing, and neither dressing task required the sink.

So my choice is conditional. With no extra reset cost, B gets us out at seven fifty-nine and saves the morning. With a large cost for returning, A can be better. I don't have to keep a room busy; I have to keep the people moving toward being ready.

And that is why, when Sam points to the empty bathroom and calls it wasted time, I can answer with the actual schedule instead of a guess.
