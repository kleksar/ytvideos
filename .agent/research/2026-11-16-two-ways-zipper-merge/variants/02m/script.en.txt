I'm looking at a solid line of cars, and right beside it, an almost empty lane. A sign says that lane closes ahead. If I stay in it, am I cutting in? Or is joining the long line actually making the backup worse?

I can choose A: merge as soon as I see the sign. Or B: keep using both lanes, then take turns where the cones narrow the road. Which one makes the backup shorter?

I won't pretend I measured traffic. Here's a deliberately simple model. Twenty identical cars have reached a two-lane stretch. Each parked car needs eight metres of road, including its gap. The one remaining lane lets one car through every two seconds. Every driver cooperates. Nothing else changes.

With A, all twenty queue in one lane. That's a line a hundred and sixty metres long. The second lane sits empty.

With B, ten cars queue in each lane. At the cones they alternate, one from the left, one from the right. The longest queue reaches eighty metres back. That's half the road space in this model.

But watch the clock. Twenty cars, two seconds each: forty seconds to clear the narrow point either way. I haven't made that bottleneck wider. I've only stopped wasting the space in front of it. Your own place in line might change, so this isn't a promise that any particular driver arrives faster.

That shorter backup matters if the long queue would otherwise reach an intersection or an exit. And it explains why transportation agencies recommend zipper merging in heavy, slow traffic. They also say something easy to miss: when traffic is moving freely, merging early can make more sense. Follow the actual signs and merge safely, not competitively.

So I won't judge the driver in the open lane just for being there. Before I choose A or B, I look at whether traffic is already backed up.
