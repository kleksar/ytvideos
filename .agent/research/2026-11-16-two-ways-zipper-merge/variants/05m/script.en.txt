The lane beside me is empty. Mine is barely moving. I can see orange signs for a lane closure ahead. If I drive all the way to the cones, everyone in this line might think I've skipped ahead. But what if the polite choice makes the queue physically longer?

I want to compare two decisions I can make at the same road closure. A: join the open lane as soon as I see the warning. B: stay in my lane until the marked merge, then alternate with the next car, like the teeth of a zipper. Before showing a winner, I'll name the test: how far does the backup reach upstream? Not how fast one lucky driver gets through.

First, a small transparent model. Twenty identical cars arrive at the same narrowing road. For the stationary queue, each needs eight metres, car plus gap. Only one lane continues beyond the cones. In the model it releases one car every two seconds. No one blocks a merge, no one cuts across several lanes, and the release rate is the same in both versions. These are invented, fixed inputs for a demonstration, not measurements from a real road.

I try A. Cars move over as early as possible, and soon there are twenty cars in a single file. Twenty times eight gives a queue one hundred and sixty metres long. Meanwhile an entire approach lane is lying unused. I might feel that everybody is being orderly, but the road behind us is filling.

Now I reset the same twenty cars. This time I try B. Ten wait on each side. At the cones, one goes from the continuing lane, then one from the closing lane. The longest of the two queues needs eighty metres. Same cars, same gaps, same single bottleneck. The queue now reaches only half as far back.

Imagine a side road joining this approach a hundred metres behind the cones. In the first drawing the queue has stretched past it. In the second drawing both lines fit before it. That is the surprise: merging at the last responsible point can leave more usable street behind the jam. This example does not prove a real junction would stay open. Real cars, gap sizes and arrivals don't align so neatly.

But here's the uncomfortable second result. Look at the narrow point, not the tail of the line. Twenty cars at one every two seconds still take forty seconds to pass through, whether they made one queue or two. The model predicts a shorter *physical backup*, not a magic extra lane on the other side. It doesn't say the last car, or I personally, will always arrive sooner.

In actual congestion, the situation is less tidy. If cars lurch across early, brake hard or fight over a final gap, the rate at the narrow point may change too. Transport departments promote a predictable merge location and alternating turns partly to reduce those disruptive speed differences. Minnesota and Wisconsin both explain this distinction. They report that a zipper merge can shorten a backup by as much as roughly forty percent under suitable conditions. That's agency guidance, not a number my twenty-car animation has measured.

So why does it feel rude? From inside the long queue, a car passing on the empty lane looks as though it has found a secret shortcut. My model changes the question. In a correctly signed, slow-moving merge, that second lane isn't a private shortcut. It is road space that all twenty vehicles can use. The courtesy happens at the cones, when drivers take turns. A driver charging past at unsafe speed, forcing their way in or ignoring instructions is doing something different.

Now let me change just one condition. The road is moving freely. There isn't a stopped line building behind the closure. No one needs two rows to store a queue, and waiting until the cones to make a sudden lane change adds no benefit. Minnesota's guidance says that when vehicles are travelling at highway speeds without backups, it makes sense to move into the continuing lane earlier, when it's safe. The local signs and actual conditions come first.

So there isn't a universal rule that late is always faster. The comparison has three honest answers. In our fixed model, B halves the space occupied by the queue. A and B clear the fixed bottleneck in the same forty seconds. And when traffic is flowing normally, I shouldn't manufacture a last-second merge just to imitate the diagram.

The next time I see one open lane and one long line, I'll still have to make a choice. I'll look for a backup, read the signs and leave room for the alternating car. The right question isn't who looks most polite from the back of the queue. It's what the road needs at the narrowing point.
