I have reached the part of a highway everyone seems to hate. My lane is almost stopped. To my right, cars are still moving toward a lane-closed sign. A driver passes me, reaches the cones, and signals to get in. I have a choice: let that driver in, or decide they should have joined this line a long time ago.

But there's a more interesting question than who deserves the gap. Why was an entire lane available to drive through in the first place? If every driver used it, would the backup get shorter? Or would we only move the argument to the cones?

I can compare two methods without pretending I have real road measurements. A means merge early, when the warning sign first appears. B means use both lanes until the marked merge, then alternate one car from each. Before either version starts, I'm choosing one metric: the distance from the cones back to the end of the queue. I will also keep an eye on a second metric, how long it takes the whole group to pass the bottleneck. A queue and a delay aren't the same thing.

For our first trial I draw twenty identical cars on an imaginary two-lane approach. They are already waiting at the start of the test. When stopped, each car and its following gap occupy eight metres. Beyond the cones, one lane remains. It can let one car through every two seconds. The drivers cooperate, there are no trucks or side entrances inside the test stretch, and the rate through the bottleneck never changes. These are rules I made for the model. They are not facts about a real construction site.

I choose A first. Everybody responds to the warning sign by moving to the lane that stays open. Twenty cars stack up, eight metres apiece. I draw a tail one hundred and sixty metres behind the cones. The other approach lane is available, but nobody uses it. From the steering wheel, I could call this orderly. From above, the picture shows a lot of road standing idle.

I put the cars back exactly where they started. Now I choose B. The drivers stay in both lanes until the cones, ten on the left and ten on the right. Each side occupies eighty metres. At the narrowing point, a left car and a right car take turns. The second lane is no longer empty, and the queue reaches only half as far behind us.

My first temptation is to say, great, B is twice as fast. That's not what I tested. Forty seconds is still needed to release twenty cars at two seconds per car. The constricted lane hasn't doubled in width. The total number of cars hasn't changed. In this particular model, A and B have identical whole-group clearance time. B wins the distance test, not the time test. Individual drivers can swap positions, so I cannot promise anyone a personal time saving.

Why should a shorter queue matter if everybody still goes through one at a time? I draw a side street connecting to the approach a hundred metres before the cones. Under A, a hundred-and-sixty-metre queue extends behind that crossing. Under B, the eighty-metre queues end before it. Nothing about the cars reaching the cones has improved. But I have stopped using so much road upstream just to store them. In an actual town, whether a crossing gets blocked depends on the lanes, turning movements, other traffic and driver behavior. This is a diagram of a possible effect, not a prediction for that junction.

There is a subtle distinction here. Saying the line is half as long doesn't mean there are half as many cars. All twenty are still waiting. I merely changed their arrangement. Some people hear that a zipper merge cuts congestion by forty percent and imagine a forty-percent shorter trip. That's not what my drawing proves. Transportation agencies in several US states report that orderly zipper merging can reduce backup length by as much as about forty percent in suitable slow-moving conditions. The specific figure comes from their guidance, not from my simplified calculation.

I want to see when the difference becomes important, so I add a stream of arriving cars. I'll keep the narrow section's capacity fixed at thirty cars per minute: one car every two seconds. If twenty cars arrive per minute, the narrow section can keep up. After any temporary queue clears, a lasting backup doesn't grow. There isn't much space to save by arranging a nonexistent jam in two rows.

Now I change only the arrival rate. Suppose thirty-five cars come every minute and only thirty can get through. Five more cars are waiting after each minute than before. After four minutes, that is twenty extra cars, ignoring the cars already on the road and all temporary variations. Arrange those twenty in one lane, and at our invented eight metres each they require another hundred and sixty metres of queue. Put them evenly in two lanes, and the longer line extends roughly eighty metres instead. Both methods still face the same bottleneck. This simple balance between arrivals and departures explains why storing a growing queue across more pavement matters most when traffic is heavy.

But no real work zone obeys a perfect clock. A car may brake early. Someone may suddenly cross two lanes. Drivers may leave different gaps or block the final merge. The service rate can fall below the tidy two-second interval I assumed. That is why traffic departments don't merely tell drivers to reach the cones as late as possible. They tell them to approach slowly, use both lanes in congestion, and take turns predictably at the designated merge.

The Indiana Department of Transportation describes the zipper as a planned sequence, not a race. Minnesota makes the same point. Keeping traffic in both lanes until a defined merge reduces abrupt differences in speed and can keep drivers from changing lanes at random locations. The alternating rule also makes the final gap a shared rule instead of a negotiation between two irritated strangers. These are the agencies' stated benefits, not something I can verify by drawing perfect cars.

Let me try breaking the model. Under B, imagine that the two lanes reach the cones, but nobody wants to give way. The first few cars brake, stop, and argue over a gap. I cannot assign a real number to that delay without observations. But I can show the arithmetic of a hypothetical. If the disrupted merge lets through only one car every three seconds, twenty cars take sixty seconds rather than forty. B has kept the queue short upstream while making its exit slower. In this second demonstration I changed the bottleneck's release rate on purpose. It is not a fair controlled A-versus-B measurement any more; it shows why cooperation is a necessary condition, not a decorative instruction.

That also explains why the driver passing me feels like a villain. If I have already joined a long single-file line, that person appears to gain places by using the lane I abandoned. I may want to close the gap to teach them a lesson. But using an otherwise open lane up to a properly signed merge in slow traffic isn't automatically cheating. The intended system asks everyone to occupy both lanes and then alternate. Forcing entry, driving dangerously fast or ignoring signs is a different choice. The distinction is between a coordinated lane-closure procedure and aggressive driving, not between nice people and selfish people.

There are dissenting situations even before anybody behaves badly. Suppose I approach the same signs hours later, when the traffic is flowing freely at road speed. With no backup, there is no growing queue to distribute between two lanes. The advantage we saw in the diagram disappears. Minnesota, Wisconsin and Indiana explicitly distinguish this case: when traffic is moving at highway speeds and there is no congestion, merging into the open lane earlier, when safe, makes sense. Local signs can direct a different arrangement, so the model isn't a substitute for the rules of the road.

Here is how I would use this comparison, not as a universal driving command but as a way to recognize the problem. First I look ahead. Is the traffic heavy and slow, with a defined merge area? If so, I expect both lanes to be used and drivers to take turns near the cones, where permitted and directed. Is it moving freely, without a line forming? Then I can merge safely well before the lane ends. In either situation I leave space, don't force another driver to brake, and follow the signs.

A new question remains: which method makes *me* faster? Our first model deliberately cannot answer that. It fixes the group's service rate but lets cars change their positions in the sequence. An early merger might leave ahead of a late merger or behind them, depending on who arrives and how they alternate. A claimed personal victory from this demonstration would be made up. What it does answer is how much road a waiting group occupies under two specified arrangements.

So the apparent paradox has an ordinary physical explanation. The empty lane isn't a second exit from the bottleneck. It's a second place to hold the queue. With the same cars and gaps, twenty cars in one file stretch one hundred and sixty metres; ten in each lane stretch eighty. Both groups still pass through the single opening. When congestion is heavy, using the approach properly can keep the backup from reaching so far behind it. When congestion is light, that storage trick isn't needed.

The next time a car passes my stopped lane and approaches the cones, I won't conclude anything from the pass alone. I'll look at the traffic, the signs and the merge point. Then I'll make room for the next car when it's their turn. Because before I can say which method works, I need to know whether the road is already backed up.
