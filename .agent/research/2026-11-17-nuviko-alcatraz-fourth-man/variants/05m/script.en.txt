How do three prisoners vanish from an island watched day and night? Why does the fourth man in their plan never reach the water? And does getting out of Alcatraz mean getting away alive?

In June 1962, Alcatraz is a federal prison on a rock in San Francisco Bay. Guards check the cells again and again. Beyond the prison walls are cold water and powerful currents. The island is not meant to be a place you can simply run from.

Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin occupy nearby cells. Their fourth partner is Allen West. They cannot cut straight through the steel bars. Instead, they work at the small ventilation openings behind their cells.

The openings lead into an unguarded utility corridor. That is their first discovery, not their exit. Even after someone can squeeze through, he is still inside the prison building. Above the cells, an upper service area offers room to work without standing in a guarded corridor.

For months they gather materials that look harmless on their own. Old saw blades and an improvised drill help enlarge the holes. They conceal the work with false covers. Above the cell block, they turn more than fifty raincoats into a raft and life preservers. They stitch the seams and use hot pipes to seal them. They fashion paddles. A musical instrument is adapted to push air into the raft.

One problem remains every night: a missing man would be noticed in his bed. So they leave a substitute. They make lifelike heads, colour them and add real human hair. From the front of a dark cell, a guard is supposed to see a prisoner sleeping.

There is another barrier above them. They must climb through the structure to a ventilator on the roof, roughly thirty feet up. The men loosen the cover and hide the preparation with a fake bolt made of soap. The escape route now runs behind the cells, up through the building, and out onto the roof.

On the night of June eleventh, they set the false heads in place and begin moving. Then the plan breaks at the point that should have been solved months ago. Allen West has not freed the grille behind his cell. He cannot get through.

The others do not wait. Morris and the Anglin brothers reach the corridor, climb out through the roof opening, descend by the bakery smokestack, cross the fence and make it to the island's northeast shore. They launch their homemade raft into San Francisco Bay.

The next morning, a guard checks Morris's bed. When the false head is disturbed, it rolls and breaks. The prisoners are gone. The entire prison goes on alert.

Investigators search the surrounding water and shore. They find a rubber-wrapped package of letters, pieces linked to the raft and paddles, and a homemade life vest. They do not find the three men.

West, the man still inside, begins explaining the preparations to investigators. That is how much of the route and equipment becomes known. He can tell them how the escape was planned. He cannot tell them what happened on the water.

The FBI thinks the men probably drowned. The currents were dangerous, and investigators could not substantiate the planned theft of clothes and a car on the mainland. But the absence of those thefts does not prove the men died. Nor does a workable raft prove they survived.

After seventeen years, the FBI closes its investigation and transfers responsibility to the U.S. Marshals. What can be said without guessing is simpler than the legend: four men helped make the escape possible; only three left the island that night. The prison's defences were beaten. The bay may not have been.
