How could four prisoners build a way out of America's best-known island prison without the guards seeing it? Why did one of them fail at the last opening? And what do we actually know about the three who vanished into the water?

The morning after the escape, a guard approaches a bed at Alcatraz. There appears to be a sleeping man beneath the blankets. But when the head on the pillow is moved, it rolls away. It is not a person. Its painted face, plaster and real human hair are parts of a model. The nose breaks when the model hits the floor. Two more beds hold the same trick. Three prisoners are missing.

To understand how this happened, go back to the prison itself. Alcatraz stands on an island in San Francisco Bay. In 1934, the federal government turns it into a maximum-security institution for men who have proved difficult to hold elsewhere. There are guard towers, close supervision and repeated checks every day. Even reaching the outside of the building would leave an escapee on an island surrounded by cold, moving water.

It is also a prison that gives its inmates one person to a cell. There are workshops, a library and controlled activities, but almost everything is organised around a fixed routine. That routine is what four men try to turn against the guards.

Frank Morris arrives in 1960. John Anglin reaches the prison later that year, and his brother Clarence follows in 1961. The three know one another from earlier imprisonment. Another prisoner, Allen West, becomes part of their plan.

They do not start by attacking the main bars. Each cell has a small ventilation opening at the back. Beyond it lies a service corridor. If the opening can be made wide enough, a man can disappear from the visible cell without stepping into the guarded aisle.

But there are two separate tasks hidden in that idea. First they need an opening large enough to pass through. Then they need a way to disguise months of work from the staff making their rounds. One discovery without the other would expose them.

The prisoners find old blades and improvise cutting and drilling tools. According to the FBI's reconstruction, a vacuum-cleaner motor becomes part of a homemade drill. They enlarge the openings and hide the marks with false covers. The moisture-worn material around the openings makes the work possible, but progress is slow. The plan depends on one cell after another being ready.

Once through, they reach a narrow service area behind the cells. This space creates another opportunity. It leads toward the upper structure of the prison building. Above the cell block they establish a concealed work area for jobs too large to complete on a prison bunk.

That does not solve the route to the outside. The ventilator above is around thirty feet up. The men climb using the building's pipes and work at the cover. Even after loosening it, they cannot leave an obvious opening for a guard to discover. A replacement bolt shaped from soap helps conceal what has been done. The intended route now runs from the beds, through the rear wall, along the hidden corridor, upward to the roof, and across the island.

Then there is the larger problem. Water separates Alcatraz from the mainland. Breaking out of a locked building is useless if the escapees cannot leave the island. They need something that floats, and it must be built from what can be gathered inside the prison.

The group collects more than fifty raincoats, obtained from other prisoners and prison supplies. In their concealed work area they turn them into life preservers and a raft roughly six feet by fourteen feet. The FBI describes stitched seams and the use of hot steam pipes to seal them. Wooden paddles are made. They even convert a musical instrument into a device for putting air into the raft.

Imagine what has to be coordinated now. A usable opening in each cell. An unnoticed route to the roof. A means of getting down to the ground. A raft able to carry several people. Paddles. Something to inflate it with. And enough time to do all of this before staff discover the empty beds. Each piece answers a different problem; none is a guarantee of survival.

The most famous part of the scheme solves the last problem inside the cells. From a guard's usual viewing position, an empty pillow would reveal the escape immediately. So the men fashion false heads with painted faces and real human hair. Other material under the blankets gives the impression of bodies. The heads do not have to withstand close inspection. They merely have to delay one.

By June eleventh, 1962, the pieces are supposed to fit together. Morris, John Anglin and Clarence Anglin are ready to go. West is part of the same effort, but he has not managed to remove the ventilator grille behind his own cell completely. This is where the plan ceases to be a four-man escape.

The other three get through their cell openings. West cannot follow them into the corridor. It is not a failure somewhere out in the bay; it happens while he is still in his cell. And once the others move upward, he has no way to make them wait without endangering the attempt.

Morris and the Anglins pass along the service area, climb through the prepared opening and reach the roof. From there they move down by the bakery smokestack, make their way past the perimeter fence, and reach the northeastern shore. They have carried a boat in pieces from a prison building to the water. They launch the raft into San Francisco Bay.

That is where the verified trail stops. The FBI can reconstruct the work in the prison and the route to the shore from what it finds and what West later explains. It cannot follow the three men across the water with the same certainty. There is no verified account of them landing safely on the other side.

Early on June twelfth the false heads are discovered. The prison is locked down. The FBI, prison authorities and Coast Guard search for signs of the men. A packet of letters wrapped in rubber turns up within two days. Investigators later find paddle-related debris and fragments of rubber. A homemade life vest washes up on Cronkhite Beach. These objects show that equipment entered the water. They do not show where the men themselves ended up.

The fourth participant now becomes an important witness. Allen West is still inside the prison. He can describe the holes, the concealed workshop and the equipment. Much of the story of the escape reaches investigators because the one man who did not leave can talk to them. That is why the detail of his unfinished grille matters beyond an unlucky twist.

Investigators also work backwards from what the prisoners reportedly intended to do after leaving the island. According to the information gathered, the plan included reaching Angel Island and eventually getting to the Marin County side of the bay, where clothes and a car would be stolen. No corresponding thefts can be confirmed. Investigators regard that absence as evidence against a successful landing.

It is evidence, but it has a limit. If a car was not stolen according to plan, the men might have died. Or they might have abandoned that part of the plan. Or they might have reached land somewhere unexpected. Without a confirmed sighting or physical proof, those possibilities cannot be sorted into a verified ending.

Cold water and strong currents make the crossing dangerous. The FBI's view is that the three probably drowned. Its investigation continues for seventeen years without credible evidence of them living openly elsewhere. On December thirty-first, 1979, the Bureau closes its case and hands responsibility to the U.S. Marshals Service.

The story, however, keeps attracting claims. A letter publicised in 2018 claimed to have been written years earlier by John Anglin. It said the men survived. Investigators' reported forensic examination was inconclusive. A letter making a claim is not a confirmed message from an escapee, and cannot settle the mystery.

Other people have tested whether a raft like theirs could float or whether some tidal conditions could carry people toward safety. A successful reconstruction proves that one route was possible under the tested conditions. It does not place these three men on that route on that night. A theory is not a recovered passenger.

Even the phrase 'the only successful escape' needs care. The men got out of their locked cells, crossed the prison grounds and launched into the bay. That part succeeded. No authority confirmed that the three survived and reached freedom. The distinction is the entire story.

There is a different, quieter conclusion about the prison. It had been designed to prevent men from getting away. But its own ventilation openings, service corridors, roof access and ordinary materials became parts of an escape route. The guards saw three apparent sleepers in their beds. By the time the false heads were examined, those sleepers were already somewhere beyond the shoreline.

For the man left behind, the answer was clear: Allen West did not escape. For Morris and the Anglin brothers, the ending never came back to the island. Four prisoners built the plan. Three launched the raft. And the last barrier, the water, still has no verified outcome.
