How do you get six Americans out of a country that is holding American diplomats hostage? Why give them Canadian passports? And what does a movie that will never be made have to do with their escape?

In November 1979, militants seized the American embassy in Tehran. Six Americans avoided capture. Canadian diplomats and their families hid them in their own homes while the hostage crisis continued outside.

The hiding place could not remain a secret forever. Journalists were asking about Americans missing from the hostage lists. Canada and the United States needed a way to move six people through an airport where they could be questioned about who they were and why they had come.

Canada agreed to issue real Canadian passports under assumed names. But that solved only half the problem. Six supposed Canadians still needed a believable reason to have been in Iran.

The CIA's Tony Mendez supplied one: they would be a film crew scouting locations for a science-fiction movie called Argo.

This was more than a story rehearsed in a living room. A company called Studio Six Productions had an office, a telephone number, business cards, and movie advertisements. If someone checked, there was something to find.

Then a Canadian official noticed a dangerous detail in the travel paperwork: a date written using the wrong calendar. It was corrected before departure.

In late January 1980, the six went to the Tehran airport with their new identities. Their flight was delayed, but they passed the checks and left on a regular passenger plane. There was no last-second chase across the runway.

The fake movie helped make their journey believable. The passports were genuine Canadian documents. And the people who had kept them alive until departure were real Canadian hosts.

Six people got out. Dozens of other Americans remained hostages until January 1981. The escape was real; the movie production never was.
