Why would a government issue genuine passports with names that were not genuine? How could a science-fiction film help six Americans leave Tehran? And whose homes kept those six alive long enough to reach an airport?

On November fourth, 1979, militant students took over the American embassy in Iran's capital. American staff were captured. Six others managed to get away.

For those six, the immediate problem wasn't a plane ticket. It was finding somewhere to sleep without becoming another name on the hostage list.

Canadian diplomats helped. Ambassador Ken Taylor and his colleague John Sheardown, together with their families, sheltered the Americans inside their homes. One of the six had initially found protection with Swedish diplomats before joining the others.

This refuge created a problem that grew with every passing week. The Americans could not simply walk around Tehran as themselves. Their Canadian hosts had to maintain ordinary diplomatic lives while keeping unexpected guests out of sight.

And the secret was beginning to fray. Reporters had noticed that the number of Americans taken hostage did not account for everyone at the embassy. Some knew people were hiding with a friendly foreign mission. Publicity might put both the guests and their hosts in danger.

In early January, officials from Canada and the United States settled on an extraordinary idea: the six would try the most ordinary way out of Iran. They would fly from Tehran's public airport.

But they could not present American diplomatic passports. Canada authorized genuine Canadian passports issued in assumed identities. Those documents could answer a border officer's first question: who are you?

The harder question was next. What had six Canadians been doing in a city where foreigners and Americans were under such scrutiny? An airline booking could take them to a counter. It could not explain their entire trip.

CIA specialist Tony Mendez built the answer in Hollywood.

The escapees would pretend to belong to a Canadian film crew scouting Iranian locations for a science-fiction picture called Argo. The film was imaginary. The evidence supporting it was not.

Mendez and the team worked with Hollywood make-up artist John Chambers. They established Studio Six Productions, gave it an office, set up a working telephone contact, and produced business cards and trade-paper advertisements. They had an existing science-fiction screenplay and striking concept art associated with artist Jack Kirby.

The point wasn't to convince an audience to buy movie tickets. It was to give an official a plausible answer if one of the supposed filmmakers was asked which company had sent them.

The team also had to make the identities work on paper. A Canadian passport could be authentic while the travel story written around it was false. The dates and entry records had to agree with that story.

Here the plan nearly betrayed itself before anyone reached the airport.

Canadian official Roger Lucy examined the fabricated Iranian documentation and noticed that an entry date had been written using the wrong calendar. Iran used a different calendar system. A mistake in a passport might make a bored official curious. The documents were corrected.

Mendez and another CIA officer arrived in Tehran in late January. They met the six and rehearsed how their supposed film crew would answer questions. No one had to become a brilliant actor; each person needed to remember a simple, consistent identity and why the group was leaving.

There was a frightening paradox in the plan. The disguise was elaborate, but the actual exit depended on behaving like unremarkable passengers. There would be no special extraction aircraft waiting in the desert. They had to walk through the public airport, show their papers and board a scheduled flight.

The morning came. The six went to the airport with their Canadian identities and their film-company cover. They cleared the checks. Their Swissair flight suffered a mechanical delay, but the delay did not turn into an arrest.

Then the plane left Iran.

No official uncovered their identities at the gate. No one raced down the runway to stop their aircraft. The famous movie chase is not how the documented operation ended.

Six Americans were safe because two pieces fitted together: Canada had sheltered them and issued their passports; the CIA had built a cover story that made those passports usable. Even the tiny correction to an Iranian date was part of the escape.

The Americans who remained inside the embassy were not rescued by this flight. The hostage crisis continued until January 1981.

For years, the public mostly knew the Canadian side of the story. The CIA's role remained classified until 1997. By then, the fictional movie Argo had accomplished its only real purpose: no one had needed to make it.
