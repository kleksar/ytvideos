How did six Americans escape Iran while other Americans were being held hostage? Why did the Canadian government give them passports bearing invented names? And why did a Hollywood company need to exist for a film that nobody intended to shoot?

On November fourth, 1979, militant students seized the United States embassy in Tehran. The diplomats and staff caught inside became hostages in a crisis that would last more than a year.

Six American State Department employees were not captured. Two married couples, Mark and Cora Lijek and Joseph and Kathleen Stafford, and two men, Bob Anders and Lee Schatz, found themselves outside the seized compound. Their absence from the hostage lists did not make them safe. They were still Americans in a city where the embassy takeover had changed everything.

The first rescue was much less glamorous than a flight from an airport. Someone had to open a front door.

Canadian diplomat John Sheardown and his wife Zena sheltered Americans in their home. Canada's ambassador, Ken Taylor, and his wife Pat also opened theirs. Lee Schatz initially found shelter among Swedish diplomats before being brought into the Canadian arrangement.

For weeks, ordinary houses became hiding places. Their hosts still had diplomatic jobs, visitors and routine responsibilities. The Americans could not use their own names and simply take a taxi to the airport. An unexpected visitor could expose people who had chosen to protect them.

Those Canadians had not been ordered by a Hollywood director to perform a dangerous scene. They made a real decision to shelter people whose discovery could have serious consequences for the hosts as well as the guests.

And now a different kind of danger began to grow.

A reporter could compare the names of the Americans known to have worked at the embassy with the people being held inside. There were gaps. Canadian journalist Jean Pelletier learned enough to understand that some Americans might be hiding with Canadians. He held back publication. That choice helped protect the secret, but it was not a permanent solution. Other reporters were also beginning to hear that not every American had been captured.

A United States diplomatic memorandum from mid-December already recorded concern about exposure. Sanctions and closer attention to foreign embassies could make concealment harder. A future search for missing Americans could turn the homes into places to investigate.

Waiting was becoming a plan with no reliable ending.

On January second, officials from both countries met in Ottawa to examine a more active option. They did not propose charging into the embassy or flying a rescue helicopter into the city. For the six hiding Americans, the most promising route seemed to be Tehran's ordinary international airport.

But there was an obstacle before any of them reached a check-in desk.

If the six presented American diplomatic passports, their nationality and jobs could immediately identify them as people connected to the seized embassy. They needed identities that could withstand questioning.

The Canadian government, led by Prime Minister Joe Clark, authorized passports in assumed Canadian names. This was genuine government documentation, deliberately issued for an exceptional purpose. It was not a collection of stolen passports.

Now imagine what a border official might ask after reading one of those passports. Why did this Canadian arrive in Iran? Where did they go? What company sent them? Why are six of them leaving together?

A real passport did not answer those questions.

CIA officer Tony Mendez specialized in getting people out of places where their real identities were dangerous. He proposed that the six become a film crew. They would be Canadians working for a Hollywood production that had come to Iran to scout locations.

This sounds like a flimsy excuse if it exists only in someone's memory. So Mendez and his collaborators worked to give it a life outside the six travellers.

Hollywood make-up specialist John Chambers helped set up Studio Six Productions. It had an office on a real studio lot. It had a business telephone number. There were company cards and film-industry advertisements. An outsider could make a call or come across printed evidence of the company.

The invented film had a name: Argo.

The team borrowed material from an existing plan to adapt the science-fiction story Lord of Light. Artwork associated with celebrated comic-book artist Jack Kirby gave the nonexistent project a visual identity.

A spy film might make that artwork seem like decoration. In the real plan, it was practical. A location scout needed to sound as though a production was happening somewhere. That production needed a title, a genre and people who could be contacted.

The rescue was beginning to depend on two kinds of truth at once. The Canadian passports were real. The identities, company and reason for travelling were a deliberately constructed fiction.

Then a document threatened to collapse the fiction.

Canadian official Roger Lucy reviewed the Iranian entry paperwork for the cover identities. One date had been written using the wrong calendar. The calendar used for official dates in Iran was not the same as the one the planners had assumed.

A passport officer might never have noticed. But if one did, a single date could lead to the question the travellers least wanted: why does your account of entering this country not match your documents?

The error was corrected before departure.

That correction reveals how the plan really worked. The aim was not to overpower security. It was to give security as few reasons as possible to look twice. A convincing advertisement could not repair an inconsistent date. A perfectly printed passport could not explain six people who knew nothing about their supposed employer.

The paperwork and the people had to agree.

Mendez and another CIA officer, Ed Johnson, travelled to Tehran in late January. They met with the six and rehearsed the film-production story. Each person had a name and a job to remember. If questioned, their answers had to fit the passports, the travel history and one another.

They were not sent to film an actual scene at a bazaar for the sake of maintaining the legend. The mission was to prepare a plausible explanation and leave. The dramatic confrontation in a crowded market familiar from the later movie is not an event the documented escape required.

By this point, a remarkable number of people had each contributed one necessary piece. Canadian households had provided shelter. Officials in Ottawa had approved identity documents. The CIA had built a production company that existed in paperwork, telephone lines and advertisements. Someone had checked the calendar. And six Americans had learned to present a shared story.

None of those parts could guarantee the next step.

A flight had to be available. Airline staff and immigration officers had to accept the documents. The travellers could not control every question at the airport or every delay before takeoff.

This is where the difference between a dramatic retelling and the record becomes important.

On a morning in late January 1980, the six Americans travelled to Tehran's airport carrying Canadian passports and the story of a Canadian film crew. They moved through the airport's checks and onto a scheduled Swissair flight.

There was a mechanical delay. It lasted long enough to make the passengers remain on the ground. But nobody uncovered their true identities during that delay.

The plane departed.

There was no documented sprint by security vehicles after it. No guard discovered the entire deception at the gate seconds before takeoff. The actual success came from the absence of that dramatic moment. The travellers passed as the people their documents and prepared answers said they were.

Six Americans had made it out of Iran without being captured. Their fake film had done its job without a camera ever rolling on its fictional production.

But if we end the story at the airport, we miss the part that explains what this rescue could, and could not, accomplish.

The flight did not end the hostage crisis. Dozens of Americans remained confined. They would not be released until January 1981, after 444 days of captivity. The Canadian houses had saved six people, not everyone at the embassy.

The rescue also depended on secrecy after the plane left. Public disclosure could affect the remaining hostages and the Canadians still working in the region. News of the escape emerged after the six were safe. Canada's embassy in Tehran was subsequently closed.

In public, the Canadian contribution was celebrated. The homes, the diplomats and the authorization of Canadian passports deserved that attention. The CIA's Hollywood cover remained largely hidden for years; its operational role was publicly acknowledged only after declassification in 1997.

That long silence had a consequence for the way the story was remembered. Some accounts foregrounded the Canadians who protected the six. Later Hollywood versions foregrounded the American officer who devised the film cover. The operation only makes sense when both parts remain in view.

Mendez could create a believable story, but he could not have offered the six those Canadian homes before he arrived. Canada could issue passports, but the passports still needed a reason for their holders to have visited Tehran. And the journalists who kept the secret for a time bought the planners room to act.

The film Argo would later become famous as a movie about this operation. The film that had actually helped the six escape, the fake Argo advertised by Studio Six, was never produced.

The six needed to look like people leaving an ordinary film job. That was why the most carefully prepared part of their rescue was a film that did not exist.
