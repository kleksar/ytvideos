# Research — Chilean miners: the note and the second hole
Date observed: 2026-11-17. Channel: Nuviko History, EN documentary. Editorial Git queue only; **not** an official runtime research receipt. Research queries contained no private repository material.

## Audience-facing promise

A drilling machine brings back proof that 33 missing miners are alive after 17 days. The hole carrying the note cannot carry a person. The compelling question is not just whether they are alive, but **how a message-sized opening becomes a human-sized route before the men run out of time**. First confirmation: the drill comes up with the note; after-hook development: short rations, supply tubes, competing drills, broken equipment, Phoenix safety, 33 separate ascents; payoff: the last miner emerges on day 69, followed by the rescuers. Strongest objection: extremely well-known worldwide news event and renewed large-scale NatGeo coverage in September 2026; an ordinary retelling will look redundant. The original angle focuses on *two different holes* and why the first discovery did not end the emergency. Avoid claiming the miners were "saved by a note alone."

## Three competing pitches (pre-script)

**A — Chile 33, winner.** First scene: a drill emerges with a scrap of paper after seventeen days. "The message said they were alive. Why were they still trapped for another seven weeks?" Middle: food supply changes before an evacuation route exists; Plan A/B/C race, Plan B equipment break; critical distinction on October 9 between breakthrough and rescue; Phoenix 33 rides. Payoff: Luis Urzúa is lifted last on October 13, then rescuers return. Viewer's value: see how proof of life, survival logistics and engineered evacuation are separate problems. Objection: NatGeo's fresh three-part 2026 series saturates exact subject; stronger angle not empirically validated. Editorial preference based on concrete beginning/middle/payoff, **not measured CTR or retention**.

**B — United 232.** First scene: airline crew attempts to move controls, but aircraft no longer responds after tail-engine failure. Middle: hydraulics lost; pilots use differential thrust and an unscheduled airport approach; crash landing and split outcomes. Payoff: many passengers survive, but many die, so title cannot promise "landed safely." NTSB case: https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/pages/dca89ma063.aspx ; FAA contextual analysis: https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/N1819U . Objection: familiar aviation genre with multiple existing Nuviko flight topics (BA9, Tenerife, Gimli), risks audience repetition.

**C — Everest 1996.** First scene: storm catches climbing teams as they attempt to descend. Middle: physical constraints and different groups' decisions; multiple rescue efforts. Payoff: documented losses/survivors and limits of certainty over individual blame. Context: https://www.history.com/articles/the-single-deadliest-day-for-climbers-on-mount-everest . Objection: distinct eyewitness accounts conflict, hard to sustain an honest short strong individual causal line; mountain-survival story differs from current channel's extensive disaster selection but easier to overdramatize.

**Selection:** Chile gives the clearest sequence of *changed goals* (find → feed → drill → individually extract) and an affirmative conclusion. Flight 232 is the strongest alternative in immediate action and crisis, but this queue recently covered another flight; Everest has stronger environmental spectacle but weaker uncontroversial causation. No unsupported claim that Chile will outperform either.
