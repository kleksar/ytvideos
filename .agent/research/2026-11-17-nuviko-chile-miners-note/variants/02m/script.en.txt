How do you find thirty-three people buried under a mountain of rock? And if you find them alive, how do you bring them back?

On August fifth, twenty ten, part of Chile's San José mine collapsed. Thirty-three miners were cut off roughly seven hundred metres underground. The road out was blocked. Above them, rescue teams drilled narrow holes, trying to locate the men.

For seventeen days, nobody on the surface knew whether they had survived. Underground, the men shared tiny emergency rations and kept watch over one another.

Then a drill came back carrying a note. It said all thirty-three were alive in the shelter.

The families finally had an answer. But a narrow search hole could carry food and messages, not people.

Rescuers began sending supplies down in long containers. They also started drilling a much wider shaft. Three different drilling approaches were pursued because no one could promise which would reach the miners first.

On October ninth, the faster Plan B drill broke through. Reaching the miners wasn't enough: engineers still had to check the shaft and prepare a capsule that could travel through it safely.

A rescue worker went down first. Then miner Florencio Ávalos climbed into the narrow Phoenix capsule and rose to the surface. The capsule went down again. And again.

The last miner to come out was shift supervisor Luis Urzúa. After sixty-nine days underground, all thirty-three men were alive above ground.

The search hole had only been big enough to bring back a scrap of paper. That paper gave the rescuers and families proof that all thirty-three men were alive.
