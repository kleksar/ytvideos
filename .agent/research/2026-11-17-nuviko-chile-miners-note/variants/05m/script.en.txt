Thirty-three men were trapped hundreds of metres underground. Rescue crews could drill down to them, but they couldn't send a person back through a hole the width of a pipe. So what turned a search for bodies into a rescue that brought every miner home?

On August fifth, twenty ten, a section of the San José copper and gold mine in Chile collapsed. The men were roughly seven hundred metres below the desert. Their normal route out was blocked by rock. No one above could call them, and no one knew whether an emergency shelter had survived.

The miners had reached a refuge, but reaching it did not solve the next problem: food. Thirty-three adults had to share supplies meant for a much shorter emergency. Under shift supervisor Luis Urzúa, they rationed what they had. Contemporary reports described tiny portions of tuna, crackers and other food shared over days, not proper meals.

On the surface, rescue teams drilled narrow exploratory holes into the mountain. This was not as simple as aiming at a point on a map. They needed to hit an underground space that the survivors could actually reach. For seventeen days, the holes returned nothing that proved anyone was alive.

Then, on August twenty-second, a drill came up with a message attached. The note said that all thirty-three were well in the shelter.

There was suddenly a reason to celebrate. But look at the size of that hole. It had carried a note. It couldn't carry a man.

The first priority changed from finding survivors to keeping them alive. Long, narrow supply containers went down the borehole with food, water, medicine and eventually ways to communicate. After weeks of silence, people above could hear the miners speaking. The families waiting at what became known as Camp Hope could follow a rescue instead of an unanswered search.

Now engineers had to build the actual exit. Three drilling plans were developed in parallel. Each used different equipment and a different route through unpredictable rock. Plan B, using a drilling system adapted for this work, moved ahead, but even that drill stopped for nearly a week while workers retrieved broken equipment. The other machines mattered because no one yet knew which plan would work first.

The human problem also changed. Surviving seventeen days on scraps was not the same as surviving many more weeks while heavy machinery worked overhead. Medical teams managed food and health from a distance. At Chile's request, NASA specialists contributed advice on nutrition, psychology and the rescue capsule's engineering requirements. NASA did not run the rescue: Chilean teams led it.

In early October, the Plan B drill finally reached a tunnel accessible to the miners. The breakthrough came on October ninth. People at the surface cheered, but the mining minister made the crucial distinction: they had reached the men; they had not yet brought anyone out.

The shaft had to be examined. A specially built capsule, Phoenix, had to fit and travel safely through the rock. The first person sent down for the final operation was a rescuer, there to help the miners prepare and board.

Then Florencio Ávalos became the first miner to rise. The narrow capsule emerged into the desert light, and the system had passed its first human test.

Thirty-two people were still below.

For almost a day the capsule repeated the journey. Each miner needed to enter, ride up and clear the shaft before the next could follow. The crew also had to bring the rescuers back.

Finally Luis Urzúa, the shift supervisor who had helped organize life underground, emerged as the thirty-third miner. On October thirteenth, after sixty-nine days trapped below the Atacama Desert, every one of the thirty-three was safely above ground.

The most famous moment is the capsule opening. But the rescue had actually turned twice: first when the note proved there were people to save, and again when engineers turned a narrow line of communication into a working way out.
