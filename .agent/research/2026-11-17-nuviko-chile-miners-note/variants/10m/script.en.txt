How do you get thirty-three people out of a mine when their only connection to the surface is a hole too small for a person? And how do you keep them alive while you build the way out? In Chile in twenty ten, the answer began with a drill, a desperate shortage of food, and a message from deep inside the rock.

On August fifth, part of the San José copper and gold mine collapses. Thirty-three men are cut off from the surface, roughly seven hundred metres below the Atacama Desert. They can reach an emergency refuge, but the road they would normally use to leave the mine is blocked. There is no telephone connection to the rescuers.

Shift supervisor Luis Urzúa helps organize the men around a rapidly disappearing supply of food. They share tiny portions of canned tuna and crackers. There are thirty-three mouths to feed every day, with no way of knowing how many days the supplies will have to last. Above ground, their families gather beside the mine as crews drill narrow exploratory holes. A borehole has to reach a passage the men can actually get to.

On August twenty-second, seventeen days after the collapse, a drill reaches the men. When the equipment comes back up, a small piece of paper is attached to it. The message is unmistakable: all thirty-three are alive and in the shelter. The families finally have proof that there is somebody to rescue.

The paper can pass through the opening because it is thin enough to fit against the drill. A man cannot. Even now, the miners cannot simply be pulled up. And the food that has lasted them seventeen days cannot feed them for the months that rescuers initially think the next stage may take.

The first job is therefore to get supplies down. Narrow containers carry food, water and medicine. A camera makes it possible to see the miners. Later, a telephone connection links the underground refuge with the settlement of families at the surface, known as Camp Hope. For the men who have spent more than two weeks without any contact, the outside world is finally within reach of a voice.

On August twenty-ninth, relatives get their first brief telephone conversations with the trapped men. The connection gives each family only about half a minute to speak. It is enough to pass along some news, but it is no substitute for being together. The phone calls also make the remaining task painfully clear: contact can be restored long before a family can be reunited.

To make a real exit, the rescue teams develop three drilling plans. Different machines work toward different accessible parts of the mine. One of the people called to Chile is Jeff Hart, an American who had been drilling water wells in Afghanistan. His team will operate the rig known as Plan B. The experience of drilling through difficult rock now has to be applied to a hole that other people will travel through.

The drill has to stay on course through rock rich in abrasive silica. A poorly mapped mine presents another problem: the equipment can meet an old piece of the mine itself. Plan B strikes a metal support beam. The drilling hammers break, leaving pieces far below the surface. The rig that was supposed to help shorten the wait is now stopped.

Workers lower powerful magnets to retrieve the broken metal. Replacement equipment has to come from the United States. The interruption lasts days. They cannot simply leave the fragments where they are and assume that the drilling can continue. The other approaches remain valuable while Plan B is being repaired. No single team can guarantee that its hole will be the one the miners ultimately use.

Meanwhile, the supplies going down the small holes have to do more than replace missing meals. Doctors are helping men who have lived on very little food return to an adequate diet. At Chile's request, a team of NASA specialists arrives to advise on nutrition, medical care, psychological support and, later, requirements for the rescue capsule. The Chilean rescuers remain in charge of the operation.

Then the communication link carries news that has nothing to do with drilling. On September fourteenth, the wife of miner Ariel Ticona gives birth to a daughter. His family records the birth so that he might be able to see it underground. He sends a message through the video link asking for the girl to be named Esperanza, Spanish for hope. His wife agrees. She has thought of the same name independently.

The girl has arrived while her father is still under the desert. The narrow connection can carry the news of her birth and his response. It cannot bring him to the hospital. The rescue still depends on the engineers finishing a completely different kind of hole.

For that second hole, Chilean engineers prepare a special vehicle called Phoenix. A miner will ride almost upright inside the cylinder, with little space to spare. It needs communications and a supply of air. The capsule must fit inside the shaft throughout the journey, including places where the rock may be uneven. So reaching the underground passage is still only part of making a usable exit.

On October ninth, after weeks of drilling, Hart's team finally breaks through to a passage accessible to the miners. Hart later describes the work as one in which he had to sense the machine's vibrations through his feet. The operation has survived the broken hammers. For the people at Camp Hope, the news means that the exit now reaches the men.

Chile's mining minister warns that no one has been rescued yet. Engineers inspect the shaft with a camera to decide where it needs reinforcement. The upper section is lined with metal. This is the passage through which the capsule will have to make not one journey, but dozens of journeys carrying people. The last few days of work are part of the rescue, even though the drill has already arrived.

There is also the question of whom to bring up first. The planned order groups the men by their condition: relatively healthy miners are to test the process, more vulnerable men can follow once it is running smoothly, and stronger men can wait longer. Rescuers will go down before the first miner comes up, to help with the preparations underground.

On the night of October twelfth into the thirteenth, rescuer Manuel González reaches the men in the capsule. The underground group can finally greet someone who has come from the surface. Florencio Ávalos boards Phoenix for the first miner's ascent. At the top, the capsule opens in the floodlights. After all the weeks of drilling and repairs, a miner has made it back alive.

Thirty-two miners remain below. Phoenix returns for another passenger. A field hospital is ready to examine the men after their journeys. Families are able to greet them briefly before they receive further medical care. The crew has to repeat the operation until nobody who was trapped remains in the mine.

As the cycles become quicker, the operation takes less time than originally expected. Near the end, Phoenix brings up Ariel Ticona. He is the thirty-second miner. His daughter Esperanza was born nearly a month earlier in the same hospital where he is now due for medical examination. The distance that had separated him from his family is no longer hundreds of metres of rock.

The next man up is Luis Urzúa, the supervisor who helped the group make its food last during the days when nobody knew if they were alive. On October thirteenth, sixty-nine days after the collapse, all thirty-three miners are above ground. The rescuers who went down to assist them are brought up afterward.

There will still be medical examinations and a return to life outside the mine. But the question that began with the families waiting for a sign of life has been answered. One narrow hole carried a piece of paper, then supplies and news. A separate shaft, built while the miners waited, carried thirty-three people back into the world.
