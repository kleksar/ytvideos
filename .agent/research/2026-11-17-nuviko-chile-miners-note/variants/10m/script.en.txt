Thirty-three miners are missing inside a collapsed mountain in Chile. The rescuers have a drill, but they don't know if anyone is alive. Seventeen days later, a small scrap of paper appears at the end of the drill. It changes everything. Yet the people who wrote it are still hundreds of metres below ground. How does a message become an exit? And why does finding the men turn out to be only the first half of the rescue?

August fifth, twenty ten. Inside the San José copper and gold mine in northern Chile, a section of rock collapses. Thirty-three men are working underground. The way they normally use to get out is blocked. The mine is deep, its passages are complicated, and the rock that has just failed cannot be treated like a door that someone can simply clear.

The men make it to a refuge, about seven hundred metres beneath the surface. Reaching shelter keeps them together, but it doesn't provide a route home. They cannot call the outside world. Their emergency food stores are limited. They don't know how much of the mine the collapse has damaged, and there is no promise that a rescue drill will reach them.

Above the desert, families gather while teams try to determine where to bore into the mountain. It's a strange search. In an ordinary rescue, the person you're looking for may be able to shout or wave. Here, the people are separated from you by hundreds of metres of rock. Search crews need to hit a space the trapped miners can reach, even though the maps and underground geometry cannot guarantee a direct line.

Boreholes go in and come out without an answer. The miners hear drilling somewhere beyond the refuge, but a sound through rock doesn't tell them which way a drill is traveling. On the surface, a failed borehole is not proof that anyone has died; it's just another hole that hasn't found the right place.

Below ground, the thirty-three face a quieter emergency. Emergency food intended for a short wait now has to last day after day. Under the organization of shift supervisor Luis Urzúa, they divide supplies into tiny portions. Reports after contact describe shared bites of canned tuna, crackers and other food. Whatever the exact portions on a given day, they are not enough to count as normal meals. The men have to make supplies last without knowing whether help will ever reach them.

Seventeen days pass. Then a drill breaks through near the area they can access. They attach a note to the equipment before it is hauled back up. On August twenty-second, rescuers recover a small piece of paper with the essential message: all thirty-three are well in the shelter.

That tells the families something the boreholes had never been able to tell them. The workers are alive. The search has reached the right underground space.

But imagine the celebration ending, and looking back at the hole in the ground. It is wide enough for a drill and a message. Not for a grown man. And the people below still have hardly any food.

The next step is to turn the search hole into a lifeline. Teams lower long, narrow containers carrying food, water and medical supplies. Later, they send ways to talk and pictures from the outside world. For the first time in weeks, voices travel between the refuge and the families. A camera sent down shows the men, giving relatives something more than a written assurance. One father recognizes the face of his son on the images.

The relatives remain outside at a settlement that comes to be known as Camp Hope. Contact has changed what they're waiting for, but not removed the wait. The first rescue estimates run into months. The men could be kept alive with supplies, but their exit had still to be built.

One drilling operation would be risky: if it failed, nothing else would be ready. So the engineers work on three separate plans. They use different machines and approaches to try to make a hole large enough for a rescue capsule. The work has to pass through rock that can deflect equipment or break tools. The fastest-looking path on paper may be the one that stops.

And the machines do stop. One promising drill, called Plan B, loses time when equipment breaks and workers have to retrieve it. A problem with the drill head far underground cannot be fixed like a flat tire at the roadside. Teams continue pursuing the other approaches because no one has yet earned the right to call a winner.

Meanwhile, life underground is no longer simply a question of rationing what was left in a cupboard. Deliveries must support weeks of waiting. Medical advisers help manage the transition from starvation-level rations to regular nutrition. Teams also have to consider light, sleep, communication and the stress of living in a confined space with no known date of escape.

At Chile's request, NASA advisers contribute expertise in nutrition, psychology, and design requirements for a rescue capsule. They are not operating the drilling rigs or directing the rescue. Chilean teams remain in charge. The point is that the job has become more than digging: people have to be healthy enough to survive the journey and life after it.

Another problem waits at the far end of the hole. Even if a drill reaches an underground passage, a human cannot climb seven hundred metres of near-vertical rock. There has to be a device that travels through the finished shaft. It must be narrow enough to fit, strong enough to carry a person, and designed so that a rider can communicate and receive air during the trip.

That is the reason for the Phoenix capsule. A person will stand almost upright inside a long metal cylinder, with little space on either side. Once attached to its lifting system, the capsule can make the journey between the refuge and the surface. But before it can carry a person, there must be a usable shaft.

In early October, Plan B is closing in. On October ninth, the drill finally breaks through to a passage accessible to the trapped men. People at the surface cheer. A path wide enough to attempt a rescue now exists.

Yet the operation still cannot simply begin. Workers examine the hole and prepare its upper section. The capsule has to pass through without getting stuck. A failure halfway up would leave a rescuer or miner suspended inside rock with no easy access. The distance between "the drill arrived" and "a person can ride out" matters as much as any single dramatic photograph.

On the evening of October twelfth, the final operation begins. Rescuers go down in the capsule to help the miners prepare for the trip. The first person brought up is Florencio Ávalos. He enters the cylinder, the door closes, and the Phoenix rises through the shaft. After weeks of watching a hole in the desert, the families finally watch a man come out of it.

This first journey proves the rescue system can carry someone all the way to the top. It doesn't mean the job is finished. Thirty-two miners remain below, along with rescuers working inside the refuge.

The capsule goes down again, then up with another miner. Each passenger has to be prepared, secured and brought to the surface before another ascent can start. Doctors examine the men as they come out. Some have special glasses because their eyes have spent so long away from daylight. For the people waiting above, each opening of the capsule is an individual reunion. For the crew operating it, each is another safety-critical cycle through the same narrow shaft.

At first, the rescue had been expected to take perhaps thirty-six to forty-eight hours. The actual cycling becomes quicker. After twenty-two hours and thirty-seven minutes of the lifting operation, all thirty-three miners are on the surface.

The thirty-third is Luis Urzúa, the shift supervisor who helped organize the group during their first days out of contact. There is no longer anybody left on the list of trapped miners. But the rescue workers who went down to help still have to be recovered; the last of them is pulled up after the miners.

October thirteenth. The men have spent sixty-nine days underground, including the seventeen days when no one above knew they were alive. There are hospital examinations and long-term effects to consider. Being brought up in a capsule ends the entrapment; it does not instantly erase everything the men have been through.

The photographs of the rescue often begin with Phoenix emerging into the floodlights. That was the end of a much longer chain. A search drill found a message. A message opened a lifeline. A lifeline kept people alive while engineers built a second, much larger route. And that route had to work thirty-three times.

At the beginning, the rescuers were trying to find one small opening through the rock. In the end, they had created a way out for every man who had answered with a note.
