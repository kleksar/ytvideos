## Viewer promise / selection

**Question:** why did a routine dropped socket lead to an eight-and-a-half-hour emergency and fatal chemical explosion, while the nuclear warhead remained intact? First concrete scene: ordinary work above a liquid-fueled Titan II; the socket falls and punctures the tank. The first real change after hook: evacuating workers does not stop fuel leaking or prevent the now pressure-starved rocket from collapsing. Next: expert advice conflicts, responders return, one dies, warhead location uncertain. **Payoff:** real rocket-fuel explosion, **not** nuclear detonation; DOD records one death, 21 injuries, intact re-entry vehicle, no radioactive contamination. Human cost is not minimized by nuclear weapon safety.

**Strong objection:** PBS's `Command and Control`, a Plainly Difficult explainer and a History Guy episode have already told this event to substantial audiences. A short retelling risks being less thorough and redundant, and nuclear sensationalism could undermine trust. The differentiator proposed here is the *decision chain involving the responders* and the distinction between chemical harm and nuclear physics, not claiming a near-certain nuclear blast.

## Three competing pitches, pre-script

**A — Damascus, selected.** Exact opening: "How can one dropped tool destroy a nuclear missile? Why did trained crews spend an entire night trying to stop a fuel leak?" First thing shown: socket bounces into tank wall, leak. Beyond hook: pressure falls, commanders disagree, Kennedy seeks readings, volunteers with limited breathing supply return, Livingston goes back, blast and missing warhead. Payoff: warhead intact/no contamination but one death/21 hurt. Objection: already well covered, danger of overselling the nuclear threat. Claim is not "a nuclear blast was narrowly averted at the last second"; it was *a chemical explosion with a safe nuclear warhead*.

**B — United 232.** Exact opening option: "The pilots moved the controls. Nothing changed. How could they steer a plane with all three hydraulic systems gone?" Development: differential thrust/crew cooperation and the attempt to reach Sioux City; payoff: 184 survivors alongside 112 deaths, not a "perfect landing." Authority: [NTSB DCA89MA063](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/pages/dca89ma063.aspx) (causes, injuries). Objection: already many recent aviation subjects in this queue (Gimli, BA9, Tenerife); competition for viewer attention. Strongest alternative by immediate tension and human decisions; **not rejected** on its merits.

**C — Three Mile Island.** Exact opening option: "The operators thought the reactor needed water; how did an instrument reading mislead them?" Development: confusing plant indications, operators' response, partial melt and communication; payoff: precise lessons and absence of widespread proven health effects, not apocalyptic catastrophe. Source: [US NRC Backgrounder](https://www.nrc.gov/regulations-legislation/fact-sheets-brochures/backgrounder-on-the-three-mile-island-accident), accessible summary only in this research; **claim-level detail UNVERIFIED until full NRC page read**. Objection: requires extra technical explanation for a compelling human protagonist; weaker immediately verifiable 10-minute narrative here.

**Comparison:** United 232 has faster physical stakes and a bigger visible human outcome; Damascus has the rare sequence of *seemingly trivial failure → multiple contradictory repair choices → people re-enter → human loss → exact nuclear/non-nuclear correction*. Three Mile Island is significant but less easily told without speculative individual blame. Select A with **medium editorial confidence**, no performance guarantee. A fresh separate story PR, not a continuation of another finalist.
