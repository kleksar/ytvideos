How does a dropped tool destroy a nuclear missile? And when the missile explodes, why doesn't its nuclear warhead explode with it?

September 1980. At a missile silo in Arkansas, two Air Force technicians are doing routine maintenance on a Titan II rocket. A heavy socket slips from a wrench. It falls down the silo, bounces, and punches through the rocket's fuel tank.

The missile is not being launched. But now it is leaking dangerous fuel inside an enclosed underground complex.

The crew evacuates. Specialists arrive. They face a problem a simple repair cannot solve: the longer fuel escapes, the less stable the missile becomes. The tank is pressurized. If its structure fails, the rocket may collapse, with its fuel and oxidizer able to react violently.

Hours pass. Teams and commanders debate what to do. Some want the silo opened to vent the vapors. Others argue against it. Eventually, volunteers go back down to check the conditions.

Then, about eight and a half hours after the socket falls, the fuel vapors ignite.

The blast tears the missile complex apart. Airman David Livingston is fatally injured. Twenty-one other Air Force personnel are hurt.

The nuclear warhead has been thrown clear. Recovery teams find it intact. The Defense Department reports no radioactive contamination.

That distinction matters. A catastrophic rocket-fuel explosion really happened. A nuclear explosion did not. The warhead's safety design helped prevent one; it did not protect the men working around the rocket.

One dropped socket began the accident. The decisions made during the next eight and a half hours determined who was still close enough to be hurt when it ended.
