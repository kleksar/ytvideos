How can one dropped tool destroy a nuclear missile? Why did trained crews spend an entire night trying to stop a fuel leak? And after the rocket exploded, what happened to the nuclear warhead on top?

Arkansas, September 18, 1980. A Titan II missile stands inside an underground silo. It has a nuclear warhead, but the crew isn't preparing a launch. Two Air Force technicians have come to work on its pressurized tanks.

One of them loses a heavy socket from a wrench. It falls down the shaft, strikes part of the structure, and punches a hole in the rocket's fuel tank. Fuel begins escaping.

That small metal socket has changed the job completely. Instead of checking a missile, the crew must now prevent the missile itself from coming apart.

The Titan II stores its liquid fuel and oxidizer in separate tanks. Together they can drive a rocket across an ocean. If they meet inside a damaged silo, they can also produce a violent chemical reaction. The leaking fuel is dangerous on its own, and the loss of pressure threatens the tank's strength.

The site is evacuated. A specialist team is summoned from Little Rock Air Force Base. The men who normally operate the missile are no longer in the control room.

But moving people away does not stop the leak.

Commanders consult engineers and the company that built the missile. They receive different advice. One proposal is to open the massive silo cover and release the trapped vapors. Another is to wait and avoid a move that could make the situation worse. Hours go by while the fuel keeps escaping.

Sergeant Jeff Kennedy arrives with the specialists. According to later accounts from the men on duty, he goes back toward the silo to get pressure readings. He is trying to answer the question that matters most: can the missile still support its own weight?

The readings are alarming. As fuel leaves the tank, the balance of pressure changes. A collapse could bring fuel and oxidizer together. So commanders now need information from a place everyone has just been ordered to leave.

After more discussion, volunteers return. The plan involves getting through secured doors and checking the conditions inside. They carry breathing equipment with limited air. The first men reach the access route and are replaced by Kennedy and Airman David Livingston.

Inside, vapors have made visibility poor. The team retreats toward the surface. Then an instruction comes to switch on an exhaust fan. Livingston goes back.

Seconds later, the rocket-fuel vapors ignite.

The explosion destroys the silo. Kennedy is badly injured. Livingston is rescued but dies from his injuries. Twenty-one other Air Force personnel are hurt. The original socket has been on the ground for about eight and a half hours.

And there is still another question: where is the warhead?

The blast has thrown the missile's re-entry vehicle out of the silo. Recovery teams locate it and inspect it. The nuclear warhead is intact. A Defense Department summary records no radioactive contamination.

This was not a nuclear detonation that somehow went unnoticed. It was an enormous chemical explosion involving the missile's propellants. Nuclear weapons require specific conditions to produce a nuclear explosion; an accidental impact or fuel fire is not the same thing as an authorized detonation sequence.

The warhead's protective design held up. The surrounding system did not. A dropped tool penetrated a fuel tank, conflicting advice cost time, and people were sent back into a complex that was getting harder to enter safely.

For David Livingston, his family, and the injured crews, that distinction between chemical and nuclear explosions offered no protection. The missile was lost, but the men sent to deal with its leak paid the heaviest price.
