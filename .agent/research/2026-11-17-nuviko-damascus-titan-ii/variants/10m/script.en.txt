What could a loose wrench socket do to a missile carrying a nuclear warhead? Why did an Air Force rescue team have to break into its own launch complex? And when the missile finally exploded, how could anyone know whether the warhead was safe?

The answer begins with a routine job in Arkansas, on the evening of September 18, 1980.

A Titan II missile stands inside an underground silo near the town of Damascus. This isn't a test firing. The missile is on alert, and a maintenance crew has come to work on its propellant system. The rocket contains separate tanks for liquid fuel and an oxidizer. The tanks are kept under pressure, partly because their thin walls need that pressure to help support the structure.

One technician is using a wrench above the missile. A heavy socket comes loose. It falls down the long shaft, bounces off part of the support structure, and strikes the rocket. The tank wall is punctured.

The men have not caused a launch. They have caused something that is much harder to switch off: fuel is now escaping inside a confined concrete silo, and the tank is losing pressure.

Why would pressure matter so much? Picture an empty drink can. It is easier to crush than a sealed, pressurized one. That is only an analogy, not a scale model of a missile, but it captures the danger the crews were watching for. Losing fuel and pressure could leave the tall rocket unable to support itself. If it bent or collapsed, the separate propellant systems might rupture. Fuel and oxidizer meeting under those conditions could ignite without any launch command.

The first task is to get people away. The crew evacuates the complex, and the surrounding area is cleared. Specialists are called in from Little Rock Air Force Base. There is a hole in a fuel tank, but nobody can simply climb into the silo with a patch kit. The air may be poisonous, the rocket remains unstable, and reaching the machinery means passing through a protected underground installation.

The men also face a problem that isn't made of steel or fuel: who is allowed to decide what happens next?

On the site, the technicians understand the equipment from everyday work. Senior officers are directing a crisis that reaches beyond the local base. Engineers at Martin Marietta, the company that built the missile, are consulted as well. They do not all recommend the same course.

At a congressional hearing days later, Air Force Secretary Hans Mark described the disagreement. One option would have been to open the huge silo cover so vapors could escape. Another approach was to hold position, avoiding a step that might create a different hazard. The command system chose to wait.

Opening the cover was not obviously harmless. If the missile failed while the silo was open, the warhead might be thrown from the installation. Leaving it shut, however, meant the leaking fuel was accumulating in an enclosed space. Neither choice could erase the puncture.

There is no need to invent a ticking countdown. The physical problem supplies its own one: each hour without a repair means more fuel gone and less confidence in the tank's strength.

Sergeant Jeff Kennedy arrives as part of the specialist response. In the later testimony collected by PBS, Kennedy and the other airmen describe returning toward the silo for direct pressure readings. Kennedy has experience with the propellant system. He knows readings from the equipment can tell the team whether the fuel tank is nearing failure.

He goes down to inspect, breaking a two-person safety rule in the process. That is one of the tensions of this story. Moving faster might yield the information the commanders desperately need. It also means taking an additional risk inside a place that has already been evacuated.

The readings make the situation look worse. Fuel is still escaping, and the pressure balance is deteriorating. A local crew wants action. But authority for the final decision rests higher in the command chain.

As the night goes on, specialists and commanders try to work out a plan. The men who operate the missile have left the underground control center, so doors that would ordinarily be opened from within now have to be reached from outside. The eventual plan calls for volunteers to enter the complex and obtain more information.

The first team is sent in with protective clothing and breathing apparatus. Their air supply is limited. The route is not an ordinary stairway to a workshop. There are locked doors, blast protections, and access points designed for a nuclear missile installation. According to the PBS recollections, the volunteers use tools to get through barriers and make their way down.

Greg Devlin and Rex Hukle are among the first to go in. They are operating in an environment where vapor readings are becoming extreme. Their job is to reach the access route, not to perform some miraculous repair of a pressurized rocket. Their time inside runs down. A second pair prepares to replace them.

That pair is Jeff Kennedy and David Livingston.

The story has changed again. What began as a question about a punctured fuel tank is now a question about whether the people sent inside can even see or breathe well enough to make the measurements.

When Kennedy and Livingston approach the inner area, they find the air clouded with vapors. Kennedy later described being unable to make out a door only a short distance away, even though the area was lit. The men withdraw toward the surface.

A further instruction comes down: operate an exhaust fan. Livingston goes back to carry it out.

The precise ignition source is not established by the short Defense Department accident summary, and later accounts differ about the chain of events. The fact that a fan was part of the final mission does not prove the fan caused the explosion.

What is certain is what happens next.

About eight and a half hours after the socket fell, fuel vapors in the silo ignite. The explosion destroys the missile complex. Enormous pieces of the installation are scattered. The rocket is lost.

Kennedy is thrown clear and badly hurt. In the PBS documentary transcript, he describes trying repeatedly to stand with a broken leg, then reaching a vehicle and radioing for help. His words are those of a later witness, not an audio recording made inside the blast.

Other members of the response team make their way back despite the danger. They find the injured and help carry them out. David Livingston suffers fatal injuries. Twenty-one other Air Force personnel are hurt.

That count comes from the Department of Defense's own summary. It is possible to tell this story entirely through the warhead and forget those numbers. But for the families, the scale of the human disaster has nothing to do with whether the explosion was nuclear.

And then comes the question that people outside the base desperately wanted answered. Where was the warhead?

The nuclear weapon had sat in a re-entry vehicle on top of the missile. The rocket-fuel blast was so violent that it hurled that section outside the wrecked silo. Local officials and residents wanted to know what had happened to it, while Air Force personnel were constrained by the policy of neither confirming nor denying nuclear weapons at a specific site.

In the PBS testimony, local radio workers and the county sheriff describe being frustrated by the lack of information. Colonel John Moser, who commanded the missile wing, later explained that he believed the policy was unreasonable in the circumstances, but was still required to obey it.

Recovery personnel located the re-entry vehicle. Sandia weapons experts assessed its condition. The nuclear warhead was intact, and the Defense Department reported no radioactive contamination.

This is the key distinction that sensational versions of the story blur. The explosion in Arkansas was a chemical explosion of missile propellants. It was not a nuclear detonation. A nuclear weapon is designed not to produce its nuclear yield from an accidental drop, fire, or nearby chemical blast. The exact safety systems belong to the weapon's design, but an enormous rocket explosion is not the same as the controlled sequence required for a nuclear detonation.

That does not make the incident harmless. The silo was destroyed, people were killed and injured, and an armed nuclear re-entry vehicle had to be recovered from the wreckage. The absence of radioactive contamination is an important fact, not a reason to erase what happened.

The investigation also showed that the original mistake was only part of the story. The dropped socket had escaped a protective barrier intended to catch falling objects. A damaged tank kept leaking while experts disagreed. The response eventually required crews to re-enter the installation. And one of the airmen who took that assignment did not survive.

The aftermath raised another uncomfortable question: was this simply the fault of the man who dropped the tool, or did the system make one dropped tool capable of producing such an accident?

Air Force officials emphasized the initial maintenance error. Powell, the technician involved, later said he had been disciplined for using the wrong wrench. Kennedy described receiving a reprimand for breaking the two-person rule during his attempts to check the silo. Those are facts from later witness accounts, not proof that either disciplinary decision was fair.

There was also history behind the silo. A 1965 fire at a different Titan II complex in Arkansas killed fifty-three workers. The Damascus disaster was not that earlier fire repeated, and its precise causes were different. But the existence of both accidents challenges any claim that handling these missiles carried no danger before they were ever launched.

In October 1980, local sheriff Gus Anglin publicly demanded better information and assurances from the Air Force. The argument was not about whether the warhead had detonated. It had not. The argument was about the people living nearby, and what they could reasonably expect to be told when a military installation was failing.

So the first question has a plain answer. A socket fell, pierced a pressurized fuel tank, and started a leak that could not be quickly stopped. The second has a harder answer. Conflicting advice, a difficult command structure, and the deteriorating conditions left volunteers entering a dangerous silo. The third has an essential correction: the missile blew apart, but its nuclear warhead did not detonate.

A tool fell for a few seconds. The men who responded lived through the consequences for the rest of the night. For David Livingston, there was no morning after.
