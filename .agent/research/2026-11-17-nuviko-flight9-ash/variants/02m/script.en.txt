Why did all four engines stop on a Boeing 747 when its weather radar showed no storm? How did they start again? And why wasn't the danger over when the engines returned?

On June 24, 1982, British Airways Flight 9 was flying from Kuala Lumpur to Perth. At thirty-seven thousand feet over Indonesia, a strange glow appeared outside the cockpit windows. Smoke and a sharp smell reached the passengers. The weather radar showed nothing alarming.

Then engine number four failed. Number two followed, and within moments the other two stopped.

A Boeing 747 without engines can glide, but every mile costs altitude. Captain Eric Moody turned toward Jakarta. Mountains lay between the aircraft and the airport. If the crew could not restart an engine before they descended to twelve thousand feet, they might have to attempt a landing at sea.

They kept trying the restart procedures.

As the aircraft descended, an engine finally came back. The others restarted too, although one later had to be shut down.

But now the pilots faced another problem. The cockpit windows were almost opaque. Something had scratched them so badly that the runway was barely visible. The airport's normal vertical landing guidance was also unavailable. The first officer used an approach chart and the aircraft's distance readings to guide the descent.

The plane landed safely in Jakarta. Everyone survived.

The cause was volcanic ash from Mount Galunggung. Weather radar looks mainly for moisture, not tiny dry pieces of rock and glass. Inside hot jet engines, ash can melt and block the airflow. After the plane left the ash cloud and the engines cooled, restarting became possible.

The same particles that stopped the engines had nearly blinded the pilots. The aircraft survived a danger its radar had never shown.
