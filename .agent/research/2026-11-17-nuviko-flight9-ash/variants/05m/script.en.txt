Why did a four-engine Boeing 747 suddenly become a glider over Indonesia? How did its engines return? And why did the crew still have to invent a way to land almost blind?

On June 24, 1982, British Airways Flight 9 was heading from Kuala Lumpur toward Perth. The Boeing 747 was cruising at thirty-seven thousand feet in the dark.

Captain Eric Moody, first officer Roger Greaves and flight engineer Barry Townley-Freeman saw no storm on their weather radar. Then an unusual glow moved across the cockpit windows. Passengers noticed flashes near the engines, and sharp-smelling smoke reached the cabin.

The crew could see that something was wrong. They could not see the cause.

Engine number four began to surge and quit. Soon engine two failed. Within seconds, engines one and three stopped as well.

All four engines were gone.

The airplane still had wings, forward speed and working controls. But without engine power, it had to descend to keep flying. Moody turned back toward Jakarta while the crew declared an emergency.

Their problem was not simply finding an airport. There were high mountains between the airplane and Jakarta. They needed either enough engine power to reach the runway or a different plan before the aircraft ran out of altitude.

The crew tried to restart the engines. They also faced a cabin-pressure problem. Greaves's oxygen mask was unusable, so descending into breathable air became even more urgent.

Moody set a limit at twelve thousand feet. Without a working engine by then, an attempted landing on the sea might be safer than crossing the mountains.

Each unsuccessful restart used some of the remaining height.

The hidden cause was a cloud of ash from Mount Galunggung on Java. Unlike a thunderstorm, the plume did not produce the moisture signature the weather radar was designed to find. Tiny fragments of rock and glass could enter all four engines.

A jet engine is extremely hot. Some volcanic particles melt inside it and form deposits. That interferes with airflow and combustion, causing power loss. Ash also grinds against any exposed surface that faces the oncoming air.

But in the cockpit, the pilots did not yet have that explanation. They just kept trying.

As the aircraft descended out of the ash, an engine restarted. Then the others. One was later shut down because of severe vibration, but enough power had returned to reach Jakarta.

The emergency appeared to be ending. Then the crew discovered how badly the ash had damaged the cockpit windows.

They could barely see through the glass. The runway they needed was ahead, yet a normal visual approach was almost impossible. And the airport's vertical instrument landing guidance was not working that night.

Greaves took the remaining distance information and the approach chart. He called out which altitude matched each distance from the runway. Moody used that sequence to keep the aircraft on a workable descent path.

The crew had solved two different problems created by the same cloud: engines that would not run, and windows they could scarcely see through.

Flight 9 landed safely. No one aboard died.

There was no thunderstorm for the radar to show. The danger was dry volcanic ash, and escaping it had helped the engines recover. Even then, the crew still needed a second solution to get everyone onto the runway.
