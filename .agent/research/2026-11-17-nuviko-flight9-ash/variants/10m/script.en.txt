What could stop all four engines of a Boeing 747 when the weather radar showed no thunderstorm? How could losing altitude help restart them? And how would the pilots land if the engines came back but the runway was almost invisible?

On June 24, 1982, British Airways Flight 9 was flying from Kuala Lumpur to Perth, part of a longer journey. The Boeing 747 cruised at thirty-seven thousand feet over Indonesia. Outside, it was night.

Captain Eric Moody, first officer Roger Greaves and flight engineer Barry Townley-Freeman had four engines and a weather radar. They did not have a warning that a volcano on the ground had put something dangerous in the sky.

Radar is an important part of this story. The equipment in the cockpit helped pilots recognize storm clouds. But it was not an instrument that could detect every substance suspended in air. A cloud containing little water could look unremarkable on the screen.

The first clue was a strange glow around the cockpit windows. Passengers saw flashes around the engines. A sharp smell and smoke spread through the cabin.

None of these signs gave the crew a clear diagnosis. The glow resembled an electrical effect sometimes seen around aircraft. Smoke raised the possibility of fire. The crew had to keep the airplane flying while deciding what was wrong.

Then engine number four began to surge and stopped.

Engine number two failed next.

Within moments, engines one and three stopped as well.

The world's most familiar image of a jet is powerful engines pushing it through the sky. Now Flight 9 had no engine thrust at all.

The airplane did not fall vertically. Its wings continued to produce lift as it moved forward. It could glide. But without power, keeping its speed meant losing altitude. Each minute used up part of a supply that could not be replaced.

At the start the 747 was high above the ocean. But land near Jakarta was mountainous. A safe airport lay within the region, yet reaching it required a route that the remaining height would allow.

Moody turned the airplane toward Jakarta and declared an emergency. The crew began restart attempts, working with an unfamiliar situation: all four engines had failed together.

A second emergency complicated their decisions. The aircraft needed to descend toward breathable air, and Greaves's oxygen mask was unusable. Descending more quickly helped with that problem but spent precious altitude.

Moody set a decision point at twelve thousand feet. If no engine restarted by then, an attempt to land on the sea would have to be considered instead of trying to cross the high ground.

That was not a reassuring alternative. An aircraft of this size landing on water in the dark would still be in grave danger. But a crew in an emergency cannot wait to choose until all choices have disappeared.

They kept trying to restart the engines.

At the time, no one in the cockpit knew the cause. Flight 9 had crossed an ash cloud from Mount Galunggung on the island of Java.

Volcanic ash is not simply harmless smoke. It includes extremely small fragments of rock and glass. At an airliner's speed, they strike surfaces facing into the airflow. They can grind away the outer layers of cockpit windows and exposed parts of the aircraft.

An engine faces a different version of the same attack. It draws in large volumes of air. Deep inside, temperatures are hot enough for some volcanic particles to soften or melt. The material can stick to engine components and disturb the airflow that keeps combustion stable.

When the flow and burning are disrupted badly enough, power disappears. This is why four engines could fail in the same flight: all four were moving through the same contaminated air.

The ash also explains the missing radar warning. Ordinary weather radar is designed to find moisture in clouds. Fine dry ash can be effectively invisible to it. The crew had entered a hazard that the instrument was not designed to display.

But the aircraft was still moving. As it descended, it left the most damaging part of the cloud. Cleaner air reached the engines, which had also had some time to cool.

The crew repeated the restart attempts.

At about thirteen and a half thousand feet, one engine came back. Soon the others followed. The aircraft now had thrust again.

There was still a limit to this good news. One engine vibrated so badly that the crew shut it down. The remaining power was enough to continue to Jakarta.

The central crisis seemed over. Then the pilots faced the damage the same ash had left behind.

From outside, the airport was there. Inside, the cockpit windows were scratched almost opaque. The crew could barely see a usable runway picture. The glass had been blasted by the particles as the aircraft flew through the cloud.

A pilot can often use electronic guidance to approach a runway in poor visibility. That night, however, the vertical component of Jakarta's instrument landing system was not working.

The crew needed another way to know whether the airplane was too high or too low as it moved closer to the runway. Having working engines was not enough to solve that problem.

Greaves used the distance-measuring information and an approach chart. The chart showed the heights that should correspond to different distances from the airport. He called those heights to Moody as they flew in.

It was a practical substitute for the missing vertical guidance, using instruments and information that were still available. It was not a guarantee. But it gave the crew a workable descent path when looking out the windshield could not.

Meanwhile, flight attendants helped passengers who had felt the engine failures, the descent and the smoke. Later accounts describe people writing messages for their families. They could not know precisely what adjustments were being made up front.

Flight 9 came down toward Jakarta. The pilots controlled the airplane on the available engines and followed the improvised distance-and-height guidance.

The Boeing 747 landed safely. Everyone aboard survived.

Notice how the danger had changed shape three times. First, invisible particles stopped all the engines. Second, getting out of the cloud helped the crew restart them. Third, the same particles left the pilots struggling to see through the cockpit windows during landing.

Restoring power solved the first emergency. It did not undo the damage.

The lesson was not simply that pilots could restart four engines. It was that a screen showing no storm could hide another kind of airborne hazard. One material could disable the engines and scratch the windows by different mechanisms. And the crew had to solve each consequence in order: decide how much height they could spend, keep trying the engines, then work out how to descend to the runway without the normal view or guidance.

The airplane reached Jakarta safely. The original danger had never appeared as a thunderstorm on its radar.
