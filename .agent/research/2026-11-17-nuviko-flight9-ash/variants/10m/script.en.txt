What could stop all four engines of a Boeing 747 when the weather radar showed no thunderstorm? How could losing altitude help restart them? And how would the pilots land if the engines came back but the runway was almost invisible?

On June 24, 1982, British Airways Flight 9 was flying from Kuala Lumpur to Perth, part of a longer journey. The Boeing 747 cruised at thirty-seven thousand feet over Indonesia. Outside, it was night.

Captain Eric Moody, first officer Roger Greaves and flight engineer Barry Townley-Freeman had four engines and a weather radar. They did not have a warning that a volcano on the ground had put something dangerous in the sky.

The radar showed no thunderstorm. That was reassuring, but it only answered the question the crew knew to ask. Another kind of danger could still be ahead.

The first clue was a strange glow around the cockpit windows. Passengers saw flashes around the engines. A sharp smell and smoke spread through the cabin.

None of these signs gave the crew a clear diagnosis. The glow resembled an electrical effect sometimes seen around aircraft. Smoke raised the possibility of fire. The crew had to keep the airplane flying while deciding what was wrong.

Then engine number four began to surge and stopped.

Engine number two failed next.

Within moments, engines one and three stopped as well.

Now Flight 9 had no engine thrust at all.

The airplane did not fall vertically. Its wings continued to produce lift as it moved forward. It could glide. But without power, keeping its speed meant losing altitude. Each minute used up part of a supply that could not be replaced.

At the start the 747 was high above the ocean. But land near Jakarta was mountainous. A safe airport lay within the region, yet reaching it required a route that the remaining height would allow.

Moody turned the airplane toward Jakarta and declared an emergency. The crew began restart attempts, working with an unfamiliar situation: all four engines had failed together.

Cabin pressure was falling, and oxygen masks dropped above the passengers. But Greaves's mask in the cockpit did not work. Moody steepened the descent toward air the first officer could breathe. Then Greaves managed to fix his mask. As the aircraft passed twenty thousand feet, the crew eased the descent rate again. A problem with one person's equipment had changed how fast the whole aircraft spent its remaining height.

Moody set a decision point at twelve thousand feet. If no engine restarted by then, an attempt to land on the sea would have to be considered instead of trying to cross the high ground.

That was not a reassuring alternative. An aircraft of this size landing on water in the dark would still be in grave danger. But a crew in an emergency cannot wait to choose until all choices have disappeared.

Moody told the passengers that all four engines had stopped and the crew was trying to restart them. Later accounts describe people writing notes to loved ones while the cabin crew helped passengers through the descent. In the cockpit, the restart attempts continued.

At the time, no one in the cockpit knew the cause. Flight 9 had crossed an ash cloud from Mount Galunggung on the island of Java.

Volcanic ash contains tiny hard pieces of rock and glass. At an airliner's speed they strike exposed surfaces like abrasive grit, leaving cockpit windows difficult to see through. Yet dry ash does not make the wet storm-cloud echoes the weather radar is meant to show. Its blank screen did not mean the air was safe.

All four engines were drawing in that same air. In the hottest sections, some volcanic particles can soften or melt and stick to internal components. Those deposits interfere with airflow and combustion. Four separate engines can therefore fail for one common reason.

But the crew could not see those deposits or name the cloud. They had to keep the aircraft moving, decide where to go and repeat the available restart procedures before the remaining height disappeared.

As the powerless aircraft descended, it entered cleaner air. The engines had time to cool and some of the deposits could break away. The pilots could not know whether that would help. They repeated the restart drills as the altimeter moved toward their decision height.

Near the point at which the sea option would have to be chosen, engine four came back. For the first time, Moody could slow the descent without spending so much height. Engine three restarted next, then engines one and two. The crew now had the power to climb toward Jakarta.

But as the aircraft climbed, the strange glow returned. Engine two surged and vibrated again. The crew shut it down and descended to lower air, rather than continuing upward into the same danger. They had recovered power, but not a safe route at every altitude.

The central crisis seemed over. Then the pilots faced the damage the same ash had left behind.

From outside, the airport was there. Inside, the cockpit windows were scratched almost opaque. The crew could barely see a usable runway picture. The glass had been blasted by the particles as the aircraft flew through the cloud.

Jakarta's instrument landing system still provided horizontal guidance. It could help Moody align with the runway. But its vertical glideslope was out: the airplane could be pointed in the right direction and still approach too high or too low.

Greaves opened the approach chart and watched the distance-measuring equipment. At each distance from the runway, he called the height they should be flying. Moody adjusted the descent to those numbers. Flight engineer Townley-Freeman monitored the remaining engines and airspeed. The improvised path depended on three people doing different jobs together.

Through a narrow, less-damaged strip of the windscreen, Moody could begin to make out runway lights. There was no clear forward view and no electronic vertical beam to replace it. The distance, the height and the small glimpse of lights all had to agree before the wheels reached the runway.

Flight 9 touched down safely in Jakarta. The passengers applauded. Everyone aboard survived.

Only after landing did the crew learn that volcanic ash from Mount Galunggung had caused the failures. In a later interview, Moody said what pleased him most was the way the crew worked together on the approach.

Restarting the engines had given them power, but it had not restored the view or the missing glideslope. Greaves called the height for each distance. Moody flew the airplane to those numbers. And the wheels reached the runway. The radar had shown no thunderstorm; it had never promised there was no ash.
