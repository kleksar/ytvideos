Why was a guitarist sending a distress call from the bridge of a cruise ship? Why could the ship's lifeboats not finish the evacuation? And how did every person aboard survive?

On the evening of August 3, 1991, the Oceanos was heading from South Africa's East London toward Durban. Bad weather had already delayed departure several times. The ship eventually sailed into a storm, with waves reported at roughly nine metres.

Moss Hills had been hired to play guitar, not to run an emergency. His wife, Tracy, played bass. Their job was to entertain the holidaymakers.

At dinner, waiters struggled to keep trays steady. Chairs and equipment began sliding. Then the lights went out, and the vibration of the engines stopped.

Emergency lighting came on, but passengers still had no clear explanation. Moss and other performers began playing acoustic music in the lounge to keep the gathering occupied. It could not tell anyone whether the ship was safe.

Moss went below with Julian, a magician from the entertainment team. They reached a set of closed watertight doors. They could not see through them, and opening them would have been dangerous. On another inspection Moss found a lower deck flooded.

Now he knew the ship was taking on water.

Some of the crew and senior officers had already left in lifeboats, alongside passengers. With no obvious evacuation leader on deck, cruise director Lorraine Betts, Moss, Tracy and their colleagues began helping people into the other boats.

But lowering a lifeboat in that sea was nothing like stepping aboard at a dock. A boat would swing toward the ship, crash against the hull, then swing away again. Moss described having to jump back onto the deck to avoid being caught in the gap.

The Oceanos was also developing a heavy lean to starboard. Boats on the opposite side became harder to launch safely. The entertainers stopped using them rather than risk tipping their occupants into the water.

That left hundreds of people on a sinking ship, with the lifeboats no longer a workable answer.

So Moss and several colleagues went looking for the captain on the bridge.

Nobody was there.

Moss tried the radio. Eventually someone answered his distress call and asked for the ship's position. He could say roughly where they were along the coast, but not the coordinates. When asked his rank, he had to explain that he was the guitarist. Tracy, a bass player, and another performer were there with him.

The people responding helped establish contact with nearby ships. But those vessels could not take hundreds of passengers off a liner in these seas. The South African authorities prepared an airborne rescue.

Moss later found the captain at the rear of the ship. According to Moss's account, the captain did not take over the evacuation. The captain's conduct would later be examined by investigators; the details of his state of mind cannot be known from this one witness.

Meanwhile the ship kept leaning.

More than three hours passed before the first rescue helicopter reached them. Navy divers descended from the aircraft onto the moving deck. They showed Moss and Tracy how to secure people into lifting harnesses. The entertainers helped organise the line at the bow; other rescuers worked toward the stern.

One or two people at a time, cables pulled passengers into the air. Wind blew them toward the ship's metalwork. The helicopters had to return repeatedly as dawn broke.

Finally Moss and Tracy climbed into harnesses themselves, among the last people still aboard.

The Oceanos sank about forty-five minutes after the last rescue. Everyone survived.

The remarkable part is not that the guitarist somehow became a ship's captain. He did not. He helped keep an evacuation moving when normal command had broken down, while sailors, divers, pilots and nearby vessels supplied the rescue the passengers needed.
