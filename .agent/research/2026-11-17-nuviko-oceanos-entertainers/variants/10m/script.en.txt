How did a cruise-ship guitarist end up speaking for a sinking liner on the radio? Why did the lifeboats stop working as an escape route? And how did every passenger and crew member get away before the Oceanos went under?

The answer begins before the sinking, during another voyage out of the South African port of East London.

On August 2, 1991, the Oceanos had been hired for a wedding celebration. Weather offshore was already rough. According to guitarist Moss Hills, the ship went out, came back to port, and went out again before the wedding was completed. Hills was part of the entertainment team. His wife, Tracy, played bass.

He had spent years working at sea, but that trip gave him a close look at how badly the ship could move when the wind rose.

The next afternoon the Oceanos was due to sail north toward Durban. Departure was postponed repeatedly in strong winds and rain. Eventually the captain decided to leave.

During dinner, Hills noticed the waiters struggling with trays. These were people who carried dishes on moving ships every day. Now chairs, bottles and stage equipment were slipping around. The performers moved their usual open-air sail-away show inside.

Then the electrical lights failed.

Emergency lighting came on, leaving the public rooms dim. The engines had gone quiet too. Passengers congregated in the lounge, expecting somebody to explain what was happening. No clear general announcement came.

Moss and some fellow performers took out acoustic instruments. They played and sang without the normal sound system. That kept people occupied for a time, but the entertainers also needed to know if the ship could stay afloat.

Moss and a magician named Julian Butler went below deck.

In the machinery spaces they found no working crew to explain the problem. Farther along, closed watertight doors blocked their route. Such doors are supposed to confine flooding to separate sections. Opening them to investigate could have made things worse.

They returned upstairs. Later Moss went below again, this time with a video camera. He found a flooded deck.

That changed his task. He was no longer trying to find out why the lights had failed. He knew people needed to get off the ship.

Up on the embarkation deck, lifeboats were being prepared. Some passengers were boarding, but Moss also saw crew members and senior officers leave. His account says there had been no ship-wide alarm or useful explanation from the bridge.

Cruise director Lorraine Betts, Tracy, Moss and other entertainers began organising people into the remaining lifeboats. Their job descriptions meant little now. There were lines of passengers and a ship moving violently under their feet.

The first obstacle was simply getting people to a boat.

One set of emergency doors had jammed, narrowing the passage. Moss forced part of it open. At another embarkation point, an opening toward the sea remained unprotected after a lifeboat left. People needed to pass it to reach the next boat.

For a few minutes Moss stretched himself across the gap, holding the sides so people could use him like a railing. He later realised that this was too dangerous. He found a rope and rigged a handhold, then helped close the doors.

That detail matters because evacuation was not one heroic instruction. Hundreds of people had to move safely through a series of small, very physical obstacles.

Next came the lifeboats themselves.

As a boat was loaded at the ship's side, a swell could hurl it against the hull, then pull it away, leaving an open gap. Moss described stepping between the deck and the boat to steady the boarding, then jumping clear before it came back.

When a loaded boat was lowered to the sea, people aboard still had to wait for help. The entertainers were not trained to drive every boat, and launching one was not the same as delivering its passengers to safety. Nearby vessels would later assist with those who had made it onto the water.

The Oceanos kept listing to starboard. Boats mounted on the other side were becoming unsafe to lower. A boat might remain jammed against the hull until a roll freed it abruptly, dropping several metres. There was a real possibility of throwing people straight into the water.

Moss and his colleagues stopped that part of the launch.

It was a difficult decision. Hundreds still needed to leave, but the obvious way off the ship could now injure or kill them.

They went to the bridge looking for officers and a plan.

The bridge was empty.

The controls and instruments were unfamiliar to Moss. He tried the radio anyway, repeating a distress call until a voice answered. The operator needed the ship's precise position. Moss could give a rough section of coastline but not the coordinates. Asked about his rank, he explained that he was the guitarist. Beside him stood his wife, the bass player, and another entertainer.

That was not a funny moment for the people waiting in the lounge. It revealed how far the rescue had slipped from the expected chain of command.

The call connected the Oceanos with vessels nearby, and they passed the emergency information to South African authorities. But these ships had only limited rescue capacity. They could not simply take hundreds of people from a listing liner in high seas.

Moss searched for the captain. In his account, he found him near the stern, smoking. Moss says the captain did not provide the direction the remaining team needed. We have that observation from a participant, not access to the captain's thoughts. After the disaster a Greek investigation found the captain and four senior officers negligent.

For the people still aboard, the immediate question was whether rescue aircraft could reach them before the ship went down.

The first helicopter did not arrive for more than three hours after the radio calls. By then the night was giving way to morning, and the liner was leaning increasingly to one side.

South African military and civilian aircraft joined the effort. Navy divers were lowered from hovering helicopters to the moving decks. They had to work in powerful wind and swells while the ship pitched and rolled beneath them.

The rescuers did not ask Moss to fly a helicopter or navigate a ship. They asked him to do a job he could learn quickly: organise a line and fit lifting harnesses correctly.

There was almost no time for training. A diver demonstrated how to secure a harness beneath a passenger's arms, and how to send people up in pairs when possible. Tracy and Moss helped run the forward lift. Other entertainers and divers worked farther aft.

One cable dropped toward the deck, two people were secured, and they rose into the wind. Then the helicopter had to return or make way for another.

Every lift had its hazards. Wind could blow someone dangling from the cable into the railings or the side of the ship. Passengers waiting for their turn were standing on a deck whose slope was increasing.

The naval divers kept checking the vessel, not just the people already gathered outside.

A contemporary account in a professional naval journal describes diver Paul Whiley finding an elderly disabled passenger who could not bring himself to move, then carrying him out toward the helicopter. In that same operation another diver, Gary Scoular, was thrown dangerously close to a propeller while handling an inflatable craft. Both stories show how much of the rescue happened away from the main passenger queue.

With the ship leaning more heavily, the rescuers eventually concentrated lifting operations toward the safer part of the deck. Helicopters shuttled survivors toward shore. Other people who had left in lifeboats were picked up by ships.

At last the remaining rescuers and entertainers could leave. Moss and Tracy were among the final people lifted off the Oceanos.

From above, they could see waves breaking over parts of the liner they had been working on. The deck that had held hundreds of people was disappearing beneath the sea.

Roughly forty-five minutes after the final rescue, on August 4, the Oceanos sank. Everyone aboard had survived.

That is the full answer to the opening question. A guitarist did call for help from the bridge, and entertainers ran parts of the evacuation. But they did not save the ship, or perform the whole rescue alone. The decisive result came from their improvised work together with the passengers, remaining crew, nearby vessels, pilots and navy divers.

The ship was lost. Every life was brought ashore.
