How could a ship full of fertilizer destroy a city? And why did the port explode a second time, long after the first ship was gone?

On the morning of April 16, 1947, workers saw smoke coming from the French cargo ship Grandcamp in Texas City, near Houston. Its holds contained thousands of tons of ammonium nitrate, a chemical used in fertilizer.

The crew tried to fight the fire. Hatches were closed, and steam was used to smother the flames. But the cargo was heating up, and ammonium nitrate can feed a dangerous reaction rather than simply burn out.

Orange smoke rose above the harbor. Firefighters arrived at the ship. People gathered to watch.

At 9:12, Grandcamp exploded.

The blast destroyed the docks, wrecked a nearby chemical plant and started fires across the industrial waterfront. The city's firefighting equipment was destroyed, and many firefighters were killed.

But rescuers still had another problem. Beside Grandcamp had been a second cargo ship, High Flyer. It, too, carried ammonium nitrate. The first explosion had damaged it, and it caught fire.

The crew had tried to move the ship away. Later, tugboats were brought in, but they could not free it from the harbor. With another explosion feared, people were moved away from the danger.

At 1:10 the following morning, High Flyer exploded. It also destroyed a third ship nearby.

The second blast was devastating, but most people had already left that part of the port. Far fewer died in it than in the first explosion.

The two explosions and the fires left an officially recorded toll of 581 dead, though the exact count remains uncertain.

So why did the disaster happen twice? Because the first blast did not just destroy one ship. It turned another ship loaded with the same dangerous cargo into the next emergency.

And while the crews could not save High Flyer, getting people away before it exploded saved lives.
