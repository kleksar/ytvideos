What could make a ship carrying fertilizer explode with enough force to destroy a harbor? Why would the same port face another explosion the next night? And what did the people trying to stop it actually manage to save?

On April 16, 1947, the waterfront of Texas City was busy loading cargo onto ships. The town stood beside Galveston Bay, with chemical factories, fuel tanks and railway lines close to the docks.

One vessel, the French cargo ship Grandcamp, held roughly 2,300 tons of ammonium nitrate. That chemical supplied nitrogen for fertilizer. It had also been used in wartime explosives.

Early that morning, workers found smoke in one of the holds. The crew tried to contain the fire. Hatches were closed. Steam was pumped in to smother the flames and protect the cargo.

But this was not an ordinary load of goods. Under severe heating and the wrong conditions, ammonium nitrate can decompose violently. It can supply oxygen to the reaction, so cutting off ordinary air is not enough to make the danger disappear.

The smoke became orange. The firefighters arrived with their trucks and hoses. People gathered near the docks to see what was happening. By the time flames were rising from the hold, there was very little time left.

At 9:12 in the morning, Grandcamp detonated.

The ship was torn apart. Hot steel and pieces of cargo flew over the waterfront. Nearby warehouses and the Monsanto chemical plant were devastated. Fires spread among oil tanks and industrial buildings. The city's fire trucks were destroyed, and many of the firefighters at the ship died.

For people away from the docks, the blast was not the end of the disaster. Buildings were damaged across the town. Glass broke. Injured residents needed help while fires were still spreading.

Volunteers began carrying people out. Other fire crews and relief workers arrived from surrounding communities. Temporary hospitals and shelters were organized as the town tried to deal with damage on an enormous scale.

But just beside the first ship had been another one.

High Flyer was in port for repairs. It carried about a thousand tons of ammonium nitrate, as well as a large cargo of sulfur. The first explosion struck the ship, tore at its moorings and damaged its hatches. High Flyer drifted across the slip toward a third ship, Wilson B. Keene.

Its crew tried to lift the anchor and move away from the burning wreckage. The anchor would not come up. After struggling to escape, they abandoned the vessel.

By then High Flyer had become a second fire in a harbor already overwhelmed by the first.

The city's emergency response faced an awful choice: let the ship burn beside the docks, or try to pull a burning cargo of ammonium nitrate away from everything else.

Tugboats were brought in. The anchor was dealt with so the ship could be moved, but the tugs managed very little. It appeared something might have trapped the damaged vessel in place.

The crews could not make the ship safe. What they could do was clear people away.

At 1:10 on April 17, High Flyer exploded.

The second explosion destroyed Wilson B. Keene and inflicted more damage on the ruined port. Debris and burning material landed across an area already struggling with fires.

Yet the human outcome was different this time. Most people had withdrawn from that section of the harbor. The second blast killed far fewer people than the first.

In the end, the commonly recorded death toll for the disaster was 581, although the exact number is uncertain. Some workers were never identified, and important employment records had been destroyed.

The chain of events was not simply one ship blowing up twice. The first explosion destroyed the fire crews and damaged the facilities that might have helped contain the emergency. It also set another vessel, carrying the same dangerous substance, on a path toward a second explosion.

The attempts to tow High Flyer failed. The decision to get people away did not.

That is the part of the story easily lost behind photographs of the wrecked port. The second blast was not prevented. But it was anticipated, and the docks were largely emptied before it happened.
