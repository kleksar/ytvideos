Why did a cargo described as fertilizer destroy a Texas harbor? Why was there a second explosion almost sixteen hours later? And if the second blast could not be stopped, why did it kill far fewer people?

To answer those questions, start at the docks, before anyone knew there would be a disaster.

Texas City stood on Galveston Bay, close to Houston. Its waterfront was lined with ships, warehouses, oil tanks, railway equipment and chemical works. A fire here would threaten more than one vessel. It could reach the factories and the neighborhoods behind them.

On April 16, 1947, the French cargo ship Grandcamp lay beside a pier. In its holds were roughly 2,300 tons of ammonium nitrate, a substance widely used to supply nitrogen to crops.

There was a reason such enormous quantities were moving through ports after the Second World War. Ammonium nitrate had also been part of wartime explosives production. Material and industrial capacity associated with war had been redirected toward civilian uses, including fertilizer.

Early that morning, dock workers found smoke inside one of Grandcamp's cargo holds.

A fire on a ship is already a difficult problem. The flames may be hidden below the deck, and the cargo can make opening a hatch dangerous. The crew tried to contain this fire. They closed the hatches and introduced steam, hoping to smother it without sacrificing the cargo.

On many ordinary fires, shutting out air can help. But ammonium nitrate is not an ordinary combustible cargo. Under the wrong conditions, strong heating makes it decompose and release gases. It can help sustain a violent reaction even when outside air is restricted.

That difference matters because the crew's actions were meant to stop the fire. They did not remove the danger growing inside the hold.

The smoke became a conspicuous orange. Firefighters came with hoses and engines. People assembled on the waterfront to watch the burning ship.

At this stage, the town could still see a fire. What it could not see was how the material below the deck was changing.

Around 9 in the morning, flames were rising from the hold. At 9:12, Grandcamp exploded.

The ship disintegrated. Steel and cargo were thrown across the port. A later investigation recorded fragments traveling thousands of feet, and damage far beyond the docks. Warehouse buildings were torn apart; railway equipment and vehicles were wrecked.

The nearby Monsanto chemical plant suffered catastrophic destruction. Oil storage tanks were hit, crushed or set alight. Fires began across the industrial area, each adding a fresh emergency to the one nobody had been able to control.

The explosion also took away the town's first line of response. The local fire trucks at the docks were destroyed. Many of the firefighters standing near Grandcamp were killed. The very people who had come to contain the original fire were now among the casualties.

Residents farther away were struck by broken glass and collapsing structures. Even schoolchildren some distance from the harbor were injured by flying glass and debris, according to an early engineering report.

Power and water supplies were disrupted. The town could not simply send its own engines back to the harbor for a second attempt.

So people began doing what they could with the resources still available. Volunteers moved injured residents. Fire crews arrived from other communities. The Red Cross and other relief organizations helped arrange temporary hospitals, morgues and shelters.

The destruction spread across a working port, but the port had not become empty. Other vessels were still there. And one of them carried a remarkably similar danger.

High Flyer had been docked for repairs near Grandcamp. Its cargo included roughly a thousand tons of ammonium nitrate. Other holds contained about two thousand tons of sulfur.

On the other side of the slip lay a third vessel, Wilson B. Keene, which had been loading flour.

When Grandcamp exploded, High Flyer was struck by the blast. Its hatch covers were damaged, and the force tore at its moorings. The ship began drifting toward Wilson B. Keene.

The crew tried to get High Flyer clear of the burning wreckage. Raising the anchor would have allowed the vessel to maneuver away. But the anchor would not come up. After repeated efforts, the crew abandoned ship.

That decision got the crew away from the vessel. It did not get the vessel away from the port.

High Flyer caught fire. It was now an abandoned ship full of dangerous cargo, trapped among ruined docks, other vessels and expanding fires. The first explosion had created the conditions for another.

The threat was no longer theoretical. People working on relief knew that this second ship might explode as well.

There was still one possible move. If tugboats could pull High Flyer away from the harbor structures, an explosion might cause less additional destruction.

City relief organizer Swede Sandberg and the ship's captain were involved in the effort to free it. An anchor connection was dealt with, and tugboats tried to haul the damaged ship away.

They made very little progress. Accounts differ in how they describe the movement: a later Texas history summary says the ship was towed roughly a hundred feet, while the city's detailed account says the tugs could barely move it and that the vessel may have become wedged.

Neither description changes the outcome. High Flyer remained dangerously close to the waterfront.

It is tempting to imagine that one more attempt, or a stronger tug, might have saved the day. The record does not tell us that. What it documents is an unsuccessful effort and a growing threat.

So attention turned to people, not cargo. Much of the threatened area was cleared. This was not a repair to the ship, and it could not recover anyone already lost at the first explosion. It was a way to limit what happened next.

At 1:10 in the morning on April 17, almost sixteen hours after Grandcamp's explosion, High Flyer exploded too.

The blast destroyed Wilson B. Keene, damaged the waterfront again and sent more burning material and metal over the wreckage. Fires that were already difficult to fight became worse.

An early engineering report later admitted something important: because smoke covered the waterfront and so much was already destroyed, investigators could not always tell which explosion had caused which damage.

But they could distinguish one human fact. The second explosion had been expected, and the threatened docks were largely emptied beforehand. The city reports that at least two people were killed in that blast, far fewer than in the first.

The ship was not saved. The neighboring vessel was not saved. Yet moving people out of reach had changed the human cost of the second explosion.

Across the full disaster, the widely recorded death toll was 581. Even that figure has a boundary: not every dock worker was known by name, some employment records were destroyed, and investigators could not establish an exact final count beyond dispute.

Thousands were injured. Homes, businesses and port facilities had been destroyed or badly damaged. The task of identifying the dead, treating survivors and rebuilding continued long after both ships were gone.

And the consequences reached beyond Texas City. Investigations examined how ammonium nitrate was packed, stored and transported. Changes in chemical handling followed the disaster. Civil claims dragged on for years, and the United States eventually used special legislation to settle thousands of claims involving federal responsibility.

There is also a memorial in Texas City for victims whose remains could not be identified. It is a quieter reminder than the surviving photographs of twisted steel, but it tells us something the images of the explosions cannot.

The central mistake would be to call this only an explosion caused by a fire.

The original fire met a cargo capable of a violent reaction. The resulting blast destroyed the emergency response and struck another ship carrying the same chemical. That second ship could not be moved far enough away. But by then people understood the danger well enough to withdraw from the docks.

Grandcamp and High Flyer were both lost. The first catastrophe could not be undone. The second could not be prevented.

The people who cleared the harbor before the second blast could not stop the explosion. They could stop more people from standing in its path.
