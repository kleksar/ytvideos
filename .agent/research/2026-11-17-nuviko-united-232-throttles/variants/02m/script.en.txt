How do you fly a passenger jet when the steering controls stop working? How can two engines become your only way to turn? And can you get hundreds of people to the ground that way?

On July nineteenth, 1989, United Flight 232 leaves Denver with 296 people aboard. It is a DC-10 with three engines. About an hour into the journey, the crew hears a bang. The engine in the tail has failed.

Then the pilots discover something worse. The control wheel and pedals barely help. All three hydraulic systems have lost pressure. Pieces from the failed engine have torn through the lines that power the flight controls.

The aircraft starts turning right. Captain Al Haynes reduces power on the left engine. The wings move closer to level. That gives the crew an idea: if they cannot steer with the controls, perhaps they can steer by changing how hard the two remaining engines push.

It is nothing like normal flying. Every power change takes time to affect the aircraft. The pilots cannot hold a stable path.

An off-duty DC-10 instructor, Denny Fitch, comes forward from the passenger cabin. He takes over the two throttles so the crew can work together on an emergency landing.

Air traffic control directs them to Sioux City, Iowa. The crew reaches the airport, but the aircraft is descending too quickly and moving too fast. Just before touchdown, its right wing drops. The wing strikes the ground, and the plane breaks apart in a fiery crash.

One hundred and eleven people die. One hundred and eighty-five survive. Investigators later test the situation in simulators and conclude that a successful runway landing would not have been possible with the loss of all hydraulic controls.

The crew did not achieve a safe landing. They achieved something different: they brought an almost uncontrollable aircraft to a place where rescuers could reach the survivors.
