What if a passenger jet loses every hydraulic flight control at once? Could you turn it using only its engines? And why did an aircraft that could barely be steered reach an airport at all?

On July nineteenth, 1989, United Flight 232 takes off from Denver for Chicago, with a further destination of Philadelphia. There are 285 passengers and eleven crew members aboard. Their DC-10 has three engines: one on each wing and a third in the tail.

For more than an hour, nothing unusual happens.

Then comes a loud bang, followed by violent shaking. The instruments show trouble with the tail engine. The pilots begin the engine shutdown checklist. But another set of instruments makes the situation far worse: hydraulic pressure is disappearing across all three systems.

Those systems are supposed to be independent. They move the surfaces that let a pilot climb, descend, bank, and turn. The crew moves the usual controls. The aircraft does not respond.

It is already beginning a descending right turn. Captain Al Haynes reduces power on the left wing engine. The aircraft's wings move toward level. That is not how a jet is normally steered, but it reveals the one thing they still have.

The remaining engines can push unequally. More thrust on one side, less on the other, can change the aircraft's direction. It can also affect pitch. But engine power works slowly compared with the control surfaces, and each adjustment can start a new movement that is difficult to stop.

The crew tries the auxiliary hydraulic pump. It does not restore control. They declare an emergency. Controllers point them toward Sioux City, Iowa.

At roughly half past three, a passenger who is also an off-duty DC-10 instructor comes into the cockpit. His name is Denny Fitch. He has the type-specific experience to help, but there is no simple procedure to follow. Haynes asks him to handle the left and right engine throttles while the crew watches the flight path and works toward the airport.

This is a team trying to fly a machine whose usual means of flying have vanished. When the aircraft banks right, they can change thrust. When it begins swinging back, they must try to catch the movement before it becomes another problem. They cannot simply point the nose at a runway and hold it there.

Below them, emergency services prepare at Sioux City. Cabin crew prepare passengers for an emergency landing. In the cockpit, the pilots have to choose a real runway with an aircraft that has difficulty turning left.

Controllers originally direct them toward the longer runway, number thirty-one. But the jet is lined up with shorter runway twenty-two. Haynes decides not to force the turn they can barely make.

The airport is in sight. The landing gear is down, but the aircraft is coming in fast and sinking much too quickly. The engines still respond; the normal flight controls still do not.

Just before touchdown, the right wing suddenly drops. Fitch changes power, trying to arrest the motion. The wing hits first. The aircraft skids, overturns, breaks apart and burns.

The outcome is devastating. One hundred and eleven people die, and 185 survive. Firefighters and rescuers work at the wreckage.

What caused all of this? Investigators trace the initial blast to a titanium fan disk in the tail engine. A flaw from manufacturing had developed into a fatigue crack. Inspections failed to find it. When the disk broke, its fragments escaped the engine and severed hydraulic lines serving all three systems.

That explains the seeming contradiction: three independent hydraulic systems were defeated by one failure because the fragments reached the places where their lines were vulnerable.

Investigators later try to reproduce the loss of controls in simulators. Their conclusion is severe: the aircraft was effectively impossible to land successfully on a runway in those conditions. The safety board praises the crew's teamwork far beyond what could reasonably be expected.

Flight 232 was not a successful landing. It was a crash with terrible losses. But the crew's improvised control brought the aircraft to an airport, instead of losing it elsewhere, and that gave many people a chance to survive.
