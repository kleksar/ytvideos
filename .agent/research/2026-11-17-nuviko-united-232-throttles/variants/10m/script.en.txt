A jet has three separate systems to keep its flight controls working. What if one engine failure destroys all three? Could two remaining engines steer an aircraft without its steering surfaces? And if the pilots reach an airport, does that mean they can land?

To understand the answer, start with a perfectly ordinary flight.

On July nineteenth, 1989, United Airlines Flight 232 leaves Denver. It is heading to Chicago, with continuing service to Philadelphia. Eleven crew members and 285 passengers are aboard. The aircraft is a McDonnell Douglas DC-10. Two engines hang beneath the wings; a third sits high in the tail.

That arrangement matters later. For now, the flight is routine. The crew climbs toward cruising altitude. More than an hour passes.

At around three sixteen in the afternoon, a loud bang breaks the routine. The airplane shakes. The instruments point to a failure of the tail engine. The crew starts the shutdown checklist. Losing one of three engines is serious, but an aircraft like this is designed to continue flying with an engine out.

Then the first officer reports that he cannot control the airplane. The jet starts a descending turn to the right. Captain Al Haynes takes the controls and finds the same thing. Moving the wheel does not produce the normal response.

The pressure and fluid readings for all three hydraulic systems have fallen to zero.

Hydraulics are not merely an accessory on this aircraft. They provide the force that moves the elevator, rudder and other surfaces used to direct a heavy airliner. The DC-10 has three systems precisely so that a single failure should not take all of them away.

But the crew has lost all three. The hydraulic backup they try does not restore power. They are still in the air, with an aircraft that wants to keep turning right and descending.

Here is the first unexpected discovery. Haynes reduces thrust on the left wing engine. The aircraft rolls back toward level.

An engine normally pushes an airplane forward, not sideways. Yet if the left and right engines push with different strength, the resulting imbalance can turn the whole aircraft. The pilots begin using that difference as a crude substitute for the missing controls.

There is a catch. A conventional control surface responds quickly. A large jet engine needs time to change thrust, and the aircraft takes more time to answer. If the pilots correct one turn, they may start another. Changes in power affect both direction and the attitude of the nose. The aircraft develops continuing, difficult-to-damp movements.

It is not a trick that gives them their airplane back. It is a limited way to influence its path.

At three twenty, the crew requests emergency help. Air traffic controllers steer them toward Sioux City in Iowa. The objective is simple to say: reach an airport. In practice, the crew cannot even make a normal left turn on demand.

Meanwhile, in the passenger cabin, a man with precisely relevant experience is travelling off duty. Denny Fitch is a United DC-10 training and check pilot. He comes forward and joins the crew at approximately three twenty-nine.

Fitch cannot repair hydraulic lines from the cockpit. He does something much more useful for the minutes they have left. He takes the left and right throttles, working them as the others monitor the flight path, coordinate with controllers and prepare for the approach.

The four people in the cockpit are not following a standard engine-only landing procedure that guarantees success. They are sharing a problem that aviation training rarely asks pilots to solve: how to place an aircraft on a runway when almost none of the intended ways to manoeuvre it still work.

Their control is incomplete. The aircraft tends to roll right. Changes in engine power can momentarily correct that tendency, but the resulting flight path is difficult to stabilize. They manage to stay airborne and turn toward Sioux City.

At the airport, firefighters and rescue crews receive warning. The cabin crew prepares passengers for an emergency landing. From the air, the airport is only a point on the ground; reaching its vicinity does not mean the pilots can line up neatly with a runway.

Controllers guide them toward runway thirty-one, which is longer. But the aircraft approaches runway twenty-two. It is shorter and closed. Haynes faces a choice: demand a further left-hand manoeuvre from a jet that resists left turns, or accept the runway now in front of him. He chooses the second.

That choice is not evidence that a shorter runway was preferable. It is evidence of how little steering authority they had left.

Now the second problem becomes impossible to hide. They cannot use the flight controls normally to slow the approach and control descent. The gear is down, but the aircraft is flying too fast and sinking too steeply for a routine landing. The engines remain their only practical control.

A high sink-rate warning sounds. The crew can see the runway. Fitch continues adjusting the two throttles as the pilots watch the instruments and the ground.

Near touchdown, the right wing suddenly falls. Fitch makes a final power adjustment, but the movement is too much. The right wingtip strikes first, followed by the main landing gear. The DC-10 skids sideways, rolls inverted, breaks into sections and burns.

It is four o'clock.

The crash kills 111 people. Others escape or are rescued from the wreckage. One hundred and eighty-five of the 296 people aboard survive. Emergency crews at Sioux City immediately begin firefighting and rescue work.

It would be false to call this a successful landing. It was a terrible accident. It would also miss an extraordinary part of the story to say the pilots simply lost control and crashed. They had enough influence over a nearly uncontrollable aircraft to bring it into the airport area, where a large rescue response could reach the wreckage.

Now ask a different question: how could three supposedly independent hydraulic systems fail together?

Investigators trace the original explosion to the fan disk inside the tail engine. The disk is a heavy titanium rotating component. During its manufacture years earlier, a defect formed within the material. In service, a fatigue crack grew from that defect until the disk broke apart.

A broken fan blade is one danger; an exploding fan disk is another. The energy of disk fragments can be so great that surrounding engine structure cannot practically contain all of them.

Pieces shot into the tail. The three hydraulic systems had separate circuits, but their lines passed through areas that those fragments could reach. The crash exposed the difference between having three systems on paper and protecting them against the same physical event.

The disk did not fail without warning in its material history. It had been in service for years. It had been inspected during engine overhauls multiple times. Investigators found that the fatigue crack should have been detectable at previous inspections if the method had been performed effectively. They identified limitations in the human procedures and quality controls used to inspect such critical parts.

This was not simply a story about a pilot missing a button. The initiating defect began in manufacturing. A crack escaped detection through repeated maintenance inspections. The exploding part then struck a weakness in the aircraft's supposedly independent control systems.

The investigation prompted recommendations on engine-part inspection, the protection of aircraft systems from high-energy debris, and the possibility of backup flight controls. Regulators and the industry subsequently worked on improved inspection methods and rotor safety standards. The causes mattered beyond this one aircraft.

But a final piece of evidence returns us to the cockpit.

After the accident, investigators used simulators to examine what could have been done with the same total loss of hydraulic flight controls. They did not find a practical path to a successful runway landing. In the assessment published by the Federal Aviation Administration, the aircraft was effectively impossible to land under those conditions.

The safety board described the crew's performance as highly commendable and beyond reasonable expectations. That praise is not a claim that the landing itself was safe. It recognizes what happened in the time before the impact.

A failed engine removed a jet's intended steering. A pair of working engines gave the crew only a narrow, imperfect way to change direction. They used it long enough to reach Sioux City, and many people survived a crash that could not be turned into a normal landing.

The real answer is therefore harder than a miracle headline. You cannot reliably land a DC-10 by steering with two engines alone. On Flight 232, the crew nevertheless used those engines to give 185 people a chance to come home.
