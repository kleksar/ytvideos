How could three whales keep breathing beneath Arctic ice? And why would Soviet ships come to help?

Three gray whales had one hole in the frozen sea through which they could breathe. And that hole was freezing shut.

October 1988. Near Barrow, Alaska, the whales had stayed in northern feeding waters too long. Ice surrounded them before they could migrate south.

An Iñupiat hunter spotted them. Local whalers cut more holes through the ice, making places for the animals to surface. But new ice kept forming. A path to open water was still miles away.

Then people watching the news began sending equipment. Chainsaws and generators arrived. Pumps circulated the water so the holes would stay open. The rescuers discovered something unexpected: the whales followed the sound of the pumps from one opening to the next.

But the smallest whale disappeared. It was presumed dead.

And beyond the new holes was a wall of ice the volunteers could not break. Two Soviet icebreakers were diverted toward Alaska. During the Cold War, US crews and Soviet sailors worked on the same rescue.

The ships smashed a channel through the ice ridge, while local workers opened the final breathing holes. The two surviving whales were seen moving toward the cleared water.

Were they saved? They were given a route out. But scientists chose not to attach tracking devices, and no one could confirm their later survival.

The operation had not proved a happy ending for every whale. It had proved that people who normally argued could work together — and that the entire effort began because three animals still had one place to breathe.
