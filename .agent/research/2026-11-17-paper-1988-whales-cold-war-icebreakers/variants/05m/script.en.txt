How did two Soviet icebreakers end up rescuing whales in American waters in 1988? What could a few people with chainsaws do that enormous ships could not? And did the whales actually survive?

An Iñupiat hunter named Roy Ahmaogak found three gray whales near Barrow, Alaska, in October. The whales had remained in northern feeding waters while others migrated south. Now the sea was freezing around them.

They could surface through one small breathing hole. Each time they dived, they had to return to water they could still reach. The ice beyond it was not a thin crust a whale could push aside. It was becoming a barrier between them and the open sea.

Local hunters were the first to respond. They knew the ice and its dangers. Using tools and eventually chainsaws, they cut other holes so the whales might move from one opening to the next. But at these temperatures, the openings kept freezing over.

At first, the whales would not reliably leave the hole they knew.

Then footage reached television news. The situation drew in marine biologists, activists, oil companies, the military and government agencies. Equipment began arriving in a remote place that had not been prepared for an international rescue.

A company supplied generators and chainsaws. Two men from Minnesota brought special pumps designed to keep water moving. The pumps prevented new ice from forming as quickly, but their sound did something nobody had planned.

The whales seemed to follow it.

Rescuers had considered using recorded whale sounds to guide the animals. Instead, the noise of the circulating pumps helped lure them into the next breathing hole. Now they could make progress, one opening at a time.

But the rescue was already losing ground. The smallest of the three whales disappeared under the ice. Scientists presumed it had died.

And even if the remaining two followed every hole, another problem waited farther away. A large ice ridge separated the route from the open sea. Hand tools could keep the whales breathing; they could not remove a massive wall of pack ice.

American rescuers tried heavy machines, including a suspended concrete weight. They considered a barge, but it could not get free of its own ice. None of those plans cleared the final passage.

Then came the unusual request. The United States sought help from the Soviet Union. The countries were Cold War rivals, yet two Soviet icebreakers were close enough to be diverted.

The Admiral Makarov and the Vladimir Arseniev smashed into the ridge and cleared a channel. But the ships could not safely charge right up to the whales. Local workers and scientists still had to connect the animals' breathing holes with the opening the ships had made.

In late October, witnesses watched the two whales pass through the holes toward the broken ice. The rescuers had finally given them a possible way to the sea.

So did the rescue work? It opened their escape route. The smallest whale was lost. The other two were not fitted with tracking devices, partly to avoid stressing them further, so their fate beyond the ice was never confirmed.

What can be confirmed is the extraordinary chain of effort: an Iñupiat hunter found the animals; local whalers kept them breathing; pumps brought them forward; Soviet ships removed the obstacle no one on shore could move.

And the most important part was not the size of the ships. It was that each group solved a different problem before time ran out.
