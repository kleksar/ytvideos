Why did the United States ask Soviet icebreakers to help rescue whales in Alaska during the Cold War? Why did the first rescuers spend days cutting holes instead of simply clearing the sea? And, after a rescue watched around the world, what became of the animals?

In October 1988, three young gray whales remained in feeding waters near Point Barrow, Alaska. Most gray whales migrate south before the sea ice closes in. These three were late.

Roy Ahmaogak, an Iñupiat hunter, found them in a breathing hole while scouting on the ice. The animals could surface there, but the open sea was beyond a growing stretch of frozen water. No one could tell a whale which way to swim under solid ice. First, the rescuers had to stop them from suffocating.

The first rescue team did not arrive in a dramatic aircraft. It came from the local community. Iñupiat hunters knew which ice was safe to stand on and which was shifting beneath them. They cut new holes and worked to keep the existing one open. When scientists later joined the effort, they followed local advice about when conditions were too dangerous to continue.

Then someone filmed the animals. The report reached television, spread beyond Alaska, and brought much more help than anyone had expected.

More people were useful. More people also made the work complicated.

Biologists knew how gray whales migrated, but many had not worked with animals confined under Arctic ice. Activists wanted the whales saved. Oil companies and suppliers offered equipment. The military could move heavy loads. Reporters arrived in such numbers that even finding accommodation in Barrow became difficult.

Marine biologist Jim Harvey later recalled how he and his colleague Dave Withrow were flown to the ice to examine the whales. They noticed a practical problem: despite the holes cut ahead, the animals were reluctant to leave the familiar place where they knew they could breathe. Rescuers could carve a perfect path and still lose the whales if they refused to use it.

The team changed the approach. Workers cut holes closer to the animals and opened them in a way the whales could see and approach. They used poles to slide large blocks of ice down beneath the surrounding sheet. Harvey recalled that the workers eventually grew so quick at it that whales sometimes surfaced soon after a new piece of ice had been removed.

But nothing stayed open for long. The water began freezing again almost immediately.

Businesses around the country saw the television reports. Chainsaws and generators were sent north. Two men from Minnesota brought devices that circulated water below the holes. The constant movement made it harder for ice to form. In a place where every new opening could freeze shut, that was valuable on its own.

Then something unexpected happened.

The rescuers had considered using whale sounds to lure the animals along their route. Instead, they noticed that the noise from the pumps seemed to attract the whales. The pumps that kept the water clear also helped guide them from one breathing hole to the next.

The operation acquired a rhythm. Cut a hole; clear ice; keep the water moving; watch the whales surface; repeat. It took the knowledge of the local hunters, new equipment, and patient observation to make even a little progress.

And it was still not enough.

The smallest whale disappeared. It was presumed to have drowned beneath the ice. In stories about Operation Breakthrough, that loss is sometimes hurried past on the way to the spectacular arrival of the ships. But it mattered. The goal had been to save three animals. Only two remained.

The obstacle ahead was no longer a hole that could be made with a chainsaw. Between the whales and the sea stood a pressure ridge: ice pressed together into a thick, high wall. The rescue team tried to reach open water with heavy machinery. The military brought a concrete weight suspended from a helicopter, intending to smash the ice. They also considered an icebreaking barge from Prudhoe Bay, but it could not escape its own icy conditions.

None of those methods solved the last barrier.

At this point the rescue became an international diplomatic operation. The United States asked the Soviet Union for help. The two countries were still political and military rivals. Yet Soviet icebreakers were working in northern waters, and two were diverted toward Point Barrow.

The names were Admiral Makarov and Vladimir Arseniev. They had immense engines and hulls built to crush ice that would stop ordinary ships. But even they presented a new problem: getting a giant ship too close to frightened whales could harm the animals the whole operation was trying to protect.

The Soviet vessels could attack the pressure ridge farther out. The smaller rescue team still had to create the final connection to the whales.

As the icebreakers approached, rescuers and reporters gathered on the ice. A Washington Post dispatch written during the operation described whales moving from one air hole to another while the Soviet ships hammered at the last ridge. Sometimes wind and fog slowed the work. Sometimes ice threatened to close openings that people had just cut.

Inupiat workers continued using hand tools. They were not replaced by an arriving superpower; their part of the operation remained essential. The big ships could make a channel through a ridge. Only the people beside the whales could keep creating safe breathing places up to that channel.

By late October the icebreakers had breached the great ridge and cleared a route. Witnesses watched two whales swim through the chain of holes toward broken and more open water.

That is the point at which some accounts say the whales were saved. It is fair to say that the rescuers opened a way out. But to understand the real result, the story needs one more step.

The scientists originally considered attaching tracking devices to the whales. After the smallest whale disappeared, they decided not to put the other two under additional stress. The animals went into the cleared channel without tags. There was no reliable way to follow them all the way down the coast or verify that they reached the distant breeding waters.

The two survivors were last seen moving toward freedom. Their ultimate survival is unknown. This is a real limit in the record, not a mystery the narrator can resolve with a hopeful picture.

What the rescuers did achieve was remarkable anyway.

An Iñupiat hunter found the animals, and local hunters kept them breathing. Scientists adapted their method after seeing what the whales actually did. Engineers supplied pumps that both kept openings clear and unexpectedly attracted the animals. Activists, oil-company workers, military crews and governments coordinated. Soviet sailors broke the final ridge.

Their cooperation did not mean they suddenly agreed about whaling, conservation or the Cold War. It meant that each group had something another group needed. Even the scientists later described the cooperation as one of the operation's clearest achievements.

There was also a difficult argument about cost and priorities. Critics asked whether such an enormous effort for three animals made sense. Some marine experts believed the event was natural and that the resources might have done more elsewhere. That debate did not disappear because the rescue looked moving on television.

And the media attention changed the way rescue decisions were made. It helped summon generators, pumps, aircraft and ships. It also meant that people everywhere expected a simple ending: three whales trapped, three whales rescued.

The actual ending was harder. One whale was lost. Two were offered a route toward open sea, but were never reliably tracked after leaving. The only entirely certain victory was the passage the rescuers opened together.

The cold had made the problem too large for a chainsaw and too delicate for an icebreaker alone. The rescue worked as far as it did because neither one was asked to do the other's job.
