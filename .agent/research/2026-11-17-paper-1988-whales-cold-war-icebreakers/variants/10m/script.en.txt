Why did the United States turn to the Soviet Union for help when three whales got trapped in ice? How do you persuade a whale to swim away from the only place it knows it can breathe? And how much do we actually know about what happened to the animals after the rescue?

In October 1988, an Iñupiat whaler named Roy Ahmaogak was out scouting the sea ice near Barrow, Alaska, when he found three gray whales. They should have been heading south to their winter waters. Instead, they had remained in the Arctic until the ice closed in around them.

Roy told people in town. Among those who went out to see the whales was a local wildlife biologist, Geoff Carroll. The group had to travel over the ice by snowmobile for roughly half an hour. There, in the middle of a frozen sea, all three whales were taking turns coming up for air.

The opening was shrinking. Carroll later recalled that the water stirred by each surfacing whale was helping to keep it from freezing completely. His fellow biologist Craig George suggested enlarging the hole with a chainsaw. That could buy time. But as soon as people cut another opening, they discovered a problem that no amount of cutting would solve on its own: the whales would not leave the hole they knew.

Before the rescuers had found a way to move them, something happened back in town that changed the scale of the operation. The group wanted to record what they had seen. They asked the local television station for a camera, and the station sent its own cameraman. Marie Carroll, Geoff's wife, went out with the group too. She worked in the borough's information office. The pictures made it onto the news in Anchorage and soon spread across the country.

There was no stockpile of chainsaws waiting in Barrow for an operation like this. Jim Harvey, one of the biologists who later worked on the rescue, recalled that a company arranged to send them north. Other supplies followed. The local hunters could cut faster and work on more openings, but every hole had to be maintained.

The news coverage also changed the scientists' position. Harvey had previously worked on gray whale tagging in Mexico. He recalled that researchers had initially been reluctant to interfere with a natural event. With the whales now receiving national attention, he and his colleague Dave Withrow were asked to go to Alaska, with plans to tag the animals.

By the time Harvey and Withrow reached the whales, there were only two. The smallest had disappeared under the ice and was presumed dead. No one had recovered the animal. For the remaining pair, making it easier to breathe at one spot was clearly not enough.

The local workers had become remarkably efficient. Harvey recalled that they could open a rectangle of ice about fifteen feet by twenty-five feet in ten minutes. They cut around the edges, then pushed one end of the block down with poles and slid the entire slab beneath the surrounding ice. That meant they could remove a piece too big to lift out of the water.

Yet the whales stayed close to the original opening. From above, the next hole might look quite near. For the animals it meant swimming away from a reliable supply of air, beneath a solid ceiling. Harvey and Withrow suggested moving the new openings much closer together. The change worked. Eventually, according to Harvey, a whale would sometimes appear in a new hole as soon as the workers removed the block of ice.

Another solution arrived from Minnesota. Two employees of a company that made water circulators saw the story on television. They brought their machines to Alaska. The devices kept water moving, slowing the formation of new ice at the breathing holes. But the crew also noticed that the whales were drawn toward the machines.

Carroll's team began using the circulating pumps and portable lights to encourage the whales to move along the route. Much of this work happened at night, when the reporters had gone back to town. Over several days, the animals began moving between the openings. They eventually travelled in both directions, even returning to holes they had already visited.

Now there was a way to move them without taking away the place where they could breathe. The team could maintain an opening behind the whales while cutting another ahead. The workers extended that chain over the ice, but there was still a barrier between the holes and the ocean.

Sheets of sea ice can be pressed together and pile into a ridge. This one rose roughly thirty-five feet above the water, according to contemporary reporting. The ice below the surface was part of the obstacle too. People cutting breathing holes could not simply carry their method through such a mass.

The Americans tried to find a machine that could finish the job. A helicopter brought in a huge concrete block to break ice. An icebreaking barge was considered, but it was unable to leave Prudhoe Bay. The whales still needed a route through the ridge.

Two Soviet icebreakers were in the region, on their way home. The United States asked the Soviet Union for assistance, and the Admiral Makarov and Vladimir Arseniev were redirected to Barrow. Their arrival raised a very particular Cold War concern. Harvey remembered the sensitivity of bringing a Soviet ship so close to the coast and the American naval research facility where he and Withrow were staying.

The ships could break through ice that defeated smaller equipment. But the closer they got to the animals, the more important it became to coordinate their movement with the people on the surface. There was no point creating a channel if a ship endangered the whales in the process.

Geoff Carroll was still working on the ice, cutting and maintaining the breathing holes. Marie had a different assignment. She and the borough's mayor went aboard a Soviet icebreaker to help keep the ship from getting too close to the animals. Carroll later remembered the whole operation as something their family had taken part in together.

On October twenty-sixth the pressure ridge finally began to give way. The Vladimir Arseniev could work in shallower water than its companion. The crews had spent the night breaking through the ridge, while local workers opened more breathing holes on the other side. As the day went on, strengthening winds threatened to close those holes again, and fog delayed the work.

The critical moment came when the ships and the workers reached the ends of each other's routes. Harvey recalled that an icebreaker reached the last hand-cut opening and then backed away, leaving a passage behind it. Now the whales had water ahead of them that did not end at another small hole.

People watching from the ice saw the two animals move toward the broken ridge. By the evening, they had been seen surfacing in open stretches amid the ice debris. The ships continued working to keep the channel clear. Those were the last observations recorded in one contemporary report, before the rescuers stopped watching for the night.

Harvey had originally come to Alaska to help tag the whales. In the end, the scientists decided not to put the animals through that extra stress. Later reports of gray whales off California could not establish that they were the same two. No reliable record tells us whether they completed their migration.

The rescue therefore has two different outcomes. One whale disappeared and was presumed dead. The other two were given a way out, but their survival beyond the ice remains unknown. What the people on that ice did accomplish was to join a line of openings made with hand tools to a channel made by Soviet ships. It took both to give the whales a chance.
