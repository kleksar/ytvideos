How did a man complaining about litter help solve a hundred-million-dollar diamond robbery? And why are most of those diamonds still missing?

In February 2003, traders arrived at the Antwerp Diamond Center in Belgium and found their vault ransacked. Thieves had emptied more than a hundred safe-deposit boxes over the weekend. The loss was widely estimated at around a hundred million dollars.

The building was supposed to be one of the most secure places in the diamond district. Cameras and several kinds of alarms protected a vault deep below street level. Yet the thieves had left without setting off a response.

One suspect was not an unfamiliar visitor. Leonardo Notarbartolo had rented an office inside the building. Investigators later reconstructed a robbery prepared over many months. By the time anyone discovered the damage, the crew and the valuables were gone.

But outside the city, August Van Camp found bags of rubbish dumped on his property. He was angry enough to call the police.

The bags held much more than litter. Investigators found documents connected with the Diamond Center, business details pointing toward the suspects, and tiny precious stones. A torn invoice and other material helped lead them to Notarbartolo.

Police arrested him on February twenty-first, only days after the robbery. He was later sentenced to ten years in prison. Several associates were also convicted.

So the case was not solved by the vault's expensive defenses. It was cracked by evidence abandoned beside a road—and by a man who refused to leave somebody else's rubbish on his land.

But an arrest is not the same as a recovery. Most of the stolen diamonds were never found. The thieves left clues about themselves. They did not leave the loot.
