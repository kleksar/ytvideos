How do thieves empty one of the most heavily protected diamond vaults in Europe without triggering an effective alarm? How do detectives identify them in less than a week? And why does solving the robbery fail to bring back the diamonds?

On Monday, February seventeenth, 2003, people returned to work at the Antwerp Diamond Center in Belgium. Down in the vault, the door had been breached and safe-deposit boxes forced open. An enormous collection of diamonds, jewelry and other valuables had vanished during the weekend.

Antwerp wasn't merely a place where diamonds were sold. The diamond district brought traders, dealers and their stock into a small area. The Diamond Center offered a shared vault for those valuables. Its security was meant to make a robbery exceptionally difficult.

That is why the discovery was so hard to explain. There had been no effective alarm response while the thieves were inside. Whoever entered knew more than a casual burglar could learn by looking at the front door.

One of the men investigators would later identify was Leonardo Notarbartolo, an Italian who had rented an office in the building. He could present himself as a regular customer. Investigators concluded that the robbery had involved long preparation, access, and a coordinated crew rather than a sudden break-in.

The thieves struck over the weekend of February fifteenth and sixteenth. They reached the underground vault despite its layers of protection and opened more than a hundred boxes. Later accounts often give the number as 109 out of 189, though contemporary totals and descriptions differed.

The commonly reported value was around one hundred million dollars. That figure was an estimate, not an audited inventory agreed by every owner. The vault contained property belonging to many different people, and the later accounts disagree about the exact haul.

For the owners, the immediate problem was simple: their boxes were empty. For detectives, the harder question was how to find people who had apparently left almost nothing inside the building to identify themselves.

The answer appeared well away from the vault.

August Van Camp owned land near a road outside Antwerp. He found bags of rubbish that someone had dumped there, and he called the police. It was not a tip about a diamond robbery. It was a complaint about litter.

Officers examining the rubbish noticed material connected with the Diamond Center. There were documents and other objects that had no ordinary reason to be lying on that property. Some of the papers pointed toward people in the building; small gemstones were also found among the debris.

The bags connected two locations that otherwise had no obvious relationship: a vault beneath a trading district and a patch of land beside a road. Investigators followed the paperwork and the leads it supplied.

Notarbartolo's name appeared in the trail. Police arrested him on February twenty-first, less than a week after the break-in. Other suspected members of the crew were identified through the investigation.

Think about that contrast. A group had spent months studying a highly protected target. Yet the breakthrough did not come from a camera catching them in the act. It came after somebody threw away things that should never have been left together, and a landowner refused to ignore them.

In 2005, a Belgian court sentenced Notarbartolo to ten years in prison. Several associates received sentences as well. The arrests and convictions answered who was involved in the robbery, at least in the account established by the investigation and the courts.

They didn't answer every question.

Notarbartolo later told a different version of events, including a claim that somebody else had arranged an insurance fraud. Investigators have disputed that claim. It should not be treated as the established explanation for the theft.

Nor does a conviction tell us exactly where every stone went. Diamonds can change hands without retaining a label tying them to a particular safe-deposit box. More than two decades later, most of the reported haul remains missing.

So this is two mysteries with two different outcomes. Investigators traced the people who broke into the Diamond Center, but they did not recover most of what those people took.

The expensive vault had failed to stop the robbery. A bag of rubbish helped undo the robbers. And the stones themselves, unlike the people accused of taking them, largely disappeared.
