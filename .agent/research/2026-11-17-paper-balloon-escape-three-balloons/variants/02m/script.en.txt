Could eight people escape a guarded border in a balloon they built at home? And what happens when the police find the wreck of their first attempt?

In 1979, East Germany wouldn't let most of its citizens simply leave for the West. Two families, the Strelzyks and the Wetzels, decided to go over the border instead.

Peter Strelzyk worked on a burner. Günter Wetzel stitched fabric into a balloon envelope. Their first design leaked too much hot air to carry them.

In July, the Strelzyk family tried another balloon without the Wetzels. It climbed into a cloud. Moisture soaked the fabric, and the balloon sank before reaching West Germany.

The family got home unseen. The balloon did not.

A hunter found it in the border area. East German secret police investigated, then published a list of objects recovered nearby, hoping someone would identify their owners.

The families read that notice. They were still free, but now there was evidence.

They bought fabric in small quantities from different towns and sewed a larger balloon. In September, both couples and their four children went to a forest clearing at night.

During inflation, the fabric caught fire. They put it out and took off anyway.

Below them were searchlights. Above them, darkness. They couldn't steer like an aircraft; they depended on the wind.

After twenty-eight minutes, the fuel was gone. The balloon came down in a field. They still didn't know which side of the border they had reached.

Then they met West German police officers.

All eight had crossed safely. The machine that carried them there was their third balloon. The second had nearly exposed them before they ever made it over the border.
