How do you build an aircraft in secret when buying the materials can give you away? And how do you know you've escaped when you land in the dark?

East Germany, 1979. A guarded border separated the country from West Germany. Peter and Doris Strelzyk and their friends Günter and Petra Wetzel wanted to take their four children across it. They couldn't simply buy tickets and leave.

Peter had mechanical experience. He worked on a propane burner that could heat the air inside a balloon. Günter stitched long strips of fabric together on an ordinary sewing machine.

The first balloon was too porous. Hot air escaped through its fabric, so it couldn't lift the group. They needed another.

By July, the Strelzyks had a balloon they believed could carry their own family. They launched at night without the Wetzels. For a few minutes, it worked. They rose high above the ground and headed toward the border.

Then they entered a cloud. The fabric took up moisture. The balloon became heavier and started descending. It came down on the eastern side, short of the border.

Peter, Doris and their two sons made their way home without being caught. But they couldn't take the fallen balloon with them.

That became a second problem, much more serious than sewing.

On July twentieth, a hunter reported a balloon in the border area. The East German secret police, the Stasi, began investigating. They had no names, but they had an object large enough to prove somebody had attempted an escape.

On August fourteenth, a newspaper carried a police appeal. It listed things recovered near the balloon: tools, a pocket watch, a barometer. Anyone who recognized them was asked to contact the police.

The families saw the list. It was a reminder that the investigators didn't have to find a new balloon in flight. A familiar object could lead them to its makers.

The two families worked together again. This time the balloon had to lift eight people, including four children. The new envelope required more than twelve hundred square metres of fabric.

Buying that much from one shop could attract attention. Instead, the families purchased small quantities in different towns. Günter spent nights sewing. Peter's burner and the basket had to work with an envelope far larger than their earlier designs.

They also had to wait for a suitable wind. A balloon goes where the air carries it; the passengers can change altitude, but they cannot turn toward a runway.

By the night of September fifteenth, the envelope was ready. They loaded the equipment and drove toward a forest clearing, taking back roads. They planned to launch while most of the country was asleep.

At the clearing, they anchored the basket, spread out the fabric and began filling it with air. Then a piece of the envelope caught fire.

Günter Wetzel later described extinguishing the flames. They had no completed test flight to fall back on, and the night offered the wind they needed.

They got all eight people aboard. The balloon lifted into darkness.

As it climbed, searchlights shone up from below. The families were visible, but getting above the border was only half the problem. They couldn't look down at a clear painted line and know when they had crossed it.

The balloon climbed to around two thousand metres. A damaged section near the top let some heated air escape, according to Wetzel's account. Keeping the burner running consumed precious propane.

Eventually the burners stopped. The gas had run out, and the balloon began to sink.

They landed roughly twenty-eight minutes after takeoff. Everyone was alive. But in the dark field, they still didn't know whether the wind had carried them far enough.

Then they encountered uniformed officers. They asked whether this was the West. It was: the officers were from West Germany, near the town of Naila.

Eight people had escaped. They had crossed about eighteen kilometres in a balloon built at home.

And the detail that nearly defeated them wasn't the guarded fence. It was the balloon they had left behind after the earlier failed flight—the evidence that forced them to build their final aircraft while the police were still looking.
