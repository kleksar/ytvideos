Could you hide the construction of an enormous hot-air balloon in a country where leaving without permission was a crime? And if the first flight failed, how long would you have before the secret police discovered who built it?

In September 1979, two East German families answered those questions with an aircraft made from fabric, a homemade burner and an extraordinary amount of sewing.

Germany was divided. To the west was the Federal Republic, and to the east a state with guarded borders and strict limits on travel. For Peter and Doris Strelzyk and their friends Günter and Petra Wetzel, taking their four children to the West was not a matter of buying eight train tickets.

They lived in Pößneck, a town in Thuringia. Peter had worked with aircraft and electrical equipment; Günter knew how to make things and had access to a sewing machine. Their plan wasn't to break a checkpoint barrier. They hoped to pass over the border at night, carried by the wind.

A hot-air balloon sounds simple. Heat the air inside an envelope, and it becomes lighter than the cooler air outside. But the balloon must contain enough hot air to lift the basket, fuel, equipment and passengers. Every square metre of fabric adds weight. Every leak means more fuel burned to stay aloft.

The two men had to solve those problems without openly asking a factory to manufacture a border-crossing aircraft.

Their first envelope, tested in 1978, was made from material that let too much air pass through. The burner couldn't overcome the losses. It was a design failure, not a near escape.

They tried again with another balloon. By July 1979, the Strelzyk family believed it could lift the four of them. The Wetzels did not take part in that particular flight.

They chose a night with a useful wind. The balloon rose from a clearing, climbed high, and carried Peter, Doris and their two sons toward the border.

Then it entered a cloud.

Moisture condensed on the fabric. The envelope gained weight; the balloon began to sink. There was no engine with which to fly the last distance under power. It landed on the eastern side, close enough for the family to know how narrowly they had missed their destination.

They escaped detection and got home. But the collapsed balloon was far too large to bring back unnoticed.

Now the story changed. The challenge was no longer simply how to make a balloon fly. It was how to build another one while investigators were looking for the people who had left this one behind.

On July twentieth, a hunter found a balloon in the border region and reported it. East German police and the Stasi, the secret police, had physical evidence of an attempted flight across the border.

The investigators searched for clues about who could have made it. They examined objects recovered near the wreck and explored possible links to their owners. The Federal Archives describes a broad search that failed to establish the escapees' identities.

Then, on August fourteenth, a notice appeared in the regional newspaper. It appealed for information about the owners of several recovered items: a pocket watch, a barometer and everyday tools.

Those sound like ordinary household objects. That was precisely what made the notice troubling. Somebody might remember seeing a particular watch or recognizing the modified instrument. The newspaper gave the Stasi a way to recruit neighbors and strangers into its search.

The families read the appeal. Their previous attempt had failed, and the investigation was now public. The next balloon could not be built at a leisurely pace.

They worked together again, this time preparing for all eight people to fly.

The envelope needed more than twelve hundred square metres of fabric. Picture a patchwork surface larger than several apartments laid flat. Yet the buyers couldn't simply walk into one shop and ask for the whole quantity.

They travelled to different towns and bought small amounts. Part of the fabric was difficult to obtain. At home, Günter pieced together immense sections of the envelope on a mechanical sewing machine.

Peter concentrated on the heating system. Propane cylinders would feed burners beneath the opening. A basket had to hold two couples, four children and the equipment needed to keep hot air flowing throughout the journey.

The engineering problem grew with the ambition. More passengers meant a bigger envelope. A bigger envelope meant more fabric and more weight. More weight meant more hot air—and fuel—to stay above the ground.

And unlike an aeroplane, the balloon had no rudder that could turn it west. Its pilots could climb or descend into different air currents, but they had to choose a night when the wind itself would carry them across the border.

In mid-September they finally heard a forecast promising the direction they needed.

There was another problem: this enormous third balloon had never flown with its passengers. They could prepare it on the ground, but they could not safely perform a conspicuous daylight rehearsal and still expect the plan to remain secret.

On the night leading into September sixteenth, they loaded equipment and travelled by smaller roads toward a clearing. The families used a car, while Günter Wetzel later recalled making part of the journey on a moped with the Strelzyks' older son, Frank. He said its engine repeatedly overheated.

At the clearing, they attached the envelope to the basket, anchored the structure and set up a blower to inflate the fabric before heating the air fully.

Wetzel's own account describes the inflation taking only minutes once the noisy blower began working. Then, as the balloon was being prepared to rise, part of the fabric caught fire.

They extinguished it. The balloon still had to lift eight people, and there was no completed test to guarantee that the damaged envelope would hold.

They climbed in and released the ropes.

The basket rose into the night.

From the ground, the heated balloon was not completely invisible. Searchlights were directed into the sky as it travelled toward the border. One soldier who saw the illuminated shape was Peter Richter. Decades later, German broadcaster BR24 would tell a story about an unexpected friendship between him and Günter Wetzel.

But that later reunion was far away. On this night, the families needed the balloon to keep moving.

Wetzel recalled a damaged section near the top of the envelope that released heated air. The burners had to keep feeding more heat into the balloon, using fuel they couldn't replace.

At roughly two thousand metres, they were high enough to look down on lights below, but not to read their own position from the landscape. A border in the dark was not a line painted across the fields.

They could only trust the chosen wind, the balloon and the remaining propane.

Then the burners stopped. The gas was exhausted.

There was no way to restart a burner without fuel. The air inside the envelope began to cool, and the balloon descended. It had flown for about twenty-eight minutes and travelled roughly eighteen kilometres.

It came down in a field near the Bavarian town of Naila.

The eight people survived the landing. But they hadn't yet received the answer to the question that mattered: had the wind carried them across the border, or had they come down just short again?

They encountered uniformed officers and asked whether they were in the West.

They were. The officers were West German. The two families had made it.

Their success also answered a different question for the East German authorities. Those objects printed in the newspaper had belonged to people who could no longer be detained at the border. The Stasi had been investigating the previous failed balloon while its builders finished another.

That did not mean the story ended neatly for everyone left behind. The East German state questioned and pressured relatives, and tightened controls intended to prevent similar flights. The Strelzyks later moved again, this time to Switzerland, before returning to their home region after Germany's reunification.

The balloon itself became a historical object, preserved and exhibited in Bavaria. And the soldier who saw it pass overhead, Peter Richter, eventually became friends with one of the men who had flown in it.

That friendship is a later chapter. The answer to the original question arrived in a field near Naila.

Eight people crossed a guarded border in a homemade balloon. But it took three envelopes, a failed flight that left evidence behind, and one final journey whose passengers didn't even know they had succeeded until they met the police.
