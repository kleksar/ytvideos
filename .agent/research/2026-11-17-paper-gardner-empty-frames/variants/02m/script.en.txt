How do two men in police uniforms take half a billion dollars' worth of art from a museum—and leave the frames behind? And why are those frames still empty?

Boston, March 18, 1990. Two men ring the employee entrance of the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum. They say they're police officers answering a disturbance call.

The night guard lets them in. He isn't supposed to.

The visitors move him away from the security desk. A second guard arrives. Both men are restrained in the basement. Now the people who should protect the museum can't call for help.

For the next eighty-one minutes, the intruders move through its galleries.

They cut two Rembrandt paintings from their frames. They take Vermeer's The Concert, works by Degas and Manet, an ancient bronze vessel, even a decorative eagle from a Napoleonic flag. Thirteen objects in all. This isn't a story about thirteen paintings; some are sketches or objects.

At 2:45 in the morning, the thieves leave. The guards aren't freed until police arrive hours later.

Investigators have followed leads for decades. The FBI says the stolen collection is worth more than five hundred million dollars. Yet the works have not been recovered, and the case remains unsolved.

So why not just hang other paintings where the stolen ones used to be?

Because the museum itself was arranged by its founder, Isabella Stewart Gardner, as a work of art. In 1994, the museum put the empty frames on display as a deliberate reminder of what was taken—and what it hopes will return.

The thieves removed thirteen objects in eighty-one minutes. More than thirty-six years later, visitors can still see the spaces they left behind.
