Why did a terrible smell make London's politicians approve a project they had delayed for years? And why did that decision help fight a disease they misunderstood?

In the summer of 1858, the River Thames was carrying much of London's sewage. The city's population had grown, and new flushing toilets made it easier to send waste away from homes. The trouble was where it went: straight into the river.

Hot weather lowered the water level. Sewage lay exposed along the banks, baking beside the Houses of Parliament.

Members of Parliament tried soaking curtains in chemicals to keep the smell out. It didn't work. The building stood beside the Thames, and the smell came straight through.

For years, engineers had argued that London needed a better drainage system. Now the people who could approve the money could hardly work in their own building.

A bill giving the authorities the means to tackle the problem became law in just eighteen days.

Engineer Joseph Bazalgette led the construction of a network of sewers that intercepted waste before it reached central London. The project took years, with major work continuing until 1875. It was not a sewer built in eighteen days.

There was a second twist. Many Victorians thought foul air itself caused cholera. Doctor John Snow had already linked the disease to contaminated drinking water, but his explanation was not widely accepted.

The new sewers helped keep sewage away from the water people used. That mattered more than making the city smell pleasant.

London's politicians acted because they could no longer bear the air outside their windows. The lasting benefit was something they couldn't smell: cleaner water, and a safer city.
