Why did a bad smell succeed where years of warnings had failed? How did a city that believed disease travelled through foul air end up building something that made its drinking water safer? And why did Parliament act in eighteen days when the work itself would take years?

In the summer of 1858, London had a problem nobody could close a door against.

The River Thames ran beside the Houses of Parliament. It also received the waste of a city of more than two and a half million people.

London had changed faster than its drains. Houses were getting flushing toilets, a real convenience inside the home. Instead of removing sewage from the city, however, many pipes carried it toward the river.

The Thames was tidal. Water could move back upriver rather than carry everything straight out to sea. Household waste mixed with rubbish from streets, markets and factories.

The river served another purpose, too. Londoners still drew water from the Thames and from supplies vulnerable to sewage contamination.

The city had already paid dearly for that arrangement. Cholera outbreaks had killed thousands. The prevailing explanation was that people became ill by breathing miasma: poisonous air from filthy places.

A London doctor, John Snow, argued that contaminated drinking water spread cholera. In 1854 he investigated an outbreak near a public pump in Soho and traced many illnesses to that water source. His findings challenged the accepted view, but they did not persuade everyone.

In 1855, scientist Michael Faraday publicly warned about the condition of the Thames. Engineers also had plans to improve drainage. London's Metropolitan Board of Works wanted a comprehensive system, but securing the money was a different matter.

Then came a hot, dry summer.

As the river level fell, sewage was left exposed along the banks. Weeks of heat made it worse. People called the result the Great Stink.

The worst location for anyone hoping to ignore it was Parliament itself.

The building sat directly beside the river. Curtains and blinds were treated with chloride of lime in an attempt to block the smell. MPs still had to conduct business inside.

The political argument changed. A sewer was no longer an expensive project someone else could worry about. The people deciding whether it should be funded were working in the fumes.

Benjamin Disraeli put forward a measure to finance the work. It became law in just eighteen days.

That number is memorable, but it is easy to misread. London did not build its sewers in eighteen days. It passed the legislation that made a huge construction programme possible.

Chief engineer Joseph Bazalgette oversaw the new network.

The principle was to intercept waste moving through older local drains before it entered the Thames in central London. Large sewers carried it east, relying on gradients where possible and pumping stations where the land was too low.

This was an enormous physical undertaking. Tunnels had to fit beneath existing streets. Some routes ran beside the river, where new embankments gave the city a different shoreline as well as space for infrastructure.

The system did not instantly stop every discharge into the Thames. Sewage was moved farther downstream; modern treatment came much later. But it changed the relationship between the city's crowded centre, its drains and its water.

The major project continued until 1875.

The disease story took longer to settle as well. Snow had died in June 1858, before the Great Stink had run its course. London suffered another cholera outbreak in 1866, concentrated in parts of east London with contaminated water supplies outside the protection of the completed works.

By then the evidence for waterborne transmission was becoming much harder to dismiss.

So what actually persuaded Parliament?

Not a sudden discovery about bacteria. Not even a new engineering plan. It was a smell coming through the windows of the very people who could authorize the money.

What London gained went far beyond comfort. By tackling its sewage, the city helped reduce the contamination that had made drinking water so dangerous.

The politicians wanted cleaner air. The lasting victory was cleaner water.
