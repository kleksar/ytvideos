How did a river that London had been polluting for years suddenly become a crisis for Parliament? Why did it take a terrible smell to get a sewer bill through in eighteen days? And what did the new sewers do for a disease the lawmakers blamed on bad air?

Start with a Victorian household that could afford a flushing toilet. Waste vanished from the room, but it still had to go somewhere. As more houses sent sewage into the old drains, more of it reached the Thames. London had over two and a half million inhabitants by the middle of the century. The river was taking the waste of a city far larger than the one those drains had been built for.

The Thames ran through the centre of London. It also received commercial and industrial rubbish, and water from the city's smaller rivers and drains. Waste did not simply rush out to sea. The river was tidal. A rising tide could push water upriver, keeping pollution in the city longer than anyone might expect.

The summer of 1858 was unusually hot and dry. Thames water fell low enough to leave sewage exposed on the riverbanks. The sun heated the waste. An appalling smell spread along the river.

People who could afford to leave London did. Those who stayed tried to keep the smell out of their rooms. At Westminster, members of Parliament were in a particularly bad position: their building overlooked the river. Curtains treated with chloride of lime offered little relief.

On the eighteenth of June, a member of the Commons named Ross Donnelly Mangles stood up and asked what the government was doing to protect the health of Parliament's members. He recalled the Thames salmon of his youth and complained that the splendid new parliamentary building greeted visitors with a dreadful smell. The issue was now part of the debate inside the building itself.

Yet the danger Mangles feared was not simply discomfort. He warned the House about what might happen if cholera returned. To understand why those concerns did not produce an effective sewer system sooner, we need to go back four years, to a doctor looking for the cause of the disease.

Cholera had repeatedly struck London. People became violently ill, and thousands died. Yet the medical explanation widely accepted at the time pointed toward the air.

The theory was called miasma. If a place smelled rotten, the air itself was thought to carry disease. It was an appealing idea in a city where dirt and illness often appeared in the same streets. The trouble was that the smell was a warning sign, not the way cholera usually travelled.

John Snow suspected drinking water. In 1854, when cholera struck a part of Soho, he investigated the pattern of illness around the Broad Street water pump. Cases clustered among people who used the source, while some people living nearby but drinking other water escaped.

The pump became famous. But there is a detail often lost when this story is told. The outbreak was already declining around the time the pump handle was removed. Removing it should not be described as the single action that instantly stopped the epidemic.

The pump was only part of John Snow's investigation. Across parts of south London, two companies supplied water to houses in the same streets. One company, Lambeth, had moved its intake upstream beyond the tidal reach of the river. The Southwark and Vauxhall company was still drawing water at Battersea, where it could be contaminated by London's sewage.

Snow traced the water supply at houses where cholera had killed someone. Neighbours could buy water from different companies even when they breathed the same air. Both suppliers served rich and poor households. That gave him a way to compare people living in similar surroundings without treating an entire district as though it had one water source.

In his published account, the death rate during one three-week part of the 1854 epidemic was nearly eight times higher among people supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall company than among the Lambeth customers, after allowing for the size of the populations served. This was an observational comparison, not an experiment in which anyone assigned people to drink dirty water. But the difference was hard to explain by the smell alone.

Snow's argument did not immediately displace the old theory. He was challenging an explanation many authorities thought obvious.

Meanwhile, other warnings were impossible to miss if anyone looked at the river. In 1855, the scientist Michael Faraday drew public attention to the Thames's filthy condition. Reformers and engineers argued for better drainage. The Metropolitan Board of Works, the authority responsible for large improvements, had ambitions for a comprehensive sewer network.

Even if everyone had agreed that sewage needed to be removed, London's engineers faced another problem: where should it go? In the June debate, William Cubitt, who sat on the Board of Works, defended the board. He explained that officials had rejected proposed sewer routes because they feared the tide would bring the waste back towards the city. A scheme that sent the sewage much farther away was expensive, and responsibility for paying the bill remained disputed.

A law had already placed responsibility for the main drains on the Metropolitan Board of Works. The board needed approval for its plans, and the men approving them wanted a solution they could defend. By 1858 the disagreement had become a loop: the city was expected to build, but the plans kept coming back for reconsideration.

Snow died on the sixteenth of June 1858, two days before Mangles made his complaint in Parliament. He never saw the sewer bill pass. The summer crisis was forcing an argument he had not been able to settle through his medical evidence alone into an urgent political decision.

On the fifteenth of July, Chancellor of the Exchequer Benjamin Disraeli introduced a bill with a new way to get the work paid for. He proposed a special local rate and a government guarantee on borrowing of up to three million pounds. The board would be able to plan the works with far greater freedom. There was still opposition: some members questioned whether the money would cover the cost or whether a new outfall would really solve the problem.

On the second of August the bill became law. Eighteen days had elapsed since its introduction. Parliament had found a way to finance and authorize the work, rather than waiting for another plan to settle the argument. Now the engineer Joseph Bazalgette had to build a system that would actually carry London's waste away.

Think of the old drainage as a collection of routes that deposited waste into the river wherever they met it. Bazalgette's system intercepted those routes before they reached the central Thames.

Large trunk sewers took the flow eastward. Smaller local drains fed into them. Gravity carried waste along gradually sloping tunnels where the ground allowed it.

But London was not flat. Some districts sat so low that sewage could not simply roll downhill along the intended route. Pumping stations lifted it to higher levels where gravity could do the work again.

At Crossness, downstream from London, four large beam engines formed part of the pumping station that opened on the fourth of April 1865. Inside the city, some new sewers ran along the river under newly constructed embankments. The works did not merely change the underground map; they changed the shape of the waterfront.

As the network opened in stages, it intercepted more of the old drains. Waste that would once have reached the Thames in the middle of London could now flow through the new system towards the eastern outskirts. The sewage was still discharged into the river farther downstream; it was not receiving the kind of treatment we expect today. A cleaner central river did not mean that every problem along the Thames had gone away.

And before the last major construction work was finished, the city faced a test that had nothing to do with whether Parliament's curtains smelled better.

In 1866 cholera struck again. This time the outbreak was concentrated in parts of east London connected to the Old Ford Reservoir, where contaminated water entered the supply. These areas were not yet protected by the new sewer network. The distribution of the illness gave renewed weight to the explanation Snow had advanced years earlier.

Think of those houses in south London that Snow had investigated. Two households breathing much the same air could have very different risks depending on their water supply. The eastern outbreak brought the importance of safe water into focus again, now while a new system was being built to keep the city's sewage away from the places it had polluted.

Major work on Bazalgette's scheme continued until 1875. London still needed improvements in drinking-water supply, and it would later need ways to treat sewage rather than merely carry it away. No single parliamentary vote or engineering project could make every glass of water safe. But this one changed the conditions under which the disease had spread.

The summer of 1858 gave the people who controlled the money a problem they could no longer postpone. They had smelled a public-health danger. In dealing with it, London began to separate sewage from the water its people depended on. The smell brought the law to Parliament. The water was what made the law matter long after the smell was gone.
