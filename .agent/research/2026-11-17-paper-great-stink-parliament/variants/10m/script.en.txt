How could a smell in the River Thames force Britain's Parliament to authorize one of London's biggest engineering projects? Why had the city been willing to live with the same waste for so long? And how did a project begun for the wrong medical reason help make people safer?

To understand the summer of 1858, start inside a London home.

A flushing toilet was a sign of progress. Press a handle, and waste disappeared from the room. For the household, the problem seemed solved.

For the city, it had merely moved.

London had been growing at an extraordinary rate. By the middle of the nineteenth century, more than two and a half million people lived there. The older system relied on cesspits, collectors and a confusing patchwork of local drains. As flushing toilets spread, more sewage flowed through pipes and ditches toward the Thames.

The Thames ran through the centre of London. It also received commercial and industrial rubbish, and water from the city's smaller rivers and drains. Waste did not simply rush out to sea. The river was tidal. A rising tide could push water upriver, keeping pollution in the city longer than anyone might expect.

Now consider the other end of the same system. Londoners needed drinking water. Some took it from sources contaminated by the city's waste. Water was both a route for disposing of sewage and a route by which disease could return to people.

That was more than a bad arrangement. It was a public-health disaster.

Cholera had repeatedly struck London. People became violently ill, and thousands died. Yet the medical explanation widely accepted at the time pointed toward the air.

The theory was called miasma. If a place smelled rotten, the air itself was thought to carry disease. It was an appealing idea in a city where dirt and illness often appeared in the same streets. The trouble was that the smell was a warning sign, not the way cholera usually travelled.

One doctor was asking a different question.

John Snow suspected drinking water. In 1854, when cholera struck a part of Soho, he investigated the pattern of illness around the Broad Street water pump. Cases clustered among people who used the source, while some people living nearby but drinking other water escaped.

The pump became famous. But there is a detail often lost when this story is told. The outbreak was already declining around the time the pump handle was removed. Removing it should not be described as the single action that instantly stopped the epidemic.

What mattered was the accumulating evidence that contaminated water, rather than foul air, carried cholera.

Snow's argument did not immediately displace the old theory. He was challenging an explanation many authorities thought obvious.

Meanwhile, other warnings were impossible to miss if anyone looked at the river. In 1855, the scientist Michael Faraday drew public attention to the Thames's filthy condition. Reformers and engineers argued for better drainage. The Metropolitan Board of Works, the authority responsible for large improvements, had ambitions for a comprehensive sewer network.

But an ambition is not a funded construction site.

The proposal meant enormous expense, years of disruption and decisions about a city divided among many local interests. There was no shortage of people who could postpone the question.

Then the weather changed.

The summer of 1858 was unusually hot and dry. Thames water fell low enough to leave sewage exposed on the riverbanks. The sun heated the waste. An appalling smell spread along the river.

Londoners called it the Great Stink.

People who could afford to leave did so. Others tried to shut the smell outside. Curtains and blinds were treated with chloride of lime. And for one particular group, that defence proved especially important.

Members of Parliament were sitting in a building beside the Thames.

The waste was on the very riverbank outside their windows. They could not vote the smell away or keep it out with treated curtains. The problem that had burdened poorer neighbourhoods for years now invaded the rooms where national decisions were made.

That was the turning point in the story.

Not because engineers suddenly discovered how a sewer could work. Not because John Snow had just demonstrated a new theory. But because the consequences of doing nothing had arrived at the politicians' workplace.

Benjamin Disraeli, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, presented legislation to provide the means for a massive drainage project. The measure became law in only eighteen days.

An eighteen-day decision is extraordinary. It is also easily misrepresented.

The sewers were not finished in eighteen days. The law made finance and action possible. Detailed engineering and building would take many years.

The job went to Joseph Bazalgette, chief engineer of the Metropolitan Board of Works. The city needed more than one big pipe leading away from Parliament.

Think of the old drainage as a collection of routes that deposited waste into the river wherever they met it. Bazalgette's system intercepted those routes before they reached the central Thames.

Large trunk sewers took the flow eastward. Smaller local drains fed into them. Gravity carried waste along gradually sloping tunnels where the ground allowed it.

But London was not flat. Some districts sat so low that sewage could not simply roll downhill along the intended route. Pumping stations lifted it to higher levels where gravity could do the work again.

The pumping stations at Deptford and Abbey Mills were part of that solution. Crossness, farther east, became another great landmark of the system. Engineers were not just digging holes. They were controlling elevation, flow and direction across a crowded city.

The Thames's banks presented another opportunity.

Some of the sewers ran beside the river. New embankments covered infrastructure and changed London's shoreline. Land was reclaimed. Streets and public space could occupy ground where the river had once reached.

For people on the surface, this meant a city being physically rearranged. For the waste underneath, it meant a new route that avoided the crowded central riverfront.

A huge network of brick-lined tunnels and pumping facilities emerged over the following years. Major construction continued into the 1870s, with the project generally described as complete by 1875.

There is a further complication, though. The early system largely moved sewage farther downstream; it did not provide the treatment a modern city would expect before discharging waste into water. It solved some problems while passing others along the river.

So did it do what Parliament wanted?

It helped remove the central city's sewage and the immediate conditions that had made the Great Stink unbearable. But the more important medical result was not what the miasma theory predicted.

Cholera spreads through water contaminated with the bacterium that causes it. In the old city, sewage could reach the sources people drank from. Reducing that contact helped protect people even when officials misunderstood why.

The evidence became clearer after the reform was underway.

London suffered another cholera outbreak in 1866. The worst affected districts were in east London, where water drawn from a contaminated supply served areas not yet protected by the completed network. The geography of the outbreak strengthened the case for waterborne transmission.

John Snow never saw that evidence. He had died on 16 June 1858, just as the Great Stink was beginning to grip London.

His ideas outlived him.

That doesn't mean the sewer scheme alone eliminated every disease, or that every resident suddenly received safe water. Improvements in sanitation and water supply took time, and the risk of contamination never vanished by one act of Parliament.

The engineering had limits, too.

Bazalgette built a system for Victorian London. The capital kept growing. Much of the network carried rainwater along with sewage. During heavy rain, more water could enter the drains than they could convey, and overflow could again reach the Thames.

More than a century later, London needed another large tunnel to help handle those storm-time overflows. The persistence of that problem is not evidence that the old system failed. It is a reminder that infrastructure is built for a city that keeps changing.

Look at what the Great Stink actually changed.

Before the crisis, the need for drainage improvements was known, but money and agreement kept slipping away. During the crisis, the legislators deciding the city's future could smell the problem in their own rooms. They approved the means to build. Engineers then spent years turning an old patchwork of drains into a much more coherent network.

The lawmakers thought polluted air was the main enemy. The system's greatest long-term public-health benefit came from reducing the contamination of water.

The strange part is not that a filthy river smelled terrible. It is that London finally found the political will to fix its sewage when the smell reached the people in charge.

They were trying to escape bad air. The city gained a better chance at clean water.
