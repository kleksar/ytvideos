Why were the first pictures from the Hubble Space Telescope blurry? How did a mistake made on Earth follow a telescope into orbit? And how could astronauts fix a mirror they couldn't take home?

Hubble was launched in April 1990 to look past the distortion of Earth's atmosphere. Instead, its early images showed stars surrounded by a haze. The telescope had made it into space. Its vision hadn't.

Investigators traced the blur to the main mirror. Its outer edge was polished just a little too flat. Light reflected from different parts of the mirror no longer met at the same focus.

The mistake began in a testing device on Earth. A lens inside that device had been positioned 1.3 millimeters incorrectly, so the mirror was polished to the wrong curve.

But grinding a giant glass mirror in orbit wasn't an option. Engineers worked on the light after it left the flawed mirror instead.

In December 1993, astronauts arrived aboard the shuttle Endeavour. Over five spacewalks, they installed a replacement camera with built-in corrective optics and a separate instrument, COSTAR, to correct light going into other instruments.

Then everyone had to wait for the results.

On December eighteenth, astronomers saw a sharply focused star. Images of the galaxy M100 soon made the difference obvious. And in 1995, the repaired camera stared into a seemingly empty patch of sky and revealed thousands of distant galaxies.

The mirror was never repolished. Hubble's repair worked because engineers had learned the shape of the mistake well enough to correct it.
