How do you repair a space telescope when the defect is in its main mirror? Why did a mistake smaller than a human hair survive testing? And what did astronauts have to replace to make Hubble see clearly?

In April 1990, the Hubble Space Telescope reached orbit. Its advantage was supposed to be simple: above Earth's atmosphere, starlight would reach its instruments without passing through the moving air that distorts pictures from the ground.

The first images did not deliver that promise. Stars appeared fuzzy, with light spread around them instead of gathered into sharp points. At first, NASA had to work out whether the problem lay in a camera, a secondary mirror or the large mirror at the front of the telescope.

Both original imaging cameras showed the same kind of blur. The evidence pointed toward the telescope's shared optics. On June twenty-seventh, NASA announced the problem publicly.

The primary mirror was beautifully smooth but had the wrong shape. Its edge was about 2.2 microns too flat. That was enough to make rays from the center and edge focus at different places. No adjustment of the camera could give every ray the same sharp point.

The investigation led back to a device used to test the mirror while it was being made. One lens in this null corrector had been spaced 1.3 millimeters incorrectly. The machine reported a good shape while guiding the polishing toward the wrong one.

So what could be done with a mirror already circling Earth? Bringing it down for repolishing wasn't a practical plan. Engineers could, however, build optics that introduced an equal and opposite distortion.

A second wide-field camera was already being developed at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. Its team redesigned the camera with small corrective mirrors inside it. The defective primary mirror would still bend light the wrong way; the new camera would bend it back.

But Hubble was not just one camera. Three other original scientific instruments needed their own correction. Engineers built COSTAR, a package of adjustable mirrors that occupied the space of an existing instrument, the High Speed Photometer. Saving Hubble's broader observing power meant giving up that device.

In December 1993, the shuttle Endeavour carried seven astronauts to Hubble. Over five spacewalks, they replaced hardware, installed the new camera and fitted COSTAR. They also had to deal with an unrelated problem: a damaged solar array that could not be rolled up. It was released into space before its replacement was installed.

The crew completed the work, but installing a solution did not prove it worked.

On December eighteenth, astronomers gathered around a monitor to inspect one of the first images from the new camera. The star was sharply focused. A comparison of the galaxy M100, photographed before and after the mission, showed how much detail the telescope had gained.

Two years later, that camera was pointed at a patch of sky that looked almost empty. After days of exposures, the Hubble Deep Field revealed thousands of galaxies. This was not an image engineers could have promised when the mirror problem was discovered. It was a direct result of getting the telescope's light into focus.

The original mirror stayed in orbit, with its original flaw. In 2009, later instruments had their own corrections, and astronauts could even remove COSTAR.

The surprising part of the rescue is what never happened: nobody fixed the mirror's surface. They fixed the path of the light around its mistake.
