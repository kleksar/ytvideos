How could a telescope pass its tests, reach space and produce blurry pictures? What would engineers have to remove to repair its vision? And how do astronauts fix a mirror without ever touching the glass?

Hubble was built to see beyond the distortion of Earth's atmosphere. Air constantly changes the path of starlight for telescopes on the ground. Put an observatory above that air and a much clearer view becomes possible. Hubble's 2.4-meter primary mirror was supposed to gather light and direct it through smaller optics to cameras and spectrographs.

In April 1990, the shuttle Discovery carried the telescope to orbit. Engineers checked its equipment; astronomers began looking at the first pictures. They expected sharp stars. Instead, light spread into fuzzy halos around bright points. Dim objects could disappear in the glare. Hubble could still perform some science, but it was falling short of its central promise.

The fault might have been inside a camera. It might have been elsewhere in the optics. Scientists compared images from two separate cameras. Both displayed the same kind of distortion. That was an important clue: they shared the mirrors before the light reached their detectors.

On June twenty-seventh, NASA publicly acknowledged the problem. Investigators went back through the manufacturing records and the equipment that had certified Hubble's optics.

A mirror can be almost perfectly smooth and still have the wrong curve. The outer edge of Hubble's primary mirror was roughly 2.2 microns too flat. A micron is a thousandth of a millimeter. That tiny shape error was enough for the center and outer edge to send light toward different focal points. When a camera made one part sharp, the other part remained blurred.

Why had the mirror passed its tests? The team found the measuring instrument that had guided the polishing process. It was called a null corrector. One lens in that device had been set with a spacing error of 1.3 millimeters. The test gave a misleading result, so the mirror was ground to the wrong shape.

This was not damage caused by the launch. The mistake had been built in on Earth, with the help of the device meant to detect it.

Knowing the cause was one problem solved. Actually repairing the mirror, already hundreds of kilometers above Earth inside a working telescope, was another. Astronauts could not simply take out that large glass disk and polish a new surface during a spacewalk.

Engineers changed the question. If one piece of glass sent light toward the wrong focus, could a second optical system send it back toward the right one?

The answer resembled how eyeglasses improve vision. Glasses do not alter the eye itself; they change the path light takes into it. Hubble needed a carefully calculated inverse correction, only its instruments were much more varied than a person's eyes.

At NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, engineers were already building an upgraded wide-field camera, WFPC2. They didn't have to start with a blank sheet. But they had to redesign it around the flaw now measured in the primary mirror. Four small corrective mirrors were built into the new camera. Their shape counteracted the distortion from Hubble's large mirror before light reached its detectors.

The original mirror would remain wrong. The replacement camera would make its images right.

But Hubble carried other scientific instruments, too. The Faint Object Camera and two spectrographs still relied on the same damaged optical path. Installing the corrected wide-field camera would not fix their observations. A different solution was necessary.

Engineers built COSTAR: a package of small, deployable mirrors that would redirect light into those three original instruments. It would give them a corrected path without replacing every instrument. But there was no empty slot waiting inside the telescope. COSTAR had to take the place of the High Speed Photometer.

That was a real cost. One existing instrument would be removed so that three others could work as intended. Meanwhile, WFPC2 would correct its own images through a separate set of mirrors. A simplified story about one pair of space glasses leaves out this engineering choice.

NASA had originally planned to service Hubble in orbit. It had accessible instrument bays and replacement equipment in development. But after the mirror problem, a scheduled maintenance mission became a public test of whether the whole observatory could be saved.

The new camera team worked to a shortened deadline. Astronauts trained to exchange hardware while wearing bulky suits and stiff gloves. They also had a list of ordinary maintenance jobs: gyroscopes, electronics and solar arrays. Successful optics alone would not help if the telescope could not point steadily at its target.

In December 1993, seven astronauts launched aboard Endeavour. The shuttle caught Hubble with its robotic arm and secured it in the cargo bay. The telescope was now a workshop, hundreds of kilometers above Earth.

The first spacewalk involved gyroscope hardware that helped keep Hubble precisely pointed. Astronauts Jeffrey Hoffman and Story Musgrave replaced equipment, then had trouble getting the compartment doors closed. The work took longer than expected, an early reminder that nothing about an orbital repair is as simple as the diagram.

Then another real problem appeared. One old solar array was bent and twisted. When the astronauts tried to retract it, it would not fold correctly. The damaged wing could not be packed away. Kathryn Thornton released the array on the next spacewalk while the shuttle maneuvered away, and a replacement array was installed.

The solar-array trouble was separate from the mirror flaw. But old arrays had also caused small vibrations as temperatures changed between sunlight and shadow. Replacing them improved more than the appearance of Hubble's hardware. It helped its ability to hold a steady view.

The crew next removed the original wide-field camera. They installed WFPC2 with its built-in correction. They also removed the High Speed Photometer and placed COSTAR into that space. The carefully designed corrective optics now had to survive an actual installation, not just laboratory testing.

The mission required five spacewalks totaling more than thirty-five hours. As they finished and released the telescope, the crew could say the physical work was complete. Nobody yet had the observation that would prove the fix.

The answer arrived on December eighteenth. Astronomers at the Space Telescope Science Institute gathered around a monitor. They examined a new image from WFPC2. A star that once would have carried a broad halo now appeared clearly focused.

An image of spiral galaxy M100 supplied an even more dramatic comparison. The old camera had shown a soft, smeared center. The corrected camera resolved much more of the structure. The result mattered because it was an observation of the same kind of object, made through the same unchanged primary mirror. The difference came from what happened after its light was reflected.

But the camera's real scientific opportunity was still ahead.

In December 1995, scientists pointed Hubble at a patch of sky that appeared almost empty. They gathered hundreds of exposures with WFPC2 over several days. The accumulated image became the Hubble Deep Field. The dark patch was filled with thousands of remote galaxies, some seen across immense stretches of cosmic time.

The Deep Field was not a photograph of the repair. It was the kind of observation the repaired telescope could now make: gather faint light and retain enough detail to distinguish objects far beyond nearby bright stars. The payoff to the engineering work was not only a sharper star; it was a much deeper view of the universe.

Years later, newer Hubble instruments were built with their own optical corrections. The separate COSTAR package was no longer needed. In 2009, astronauts removed it and brought it back to Earth. It became a museum piece.

And the giant primary mirror? It stayed in Hubble, still carrying the curve polished into it before the launch.

The telescope's rescue was possible because the engineers knew the error accurately enough to cancel its effects. They didn't repair the old glass. They repaired the journey of the light, and the same flawed mirror went on helping reveal galaxies that had seemed hidden in a dark patch of sky.
