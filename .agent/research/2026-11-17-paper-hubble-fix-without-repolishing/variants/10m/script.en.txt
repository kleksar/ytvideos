How could a telescope pass its tests, reach space and send back blurry pictures? What would NASA have to give up to restore its vision? And how could astronauts repair a mirror they couldn't remove?

In April 1990, Discovery carried the Hubble Space Telescope into orbit. With a mirror nearly eight feet across, it could collect light that had never been disturbed by Earth's atmosphere. Astronomers were waiting for pictures in which faint objects would stand out clearly against the black sky.

Instead, the bright stars came back with fuzzy halos around them. Hubble could still do some science, but light that should have been concentrated in a point was spreading across its images. The very thing NASA had put the telescope in space to achieve was compromised.

There were two cameras aboard Hubble. If one had been defective, the engineers might have been able to blame the camera. But both produced similar distortion. They shared the mirrors that collected the light. On June twenty-seventh, NASA announced the problem.

The primary mirror was smooth, but its outer edge was about 2.2 microns too flat. The light reflected near the center came to a different focus from the light reflected near the edge. The telescope could be adjusted, but there was no single position that brought all that light together.

The investigation led back to the factory. The people polishing the mirror had relied on a device that was supposed to tell them when the surface was right. In that device, a lens was out of position by 1.3 millimeters. The mirror had been shaped to satisfy a faulty measuring instrument. NASA now had a precise description of the error, but its huge piece of glass was already in orbit.

At the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California, John Trauger was leading the development of a second wide-field camera. It was originally intended as an upgrade or replacement for the one already aboard Hubble. Now an optical specialist, Aden Meinel, suggested that the team might use this new instrument to compensate for the damaged telescope's vision.

Trauger and optical engineer Norm Page worked with their model of the camera. Hubble's primary mirror was too shallow at its edge. They found that they could introduce the opposite error in four much smaller mirrors inside their own instrument. Each of the four cameras within the new device would get one corrective mirror. In effect, they could build a camera that was deliberately wrong in exactly the way that made the telescope right.

There was an important consequence. NASA would not have to send a new primary mirror into space. It could send the new camera, WFPC2. But changing its little mirrors affected the rest of the instrument, and the replacement had to be ready for the first planned servicing mission in 1993. The camera already under construction suddenly had a very different job.

The camera could only correct the light entering its own detectors. Three other scientific instruments still received distorted light from the same primary mirror. A team at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center developed a separate device called COSTAR, with small mirrors that could be deployed to redirect the light into those instruments.

There was nowhere vacant to put it. Hubble's High Speed Photometer occupied one of the instrument bays. To restore the other instruments, the astronauts would remove the photometer and install COSTAR in its place. One scientific capability had to be sacrificed to recover several others.

The California team also had to make sure the astronauts could install their new camera safely. The seven crew members visited the laboratory to learn how to fit it into the telescope. It would slide into a bay, with electrical connectors hidden at the back. Once the camera was going in, the crew could not reach those connectors directly.

And the instrument was awkward to handle. It weighed more than six hundred pounds on Earth, and although the astronauts would not feel that weight in orbit, they would still have to control its movement. One exposed mirror sat at the end of a short arm outside the casing. A collision with that mirror could spoil the alignment of the entire instrument. During development, an astronaut examining a model actually bumped that part.

In December 1993, Endeavour arrived at Hubble. Its robotic arm caught the telescope and secured it in the cargo bay. Before the crew got to the camera, they had other work to do. They ran into difficulty shutting the doors of a gyroscope compartment, then discovered that one of the old solar arrays was too distorted to fold away. Kathryn Thornton released the damaged array, and the crew fitted its replacement.

Those problems were separate from the flawed mirror, but they made it clear that a successful optical design was only one part of the mission. The telescope had to survive the work and keep pointing steadily after the shuttle left.

Then Jeffrey Hoffman and Story Musgrave removed the original wide-field camera. They guided the replacement into its bay. The crew also exchanged the photometer for COSTAR. Across five spacewalks, they completed more than thirty-five hours of work on the telescope. The two optical corrections were now inside Hubble, but no one had yet seen the pictures they would produce.

Endeavour returned to Earth on December thirteenth. Five days later, the first raw data from the new camera arrived. At the Space Telescope Science Institute, the astronomers saw a focused star. The halo that had spoiled the early pictures was no longer the dominant feature.

When NASA released the new pictures in January, the change was particularly obvious in the galaxy M100. The old camera had recorded a blurred central region; the new one separated its structure much more clearly. On the following day, the camera team presented results at a meeting of the American Astronomical Society. Trauger later recalled that the audience got to its feet and applauded.

Now that the telescope could distinguish objects that its earlier pictures had blurred together, astronomers could try something far more ambitious. In December 1995, they pointed the camera at a tiny patch of sky that appeared almost empty. Instead of moving on to a more obvious target, they kept collecting exposures over many days.

When those exposures were combined, the apparent darkness gave way to thousands of galaxies. The picture became known as the Hubble Deep Field. The repair had made more than a beautiful photograph possible: it had restored the ability to resolve extraordinarily faint and distant objects.

By 2009, newer instruments had corrective optics of their own. COSTAR could finally be removed, and the space it occupied could be used for another scientific instrument. It came back to Earth, along with the camera that had helped save Hubble.

The one piece of hardware that had started the entire crisis was still in place. Its shape had never been corrected. The same glass that had blurred the first photographs had, with the help of those other mirrors, collected the light of thousands of galaxies nobody could see in that tiny patch of sky before.
