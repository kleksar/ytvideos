How could a private fishing lake kill more than two thousand people in a town miles away? Why didn't the warnings save them? And who paid for the dam that broke?

In Pennsylvania, in 1889, an exclusive fishing club had a lake above the industrial town of Johnstown. An earthen dam held back its water.

The dam had a troubled history. Years earlier, a previous owner had removed its discharge pipes. Later, the club repaired the old embankment, lowered its crest and put screens across the spillway to keep fish in the lake.

Those changes mattered when heavy rain arrived. Water was rising faster than it could escape.

On May thirty-first, workers tried to save the dam. Engineer John Parke went to send warnings down the valley. But warnings about that dam had circulated before. Many people did not believe this one would be different.

At about ten past three, the dam gave way.

The flood tore through the valley, carrying trees, buildings and railway debris. Less than an hour later it reached Johnstown. At the stone railway bridge, wreckage piled up and caught fire.

The official death toll was two thousand two hundred and nine.

Now comes the second part of the story. The South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club was never held legally responsible for the flood. The lake was gone, the town was devastated, and the people who had lived below the dam bore the cost.

So what turned a beautiful lake into a disaster? Not simply one day of rain. A dam with fewer ways to release water had been allowed to fail above a town that could not get out of its path.
