Why did a lake used for fishing send a deadly wall of water into an industrial town? Who tried to warn the people below? And why was nobody from the lake's private club held legally responsible afterward?

To understand the Johnstown Flood, begin fourteen miles upstream, in the mountains of Pennsylvania. There was an artificial lake surrounded by cottages. Wealthy members of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club came there to escape the smoke of Pittsburgh. Below the lake stood Johnstown, where families lived beside the rivers that powered the town's factories.

Between the holiday lake and the town was an old earthen dam.

It had not been built as a private resort. It was originally part of a canal system. After the canal became obsolete, the dam changed hands and deteriorated. A previous owner removed five discharge pipes, so the lake could no longer be drained through them. When the fishing club restored the damaged embankment, it did not replace those pipes. It also lowered the crest to widen the road across the top, and installed screens in the spillway to keep fish from escaping.

The alterations sound like separate maintenance decisions. Together, they left fewer ways for unusually high water to get out. A screen that stopped fish could also catch debris. And a lowered crest gave rising water less room before it spilled over the top.

On the night of May thirtieth, 1889, the rain kept falling. By the next morning, the lake was dangerously high.

At the dam, club president Elias Unger and others tried to stop the rising water. Workers attempted to clear the spillway and protect the embankment. Engineer John Parke went to the village of South Fork to get warnings sent down the valley.

Those warnings faced a problem of their own. People had heard frightening stories about the dam before, and not all had come true. The valley was already flooding from the rain. For a resident trying to decide whether to leave a home, a distant alarm about an old dam could sound like another false warning.

But this time the water reached the top.

When an earthen dam is overtopped, the escaping water can begin to eat away at the soil holding it together. At about 3:10 that afternoon, the South Fork Dam broke.

A reservoir of water rushed into a narrow valley. The current gathered trees, houses and railway debris. It did not remain a clean wall of water. Everything it picked up became part of the force moving downstream.

At 4:07, the flood struck Johnstown.

Buildings were torn apart. Some of the wreckage stopped against the city's stone railway bridge and piled into a vast tangle. Then that debris caught fire. For people trapped among it, even reaching the bridge did not necessarily mean safety.

When the water withdrew, the official count reached 2,209 dead. Entire families had disappeared. The loss was not just in the valley directly below the dam: the current had carried destruction all the way through an industrial city.

The response brought help from across the country. Clara Barton and the American Red Cross arrived to provide relief. The flood became one of the first great disasters in which the young organization played a major role.

And then came the question the survivors could not escape: who was responsible?

Some of the club's members were among the country's wealthiest industrialists. The dam had been altered over time, and critical means of releasing water were gone or obstructed. Yet the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club was never held legally liable for the catastrophe. Public outrage and legal accountability turned out to be very different things.

So the answer is not that one storm mysteriously made an ordinary lake dangerous. The storm exposed a long chain of decisions about an old dam. Warnings were sent, but the water arrived first for thousands of people. The lake's owners lost their private retreat. Johnstown lost 2,209 lives.

The lake was gone in an afternoon. The argument over responsibility outlasted it.
