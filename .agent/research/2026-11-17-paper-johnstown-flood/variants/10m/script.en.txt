How could a private fishing lake destroy a town more than a dozen miles away? Why did a written warning, delivered years before the disaster, fail to make the dam safe? And why did the argument over responsibility continue after the flood was over?

In western Pennsylvania, high above the steel town of Johnstown, an artificial lake offered wealthy families a place to spend the summer. There were cottages along its shore and boats on the water. The lake belonged to the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. Downstream, the Cambria Iron Company depended on the people who lived and worked in the valley.

There was a reason the iron company's manager, Daniel Morrell, took an interest in the club's lake. A wall of earth held it in place. If that wall broke, the water would head straight into the valley where his workers lived. In eighteen eighty, he arranged for his engineer, John Fulton, to inspect the dam while the club was developing the property.

Fulton reported serious problems. And in December of that year, Morrell put his objection in writing. He wanted the owners to install a pipe or a gate that would allow them to drain the lake if trouble developed. He even offered financial cooperation from his company if the reservoir could be made safe and provide water during a drought. No reply to that final letter is known. Less than nine years later, men were standing on the dam with the lake rising around them, trying to find a way to get rid of the water.

The dam had actually been built with a way to do exactly that. Long before the fishing club existed, it stored water for Pennsylvania's canal system. In eighteen sixty-two, when the dam was in poor condition, part of it broke. The river at Johnstown rose only a few feet that time. The damaged reservoir eventually passed to John Reilly, a former congressman. In eighteen seventy-five he removed five discharge pipes from the base of the dam. When Benjamin Ruff acquired the property for the fishing club four years later, those pipes were already gone.

The club repaired the old breach without replacing them. It also lowered the crest to make room for a wider road and placed fish screens across the spillway. The lake could discharge water through that spillway once its surface rose high enough, but the owners had lost a way to lower the reservoir deliberately. In a storm, a lower dam also meant less clearance above the rising water. This was the problem Morrell had been asking them to address.

On May thirtieth, eighteen eighty-nine, people in Johnstown took part in the annual Decoration Day parade. Rain arrived later that day and grew heavier at night. The following morning, the rising rivers had already changed the town's routine. The ironworks sent its morning shift home. Schools dismissed their pupils. It was a town that had experienced floods before, but the water was reaching unusual levels. At the telegraph office, operator Hettie Ogle kept sending reports to Pittsburgh.

At the lake, John Parke was an engineer hired to supervise sewer work for the club. He later described waking in fog and finding that the reservoir had climbed roughly two feet overnight. The club's president, Elias Unger, and the men at the dam were trying to protect the embankment and clear the spillway. Parke went to South Fork that morning to arrange warnings for people below. Now the problem was getting those warnings through.

A warning about the dam reached South Fork's telegraph operator, Emma Ehrenfeld. She had doubts about the reliability of its source and contacted the operator at Mineral Point, William Pickerill. He considered the danger serious enough to pass the message on. But the storm had broken his telegraph connection. A man named William Reichard had to carry the information farther along the railway to get it transmitted. The very weather threatening the dam was also interfering with the means of warning the valley.

Another warning followed as the situation at the dam deteriorated. Meanwhile, Ogle could no longer report the exact river level: even the rain gauge had been swept away. The people of Johnstown faced a flood already underway, and not everybody could know whether the warnings from miles upstream meant something worse was coming. At least three warnings were sent from South Fork that day. The last is believed to have reached Johnstown shortly before the dam failed.

At the reservoir, water was beginning to run over the crest. That is especially dangerous for a dam made of earth. Once the water cuts a channel through the soil, more water can rush into that channel and enlarge it. The men could no longer save the embankment. In the afternoon of May thirty-first, the South Fork Dam broke, releasing the reservoir into the valley.

The torrent carried parts of buildings, trees and railway structures into Johnstown. One of the people caught in its path was sixteen-year-old Victor Heiser. His later autobiography describes seeing his family home collapse. He survived on the roof of a barn that was being carried away, briefly took hold of another building and then ended up back on the barn. It was floating toward the stone railway bridge, where the debris from upstream was piling up.

The bridge became the site of another disaster when the wreckage caught fire. Heiser survived the night in a house with nineteen other people. The next day, while people were still being rescued, he began looking for his parents. He eventually learned that his mother's body had been identified. He was never able to identify his father's remains with certainty. The boy who had survived now faced the loss of both parents. In the official tally, the flood claimed two thousand two hundred and nine lives; that figure may exclude some victims.

Five days after the flood, Clara Barton arrived in Johnstown with a Red Cross team. It was not enough to bring bandages and leave. Many of the survivors had nowhere to live. Her organization helped create hospitals and put up six buildings where people could sleep, eat and wash their clothes. Barton stayed until October. Heiser, meanwhile, went on to train as a doctor and work in international public health. His later life is one of the documented consequences of the flood, although we cannot know how every survivor would have lived without it.

The lawsuits that followed brought the question back to the owners of the lake. The club had inherited a damaged dam and made alterations of its own. Morrell's letter showed that a way to drain the reservoir had been proposed long before the catastrophe. Yet the club was never held legally responsible for the flood. The successful lawsuits identified in the historical record were two property cases against the Pennsylvania Railroad. That was a very different outcome from a finding of liability against the club.

The engineering argument did not end there either. An investigation published in eighteen ninety-one concluded that even without the club's changes the storm would have caused the dam to fail. More than a century later, researchers reconstructed the hydraulics of the earlier and later dam designs. In their twenty sixteen study, they estimated that the original design could have released more than twice as much water at the point of overtopping as the reconstructed dam. They concluded that a properly rebuilt dam would probably have survived the storm.

That is a modern engineering estimate, not a measurement made in eighteen eighty-nine or a new court judgment. But it gives the question raised in Morrell's letter particular weight. He had asked for a way to let water out of the lake in an emergency, and had offered to help make it safe. The danger he described was the one the workers confronted when rain filled the reservoir. The messages sent downstream that day could warn people about the danger, but they could not undo the choices that had been made at the dam.

Today, the old lakebed is part of a national memorial. The holiday cottages and the gap in the dam tell two sides of the same story. The owners lost their summer retreat, while thousands of people below lost their lives. Years before that happened, someone had offered to help make the dam safe. The warning survives. The opportunity it described does not.
