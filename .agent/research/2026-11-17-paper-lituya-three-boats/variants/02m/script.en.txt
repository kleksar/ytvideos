What could lift a fishing boat over the tops of trees? And why did the largest measured tsunami run-up happen in a quiet Alaskan bay, rather than out at sea?

On July 9, 1958, three fishing boats sheltered for the night inside Lituya Bay. It was surrounded by mountains, with a narrow opening to the Pacific.

At about ten sixteen, an earthquake shook the bay. Howard Ulrich hurried onto the deck of the Edrie. His seven-year-old son was aboard. At the far end of the water, rocks were falling from the cliffs.

Then came a crash. An enormous mass of rock had dropped into a small inlet, throwing water against the opposite mountainside. The water swept the trees away and raced along the bay.

Ulrich could see the wave coming. He tried to free the anchor, but it wouldn't release. He let out the rest of the chain and started his engine. When the wave reached the boat, the chain broke. The Edrie rose, rode the surge, and came back into the bay.

Closer to the entrance, Bill and Vivian Swanson were on the Badger. Their boat was still anchored when another part of the wave arrived. It lifted them over a wooded spit. Bill could look down at the trees. The boat landed hard beyond the spit and sank, but the couple got away in a small skiff. Another fishing boat later picked them up.

The third boat, the Sunmore, disappeared. Its two occupants did not survive.

A geologist measured the highest line of destroyed forest at 1,720 feet, or 524 metres. That was how high water ran up a slope at the head of the bay. It was not a single, vertical wall of water that height sailing across the ocean.

Four people survived on two boats. The mountain was left with a bare scar. And the terrifying number attached to this story describes where the water reached, not how tall the wave looked to the people inside it.
