How did a fishing boat end up looking down on a forest? Why did two crews live through the biggest tsunami run-up ever measured, while a third boat vanished? And what does that famous height of 1,720 feet actually mean?

The story begins in a sheltered Alaskan bay on the evening of July 9, 1958. Mountains surrounded Lituya Bay on almost every side. At its mouth, a narrow passage led into the Pacific. It looked like a good place to spend a night, and three fishing boats were anchored inside.

Howard Ulrich had his seven-year-old son on the Edrie. A married couple, Bill and Vivian Swanson, were aboard the Badger, farther toward the entrance. Orville and Mickey Wagner were on the Sunmore.

At about 10:16 that night, the ground began to shake. Ulrich got onto his deck and saw avalanches coming down the distant slopes. Then, near the back of the bay, he heard a tremendous crash.

A large part of a mountain had broken away. About thirty million cubic metres of rock fell into Gilbert Inlet, a narrow arm of the bay. Water had nowhere easy to escape. The impact hurled it up the mountainside opposite the slide, and the surge spread into the main bay.

Ulrich watched the water approach. His anchor was stuck. He could not simply pull it up and steer away. He paid out the chain and started the engine, with his son still aboard.

The wave reached the Edrie. The anchor chain snapped, and the boat rose with the water. Ulrich later reported being carried toward the shore and then washed back into the main bay.

That was not the end of the danger. The water kept surging from bank to bank. Logs were moving across it, and sharp waves continued after the first great crest. Ulrich managed to keep the boat under control and sailed out through the entrance.

The Badger had a different journey. Bill Swanson was looking toward the head of the bay when he thought he saw a glacier rise into view and shake. What he was seeing from that distance was part of a violent collapse and a huge surge of water.

The Badger remained anchored as the wave approached. Then it was picked up, stern first, and carried straight over a wooded spit near the bay's entrance. Swanson looked down and saw treetops far below his boat.

Outside the spit, the crest broke. The Badger struck bottom and was lost. Bill and Vivian escaped in a small skiff. Roughly two hours later, another fishing boat picked them up.

The Sunmore was not so fortunate. The Wagners had tried to head toward the entrance. Their boat disappeared, and neither survived.

Now for the height that made Lituya Bay famous. A geologist named Don Miller surveyed the stripped forest. The highest damage line, on the steep hillside opposite the rockslide, stood 1,720 feet above sea level: 524 metres.

That is a run-up height. Water had rushed up that particular slope to that elevation. It does not mean that a single upright wall of water, more than half a kilometre tall, travelled through the whole bay. The waves the fishermen described nearer their boats were far lower, though still powerful enough to lift and wreck vessels.

Miller had seen signs of earlier giant waves here: unusually sharp boundaries between old forest and newer growth. After 1958, the bay was marked by an even more dramatic bare stripe, visible in aerial photographs and later satellite images.

Two boats, four survivors, and one lost crew. The record is not simply a number to put beside a skyscraper. It is the trace of water forced into a mountainside, and of three small vessels that happened to be in its path.
