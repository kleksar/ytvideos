Why did one Alaskan bay throw a fishing boat over the tops of trees? How could its wave leave a mark more than half a kilometre up a mountain? And why were four people able to tell the story when two others never returned?

There is a detail about Lituya Bay that makes the night of July 9, 1958 even stranger. Just hours before the earthquake, a party of mountaineers had flown out of the bay. They had been exploring the surrounding peaks for weeks and were waiting for a flight the following day. Their pilot arrived early instead. By evening, they were gone.

Three fishing boats stayed.

Lituya Bay cuts into the Alaskan coast like a long, steep-walled pocket. It opens onto the Pacific through a narrow entrance. Behind the mountains at the far end are glaciers and two smaller inlets, where the Fairweather Fault passes through the landscape.

For a fishing boat looking for shelter, the bay offered protection from open ocean weather. But calm-looking water was not the same as a safe harbour.

Long before 1958, visiting mariners had discovered how dangerous the currents at the entrance could be. French explorers lost boats there in the eighteenth century. The island inside the bay was later named Cenotaph, meaning an empty tomb.

There was another warning, higher up the hills. A geologist named Don Miller had noticed abrupt boundaries in the forest: older trees above, younger growth below. They looked like the marks of earlier enormous waves. Miller had studied the bay and identified evidence of several previous events, including one in 1936. He could see the damage but did not have the complete explanation.

That July evening, the Edrie was anchored in a small cove along the southern shore. Howard Ulrich was aboard with his seven-year-old son. Closer to the entrance, the Badger carried Bill and Vivian Swanson. The Sunmore carried Orville and Mickey Wagner. All six people were inside a bay that was about to be reshaped.

At around 10:16 p.m., the earthquake struck.

Ulrich was woken by the shaking and hurried up onto the deck. The mountains at the head of the bay were shedding rock and snow. Farther toward the entrance, Bill Swanson also felt the boat vibrate violently.

For a moment, Swanson saw something he could hardly account for. Looking past the island, he thought the glacier at the back of the bay had risen into the air and moved forward. Pieces of ice seemed to fall from it.

Then Ulrich heard a great crash.

The earthquake had shaken loose a huge mass of rock from a high cliff above Gilbert Inlet. Roughly thirty million cubic metres of material crashed down into the water. The narrow inlet did not give that displaced water much room to spread sideways.

Water shot across the inlet and up the opposing mountainside. Some spilled over a separating ridge. More of the disturbance pushed into the main bay, where the shorelines guided it outward. This was a landslide-generated tsunami, very different in scale and shape from the long waves that can cross whole oceans after an offshore earthquake.

Now the boat crews could see what was coming.

Ulrich's anchor would not come free. On a clear night with a working anchor, pulling it up would be a routine job. With the water approaching, it became the thing preventing him from moving.

He let out all the chain he had, started the engine and prepared to take the force of the wave. His son was still on the boat.

As the crest approached the Edrie, the front looked steep. The island in the middle of the bay divided the water: on one side it was breaking, while on the other the face was smoother. Ulrich later told the geologist that the height near his boat was measured in tens of feet, not thousands.

Then the Edrie began to rise.

The pressure broke the anchor chain. The boat travelled up with the wave and was carried toward the southern shore. A returning rush of water swept it back into the main bay.

Ulrich had survived the first impact. That did not mean he could simply sail away. The surface was still moving violently from shore to shore. Tree trunks and floating debris made an already dangerous passage harder. He kept the boat under control, and by eleven o'clock that night he was able to leave through the entrance.

The Badger was farther from the rockslide but much closer to the exit. The Swansons had anchored near a low, tree-covered spit separating the bay from the ocean. Bill had already watched the apparent movement of the glacier and the new wall of water advancing down the bay.

When the surge reached them, the Badger was still on its anchor.

The wave picked up the boat and carried it backward, stern first, toward the spit. Bill Swanson looked over the side. The trees were below him. He estimated that the boat was more than eighty feet above their tops.

Think about what that means. A place where a fisherman would ordinarily have seen shoreline and branches was now beneath a boat riding water over the land.

But the crest broke beyond the spit. The Badger struck bottom outside the sheltered bay and foundered. The couple survived the crossing only to lose their vessel.

Bill and Vivian left in a small skiff. They were not instantly safe, but they were alive. About two hours later, another fishing boat found them and took them aboard.

There was no equivalent account from the Sunmore.

According to the later reconstruction, the Wagners had moved toward the entrance as the danger grew. Their boat was lost, and both occupants died. The three boats that had shared an anchorage produced three very different outcomes.

By the next day, the tide and the light made another story visible. Around much of the shoreline, whole stands of trees had vanished. There was a sharp boundary: intact forest above, bare land below.

Don Miller returned to examine it. He could compare what he saw with the older damage lines he had already been studying. This time the evidence was unmistakable. The bay had experienced another giant wave, far larger than the earlier scars had suggested.

He measured the highest limit of destruction at 1,720 feet above sea level. That is 524 metres.

This is where many retellings leave viewers with the wrong mental picture. The number is the height of run-up: water that raced up a particular mountainside, opposite the falling rocks, reached that elevation. It is not a measurement of a vertical wall, 524 metres high, moving across the entire bay.

Imagine throwing a great mass into one end of a narrow bathtub. The water can surge far up the wall nearest the impact, and then slosh back through the tub. A mountain fjord is much larger and far more violent, but the distinction matters. Local splash, the angle of a slope, and water moving back and forth all affect where the highest mark is left.

Farther down Lituya Bay, the damaged forest lay at lower elevations. The fishermen also described crests much smaller than the record run-up. Smaller does not mean harmless: the Badger was lifted above trees, and the Sunmore was lost.

The marks outlived the eyewitnesses' night. Aerial photographs taken after the disaster show a pale strip wrapping around the bay. Decades later, satellite images still show younger forest growing where the older trees were wiped out.

And Lituya Bay's past makes those images more than a record of one extraordinary evening. Its steep unstable slopes, glaciers, deep inlets and earthquake fault have not disappeared. The geological conditions that produced the wave are still part of the landscape.

The mountaineers had left just in time. The Edrie escaped with father and son aboard. Bill and Vivian Swanson were rescued from their skiff after the Badger sank. Orville and Mickey Wagner did not come home.

The record of 1,720 feet tells us how far water climbed. The three boats tell us what that force meant at human scale. And the forest line is still there, because the most revealing part of this wave was not a wall moving across an ocean. It was the height the water reached on a single mountain.
