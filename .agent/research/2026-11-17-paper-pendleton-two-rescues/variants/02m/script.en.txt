How do you bring thirty-three men off a broken tanker when your rescue boat is barely eleven metres long? And if you manage that, how do you find the shore without a compass?

In February 1952, a winter storm split two oil tankers near Cape Cod. One, the Fort Mercer, sent a distress call. The other, Pendleton, couldn't: the section containing its radio room had been destroyed.

A Coast Guard aircraft discovered Pendleton while searching for the first ship. Eight men were stranded on its bow. Thirty-three remained aboard the stern, which was still being driven through the waves.

Bernie Webber and three other Coast Guardsmen went after them in a thirty-six-foot wooden lifeboat.

Before they reached the wreck, a wave smashed the boat's windshield and tore away its compass. The engine kept stalling. Engineer Andy Fitzgerald climbed into the machinery space and got it running again.

They found Pendleton in the darkness. Its surviving crew lowered a rope ladder. The broken tanker rose and fell beside the little boat, making each transfer dangerous. Webber had to bring his boat in, take one man, and pull away before the next wave.

They brought thirty-two men aboard. George Myers, who had helped the others, was lost during the transfer. The eight men on the bow did not survive either.

Now thirty-six people crowded a boat that had started with four. It had no working compass, and the storm hadn't stopped.

Webber steered toward the coast, searching for a familiar light. A red harbour buoy finally appeared through the darkness. It marked the route home.

The rescue wasn't simply a small boat reaching a big ship. Thirty-two people survived because the boat made it back.
