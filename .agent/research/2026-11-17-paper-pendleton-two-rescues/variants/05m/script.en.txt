How could four Coast Guardsmen get thirty-three sailors off a tanker that had snapped in two? Why did another rescue, happening in the same storm, almost leave those men undiscovered? And what use is a successful rescue if you cannot find your way home?

Off Cape Cod in February 1952, two enormous oil tankers were fighting a winter storm. They were vessels built during the Second World War, with long steel hulls carrying their weight across the waves.

The first ship, Fort Mercer, broke apart and sent a distress call. Coast Guard cutters and aircraft went to find it. Their crews were already trying to save men from a ship in pieces.

But the storm had broken a second tanker: Pendleton.

Its forward section contained the bridge and radio room. After the ship separated, Pendleton couldn't send a distress call. The men stranded at the back had no way of making their situation known from there.

A Coast Guard aircraft looking for Fort Mercer found the other wreck. Near the town of Chatham, there were now two broken ships in the same storm.

Pendleton's bow carried the captain and seven others. None would survive. Its stern still held thirty-three men, and this was the section rescue crews could try to reach.

Bernie Webber had already spent the day helping grounded fishing boats. Now he was assigned a new crew. Andrew Fitzgerald would look after the engine; Richard Livesey and Ervin Maske would help bring people aboard.

Their boat, CG-36500, was thirty-six feet long. Pendleton was more than five hundred feet long before it broke. The four Coast Guardsmen would have to bring their little wooden hull alongside the broken steel one, in darkness and heavy seas.

First they had to cross Chatham Bar, a dangerous stretch of shallow water where offshore waves rise and break. They passed a red harbour buoy as they left. That light would matter again.

At the bar, a wave lifted their lifeboat, turned it and hurled it back toward the harbour. Waves smashed the windshield and tore away its compass. The boat could still move, but its crew had lost a vital way of knowing their direction.

Farther out, the engine repeatedly stopped as the boat rolled. Fitzgerald had to reach into the cramped machinery space and restart it. He was burned and bruised doing the work. Every restart gave Webber another chance to steer into the sea rather than be pushed helplessly through it.

The four men couldn't simply drive to a point on a chart anymore. Webber heard a heavy crashing noise through the storm. Then their searchlight picked out twisted steel: Pendleton's broken stern.

The men on the tanker lowered a rope ladder.

It was not a ladder hanging quietly against a wall. The stern rose and plunged; the lifeboat did the same at a different moment. A man could reach the bottom just as the hulls moved apart. Or the small boat could be driven beneath him.

Webber handled the wheel and throttle, repeatedly bringing the lifeboat under the ladder for one person, then pulling away to line up another attempt. Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske hauled men out of the water and onto a boat that was filling with survivors.

Every new person made the return journey more crowded. But the men still waiting above couldn't simply be left where they were.

Thirty-two of the thirty-three men came aboard. George “Tiny” Myers, who had helped others leave the tanker, was killed during the transfer. The rescue had saved almost everyone on the stern, but not everyone.

And it was still only half done.

The lifeboat now held thirty-six people: four rescuers and thirty-two survivors. Its compass was gone. Snow and darkness hid the shore. The engine that had already failed more than once still had to carry all of them across dangerous water.

Webber searched for something he knew from the way out: a light, land, a navigation marker. Eventually a small red light appeared. It was the harbour buoy near Chatham Bar.

The four rescuers had left past that marker with space all around them. They returned to it with thirty-two men taken from a broken ship.

The Coast Guard later awarded all four rescuers the Gold Lifesaving Medal. The number remembered from that night is thirty-two. But it only became a rescue when thirty-six people made it through the bar and back to shore.
