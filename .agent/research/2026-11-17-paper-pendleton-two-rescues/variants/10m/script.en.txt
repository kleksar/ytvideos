How do you rescue thirty-three sailors from half a tanker with a wooden boat built for a handful of people? What happens when the rescue boat loses its compass before it even reaches the wreck? And why did the crew have to solve the same problem twice: first at the tanker, then on the way home?

In February 1952 a winter storm struck the coast of Massachusetts. Snow buried roads on land, while far offshore two long oil tankers met seas powerful enough to break their hulls.

Both ships belonged to a wartime type called the T2. They had been built to move fuel during the Second World War. They were large, useful ships, but a long steel hull in cold, heavy seas could be put under enormous stress as successive waves lifted different sections of it.

The first tanker to become the centre of an emergency was Fort Mercer. Its hull split. A distress message went out, and Coast Guard ships and aircraft were sent to reach the crew.

Then the rescuers discovered that Fort Mercer was not the only tanker in trouble.

The same storm had broken Pendleton in two. It was closer to Chatham, a town on Cape Cod. But the broken ship had never sent a distress call.

The reason was brutally practical. Pendleton's bridge and radio room had been in the forward section. When the hull separated, that equipment was lost. The sailors left aboard the stern could not use it to tell anybody where they were.

A Coast Guard aircraft, already searching for Fort Mercer, found the second wreck. The radio silence had hidden a separate emergency.

There were now four major pieces of ship in the sea: two halves of Fort Mercer and two of Pendleton. Larger rescue vessels were already occupied with the first tanker.

Pendleton's bow carried its captain and seven other men. They would not survive. The other thirty-three crew members remained on the stern, a vast section of steel still floating and pitching in the storm.

A first Coast Guard lifeboat went out to investigate one part of the wreck but found no survivors. The people on the stern still needed a way off.

At Chatham station, Bernie Webber was assigned to take a second small boat out. He had spent much of the day helping grounded fishing vessels. This new trip was different. There would be no sheltered shoreline beside the damaged ship, and no safe pier waiting to receive the people on board.

Webber put together a crew of three. Andrew Fitzgerald was responsible for the engine. Richard Livesey and Ervin Maske would help with the rescue. Their wooden motor lifeboat was designated CG-36500.

It was thirty-six feet long, around eleven metres. Before it broke, Pendleton had stretched more than five hundred feet. The difference in size mattered. If the lifeboat found the tanker, the men above would not step onto a steady platform. They would have to get from the side of a massive, moving steel wreck into a tiny boat moving independently below it.

The four Coast Guardsmen left Chatham after dark. To get to open water they first had to cross Chatham Bar, a shallow area where incoming waves grow steeper and break. Webber knew the route. They passed a red navigation light marking their way out of the harbour.

Soon the sea took away their advantage.

A huge wave picked up the lifeboat, turned it and threw it back toward the harbour. Other seas broke across the small craft. The windshield shattered. The compass was ripped from its mount.

A compass cannot stop a wave. But once the shore and the horizon disappear behind snow and spray, it helps tell a helmsman which way the boat is heading. Without it, Webber could still steer. He just had much less information to steer by.

The crossing had already damaged their ability to find Pendleton. Turning around would also mean leaving thirty-three men on a broken stern, waiting for help that might not reach them in time.

Webber continued into the storm.

On the open water the engine repeatedly stopped as the lifeboat rolled. Its engineer, Fitzgerald, climbed into the cramped machinery space and started it again. In the process he suffered burns and bruises.

This was not merely an inconvenience. On a steep wave, the boat had to meet the next surge at a controlled angle. If the engine stayed dead, the four rescuers could not reliably position their hull or keep it moving into the seas.

Webber had another problem. In darkness with the compass gone, the wreck was difficult to locate. The sea itself made noise, and nothing in that noise gave them a neat direction to follow.

Then he heard something different: a deep crashing and banging.

The sound was coming from heavy steel moving among the waves. Through the snow, the boat's searchlight finally caught twisted metal. Webber had found Pendleton.

At first he approached a dark side of the broken tanker, where he could not see the crew. Working around the wreck, he found lights and men near the rail on the other side.

For those sailors, the small light of CG-36500 had arrived at the bottom of a towering wall. The boat was real. The escape route still looked nearly impossible.

They lowered a rope ladder over the side.

Imagine a ladder hanging from the stern of a ship while the whole stern rises and drops with the seas. Now put a small rescue boat beneath it, rising and dropping at different moments. A man moving down the ladder might find the boat suddenly gone. Or the boat could surge upward into him.

Webber could not hold the lifeboat motionless. Instead, he worked the wheel and throttle constantly, bringing the bow alongside for a single pickup, then steering clear before the next wave drove the craft into the tanker.

The others hauled people from the water or pulled them into the lifeboat when the two vessels came close enough. Sometimes one person required more than one approach. Sometimes a sailor dropped from the ladder into the freezing sea and had to be lifted out.

For the men waiting on deck, the order of the rescue meant something else. Every time the little boat backed away, someone remained above on a section of ship that was still being struck by waves. Every time it returned, there was less room below.

One man, George “Tiny” Myers, helped others get off the tanker. Thirty-two men made it onto CG-36500. Myers did not. He was fatally struck during the dangerous transfer and lost in the water.

That is the part of the story that cannot be changed into a perfect rescue. Thirty-three men were on Pendleton's stern. Thirty-two survived the evacuation. One died. And the eight men who had been in the bow section did not survive either.

The lifeboat was now crowded with people. Webber and his crew had started the mission as four men. After the transfers, there were thirty-six people aboard.

The big tanker was behind them. But being away from the wreck did not mean being safe.

Their compass was still missing. The windshield was broken. Snow and spray had erased ordinary landmarks. The boat's motor, which Fitzgerald had already struggled to keep alive, had to carry a much heavier load through the same sea.

They could not simply call this a success and wait. There was no harbour underneath the boat, and remaining out there meant exposing every rescued sailor to the continuing storm.

Webber took the lifeboat toward where he believed Chatham lay. The Coast Guardsmen searched for anything useful: a patch of land, a shore light, a buoy.

The return voyage brought them to the same shallow water where the windshield and compass had been lost. The Chatham Bar had not become gentle just because the lifeboat was full.

Then a small red light came into view.

It was the navigation buoy they had passed on the way out, near the entrance to the harbour. That one light supplied what the compass no longer could: a recognizable position and a route through the dangerous bar.

The boat made it back.

Elsewhere in the same storm, Coast Guard teams rescued thirty-eight men from Fort Mercer. Counting those and the thirty-two saved from Pendleton's stern, seventy sailors were brought to safety from the two tankers. That total did not erase the people lost.

Webber, Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske later received the Gold Lifesaving Medal. The lifeboat CG-36500 became a symbol of the operation, not because it was large or technologically extraordinary, but because its crew kept solving the next obstacle with the equipment they still had.

Finding Pendleton without a compass was an achievement. Taking thirty-two people off a moving wreck was another. But the final result came from something much smaller than the tanker or even the lifeboat.

In the dark, on the way back, they recognized one red harbour light. Without that last part of the journey, the thirty-two men taken from Pendleton would still have been stranded at sea.
