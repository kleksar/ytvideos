My coffee is too hot. Should I add the cold milk now, or wait until I'm ready to drink?

I'm comparing two imaginary mugs, not claiming I measured a real one. Both start with the same coffee at ninety degrees Celsius. Both will contain fifteen percent milk, straight from a five-degree fridge. The room is at twenty degrees, and I have ten minutes.

Pick A: pour the milk in now. Or B: keep it cold and add it at the end.

A looks obvious if I want cooler coffee. The milk drops its starting temperature to seventy-seven point two five degrees. B stays at ninety. For a while, B is definitely hotter.

Here's the catch. The hotter a drink is compared with the room, the faster it can lose heat. In this simplified model, the temperature gap shrinks at the same fixed rate for either mug.

After ten minutes, A reaches about fifty-four point seven degrees.

B's black coffee has fallen to about sixty-two point five. Only now do I pour in the milk. Its final temperature: fifty-three point eight.

B is cooler, but by less than one degree. The model doesn't promise that difference in my kitchen. Real cups have different heat loss, evaporation and changing volumes.

And if I wanted to KEEP the coffee hot during that wait? I'd choose A. Same two methods, opposite goal.

My answer isn't “always add milk late.” It's “decide whether I'm trying to cool the drink or preserve its warmth.”
