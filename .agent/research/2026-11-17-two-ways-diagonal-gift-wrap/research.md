# Research — straight versus diagonal wrapping (Two Ways Lab)
Observed 2026-11-17. Audience: English-language viewers facing a relatable gift-wrapping problem. Channel status: active **Two Ways Lab** as of remote main `fe4460045cae21f192b6d539ac9bfe36b536fd6c`. ## Screening: ten separate everyday scenarios
These are research candidates, **not ten validated experiments**.

1. Straight vs diagonal gift wrapping on the same inadequate sheet — **finalist**: clear initial state and a real unexpected completion; main weakness is the trick has been widely publicized since 2017.
2. Individual versus grouped weighing of parcels to locate one underweight gift — **finalist**: multiple measured operations and elegant information-theory payoff; weakness is it easily becomes a contrived coin puzzle rather than a recognisable everyday task, and 10m risks invented procedural drama.
3. Print jobs in request order vs shortest first at a home printer — **finalist**: legitimate reversal of average waiting time and fairness; drawback is appliance times and reactions are unobserved, viewer stakes are more abstract, and group discussions might overpromise a universal winner.
4. Sort mixed socks once vs search one pair at a time — promising across repeated days, but user demand distribution and number of times each sock is handled would be authored assumptions.
5. Move cleaning supplies with me vs group tasks by tool — could give a measurable count of trips; likely dominated by apartment layout and invented timings.
6. Cut a roll of ribbon longest-first vs shortest-first — reproducible cutting stock model, but a less familiar A/B question and may resolve in one step.
7. Grocery-store closest-next versus aisle-by-aisle route — location/obstacle complexity may dominate the clear metric and require unsupported real layout claims.
8. One batch of sandwich ingredients vs finish one sandwich at a time — a useful model, but four-sandwich throughput is already clear after a simple operation count, making 10m difficult.
9. Same suitcase: rolling clothes vs folding — heavily saturated and physical packing effectiveness cannot be confidently settled by an illustration without actual materials.
10. Put the heaviest item first vs the largest item first in a suitcase — packing is not determined without dimensions, fragility, and objective; winner easy to manipulate.

Scope: 10 distinct household challenges considered within this run; only three advanced to detailed competitor/source comparison. No made-up channel analytics, survey, or quoted comment thread.

## Three competing pitches before scripting

| Topic | Exact opening / first visible action | Why stop; development after hook; full payoff | Strongest objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diagonal gift wrap** | **“Will turning this box forty-five degrees make the wrapping paper fit?”** / a flat mock-book beside one small sheet, then A/B same conditions | One last sheet, conventional folds fail; diagonal folds succeed in published demo. Later a second explicitly changed model gives only 1.18% calculated saving, then a 12-inch roll makes straight cut usable. Viewer learns which objective to measure. | Large historic viral coverage; an illustrated recap alone would be redundant; hand-measured evidence for our own footage is absent |
| Group weighing of a missing parcel | “I have eight sealed parcels, and one is missing a part. How few times must I use the scale?” / identical parcels and balanced weighing | Group comparisons shrink suspect set, final exact culprit; compare naive vs grouped count. | Risks a mathematical riddle disguised as real delivery task; assumptions about precisely one missing item are not observed |
| Home printer queue | “Will printing the shortest file first make everyone finish earlier?” / physical four-job queue | Reorder same job durations; average wait improves but a long job may finish much later; ethical/fairness payoff. | Complicated numbers without memorable physical visual; no compelling observed audience signals |

**Strongest alternative:** group weighing has a crisper Short surprise and cheap illustration, but its single reveal is largely exhausted in <2 minutes; extending it to 10 would introduce variants of the puzzle rather than the same household story. Gift wrap has three genuinely different measures (fit, area, availability from a width-limited roll), each with sourced numerical inputs. The winner still risks dullness after the initial reveal; 10m is specifically REWORK.

## Evidence for each actual claim

1. **Published physical demonstration, NOT this channel's test:** Caroline Delbert, “For Perfectly Wrapped Gifts Every Time, Geometry Says Use the Diagonal Hack—Here’s How,” *Popular Mechanics*, **2025-12-09**. URL: https://www.popularmechanics.com/science/math/a69676779/gift-wrapping-trick-geometry-tip/ (accessed 2026-11-17; paragraphs about her 4×5 flat book / 7×9 paper, drawing, and diagonal). Delbert describes a flat `4×5 in` mock book and a `7×9 in` paper. Her diagram and report support the straight shortfall and diagonal success under that demonstration's conditions, not that any 4×5 object of unknown thickness works or that *we* physically wrapped it. Its sheet is `7×9=63 in²`. **Limitation:** thickness unspecified and no rigorous independently repeated test of identical physical objects by this production.
2. **Separate classroom numerical model, NOT same box:** Southern Regional Education Board, *Ready for High School: Math*, Geometry Lesson 1, **solution to 8×6×3-inch box**, printed exercise pp. 10–11, PDF text around lines 20450–20464, URL: https://www.sreb.org/sites/main/files/ready_for_high_school_math_teacher_manual.pdf (accessed 2026-11-17). The stated traditional cut: `11.5×18.5=212.75 in²`. Stated diagonal square: `14.5×14.5=210.25 in²`. Author arithmetic: `212.75-210.25=2.5 in²`; `2.5/212.75 ×100 = 1.175088...%`. This is the **exercise's prescribed allowances**, not independently measured minimum for all methods or necessarily a reproducible physical wrap with every paper type. **Source caveat:** another unrelated `6×6×4` worked sample in the same PDF incorrectly multiplies `14.5×14.5` as `220.25`; that sample is *not used* here. Browser's PDF screenshot endpoint failed due file size (HTTP 400), while the relevant text lines were readable; physical printed page not independently visually inspected. No claims depend on the erroneous sample.
3. **Twelve-inch-wide roll case is explicitly hypothetical:** from source #2, cut A requires width `11.5 in`, B requires `14.5 in`. A continuous roll `12 in` wide therefore permits the former but not the latter cut as a single intact piece. The roll width is an **authored example condition**, not a reported experiment; length assumed sufficient and no reorientation/splicing/new bespoke sheet allowed for that comparison.
4. **Ideal cube geometry, not practical tape result:** Cambridge NRICH, *Wrapping Gifts*, “Student Solutions,” https://nrich.maths.org/problems/wrapping-gifts (accessed 2026-11-17), proves smallest **square** for a cube edge `a` has side `2√2 a`; square area `8a²` compared to surface area `6a²`. The model assumes the specific no-cut square folding geometry, with edges just meeting. It does not guarantee an easy physical fold or adequate tape allowance.
5. **Applicability caveats:** material thickness, creasing/tape, aesthetics, relative paper roll availability, skill, time, and failure rate remain UNVERIFIED. The three script versions do not assert a measured time/success rate, universal angle benefit, or new real wrapping trial.

## Selection risks and remedies
- Risk: “same sheet fits if turned” is old widely copied trick. **Remedy:** new contribution is careful decomposition of claims, then unexpected 1.18% and width reversal; not claiming a newly invented hack.
- Risk: the result for a different box sounds like changing rules. **Remedy:** scripts explicitly disclose that case and metric change before each result; do not present it as a second trial on the first box.
- Risk: 10m becomes a geometry lecture. **Remedy:** publishable 05m is editorially stronger; 10m classed REWORK rather than stretched into a false high score.
- Risk: a 2m Short is above channel's normal 35–50s length. **Remedy:** leave format compatibility UNVERIFIED and require official start/spec confirmation, not a fictitious Long conversion.
- Risk: source allowance choices or imperfect author arithmetic. **Remedy:** show every input and recompute the arithmetic; source #2's unrelated typo flagged, not propagated.
