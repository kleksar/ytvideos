Will turning this box forty-five degrees make the wrapping paper fit?

I have a flat, book-shaped gift and one small sheet left. The sheet looks short when I put the gift straight on it. I can either wrap it with the box lined up against the paper’s edges, or turn the box diagonally.

My rule is simple: same box, same sheet. A wins only if it covers every face. B has to do the same. Extra patches don't count.

This is an illustrated comparison, not footage of an experiment I performed. The starting example comes from a published demonstration: a flat four-by-five-inch mock book and a seven-by-nine-inch sheet.

With A, the usual straight folds run out of reach. The corners leave an uncovered patch. I could cut another piece, but that would change the challenge.

For B, I rotate the box and bring the corners of the paper over it in sequence. The published demonstration reports a complete wrap using that same sheet. Turning the box didn't produce any new paper. It changed where the remaining paper could reach.

So B wins this particular fit test.

But I shouldn't turn that into “diagonal always saves more.” On a thicker box, I may need extra paper to reach around the sides. And a sheet that uses less area can still be too wide for the roll I own.

The useful test is not whether the paper looks larger when it's turned. I need to check whether the folds actually meet, with enough overlap to secure them.

Here, the diagonal layout rescues one sheet. It isn't proof of a universal shortcut. I still have another gift to wrap, and before I cut another sheet, there's one thing I need to know.
