My dishwasher can spend two hours washing dishes I could clean myself much faster. Does all that spraying really use less water? I'll compare two methods, but first I have to stop confusing time with consumption.

I've cleared up after a family gathering. The counter holds fourteen place settings: the same dirty plates, cups, bowls and cutlery on both sides of my comparison. A is washing by hand with a bowl in the sink. B is scraping off the larger scraps, filling a modern dishwasher and running eco. I announce the score before choosing: litres of water for the whole pile. Not electricity, labour or time.

I'd instinctively choose A. I can see exactly how big my bowl is. I cannot see through the dishwasher door, and eco mode seems to run forever.

But the machine is not a tap left open for two hours. It pumps and recirculates water, using it repeatedly. When I drain the dirty bowl, I replace its water if more dishes remain. That's the first clue.

Now I use figures I can check instead of claiming I ran a new kitchen experiment. In September 2026, Which? published a comparison for fourteen place settings. Its handwashing calculation assumes a nine-litre bowl can wash around two settings before the water needs changing. Fourteen divided by two is seven bowls, or sixty-three litres. That's an estimate for a specified method, not a measurement in my kitchen.

For the machine, Which? used results from thirty-three fairly recent dishwashers. The average main program took nearly thirteen litres per fourteen settings. Eco used just under ten litres. Those figures describe tested models and conditions, not my personal machine.

The result is hard to ignore. A: sixty-three litres. B: about ten. For this full load, B saves about fifty-three litres. The time the pump runs simply isn't the number of times fresh water gets poured away.

Then I spot a way to sabotage B. Suppose I rinse every dish under the tap before loading it because I don't trust the machine. The no-pre-rinse cycle figure doesn't include that water. With an illustrative tap flow of six litres per minute and three minutes of rinsing, I've added eighteen litres. B now uses about twenty-eight instead of ten. It still beats sixty-three in this particular model, but I've wasted a big chunk of the advantage. Modern dishwasher guidance generally says to scrape, not pre-rinse. The six litres per minute is an assumption, not a measured tap.

What if I have one dirty mug rather than fourteen place settings? Then this comparison cannot tell me the winner. A careful handwash may take very little water, and some machines adapt to the load. I would need the actual values for the new task. Same starting conditions always matter more than a slogan.

And water isn't the whole environmental story. A different study, from the University of Michigan in 2020, compared life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions under different washing habits. Typical machine washing created substantially less than typical handwashing in its models. But when manual washing meant using one basin of soapy water and another for rinsing, that careful method produced around eighteen percent fewer emissions than the study's recommended machine method.

That's not the opposite result from one experiment. The British comparison asked about litres for a large load. The American study asked about long-term emissions, with its own energy and behaviour assumptions. I can't combine them into one made-up test.

So here is my actual decision. For the big pile, measuring water and using the published conditions, B wins. I should skip the preliminary tap rinse. If I'm asking about climate impact, I need to consider how water is heated and electricity generated, and how carefully I wash by hand. If the pile is tiny, I need a new comparison.

I was watching a two-hour timer when the useful thing to watch was the water.
