A dishwasher can run for hours while I could wash a few plates in minutes. I used to think that made the machine wasteful. But the longer I looked at the water figures, the less the clock seemed to matter. Then I found another comparison in which the apparent winner changed. I want to find out how both conclusions can be true.

After a family gathering, my kitchen counter is full of dirty dishes. I imagine two methods for that identical pile. A is washing by hand using a bowl in the sink. B is scraping food scraps into the bin, loading a modern dishwasher and running its eco program. I haven't carried out a physical experiment for this video. I'm building a comparison from published tests and openly stated assumptions.

I choose my first scoring rule before the numbers appear: total litres of water used to wash the same load. I'm not awarding bonus points for finishing sooner or avoiding a chore. Those are important, but changing the metric halfway through would make the contest meaningless.

My intuition still favours A. I can pick up the washing-up bowl. It doesn't look capable of holding much water. Behind the closed dishwasher door, jets seem to spray nonstop, and eco cycles can last an astonishingly long time. Yet noise and elapsed time tell me almost nothing about how many litres the machine takes in.

I'll use fourteen place settings because that's a defined large workload, like the dishes from a group meal. A place setting isn't just one plate. It's a collection of dishes, glasses and cutlery. If I quietly reduced one side to two cups, I would not be comparing the same work.

For the handwashing side, I can use a September 2026 analysis by the British consumer group Which?. It assumed a nine-litre bowl serves about two place settings before the water must be replaced. The arithmetic is straightforward: fourteen settings need seven bowls, or sixty-three litres. This isn't a claim that Which? watched me wash fourteen sets. It's a model of a particular handwashing routine, and other routines may use less or more.

For the dishwasher side, Which? reported tests of thirty-three relatively recent machines. Its average water consumption for fourteen place settings was 12.96 litres on a main program and 9.74 litres in eco. Different machines and cycles vary. These are published test averages, not a reading of a meter attached to my own dishwasher.

Now I have a meaningful comparison. Under these stated conditions, A needs sixty-three litres. B in eco needs 9.74 litres. The difference is 53.26 litres, or roughly eighty-four percent of A's consumption. The machine has used a little more than one of those nine-litre bowls to do what handwashing was assumed to need seven bowls for.

This doesn't mean the British report performed my exact A/B experiment. It combined a handwashing estimate with machine test data. The shared workload makes the comparison informative, but the evidence types remain different. If I want to be precise, I must say that out loud.

Why isn't the long cycle wasteful? Because the machine behaves more like a fountain than like an open faucet. Water is pumped across dishes, collected and circulated again, with drains and refills at stages of the program. Spraying for a long time does not require new tap water for every second of spraying. At my sink, once I pour the dirty bowl away, the next bowlful is new water.

An independent US yardstick helps me check plausibility. The ENERGY STAR certification criteria effective from July 2023 require standard-size certified dishwashers to use no more than 3.2 US gallons in the relevant test cycle, roughly 12.1 litres. It's a certification limit, not every machine's actual use. And it's not part of the British test sample. Still, it supports the idea that a few gallons per machine cycle is realistic.

But there's a hidden habit in the kitchen that can erase savings. Before using B, I might rinse the dirty dishes under the running tap. If the dishwasher estimate assumes no pre-rinse, I can't pretend that extra water has disappeared. With a hypothetical flow of six litres a minute and three minutes of pre-rinsing, I add eighteen litres. My model for B rises from 9.74 to 27.74 litres. It still beats sixty-three here, but the advantage shrinks. No actual tap was measured for these figures. They show why I need to count the whole process, not just the appliance's cycle.

ENERGY STAR advises scraping food instead of pre-rinsing because modern machines and detergents can handle the remaining soil. That advice doesn't guarantee every machine will perfectly clean every dish. If a dirty load needs a second full cycle, I must count that cycle too. A/B only makes sense if the methods achieve a comparable end condition: clean dishes.

There is another way to make B look better without earning it: compare a fully loaded dishwasher with inefficient handwashing, then forget that I usually run the dishwasher half empty. The load matters. For one mug and a spoon, my water requirements and the machine's response could look different. Some machines adjust their programs; others use nearly the same water regardless of load. Without data for the exact appliance and the water my careful handwash would require, I can't announce a universal winner for one mug. The full-load result isn't transferable by wishful thinking.

At this point B wins for the metric I chose, a full load of dishes and the conditions we stated. But I also care about environmental impact. Water volume is only part of that. Heating water takes energy. Running a pump takes electricity. Manufacturing and replacing a machine have impacts. Different households obtain hot water and electricity in different ways.

A University of Michigan life-cycle study published in 2020 examined that wider question. The researchers modelled four dish loads per week, with eight place settings per load, over ten years. Under their typical-use assumptions, manual washing accounted for about 5,620 kilograms of carbon-dioxide-equivalent greenhouse gases. Machine washing accounted for about 2,090. These are modelled long-term scenarios, not something I measured at home and not numbers that belong to the British fourteen-setting water comparison.

Under typical habits, then, the machine again comes out ahead. But the researchers also explored better habits, including manual washing with two basins. One basin is for washing with warm soapy water, the other for rinsing. Under that study's recommended-use assumptions, this careful manual routine produced about eighteen percent fewer life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions than the recommended machine method.

Now A can win on a different metric. That does not reverse the British water result. It changes the question, the country, the workload and the assumptions. It's also not evidence that any random handwashing session beats any dishwasher. It tells me technique matters and that a water-saving winner need not lead on every environmental score.

The distinction between a running tap and two basins is crucial. In the Michigan research, leaving the tap running was the worst of the studied options for water and energy. Switching to the two-basin method substantially reduced the manual method's emissions compared with the running-tap habit. If I just say "wash by hand," I'm hiding the action that may determine the outcome.

Energy sources matter too. The study considered different ways to heat water and differences in the electricity mix. A gas water heater and an electric heater don't have identical impacts under every condition. The same dishwasher in two places need not have the same carbon footprint. I cannot take an eighteen-percent finding from one life-cycle model and promise it for every household.

And when somebody asks which is cheaper, I need another scoring rule again. If I already own a machine, I'm comparing the water, electricity and supplies for tonight's dishes. If I'm thinking about buying one, the price, repair costs, useful life and even the value of my time become relevant. The Michigan work examined life-cycle costs, too. Emissions and the household bill are not interchangeable.

I can now see why one overconfident sentence—"machines always win" or "washing by hand always wins"—is so tempting and so unreliable. It compresses four different questions into one. My better method is to name the workload and metric, use the same starting pile, record exactly how each method is performed, include extra rinses or repeated cycles, and keep model results separate from actual measurements.

For a large fourteen-place-setting load, under the published UK comparison and with litres as the score, I choose B, the dishwasher in eco mode without a tap pre-rinse. For long-term greenhouse gases, the US study shows that a carefully managed two-basin handwash can beat recommended machine use within its model. For one cup, or another machine, I would need to do the calculation again.

I haven't secretly tested a dishwasher and a sink. I've used reported machine tests, a documented handwashing estimate and a separate life-cycle study. Their limits are part of the conclusion.

The reason my two-hour dishwasher looked wasteful was simple: I was timing the spraying, while the useful number was fresh water. And before I choose a winner again, I'll decide what I actually want to save.
