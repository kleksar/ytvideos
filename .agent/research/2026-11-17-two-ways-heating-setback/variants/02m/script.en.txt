I'm leaving home for eight hours. Do I keep the heat at twenty degrees all day, or let the room cool and warm it up before I return? Pick A or B.

I haven't tested an actual house. Here's a transparent mathematical model. Outside stays at five degrees. The same room starts at twenty and must be back at twenty when I return. No sunshine, cooking or open windows change the comparison.

With A, my thermostat holds twenty degrees for eight hours. The heating has to replace all the warmth leaking outside. In this model that's fourteen point four kilowatt-hours of delivered heat.

With B, I supply no heat for six hours. The room gradually falls to about sixteen point eight degrees. Then a sufficiently powerful heater runs for the last two hours. By the time I arrive, the room is back at twenty. That takes about twelve point eight kilowatt-hours.

So B wins by roughly one point six kilowatt-hours of heat. Here's why: a cooler room loses warmth more slowly. Reheating isn't a magical extra bill. A was paying to replace those losses the entire time.

But I can't call that a guaranteed saving on electricity or gas. An actual heat pump might become less efficient during fast recovery. Some homes can't reheat in two hours, and cooling can be unsuitable for people, pets, pipes or damp rooms. This is a comparison of useful heat, not measured household bills.

I would check my equipment's instructions and compare real meter readings before changing my schedule. In this model, B wins; in my actual home, the heating system could change the answer.
