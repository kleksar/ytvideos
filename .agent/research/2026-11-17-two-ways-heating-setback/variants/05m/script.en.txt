I'm putting on my coat to leave for eight hours. The thermostat is set to twenty degrees. Should I keep that temperature all day, or let the room cool and warm it before I come back? My first instinct is that reheating a cold house must cost more. Is that true? Choose A or B.

Before I compare anything, I need fair conditions. I am not recording a real experiment. This is a mathematical model with one room, five degrees outside, and the same starting and finishing temperature: twenty degrees. The building doesn't change between trials. I also ignore sunshine, cooking and open windows. I compare useful heat delivered to the room, not a household energy bill.

I give this hypothetical room two properties. It loses one hundred and twenty watts for every degree between indoors and outdoors. And it takes three kilowatt-hours to change the temperature of the room and everything represented inside by one degree. Those are invented inputs, not claims about my apartment.

First, method A. The indoor-outdoor difference is always fifteen degrees. Multiply that by the heat-loss rate and the room loses one point eight kilowatts. A thermostat constantly calls for replacement heat. Over eight hours the heater delivers fourteen point four kilowatt-hours. I come home to twenty degrees.

Now method B. For the first six hours, I add no heat. The room doesn't immediately turn cold. Its stored warmth leaks away gradually, and by the sixth hour it's about sixteen point eight degrees. During that cooling, the temperature difference with outdoors shrinks. Less heat escapes each minute.

That leaves two hours before I arrive. I need a heater powerful enough to return the room to exactly twenty degrees in those two hours. My model requires about six point four kilowatts of heat during recovery. Over those two hours it delivers about twelve point eight kilowatt-hours. I reach the front door at the same temperature as in A.

Here are the results. A uses fourteen point four kilowatt-hours of delivered heat. B uses twelve point eight. B saves about one point six kilowatt-hours, almost eleven percent during this one modeled eight-hour absence. It is not a claim of eleven percent off anyone's annual heating bill.

Why doesn't the final warm-up erase the saving? Because A spent the entire absence replacing heat losses from a warm room. B allowed the room to cool, so it lost less heat. The warm-up restores that smaller loss. Both methods end with the same stored warmth.

Let's change the building. If I halve its heat leakage while keeping the same thermal capacity, the room barely cools during the six-hour pause. The modeled saving drops to about four tenths of a kilowatt-hour. Better insulation is still good; it simply gives a thermostat setback less unused heat to save.

But there is a second twist. I have counted heat delivered, not electricity purchased. Imagine A's heat pump delivers three units of heat for each unit of electricity. Then fourteen point four kilowatt-hours of heat cost four point eight of electricity. If B maintains the same efficiency, it wins. But if rapid recovery drops its efficiency to two, its twelve point eight kilowatt-hours of heat cost roughly six point four of electricity. Now A wins the electricity comparison. Those efficiency figures are hypothetical, not measurements of a particular heat pump.

And not every home can make the recovery in two hours. Slow underfloor heating might require an earlier start. Cooling below eighteen degrees could also be unsuitable for vulnerable occupants, pets or damp-prone buildings. Comfort and safety aren't problems a spreadsheet can decide.

So my result is conditional but useful. For a heater with unchanged efficiency, the timed setback B needs less delivered heat. For a real home, I would check the heating equipment, compare similar-weather meter readings, and make sure the room is actually comfortable when I get back. The answer isn't hidden in the word 'reheating'. It's in the heat escaping while I'm away.

Tomorrow, when I pick up my keys, I will check my equipment and choose the schedule that can actually deliver the warmth I need.
