One mug of hot water. That's all I want this morning. My electric kettle has a minimum-fill line at half a litre. The microwave can heat just the mug. Which one uses less electricity?

I set up a calculation, not a kitchen experiment. Same 250-millilitre drink, same starting temperature, and the same temperature rise. My score is electricity from the wall, not which machine looks faster.

A is the kettle. B is the microwave. Pick one before the numbers appear.

Heating the water in my mug from 20 to 100 degrees Celsius needs about 23 watt-hours of heat. That's the physics part. Now come the model assumptions: 80 percent of a kettle's electricity reaches the water; for the microwave, 60 percent does. Those are example efficiencies, not measurements of my appliances.

B needs about 39 watt-hours to heat the mug. A looks more efficient. But its half-litre minimum means I have to heat twice as much water. A needs about 58 watt-hours. B wins this round by about 19.

Now I change one thing: the kettle. A different model can safely heat just 250 millilitres. With the same assumed efficiency, that kettle needs only 29 watt-hours. Suddenly A wins.

The lesson isn't that microwaves beat kettles. It's that heating extra water can wipe out an efficiency advantage. And I wouldn't choose a microwave on these numbers alone: overheating plain water can cause a dangerous eruption. I follow the appliance's safety instructions.

My model gives two different winners because the minimum fill changes. Before I make another claim about the best appliance, I'll check how much water I actually need.
