Why would a microwave use less electricity than a kettle if the kettle is designed to boil water? And why could the answer change when I make a second cup?

I walk into my kitchen wanting one 250-millilitre mug of hot water. The kettle says I must fill it to at least 500 millilitres. The microwave can hold just the mug. That tiny line on the kettle is going to decide the first round.

This is a transparent calculation, not an experiment I secretly filmed. Both methods start with water at 20 degrees Celsius. Both aim for the same 80-degree rise. My metric is electrical energy taken from the wall. I am not scoring taste, speed, or which appliance I'd recommend using.

Method A is the kettle. Method B is the microwave. Which would you pick?

First I calculate the heat that the water actually needs. One litre of water needs about 93 watt-hours to rise by 80 degrees. My mug holds one quarter of that, so the water itself needs about 23 watt-hours. That demand doesn't care which appliance does the heating.

But the appliances don't deliver every watt-hour from the socket into the water. For this model I assign the kettle 80-percent efficiency and the microwave 60-percent efficiency. These are illustrative assumptions. They are not readings from my kitchen, and the answer could change with different machines.

For B, I heat only my 250-millilitre mug. Twenty-three divided by 0.60 gives roughly 39 watt-hours from the wall. Now for A. If I could put just one mug in the kettle, its higher efficiency would give me about 29 watt-hours. That would be an easy A victory.

Except I can't ignore the minimum line. In this first scenario, the kettle needs half a litre for safe operation. Twice as much water means roughly twice the heat. At the assumed 80-percent efficiency, A now needs about 58 watt-hours.

So the less efficient appliance wins: 39 versus 58. B saves about 19 watt-hours in this particular model. It's not because the microwave suddenly got better at heating water. The kettle did more work than I needed.

Now I open the second round. I switch to a kettle whose instructions allow a 250-millilitre fill. Same mug. Same temperatures. Same efficiencies. Only the minimum-fill restriction changes. A now takes about 29 watt-hours, while B still takes 39. The winner flips back to A.

There is even a useful boundary. With these assumptions, the kettle breaks even when it heats roughly 333 millilitres. Below that, A has the energy advantage; above it, B does. That's not a universal magic number. It comes from the 80- and 60-percent efficiencies I chose. Change those, and the threshold changes too.

What if I'm making two mugs? Now I actually need 500 millilitres, so the first kettle wastes none of its required fill. A takes about 58 watt-hours. B, heating the same half-litre under its assumed efficiency, needs around 78. The first kettle wins again.

That tells me something more useful than a slogan about appliances. The result is really about matching the amount heated to the amount used. Research on electric kettles identifies overfilling as a significant source of avoidable consumption, and minimum-fill requirements genuinely vary by model.

There's a second distinction I won't hide. Wattage is the rate at which a machine draws power; watt-hours describe the energy used across the task. A high-power kettle can finish sooner without automatically using more electricity. And the cost of the nineteen-watt-hour difference in my first round is small: at an illustrative electricity price of 25 cents per kilowatt-hour, it's less than half a cent.

Finally, a low electricity result isn't a safety recommendation. The FDA warns that plain water overheated in a microwave can erupt when disturbed. I follow the actual appliance instructions and won't treat this simulation as a reason to risk a scald.

My answer: for one mug and this half-litre kettle, B wins on electricity in the model. For one mug and a low-minimum kettle, A wins. For two mugs, A wins again under the same assumptions. No real meter readings, no universal winner.

The first thing I check isn't the big wattage number on the label. It's the small minimum-fill line, and how much water I'm really going to use.
