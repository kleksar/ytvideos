I'm one hundred metres from a dry doorway. The rain has started, I have no umbrella, and I have two choices: A, walk, or B, run. Which way gets less rain onto my clothes? Choose before I calculate.

I can't honestly claim I tested this in a storm. I'm comparing two routes through the same simplified rain model: the same one hundred metres, the same person, the same rain, and no wind. I count water intercepted, not how quickly I arrive.

Walking at one and a half metres per second takes about sixty-seven seconds. Running at four takes twenty-five. But running means hitting more drops from the front every second. That's the reason this question isn't quite as obvious as it looks.

Here's the catch. Along the same distance, my simplified body passes through the same amount of rainy air with its front, regardless of speed. What changes is the time the rain has to land on top.

With one fixed set of body dimensions and falling-rain assumptions, the model gives A a score of roughly 147, and B 105. Those are relative exposure units, not litres and not a measurement of me. B wins: running intercepts about twenty-eight percent less rain in this calm-air example.

So should I always run? No. A 2026 numerical study of moving human bodies found a narrow exception: with a suitable wind from behind and almost no crosswind, walking can sometimes intercept slightly less rain. Most conditions still favour running.

My answer for this calm, open path is B. The exception is real in a model, not a rule to stroll through every storm. And the next shower leaves me facing the same choice again.
