I need to clean three rooms. I can complete one room and move on, or I can do all the dusting at once, all the vacuuming at once, and so on. Both sound sensible. But one gets the whole place ready nearly four minutes earlier in my model. Can you guess which?

Before I choose, I need a fair starting point. These are not readings from my apartment. I'm comparing two routes through a made-up, fully specified set of rooms. All five cleaning jobs take exactly the same amount of time under both routes. The tools ride on one cart, so neither method gets secret help from a second person or a conveniently placed vacuum.

I begin at the first of three doorways along a straight hall. Eight metres separate one doorway from the next. Each room needs the same five jobs in the same sensible order: clear loose things, dust higher surfaces, wipe lower surfaces, vacuum the rug, and mop the hard floor. In this example, each job takes one minute. No matter how I travel, that's fifteen minutes of cleaning. The only differences I'm allowed to count are walking and switching tasks.

My first option, A, is the satisfying one. I enter room one and do everything. The clutter goes away, the dust is cleared, the rug is vacuumed and the floor is mopped. I leave a finished room behind. Then I take the cart to room two and repeat. Room three comes last.

The walk is tiny: eight metres, then eight more. That's sixteen seconds at the model's walking pace of one metre per second. But I keep stopping to put down one tool and get the next ready. Four changes inside the first room, another change before starting the second, four more inside it, another between rooms, and four in the last room. Fourteen switches altogether.

For this first trial I assign thirty seconds to each switch. That isn't a measured household average. It's a deliberately visible model input for putting a tool away and preparing the next one. Fourteen times thirty seconds makes seven minutes. Add the fifteen minutes of work and sixteen seconds of walking: A takes twenty-two minutes sixteen seconds.

Now I reset all three rooms. Same mess, same five jobs, same cart. Method B does the entire first job across rooms one, two and three. Then I switch tools once and come back through the rooms for dusting. I reverse direction again for wiping, then vacuuming, then mopping. I still do every job in every room. I just group identical jobs together.

This time the hall gets a workout. Five trips from one end to the other add up to eighty metres. At one metre per second, that's eighty seconds, not sixteen. But I change what I'm doing only four times, between the five groups of jobs. At thirty seconds per change, that costs two minutes, not seven.

The arithmetic gives me a surprise: B finishes in eighteen minutes twenty seconds. A needs twenty-two sixteen. B walks five times as far along that hall and still wins by three minutes fifty-six seconds.

Why? In A, the short route saved sixty-four seconds of walking. But it cost ten extra switches, or five whole minutes. The switches outweighed the steps. Nothing got cleaned faster; I simply spent less of the day putting tools down and picking them up.

It sounds like B is the obvious winner. Until I change the question without changing the rooms.

Suppose somebody is arriving in eight minutes. I don't need the entire apartment finished. I only need the first room ready. Under A, I finish that first room in seven minutes: five minutes of cleaning plus four thirty-second changes. I can close the door on a completed room.

Under B, I visit that first room at the beginning of every sweep, but I don't mop it until the final sweep. It becomes fully clean at sixteen minutes four seconds. B wins the total-time race, yet A gives me one usable room more than nine minutes earlier. The fastest plan for the whole place isn't necessarily the fastest plan for the first result.

Now let me turn the other dial: the time it takes to switch tools. If everything is already in my hands and a switch costs only five seconds, A finishes in sixteen minutes twenty-six seconds. B takes sixteen forty. The method with fewer footsteps finally wins, by fourteen seconds.

There is a precise reason the result flips. B walks sixty-four extra metres. It also avoids ten switches. Those balance when a switch costs six point four seconds. Longer than that, grouping tasks wins this particular full-house model. Shorter than that, finishing rooms wins.

I wouldn't claim that six point four seconds is a rule for real houses. A hallway could be longer. A tool might have a cord. A wet floor might force a detour. Cleaning quality could change if I rushed. Those belong in a real test, and I haven't done one.

What I can use is the decision. If I need every room done and changing tools is slow, I batch the same job. If I need a single room now, or switching takes no time, I work room by room. I check the goal before I choose the route. That's the difference the two methods actually reveal.
