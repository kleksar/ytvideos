How should I clean three messy rooms: finish one room from start to finish, or do the same job across the whole apartment? Which route saves time? And what happens if a guest needs just one room before everything is done?

I like the feeling of shutting the door on a finished room. I can see progress immediately. But when I put down the cloth, unfold a vacuum hose, put that away and pick up a mop, I can spend a surprising amount of time not actually cleaning. Batching feels efficient for the opposite reason: one tool stays in my hand while I move between rooms. It also means I keep walking past unfinished rooms. Those are two genuinely different advantages, and I need to count both.

I am not secretly showing footage from an experiment I ran at home. Everything you are about to hear is a specified model. I can calculate the route and change its assumptions in public. That makes the result reproducible, but it doesn't make the numbers measured facts about your home.

Imagine three rooms along a straight corridor. The first door is my starting point; the next is eight metres away; the third is another eight metres along. The tools are on one mobile cart. I can carry the same cart with me under either plan. There is no helper, no second vacuum, no magical supply cupboard appearing in the right room. I can reverse direction at the far end, and each method finishes after all three rooms have received the same work.

I give every room five separate jobs. First I pick up loose clutter. Then I dust the higher shelves. Then I wipe lower surfaces. Next I vacuum the rug. Finally I mop the hard floor. That order matters: if I mop before dusting and vacuuming, I may create another job for myself. Both strategies keep exactly that sequence inside every room. The plan is a comparison of scheduling, not a license to skip work.

For simple arithmetic, each of the five jobs takes sixty seconds in every room. The model doesn't claim a real sofa can be cleaned in sixty seconds. These are fifteen identical work units: five in each of three rooms, nine hundred seconds in all. Both plans must pay those fifteen minutes. I walk one metre per second along the hall; movement inside a room is already included in its one-minute work unit. Finally, every time I switch from one kind of work to another, I spend thirty seconds putting away one piece of kit and preparing the next. I charge that transition equally to both plans.

Let's take the room-by-room option, A. I enter the first room and clear clutter for a minute. I change tools, dust for a minute, change tools, wipe for a minute, change tools, vacuum, change tools again, and mop. Four changes in that room. Now the next room begins with clearing clutter, so changing from the mop back to the first tool is another transition. Four more changes inside room two, one transition to restart in room three, and four final changes there.

Count the jobs: fifteen. Count the boundaries between fifteen consecutive different jobs: fourteen. That is the easy number to miss. If I counted only four switches in each room, I would forget the two resets between rooms. At thirty seconds each, fourteen switches consume seven minutes. The cart travels from door one to door three only once, sixteen metres, so the corridor costs sixteen seconds. Work plus transitions plus walking: fifteen minutes plus seven minutes plus sixteen seconds. A is finished at twenty-two minutes sixteen seconds.

What have I gained during that time? The first room was already complete after seven minutes: five minutes of jobs plus four switches. It is a real finished result, not a room with only the dust removed. That early win is part of what makes the method appealing, even though the whole apartment takes longer.

Now I reset the rooms and start option B. First I collect clutter in room one, move to room two and do the same, then room three. With that job complete everywhere, I switch tools once. I dust on the way back: room three, room two, room one. One switch, then I wipe on the outbound trip. One switch, then I vacuum coming back. A final switch and I mop from room one to room three, leaving the just-mopped areas behind me.

The job order inside every room is still clutter, dust, wipe, vacuum, mop. There are five corridor traversals, not one. Each spans sixteen metres. That's eighty metres of walking, or eighty seconds. There are four task switches, one between each of the five groups. Four times thirty is two minutes. Add the same fifteen minutes of actual cleaning. B finishes at eighteen minutes twenty seconds.

So B walks sixty-four metres more than A, yet finishes three minutes fifty-six seconds earlier. That sounds backwards until I separate the two costs. The extra walking is sixty-four seconds. The ten switches I avoid save three hundred seconds. Three hundred minus sixty-four is two hundred thirty-six seconds, exactly three minutes fifty-six.

Notice what I didn't do. I never declared that a professional cleaner moves their arms faster when they batch jobs. I never shortened the vacuuming or removed a dirty corner. I kept the work identical. The whole advantage came from how often I interrupted it.

There is a trap in calling B the winner, though. It answers only the question I asked first: when is the entire apartment ready? It doesn't answer when I get the first useful room.

Imagine I have a guest arriving in eight minutes. The guest doesn't need all three rooms; they only need a clean place to sit. Under A, the first room is done after seven minutes. I can use it with a minute to spare. Under B, I'm moving through every room but leaving the final mop until the last pass. The first room receives its last job at sixteen minutes four seconds. It's nearly nine minutes later than under A. The batch method is still ahead for the full apartment, but it fails this different deadline. A succeeds.

It would be dishonest to change the metric silently and announce two contradictory champions. The first race was time to finish all fifteen jobs. B wins. The second question is time to finish one designated room. A wins. Both were evaluated from the same start using the same schedule, not two invented events picked to favour my preferred method. I have to decide which outcome I care about before I start.

What if the switch itself is much faster than thirty seconds? Maybe my cart is beautifully organized, every attachment already in reach, and moving from dusting to wiping takes just five seconds. Nothing else changes. A still walks sixteen metres and makes fourteen switches. Its total becomes fifteen minutes plus seventy seconds plus sixteen seconds: sixteen minutes twenty-six seconds. B walks eighty metres and makes four switches. Its total becomes fifteen minutes plus twenty seconds plus eighty seconds: sixteen minutes forty. A now wins the full-apartment race by fourteen seconds.

At five seconds per switch, extra walking is no longer worth avoiding ten transitions. The crossover occurs at six point four seconds per switch. To see it, take B's extra sixty-four seconds of walking and divide by the ten switches it saves. With a transition longer than six point four seconds, B has the cheaper overhead. With a shorter one, A has the cheaper overhead. Exactly at the crossover, their full-house times tie.

That threshold is not a secret number that applies to every home. It belongs to this model with three rooms, five jobs, and sixteen metres from first door to last. I can change the geometry without changing the logic.

Suppose those doorways were spread across a very long corridor, with eighty metres between the first and last. A would walk eighty metres. B would cross that long stretch five times, four hundred metres in total. At one metre per second, the walking difference becomes three hundred twenty seconds. B still saves ten thirty-second changes, only three hundred seconds. A finally wins by twenty seconds, even with slow equipment changes. The longer walk overturns the batch advantage.

The balance point at thirty seconds per tool change is a hallway span of seventy-five metres. In a compact apartment, batching may beat repeated tool changes. In widely separated rooms, returning repeatedly can eat that saving. I don't need to pretend the long corridor is common to learn that the layout belongs in the calculation.

There is another way to make the advantage grow: more rooms, if the end-to-end corridor distance stays the same. With four rooms instead of three and the same five tasks per room, A performs twenty jobs in sequence and has nineteen switches. B still changes task category just four times. The actual work grows to twenty minutes for both. The walking gap remains sixty-four seconds with the same corridor endpoints and five passes. Fifteen avoided switches at thirty seconds each save four hundred fifty seconds, giving B a six-minute twenty-six-second advantage. That isn't a forecast for every four-room home; it's the result of keeping this particular corridor length and this particular workload rule.

A real cleaning day could break these assumptions. A full cart might not pass a narrow doorway. A vacuum might need another power socket; a mop might need rinsing elsewhere. Wet floors may be unsafe to cross, children may walk back in, and some surfaces need time to dry. Tools and cleaning products aren't interchangeable, and using the wrong product or rushing a surface can change both quality and safety. None of those events occurs in this simplified comparison. If any matters, the route and times must be recalculated instead of declaring a universal winner.

I would test it for real by picking comparable rooms, recording the sequence of tasks, keeping cleaning quality constant, and measuring walking and every equipment change separately. I'd alternate which method goes first on different days so one plan doesn't simply benefit from the home already being cleaner. And I'd record the first completed room as well as the final finish, because I now know those are distinct outcomes. I have not performed that test here.

What have I learned from this imaginary cleaning day? Not that professionals must always batch, and not that the shortest route must win. I've learned that the time between actions can outweigh the travel between rooms. In this three-room example, batching finishes the entire job in eighteen twenty instead of twenty-two sixteen. But if I need one room by the eight-minute mark, room by room gives it to me. If tools switch almost instantly, it can even finish the whole place first.

So I ask myself two things before I begin: what counts as finished, and what actually takes time between jobs? The answer decides the method. The cart, the rooms, and the mess were the same; my choice of goal and overhead changed the winner.
