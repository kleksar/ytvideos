How could an airliner lose nearly eleven kilometres of altitude while its engines kept running? And why did the warning about its failing wings sometimes fall silent?

On the night of June first, 2009, Air France Flight 447 was crossing the Atlantic from Rio de Janeiro to Paris. There were 228 people aboard. The Airbus A330 was cruising high above the ocean when its captain left the cockpit for a scheduled rest. Two copilots remained at the controls.

The plane entered an area of turbulent weather. Ice crystals most likely obstructed the small probes that measure airspeed. At 2:10 in the morning, the speed readings disagreed. The autopilot disconnected.

One copilot took manual control and raised the nose. The aircraft climbed, lost speed, and entered an aerodynamic stall. A stall means the wings can no longer produce enough lift because they meet the airflow at too steep an angle. It does not mean the engines have stopped.

The warning sounded. But the crew did not recognize the stall. The captain returned; the aircraft was already descending rapidly. At times, the warning stopped because the system rejected extremely low measured speeds as unreliable. When the readings became valid again, it could sound again. Silence did not mean recovery.

The nose remained above the horizon while the aircraft fell. At 2:14, it struck the Atlantic. Nobody survived.

For almost two years, investigators lacked the flight recorders. After they were recovered from deep water, the final investigation described a chain of failed information, control decisions, training and communication. The initial airspeed problem was temporary. The fatal stall was not recognized in time.

An aircraft can point upward and still be falling.
