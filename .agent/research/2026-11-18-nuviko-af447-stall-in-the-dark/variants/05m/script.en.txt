Why did a passenger jet fall into the Atlantic with both engines still working? How could a warning stop sounding in the middle of a stall? And what did investigators find nearly two years after the crash?

Flight 447 left Rio de Janeiro for Paris on the evening of May thirty-first, 2009. There were 216 passengers and twelve crew members aboard an Airbus A330. On long overnight flights, a third pilot allows the captain and copilots to take turns resting.

By two in the morning, the aircraft was crossing the Atlantic at about 35,000 feet. Far below was open water. Ahead were large clouds and storms common to that region. The pilots had adjusted their route to avoid the worst weather visible on radar. The captain left for his rest; two copilots remained.

At 2:10, the autopilot disconnected. The airspeed numbers no longer agreed. One display appeared to lose most of its speed in seconds. The crew had just lost a measurement it relied on to manage the aircraft.

The most likely cause was ice crystals temporarily obstructing the Pitot probes: small tubes that sample the pressure of oncoming air. The aircraft had not lost its engines. And a faulty speed reading does not by itself make an aircraft fall from the sky.

But this was the moment the story changed. When the autopilot disconnected, the flight controls moved into a backup mode that lacked the usual automatic protection against excessive angle of attack. The copilot flying took control, pulled back and raised the nose.

For a little while, the aircraft climbed. That climb spent precious airspeed. The other copilot asked for a descent. Some speed indications recovered within roughly half a minute. The aircraft was now in a different problem from the one that had started the emergency.

An aircraft wing needs air to meet it at a manageable angle. If the nose rises too much relative to its path through the air, that angle becomes too steep. Airflow separates; lift falls. This is an aerodynamic stall. The engines can still be producing thrust.

A stall warning began sounding repeatedly. The flying pilot applied high thrust but continued making nose-up inputs. The aircraft reached approximately 38,000 feet, then began to descend. More power could not compensate for the angle at which its wings met the airflow.

The captain returned about a minute and a half after the autopilot disconnected. By then the aircraft was descending at thousands of feet per minute. The warning, however, sometimes went quiet.

This silence had a specific explanation. When the air-data system received extremely low measured speeds, it treated the angle-of-attack measurements as invalid and suppressed the audible stall warning. When the sensed speeds returned to a valid range, the warning could sound again. A useful change in pitch could therefore bring back the very warning the crew had been hearing.

The investigators did not say that this odd warning behavior alone caused the crash. The crew faced unreliable displays, unexpected changes of automation, confusing cues and a situation they never correctly identified. Nor could the crew see a direct angle-of-attack reading.

The plane continued descending with its nose raised. At 2:14:28, its recording ended as it hit the Atlantic. All 228 people died.

For months, investigators knew that automatic maintenance messages had followed the loss of speed information, but not what the pilots had done afterward. The wreckage was located in April 2011, almost 3,900 metres below the surface. Flight recorders recovered from the seabed made it possible to reconstruct the final minutes.

The 2012 report described not one broken part acting alone, but a chain: temporarily inconsistent airspeeds, control inputs that destabilized the aircraft, failure to connect the lost speed readings to the correct procedure, and failure to recognize and recover from the stall. It also examined training, cockpit indications and how the crew shared information.

Flight 447's first fault lasted less than a minute. The aircraft remained stalled until impact. The final, devastating distinction is that pointing the nose upward is not the same thing as flying upward.
