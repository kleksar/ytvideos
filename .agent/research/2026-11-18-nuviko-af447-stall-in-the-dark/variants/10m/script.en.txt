How can a modern passenger jet descend from cruise altitude while both engines are still running? What does a pilot do when three airspeed measurements disagree? And why would a stall warning sometimes stop as the aircraft fell faster?

The story of Air France Flight 447 does not begin with a broken engine or an explosion. It begins with a plane flying normally over the Atlantic, and a temporary failure in the information reaching its cockpit.

On May thirty-first, 2009, an Airbus A330 left Rio de Janeiro for Paris. On board were 216 passengers and twelve crew members, including three pilots. An overnight transatlantic crossing is long enough for the pilots to rotate rest periods. That detail matters because the captain was not in the cockpit when the first warning appeared.

The aircraft was equipped with automatic systems that normally help the crew hold speed, altitude and a stable flight path. Its flight-control computers could also prevent certain extreme commands from taking the plane beyond safe aerodynamic limits, provided the system had the information it needed.

Far below the aircraft was the Atlantic. Above it, at approximately 35,000 feet, were storm clouds in the region where tropical air currents meet. The weather was not an extraordinary surprise for a flight across that part of the ocean. The pilots adjusted their route to avoid returns on their weather radar. Around two in the morning the captain left for a planned rest, and two copilots continued the flight.

At 2:10:05, the autopilot disconnected, followed by automatic thrust control. On the left instrument screen, the displayed airspeed dropped sharply, from roughly 275 knots toward 60. Another speed display also became inconsistent. A crew that had been monitoring a stable cruise was suddenly flying manually while its most familiar indication of speed was unreliable.

An airspeed indicator does not measure how quickly an aircraft crosses the ground. It estimates how fast air moves past the aircraft. Small pressure probes outside the fuselage provide data for that calculation. Investigators concluded that ice crystals most likely obstructed these Pitot probes temporarily.

Ice in a probe did not stop either engine. The flight controls still responded. But the mismatch in speed information caused the autopilot to disconnect and the flight-control system to switch out of its normal mode. In the backup mode, called alternate law, the usual automatic protection against excessive angle of attack was no longer available.

That last phrase needs an explanation, because it is at the heart of the accident. The angle of attack is not simply how high the nose points above the horizon. It is the angle at which the wing meets the air moving past it. A plane can have its nose pointing up and still be travelling downward. If the angle becomes too steep, the airflow can separate from the wing and it will stop producing enough lift. This is a stall.

The copilot who was flying took manual control. In the first seconds he made a series of inputs, including pulling the nose up. The aircraft started climbing. For a moment, the height increased, which could look like progress. But the climb used up airspeed, precisely the margin the aircraft needed at high altitude.

The other copilot asked for a descent. There were nose-down corrections, but the aircraft continued to climb through much of this period. Some speed indications recovered rapidly. The left display was incorrect for about 29 seconds. The inconsistency between recorded indications lasted less than a minute.

A crucial turning point followed: the original information fault was subsiding, while the consequences of the aircraft's changed flight path were growing. That is why the brief icing event cannot be treated as a complete explanation of the loss of the aircraft.

At 2:10:51, the stall warning began sounding continuously. The aircraft had climbed toward 38,000 feet. The flying copilot applied high thrust and continued making substantial nose-up inputs. The aircraft's stabilizer also moved toward a nose-up position in response to control commands.

An engine can provide thrust without guaranteeing lift. Pushing a jet forward is not the same as making its wings meet the air at the correct angle. The higher the nose was held in this situation, the farther the aircraft moved from a recoverable flight path.

The plane reached its highest point and began descending. By the time the captain returned to the cockpit, roughly a minute and a half after the autopilot disconnect, the aircraft had already lost the ability to remain aloft. Its downward speed was approaching 10,000 feet per minute.

This is where one of the investigation's strangest findings appears. After the captain returned, the stall warning went silent for a time, even though the aircraft was in an extreme stall.

The warning used a measurement called angle of attack. But the control system only treated those measurements as usable when the recorded airspeed satisfied its validity rules. When the measured speeds dropped below a very low threshold, the system considered the angle measurements invalid and suppressed the warning. The threshold was around 60 knots in the recorded conditions.

As the crew made some nose-down inputs, the measurements could become valid again and the warning could return. As the nose was raised again and the recorded values became invalid, it could disappear. From inside the cockpit, that behavior was exceptionally difficult to interpret: an action that moved the aircraft in a more useful direction could coincide with the return of a loud alarm.

It is important not to turn this into a tidy story in which a single faulty warning caused the disaster. Investigators considered several interacting problems: unreliable speeds, unfamiliar handling at high altitude, inadequate recognition of the stall, cockpit workload and communication, and confusing instrument or automation cues. The warning's on-and-off behavior was one element in that larger picture, not an exclusive cause.

The crew did not have a direct display of the angle of attack. A traditional sense that the nose points up and the aircraft therefore must be climbing was not enough here. The flight path and the nose were doing very different things.

The airplane remained stalled as it descended. On the recording, its engines still responded to commands. Flight-control surfaces moved. But the crew never identified the situation as an ongoing stall and never sustained the recovery action that would reduce its angle of attack sufficiently.

At times both copilots operated their sidestick controls, adding another complication to who was doing what. The captain had returned to a cockpit already full of changing indications and urgent decisions. No emergency radio call was transmitted.

Near the end, a warning about the rapidly approaching ground sounded. At 2:14:28, the recording stopped. The aircraft struck the Atlantic with its nose about sixteen degrees above the horizon and a descent rate of almost eleven thousand feet per minute. All 228 people on board died.

At first the investigators did not have this detailed timeline. Automated maintenance messages, sent from the plane during its last minutes, showed problems with speed measurements and several systems. They could not reveal the sequence of human actions inside the cockpit. Searches recovered floating wreckage and some victims, but the deep-water site remained undiscovered.

There were repeated underwater searches. The wreckage was finally located in April 2011 at a depth of approximately 3,900 metres. The two flight recorders were recovered soon after. They preserved flight parameters and cockpit audio that transformed an unexplained disappearance into a minute-by-minute reconstruction.

In July 2012, the French investigation authority published its final report. Its conclusions extended beyond the pilots' control inputs. It examined why an airspeed inconsistency, which crews were expected to handle, had not triggered the appropriate procedure; why the aircraft's developing stall was not identified; why the training available at the time did not sufficiently prepare crews for high-altitude manual flight and unexpected loss of automation; and why the cockpit's indications did not support a clear shared understanding.

Recommendations addressed pilot training, the design and behavior of warnings, procedures for unreliable speed data, high-altitude icing, and flight-recording and search capabilities. The report treated the accident as a succession of technical, human and organizational factors.

There is a difference between the first failure and the failure that became fatal. The Pitot-probe obstruction was likely temporary. The incorrect speeds were not the reason the engines stopped, because the engines had not stopped. During the emergency, control inputs took the aircraft into a stall, and the crew failed to recognize it before impact.

The final image is not of an aircraft diving nose-first. It is of an aircraft descending toward the sea with its nose still raised. Flight 447 shows why the direction a plane points, the direction it moves, and the lift its wings produce must never be mistaken for the same thing.
