How could a space shuttle fall apart fifteen minutes before landing when the fatal damage happened sixteen days earlier? And why did the crew return without knowing their wing was broken?

On January 16, 2003, Columbia launched with seven astronauts for a scientific mission. Eighty-two seconds later, a piece of insulating foam broke away from the large external fuel tank and struck the shuttle's left wing.

Foam sounds harmless. But the wing was moving so fast relative to that falling piece that the impact could break its heat shield. The leading edge had to survive the extreme temperatures of reentry.

The next day, NASA engineers spotted the strike in launch images. They could see the impact but not its result. Similar foam had come loose on previous flights without destroying a shuttle. This time, the damage was treated as unlikely to threaten the return.

Engineers sought better images of Columbia in orbit. Those requests never produced the needed photographs. On January 23, Mission Control told the crew about the impact and said there was no concern for reentry.

Columbia carried out its experiments and headed home on February 1.

During descent, superheated gas entered a breach in the left wing. Internal structures weakened; sensors stopped reporting normally. Controllers on the ground began to see problems, but by then there was no way to repair the wing. Columbia broke apart over the United States. All seven astronauts died.

Investigators later fired a piece of foam at a real shuttle wing panel. It punched a large hole through the protective material.

The investigation found more than a vulnerable wing. It found warnings that had not become an effective safety decision. Seeing the strike was not the same as finding out what it had done.

The crew flew sixteen days after the impact. The disaster was set in motion before they reached orbit.
