Why did seven astronauts come home in a shuttle that had already been fatally damaged? And how could engineers see the danger on launch day, yet never get a clear look at the wing?

On January 16, 2003, Columbia rose from Florida with seven people aboard. It was not flying to the International Space Station. The crew had a sixteen-day scientific mission, with experiments in the cabin and the research module in the payload bay.

Eighty-two seconds after liftoff, a piece of insulating foam broke from the big orange fuel tank. It hit the front edge of Columbia's left wing.

The foam was light enough to hold in your hands. The collision was not. The shuttle and the falling foam had different speeds, and the impact was concentrated on a heat shield built to withstand reentry, not debris strikes.

The launch continued. Columbia reached orbit. There was no cockpit alarm announcing a hole in the wing.

The following day, people reviewing launch videos spotted the collision. Engineers could tell where the foam appeared to strike, but the images did not show what it had done. A crack or missing piece could be hidden on the underside of a wing more than a hundred miles above Earth.

That uncertainty should have been the main question of the mission. Instead, it became a disagreement over whether the uncertainty mattered.

Foam had fallen off other shuttle fuel tanks before. Those earlier flights had ended safely. Some managers interpreted that history as evidence that foam was unlikely to threaten this mission. But each safe landing said little about what this particular strike had broken.

Engineers tried to analyze the collision with the information available. Their estimates were limited by the pictures and by assumptions about how debris would damage the wing. Some sought better images from outside the shuttle program. Requests to arrange additional orbital photography did not lead to the pictures the engineers needed.

Columbia kept circling the planet. The crew conducted its experiments, unaware that the most important technical question about their return was still unresolved.

On January 23, Mission Control sent the commander and pilot a message about the foam strike, including a short video. The message said there was no concern for reentry. The commander shared it with the crew.

Why would a missing piece of insulation matter so much during landing?

Columbia's belly was covered with heat-resistant tiles, but the curved leading edge of the wing used especially tough reinforced carbon panels. They protected the structure when air around the returning spacecraft became extremely hot. If the foam had opened a breach, that protection would no longer be continuous. Hot gas could enter behind it.

On February 1, Columbia fired its engines to leave orbit. The descent began normally. The crew prepared for landing in Florida.

Then the atmosphere started to reveal the damage that had been invisible from the cabin. Hot gas entered the left wing. It weakened the aluminum inside, damaged internal components and altered the way the wing behaved in flight.

On the ground, flight controllers began receiving abnormal sensor readings. The crew did not have a way to inspect and repair the hidden damage during those final minutes. Communications were lost. Columbia broke apart over the United States, about sixteen minutes before its scheduled landing. All seven crew members died.

Investigators now had a question that could be tested on Earth: could a soft-looking piece of foam really punch through the front edge of a spacecraft wing?

They built a full-scale section of the shuttle's leading edge and fired foam at it under conditions modeled on the launch. In a decisive test, the impact tore a hole roughly forty centimeters across in a reinforced carbon panel.

The investigation concluded that a foam strike had breached Columbia's left wing and that superheated gas entered during reentry. But the board also examined the decisions made while the shuttle was still safely in orbit. Its report described communication failures, assumptions drawn from earlier flights and a safety system too closely tied to the pressures of running the program.

The painful distinction was this: a launch video showed the impact. It did not establish that the wing was safe. Better evidence was never secured.

NASA later changed how returning shuttles were inspected. On the first shuttle flight after Columbia, the crew used a new inspection process and photographs taken from the space station to examine the vehicle's thermal protection.

No one could go back and inspect Columbia's wing in January 2003. But the investigation showed exactly why that missing inspection had mattered.
