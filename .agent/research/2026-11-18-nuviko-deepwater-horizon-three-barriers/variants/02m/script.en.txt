How could a test warn that an oil well was leaking—and still be called a success? And why didn't the emergency shutoff stop what followed?

On April twentieth, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig was working above the Macondo well in the Gulf of Mexico. The crew was preparing to leave the well temporarily sealed.

Before leaving, they had to check that the cement at the bottom could hold back the pressure of oil and gas. They lowered pressure inside the well and watched for signs of a leak.

The readings did not agree. One line showed zero. Another showed about fourteen hundred pounds of pressure per square inch. The test was nevertheless accepted, even though that pressure was a warning that the well might not be sealed.

Work continued. Heavy drilling mud, which had helped hold the underground pressure back, was replaced with lighter seawater. Gas and oil entered the well and rushed upward.

That night, explosions and fire tore through the rig. Eleven workers died. Others escaped. Deepwater Horizon burned and sank two days later.

There was another line of defence nearly a mile below the sea: a giant valve assembly designed to cut the drill pipe and seal the well. It did not seal it.

A later U.S. Chemical Safety Board investigation concluded that the pipe had probably bent off-centre during emergency efforts, preventing the cutting blades from doing their job. That is a technical finding, not proof that one action by one worker caused everything.

The well continued releasing oil for eighty-seven days. A new capping stack finally stopped the flow on July fifteenth.

The disaster was not caused by a single broken switch. Cement failed, a pressure-test warning was dismissed, and the last barrier could not contain the blowout. The point when a dangerous reading was called safe came hours before the flames.
