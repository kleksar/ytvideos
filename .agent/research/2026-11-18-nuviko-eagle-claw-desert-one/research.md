## Three serious, comparably formed editorial bids

### Eagle Claw — selected (real 1980 mission)
- **Why stop:** “They called off the rescue. Eight men died trying to leave.” An outcome with a real contradiction, not a manufactured cliffhanger.
- **Opening:** “Why did an American hostage rescue mission turn around before reaching Tehran? And how did eight servicemen die after the decision to go home?”
- **Development beyond opening:** eight launched → rotor warning → seven → dust and instrument loss → six → one hydraulic pump failure → five versus six minimum → abort → collision during repositioning → deadly evacuation.
- **Payoff:** the withdrawal accident; hostages were not rescued; subsequent findings prompted changes to joint special operations.
- **Strong objection:** closely adjacent to Canadian Caper #191 in the same Iran crisis; some military specifics may alienate viewers. Explaining two distinct groups of hostages/operations is essential. The eight deaths cannot be used as a speculative “what if they continued” claim.

### Operation Barras — strong alternative (UK National Army Museum direct source)
- **Why stop:** captured military patrol facing unpredictable captors, and rescuers arrive by helicopter at two sites.
- **Opening concept:** “The captors released some soldiers. The British were preparing to rescue the rest—and prisoners in another village.”
- **Development:** negotiations, secret map passed by prisoner, nearby armed group, helicopter insertion to two villages, fighting and extraction.
- **Payoff:** captives and civilian prisoners freed; one British soldier killed, others wounded.
- **Strong objection:** many existing military rescue stories in the queue. It is a stronger *successful* human ending than Eagle Claw but the action-film grammar risks feeling generic.
- **Evidence:** National Army Museum, https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/operation-barras (accessed 2026-11-18), paragraphs “British patrol captured”, “Negotiations”, “Special Forces arrive”, “Hostages freed”, “Assault on Magbeni”, “Legacy”; 2000 date, 10 September rescue. Avoid copying audio testimony not directly heard.

### Andes Flight 571 — strongest alternative for broader audience
- **Why stop:** how did 16 people survive 72 days in the mountains after a plane crash?
- **Opening concept:** “A rescue plane had stopped looking. Two survivors set off on foot across the mountains.”
- **Development:** crash, evacuation search discontinued, improvised shelter, avalanche, lack of food, party crossing the mountains, contact with herdsman.
- **Payoff:** 16 survivors rescued, after the arduous trek; the cost and human moral dilemmas are integral, not sensational garnish.
- **Strong objection:** a much more universally powerful human story with movie visibility, but familiar and extensively adapted, with difficult ethical narration and high saturation. Selecting Eagle Claw is a **fresh-angle editorial hypothesis**, not a statement that Andes has lower actual viewer demand.
- **Evidence:** National Geographic, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/premium/article/flight-571-andes-crash-true-story (accessed 2026-11-18); accessible extracts include avalanche, the three who depart, one returning, herdsman Sergio Catalán, last survivors rescued. Supplement https://www.nationalgeographicla.com/historia/2024/01/tragedia-de-los-andes-el-paso-a-paso-de-la-historia-real-del-accidente-en-la-cordillera (published 2024-01-16).

**Comparative decision:** Barras has cleaner rescue completion; Andes has greater immediate broad-human appeal. Eagle Claw wins this *particular* Nuviko bid on a distinct, documented double-turn: a safety abort followed by a catastrophe while leaving. The last beat is a concrete consequence and a caution against attributing success or failure to a single individual. **Main weakness:** highly technical middle, political sensitivities, topic-adjacent #191. If an independent first listener would leave after the pump arithmetic, mark REWORK or reject; scores are not evidence of retention.

## Eagle Claw factual chain and provenance

Primary accessible contemporary report:
**U.S. Department of State, FRUS, volume XI, part 1, document 267, “After Action Report”, originally 1980**: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v11p1/d267 (full HTML read 2026-11-18).
- Lines 13–17: planning window, helicopter-desert refueling rehearsal, JCOS approval 16 April, Carter approval, 24 April target.
- Lines 21–25: eight RH-53 helicopters, nearly 600 *nautical miles* low at night, first rotor warning, crew transfer, two dust areas, second instrument failures / return, six arrive 50–85 min late.
- Lines 26–29: third helicopter partial hydraulic failure; pump leak, no replacement and insufficient daylight, five serviceable vs six minimum; commander aborts, Carter concurs.
- Lines 30–31: rotor collision while repositioning for refuel, immediate fire, **eight killed and five injured**, rapid evacuation, ammunition explosions, crews transferred to other transports, departure.
- Limitation: report is military viewpoint and excludes civilian-hostage perspectives. It does not prove the full mission would have succeeded with one more helicopter; avoid counterfactual causation.

Additional primary/official:
1. **DoD, 2025-04, Failed Iran Hostage Rescue Continues to Teach Lessons 45 Years Later**, https://www.defense.gov/News/Feature-Stories/Story/Article/4166790/failed-iran-hostage-rescue-continues-to-teach-lessons-45-years-later/ (search-retrieved text 2026-11-18; full site open returned 403). The article's accessible excerpts report detained 44-person bus, truck fire, failure, eight deaths, abandoned helicopters, televised wreckage and moving hostages. These are usable but LOWER CONFIDENCE than a read full HTML; no invented details about civilian injuries.
2. **U.S. Air Force Special Operations Command, “Sacrifices made during Operation Eagle Claw remembered”,** https://www.afsoc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/586537/sacrifices-made-during-operation-eagle-claw-remembered-35-years-later/ (search-accessible excerpts): eight launched; six arrived; faulty pump; abort; eight deaths (five Air Force, three Marines); hostages released 1981-01-20.
3. **US Army, “Operation Eagle Claw remembered 40 years later”,** https://www.army.mil/article/235436/operation_eagle_claw_remembered_40_years_later (search-accessible): unit-veteran account emphasizes failed coordination, reform; no claim that reforms were solely created by this incident.
4. **National Security Archive, July 1980 Holloway special operations review**, https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/19709-national-security-archive-doc-10-final-report (full HTML introduction read 2026-11-18): review addresses planning, command/control and inter-service capacity; descriptive abstract is not a read of the attached PDF and is never cited as though full report was inspected.
5. **U.S. DoD Airborne and Special Operations Museum**, https://www.asomf.org/search/eagle%2Bclaw/ (search excerpts, accessed 2026-11-18): overall plan and JSOC legacy.
6. **National military-history summary, GlobalSecurity**, https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/ops/eagle-claw.htm (search excerpts only; source of the two-night Desert One/Desert Two/Tehran/evac chain). Terms verified in full FRUS and secondary material; specific city assault route still treated less certain than the after-action report.

**Narration safety:** do not turn planning contingencies into an event that occurred. A plan to storm the embassy or reach a stadium remained a plan. “The Americans were released in January 1981” is documented; do not attribute their release to Eagle Claw. Keep Canadian Caper #191 separate: it extracted six escapees; this attempted a different rescue. The exact US hostage count varies across phase/dates; scripts deliberately omit it.
