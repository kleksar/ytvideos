Why did an American hostage rescue mission turn around before reaching Tehran? And how did eight servicemen die after the decision to go home?

In April 1980, Americans were being held hostage in Iran. A secret rescue force planned to reach them in Tehran. First, eight Navy helicopters had to meet transport planes at a remote desert refueling site called Desert One.

The helicopters left an aircraft carrier after dark. One crew landed when an instrument warned of possible rotor damage. The crew transferred to another helicopter, but their aircraft stayed behind.

Then came a thick dust storm. Another helicopter developed instrument problems and turned back. Six reached Desert One, the exact number the mission needed.

But one of those six had a hydraulic leak. There was no replacement pump, and no time to repair it before daylight.

Five usable helicopters. Six required.

The commander reported that the rescue could not continue. President Jimmy Carter agreed. The team began preparing to leave. No one had reached the hostages.

While one helicopter was being moved so another could take on fuel, its rotor struck a C-130 transport plane. Fire engulfed both aircraft. Five Air Force crewmen and three Marines died.

The survivors evacuated on the remaining transport planes, leaving helicopters behind in the desert.

The hostages remained in Iran until January 1981. The failed rescue later helped drive changes in how American special operations forces worked together.

The decision to cancel had been meant to stop a dangerous mission. It could not prevent the disaster that followed during the withdrawal.
