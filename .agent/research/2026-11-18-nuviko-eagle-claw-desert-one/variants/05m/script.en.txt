How can a rescue force travel deep into another country and turn back without reaching its hostages? Why did its commanders need six helicopters, and why were eight men killed after the mission was canceled?

The story begins in Tehran, in April 1980. Americans had been held inside Iran since the seizure of the United States Embassy the previous November. President Jimmy Carter approved a secret military attempt to bring them home.

There was no simple flight to the embassy. The plan needed two nights. On the first, transport planes would carry the rescue force into the Iranian desert. Navy helicopters would fly in separately from the aircraft carrier Nimitz. Both groups had to meet at a remote landing strip known as Desert One.

There, the helicopters would refuel, load the rescue team and fly closer to Tehran. The team would hide during the day, then travel into the city and attempt the rescue the following night. The entire operation depended on the aircraft arriving in working order.

On April 24, eight large helicopters lifted off the Nimitz after dark. Their crews faced a long, low flight across unfamiliar territory.

One helicopter received a cockpit warning of possible damage to its rotor. Its crew landed, checked the problem and abandoned the aircraft. They transferred to another helicopter. Seven aircraft remained.

Then the formation flew into suspended desert dust. Visibility fell sharply. A second helicopter suffered trouble with its navigation and flight instruments. Its pilot judged that continuing was unsafe and returned to the carrier.

Six were still headed for Desert One. Six was also the minimum number the ground commander needed.

The transport planes reached the landing zone ahead of the helicopters. The supposedly remote location was not empty: a passenger bus approached and the force detained its occupants. A fuel truck failed to stop and was set alight. The mission was already attracting attention before the helicopters arrived.

Eventually all six surviving helicopters reached the site, substantially later than planned.

One of them, however, had lost hydraulic fluid during the flight. The crew had continued in the hope of repairing it on arrival. At Desert One, the technicians found a failed pump and no spare. Even with the right part, there was not enough time to complete the repair before daylight.

That left five aircraft able to continue. The requirement was six. Taking off with fewer would mean abandoning the plan's own capacity limit.

The commander on the ground recommended ending the mission. The decision went up the chain of command, and Carter agreed. The rescue team would fly out in the transports. The helicopters would refuel and head back toward the Nimitz. The hostages were still in Tehran.

Then the withdrawal itself went wrong.

To let another helicopter take on fuel, a crew began repositioning one of the remaining machines. Its rotor struck a C-130 transport plane. Both aircraft caught fire. Five Air Force crewmen and three Marines were killed. Other members of the rescue force escaped the burning aircraft.

Ammunition began exploding, and the remaining crews were ordered aboard the transports. The force flew out, leaving helicopters and wreckage behind in the Iranian desert.

It was a rescue attempt in which nobody reached the people they had come to save. The hostages were eventually released in January 1981, after many more months in captivity.

Investigators examined the planning, equipment, coordination and command arrangements. The failure contributed to major changes in American special operations.

But the hardest fact is also the simplest. The commanders had stopped because five helicopters were not enough to risk the rescue. Eight people died while the force was trying to leave.
