Why did the United States send an entire rescue force into Iran, only to turn it around before its soldiers reached Tehran? Why was the mission built around a minimum of six helicopters? And why did the deadliest moment come after the decision to stop?

The answer is not a single breakdown. It begins with an operation that required aircraft from different services to meet at night, refuel in a remote desert and then disappear again before daylight.

In November 1979, the American embassy in Tehran was seized and its personnel were taken hostage. Negotiations did not bring them home. By April 1980, President Jimmy Carter had approved a secret military rescue. The operation was called Eagle Claw.

The first part sounds almost modest: bring a rescue team and its helicopters to one desert landing site. But that rendezvous was only the first step in a much longer journey.

Transport aircraft would carry troops and fuel into Iran. Separately, eight large Navy helicopters would fly in from the aircraft carrier Nimitz. They would meet at a site called Desert One. There the helicopters would take fuel and passengers, then fly to another location closer to Tehran.

The rescue force was supposed to hide during the following day. On the second night, vehicles arranged inside Iran would take the soldiers into the city. They would assault the places where the hostages were being held, gather them for a helicopter pickup, and move them toward a captured airfield where larger aircraft would carry everyone out.

Notice how many links had to stay intact. The transport planes needed the helicopters. The helicopters needed fuel, working flight systems and enough capacity for the assault team. The team then needed the vehicles and the final evacuation aircraft. A successful meeting in the desert would not, by itself, rescue a single hostage.

Military commanders had rehearsed key parts of the operation, including helicopter refueling on desert ground. Carter approved the April 24 mission date after a review of its readiness. The flight was to take place under tight secrecy, at night and at low altitude.

On the evening of April 24, eight RH-53 helicopters launched from Nimitz. The transport aircraft approached Desert One from a separate direction.

About two hours into the helicopter flight, the crew of one machine received an indication of possible rotor-blade failure. They landed and verified the problem. That helicopter could not continue. The people and equipment went aboard another machine.

Seven helicopters were now moving toward Desert One. The team had lost one before reaching the meeting point, but the plan could still go ahead.

Then the crews entered a cloud of desert dust.

It was not a quick patch of haze. According to the official after-action report, they passed through one area of suspended dust and, later, an even denser one. Navigation and visibility became extremely difficult. A second helicopter suffered failures of important flight and navigation instruments.

Its pilot decided that continuing was unsafe and returned to the carrier. Six helicopters remained on course.

This number mattered. Six was the minimum previously established for the mission. It was not the number originally launched, but it was still enough—provided all six could fly the next leg.

Meanwhile, the transport planes had reached Desert One. The desert landing site was supposed to be an isolated meeting place, but a civilian bus approached. The troops stopped it and held its passengers. Then a fuel truck failed to stop. Soldiers opened fire and the truck caught fire, creating exactly the kind of visible disturbance a secret operation could not afford.

Despite these interruptions, the commanders continued to prepare for the rendezvous. The helicopters arrived in separate intervals, roughly an hour behind schedule. There were six on the ground.

Yet arriving was not the same thing as being ready to depart.

One helicopter had developed a partial hydraulic failure during the flight. Its crew had continued in the hope that the fault could be repaired at Desert One. On the ground, the mechanics discovered a failed pump and a leak. They had no replacement pump.

Even if the part had been there, the official report said there was not enough time left before daylight to replace it, fix the leak and complete the next stage.

The arithmetic changed in an instant. Six helicopters had landed. Only five were fit to continue.

The ground commander had a decision to make. He could not simply assume that the rescue team, its equipment and ultimately the hostages would fit into five machines when the mission's minimum was six. Nor was the damaged helicopter judged safe for the trip.

The commander reported his intention to abort. The message went through the joint task force to Washington. Carter agreed. The decision to stop was made before the assault team attempted the rescue in Tehran.

That might seem like the end of the story: a dangerous operation abandoned, everyone returns to base, and the hostages remain where they were.

But the rescue force still had to get out of the desert.

The transport planes had carried the people and fuel. Now the men needed to board those planes again. The remaining helicopters had to be refueled and moved so that they could begin their return flight toward the Nimitz.

As one helicopter was repositioned to make room for another to take fuel, its rotor collided with a C-130 transport plane. The aircraft caught fire.

Five Air Force crewmen aboard the transport and three Marines aboard the helicopter were killed. Five other members of the team were injured, according to the official after-action report. The quick evacuation of the burning transport prevented further losses among the troops waiting aboard.

Soon ammunition in the wreckage began exploding. Shrapnel struck other helicopters. The aircraft still on the ground were no longer a practical way to leave, and the commanders ordered their crews into the surviving transports.

The Americans flew out of Iran, leaving helicopters and debris behind.

No hostages had been rescued. The mission did not even reach Tehran. The Iranian authorities later televised the wreckage. According to a subsequent Defense Department account, the hostages were moved to different locations, making another rescue attempt harder.

The failure was devastating, but the story has a further layer. There was an investigation. Its purpose was not simply to find a pilot to blame for the final collision.

Investigators examined how the operation had been planned and supported: the available helicopters, the narrow margin for mechanical failure, the equipment, communications and coordination across the military services. A 1980 review recommended major improvements in special operations capabilities.

Could a sixth serviceable helicopter have guaranteed that the assault on Tehran would work? No. It would only have allowed this first phase to proceed. Everything that followed—the journey into the city, finding the hostages and bringing them out—still carried serious risks. The mission was stopped at its first bottleneck, so the rest was never tested.

The eight men killed at Desert One never reached the hostages they had gone to help. Their deaths became part of the history behind reforms in how U.S. special operations forces train, plan and operate together.

The Americans held in Iran remained there for months after the rescue attempt. They were released on January 20, 1981.

There were two separate failures in that desert, and the distinction matters. A series of aircraft problems left too few helicopters for the rescue, prompting a decision to withdraw. Then, while trying to make that withdrawal, the force suffered a fatal collision.

Stopping the mission may have avoided the risks of sending five helicopters on a job that required six. It did not make the journey home safe.
