How could two walkways fall during a hotel dance while a third, just a few metres away, stayed in place? And how could a small change to a steel rod make that possible?

On July 17, 1981, guests gathered for a dance at the Hyatt Regency Hotel in Kansas City. Three enclosed walkways crossed its open lobby. One hung at the fourth-floor level, another directly below it on the second. The third stood off to one side.

The two stacked walkways were suspended using steel rods. In the original drawings, long rods ran down from the roof, past the upper walkway, to support the lower one. The fabricator proposed a simpler arrangement: separate rods for each level.

That sounds like a minor change. But it changed where the weight went.

With the shorter rods, the lower walkway hung from the upper walkway's steel beams. The connections under those upper beams now carried the weight of both walkways, not just one. The load on them was effectively doubled.

The change went through the design process without a proper check of that new load. Then, during the dance, connections on the fourth-floor walkway gave way. It crashed onto the second-floor walkway, and both fell into the crowded lobby. The walkway on the third floor remained standing because it had its own supports.

A later investigation found that the collapse happened under loads far below those required by the building code. The crowd's movement wasn't the explanation. The connections were too weak. Investigators also found that even the original arrangement had not met the code's minimum requirements.

The commonly reported final toll was 114 people killed and more than 200 injured.

So the fatal mistake wasn't merely choosing two shorter rods. It was letting that change alter the entire load path without checking whether the connection could carry it.
