Why did two suspended walkways collapse at a Kansas City hotel while a third one survived? How did a change that looked easier to build double the force on a critical connection? And why did investigators find a serious flaw in the drawings that existed before that change?

The Hyatt Regency opened in 1980. Its lobby was a tall, open atrium, with walkways crossing above the floor. They made it possible to look down on the activity below. On Friday, July 17, 1981, a dance brought a large crowd into that space.

From the floor, the arrangement looked balanced. There were three walkways: one at the second floor, one at the fourth, and another at the third. But the third-floor walkway was offset. The second and fourth were stacked directly above each other, and the way they were suspended would decide what happened that evening.

To see the problem, imagine hanging a shelf from the ceiling with a long metal rod. Now imagine a second shelf partway down that same rod. The rod takes both weights toward the ceiling.

That was broadly the idea behind the original Hyatt drawings. Long steel hanger rods ran from the roof down through the upper, fourth-floor walkway to support the lower, second-floor walkway. Each walkway transferred its weight into the rod at its own connection.

The fabricator found the long-rod arrangement impractical and proposed using shorter rods instead. One set would run from the roof to the fourth-floor walkway. A second set would hang the second-floor walkway from the fourth-floor beams.

To someone looking only at the number of walkways, nothing had changed. There were still two decks, still hanging from steel. But their paths to the roof were different. The bottom walkway now pulled directly on the upper walkway's connection.

The force at that upper connection had roughly doubled.

The proposed change was verbally approved. The detailed drawings passed through the construction review process, but the new load at that connection was not properly calculated and checked. That missing calculation mattered more than the apparent simplicity of the new rods.

On the night of the dance, the fourth-floor walkway connections failed. The upper walkway fell onto the second-floor walkway. Both plunged into the lobby. The third-floor walkway, hanging separately, stayed in place.

People on the walkways and beneath them were caught in the falling steel and concrete. Broken pipes sent water into the wreckage. Rescuers had to move heavy debris and reach people pinned below it. Construction equipment was brought into the hotel to help. One survivor was pulled out alive more than nine hours after the collapse.

The commonly reported final toll was 114 dead and more than 200 injured.

The National Bureau of Standards arrived to investigate. Its team examined distorted steel, construction drawings and the remains of the connections. It also tested components and models. The evidence pointed to a box-beam and hanger-rod connection on the fourth-floor walkway as the likely starting point.

And the result overturned a tempting explanation. This was not a dance crowd overloading a sound structure. The estimated loads were well below what the building code required the walkways to withstand. Investigators found that movement by people on the decks was not a significant part of the load.

The connection was inadequate. The two-rod change made it dramatically worse, but even the original single-rod design would not have met the code's minimum capacity.

There were consequences beyond the hotel. Two engineers involved in the project lost their professional licences in Missouri. The collapse became a case taught in engineering because it revealed a failure of design review, not simply a broken piece of steel.

The two walkways did not fall because somebody forgot to strengthen a rod after a busy party was announced. Their weak connections were built into the structure. A change in how the lower walkway hung from the upper one made those connections carry weight they were never properly checked to support.

And that explains the third walkway. It survived the same evening because it did not depend on the failing upper connection.
