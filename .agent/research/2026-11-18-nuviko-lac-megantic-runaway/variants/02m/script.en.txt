A fire was put out on a parked train. Later that night, that same train was racing downhill with nobody driving it.

How could extinguishing a fire help set seventy-two oil tank cars loose?

On the night of July fifth, 2013, a freight train stopped near Nantes, Quebec. Its route continued downhill toward the town of Lac-Mégantic.

The engineer set hand brakes on the five locomotives and two other cars. He also tested whether the train would stay still. It did.

But there was a problem with that test: the locomotive's air brakes were still applied. The engineer had not proved the hand brakes could hold the train alone.

The lead engine was smoking. After the engineer left, a fire broke out, and firefighters came to extinguish it. They shut down the locomotive's fuel and electrical power.

That stopped the fire. It also stopped the engine-driven compressor that maintained air pressure for the brakes.

The air leaked away gradually. There weren't enough hand brakes to hold the weight on the slope.

Just before one in the morning, the train started moving.

It rolled more than seven miles downhill, reaching about sixty-five miles per hour. In the centre of Lac-Mégantic, tank cars derailed and spilled crude oil. Fire and explosions destroyed much of the downtown. Forty-seven people died.

Investigators found that no single switch or single person explained the catastrophe. The false brake test, an engine fire, insufficient hand brakes, vulnerable tank cars and weak safety oversight formed a chain.

The firefighters had stopped the fire. The train was never securely held by its hand brakes in the first place.

A parked train can look perfectly still, even when the test meant to prove it is safe has failed.
