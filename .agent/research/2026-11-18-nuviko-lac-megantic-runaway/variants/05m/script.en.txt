Why did a train with its brakes set leave a hill by itself? And why did putting out a relatively small fire make the danger worse?

The answer starts in Nantes, Quebec, on July fifth, 2013. A freight train loaded with crude oil was parked for the night. Down the track lay the town of Lac-Mégantic. The train was supposed to stay in Nantes until another engineer took over.

The railway line sloped toward the town. That made one routine job especially important: securing the train while nobody was aboard.

The engineer applied hand brakes on all five locomotives and two other cars. He then checked whether the train would move. It stayed in place, so he reported that it was secure.

The trouble was invisible. During his test, the locomotives' air brakes were still applied. Those brakes were helping the hand brakes. He had tested the two systems together, not whether the hand brakes could hold the train by themselves.

That distinction would matter hours later.

The lead locomotive had been giving off unusual smoke. The engineer reported the mechanical problem to railway controllers before leaving. The plan was to deal with it the following morning.

After he left, a fire broke out on that engine. Someone called the fire department. Firefighters arrived and stopped the fire by shutting off the fuel supply and electrical breakers. A railway track foreman was there, but he did not have a locomotive operating background.

The fire was out. From the outside, the immediate emergency appeared to have ended.

But a running locomotive was doing another job: its compressor was supplying the air that helped hold the brakes on.

When the engine was shut down, the compressor stopped. Air pressure did not disappear instantly. It gradually leaked away. For a while, the remaining pressure and the hand brakes still held the heavy train.

Then the pressure fell too far.

Just before one in the morning on July sixth, the train began to roll. Nobody was in the cab. No engineer was there to use the controls. The freight train followed the descending track toward Lac-Mégantic.

Over the next seven miles, it accelerated to roughly sixty-five miles per hour. Near the town centre, it entered a curve far too quickly and derailed.

Many of the oil tank cars were torn open. Roughly six million litres of crude oil escaped. Fires and explosions tore through the downtown area. Forty-seven people were killed, and about two thousand people had to leave their homes.

There was no quick mechanical explanation of the whole disaster. The investigators had to work backward from a destroyed train and a burned town.

They examined the brakes, what the crew had done in Nantes, the engine fire, the firefighters' response, and the condition of the tank cars.

They found the first failure before the locomotive caught fire: the train had never passed a proper test of its hand brakes alone.

Imagine holding a heavy door with two different latches. You test whether the door stays shut while both are fastened. That tells you nothing certain about whether the first latch will hold after the second lets go. On this train, the air brakes were the second latch.

The fire did not create the shortage of hand-brake force. It removed the extra braking force that had been hiding the shortage.

The investigation went further. The railway had poor safety practices, and regulatory oversight had failed to correct recurring problems. The tank cars met older standards but offered limited protection when they derailed. The crude oil they carried was also more volatile than its shipping description indicated.

Those weaknesses did not all make the train start moving. Some made the consequences of the derailment far worse.

The investigation identified eighteen causes and contributing factors. It did not conclude that putting one more person on the train would necessarily have prevented the accident, and it did not make the firefighters the single cause.

What it did reveal was a chain that could have been broken at several points: enough hand brakes, a proper independent holding test, a safer response to the smoking engine, stronger equipment and more effective oversight.

The people of Lac-Mégantic paid for failures that began miles away from their homes.

So why did a train with its brakes set run away? Because a stationary train had been mistaken for a secured one. The test looked successful only while a second braking system was quietly doing part of the work.
