At the end of a summer night, a freight train entered the centre of a Canadian town with no driver and almost no braking left.

It was carrying crude oil. When the tank cars derailed, the town centre burned and forty-seven people died.

But how could a train that had been parked and tested for movement escape? And what did a small locomotive fire have to do with a catastrophe miles away?

To answer that, go back to the evening of July fifth, 2013, outside the town of Lac-Mégantic in Quebec.

A Montreal, Maine & Atlantic Railway freight train arrived at Nantes at about ten fifty. It had seventy-two tank cars carrying roughly 7.7 million litres of crude oil. The journey was not over. Another engineer was due to take the train east in the morning.

For the night, the first engineer parked it on the main track. The rails descended toward Lac-Mégantic, more than seven miles away. With so much weight behind him, the engineer needed a way to keep the train still after he left the cab.

Locomotives have air-operated brakes, but compressed air is not a permanent guarantee. Pressure can change when an engine is shut down and can leak from the system. Parked railcars also have hand brakes. They are mechanical restraints intended to keep a train from rolling when it is left unattended.

The engineer set hand brakes on the five locomotives and two other cars. He then conducted a test to see whether the train would move.

It stayed where it was.

That result looked reassuring. But it had answered the wrong question.

The locomotive air brakes had remained applied during the test. In other words, the train was being held by both systems. To know that the hand brakes could hold it alone, the engineer needed to test them without that extra air-brake force.

Because he had not done so, the apparent success gave no proof that the train would remain in place after the air pressure fell.

A different problem was already developing on the lead locomotive. During the trip, it had been producing unusual black and white smoke.

The engineer reported the mechanical trouble to the railway's controllers. They decided to deal with it the following morning. All the other locomotives had been shut down; the lead unit was left running.

That detail now mattered for two reasons. The engine was a potential fire problem. It was also keeping an air compressor running.

After the engineer left, a fire broke out on the lead locomotive. Someone called the emergency services, and the Nantes firefighters arrived.

Their immediate job was clear: stop the engine fire. They cut off the fuel supply and switched off the electrical breakers in the cab, following railway instructions.

A railway track foreman joined them. He had been dispatched to the scene, but he did not have experience operating a locomotive. The firefighters and the foreman spoke with railway control and left after the fire had been extinguished.

A dangerous engine fire had been brought under control. But another process, much harder to see, was now underway.

The engine-driven compressor had stopped along with the locomotive. Air was no longer being supplied to maintain pressure in the braking system. The air already in the system did not vanish at once; it gradually leaked away.

For a period of time, the remaining air-brake force and the mechanical hand brakes still held the train in place. Nothing on the outside revealed how close it was getting to the point where the combined force would no longer be enough.

Then the air pressure dropped below that point.

Just before one in the morning, the train started moving. There was nobody aboard to stop it.

On a long downhill stretch, even a slow roll could turn into a serious problem. The weight of the locomotives and tank cars pulled the train along the rails. It travelled more than seven miles from Nantes toward Lac-Mégantic and accelerated to about sixty-five miles per hour.

At about one fifteen, the runaway train derailed near the centre of town.

Sixty-three tank cars came off the rails. Almost all were damaged, and many split open. Approximately six million litres of crude oil escaped. Fire followed almost immediately; flames and explosions devastated much of the downtown.

Forty-seven people died. Around two thousand were forced from their homes.

This was not just a mechanical failure on a quiet stretch of railway. A town had been hit by a train that nobody was driving.

Investigators now had to work in the other direction: from the damaged cars and ruined streets back through the journey, the parking procedure, the fire and the brake test.

They had information from the locomotive event recorder, railway control recordings, physical evidence and accounts from people involved. Together, these allowed them to reconstruct what had happened.

The first crucial distinction was between two kinds of brakes.

Air brakes help control a moving train. On this parked train, the running locomotive had been maintaining the air supply that helped keep its independent brakes applied. Hand brakes were supposed to provide the separate mechanical force needed to secure the train even without that help.

An everyday comparison is a heavy door held shut by two catches. If you pull on the door while both catches are fastened and it stays shut, you have not proved that either catch could hold it by itself.

The engineer's test had been just like that. It showed a stationary train, but did not prove the hand brakes were sufficient.

The engine fire and the firefighting response did not create the original shortage of hand-brake force. Shutting down the locomotive removed the extra air-brake support that had concealed it.

This matters because it would be easy to tell the story as one person's mistake, or as firefighters unknowingly releasing a train. Neither explanation captures what the investigation found.

The firefighters had responded to an actual fire. The track foreman who attended did not have a locomotive operating background. The railway's practices and instructions left serious weaknesses in how unattended equipment was secured and managed.

The investigators identified eighteen causes and contributing factors. Some explained why the train moved. Others explained why its derailment became so destructive.

Consider the tank cars.

The seventy-two cars were Class 111 tank cars, built to standards in force when they were manufactured. But they lacked some protective features, such as full head shields and thermal protection, that could reduce damage in a derailment.

As the train struck the town, many tank shells and fittings were breached. Those openings let oil escape in enormous quantities.

And the cargo itself was another part of the problem. The crude oil was more volatile than its shipping documents indicated. That increased the danger posed by a severe spill and fire.

The railway was supposed to manage those risks before a train ever reached a town. The investigation found gaps between written operating rules and everyday practice. It also found weaknesses in employee training and safety management.

Regulators had noticed problems, but their oversight did not ensure that the railway fixed them effectively. The investigation described a company with a reactive safety culture, rather than one consistently identifying and controlling hazards in advance.

Another question was whether having a second crew member would have changed the outcome. The investigators considered single-person operations carefully. They did not establish that a second person would have prevented this accident.

That qualification is important. A simple counterfactual cannot replace a finding.

The report instead described multiple places where the chain could have been interrupted: enough properly applied hand brakes; a genuinely independent test of their holding power; safer management of a malfunctioning locomotive; stronger tank cars; correct classification of dangerous cargo; and regulatory scrutiny that corrected known weaknesses.

In the aftermath, the railway and Canadian authorities introduced changes to procedures and rules. Investigators also called for stronger tank-car protection and closer oversight. Such measures address different parts of the chain, because no single device can solve all of them.

And the losses were not erased by finding an explanation. Homes and businesses were destroyed. Thousands were displaced. Families had lost people who had simply been in their town that night.

The sequence is now clear.

The train was parked on a slope. The hand brakes were set, but the test had left air-brake assistance in place. The lead engine caught fire. Firefighters extinguished it by shutting down the locomotive. The air gradually leaked from the brakes. The hand brakes alone could not hold the weight. The train rolled toward a town and derailed.

The fire was real, and stopping it was necessary. But the deeper failure happened earlier, when staying motionless was treated as proof of being secure.

That is the difference this disaster exposed: a train can pass a test of standing still while failing the test that actually matters.
