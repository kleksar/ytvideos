Why would you leave your home when the mountain behind it had been quiet for centuries?

In April 1991, Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines started shaking. Steam explosions opened cracks in its slopes. Filipino and American scientists installed instruments to follow the earthquakes and the gases escaping from the volcano.

They could see danger building. What they couldn't give people was the exact hour of the big eruption.

By early June, earthquakes were concentrating near the summit. The amount of sulfur dioxide escaping had dropped. That wasn't necessarily good news: rising magma might have blocked the gas's way out. Then a new mound of lava appeared.

Authorities raised the alert and moved the evacuation boundary farther out. Thousands left their villages. On June tenth, about fourteen thousand five hundred people evacuated the nearby Clark Air Base.

Two days later, Pinatubo sent a towering cloud of ash into the sky. But the worst came on June fifteenth.

The volcano erupted with extraordinary force. At nearly the same time, a typhoon brought heavy rain. The ash absorbed water, grew heavier and collapsed roofs. People were killed even outside the paths of the hottest volcanic flows.

Much of the area near the mountain had already been evacuated. The U.S. Geological Survey later estimated that the warnings saved at least five thousand lives. That's a modelled estimate, not a count of people anyone actually saw being saved.

For years afterward, rain washed loose ash down the valleys in destructive mudflows. Getting away from the eruption did not mean everyone could go home.

The scientists never promised an exact minute. They gave people enough warning to act before the mountain broke open.

And for the families hearing those warnings, it came down to a question:

Why would you leave your home when the mountain behind it had been quiet for centuries?
