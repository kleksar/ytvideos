How can you persuade thousands of people to leave their homes before a volcano erupts? And what happens when the volcano does erupt, but the weather makes the disaster worse?

In the spring of 1991, Mount Pinatubo was a forested mountain on the Philippine island of Luzon. It had not produced a major eruption in centuries. People lived around its slopes, including Aeta communities whose home was the mountain itself.

Then the ground began to shake. On April second, steam explosions burst from the mountain. They were not yet the enormous eruption that would follow. They were a warning that something underground had changed.

Scientists from the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology were joined by a team from the U.S. Geological Survey. They installed instruments that recorded earthquakes and measured gases. They mapped where fast, hot volcanic flows might travel if a larger eruption came.

Their problem wasn't simply finding evidence. They also had to turn uncertain evidence into a decision. Tell people to leave too early and the warning might lose credibility. Wait for an unmistakable eruption, and the roads could already be too dangerous.

By early June, the signals were getting harder to dismiss. Earthquakes were moving closer to the summit. On June fifth, the measured sulfur dioxide fell. Usually, less gas sounds reassuring. Here it could mean magma had sealed passages that previously let the gas escape.

On June seventh, shaking and an ash explosion pushed the alert higher. A growing dome of new lava appeared. The volcano was no longer merely producing steam from underground heat.

Evacuation zones expanded. By June ninth, about twenty-five thousand people, many from Aeta communities, had been moved away from the mountain. The decision was costly: these were homes and communities, not just marks on a hazard map.

On June tenth, around fourteen thousand five hundred people left Clark Air Base. Vehicles carried personnel and families away while scientists continued watching Pinatubo. A major military installation had been cleared even though the largest eruption had not happened.

Two days later, the reason became visible to everyone. On June twelfth, Pinatubo threw a huge cloud of ash high into the atmosphere. More eruptions followed. The mountain was not finished.

On June fifteenth, the climactic eruption began. Hot ash and gas swept down the slopes. The summit collapsed into a vast crater. Ash spread over an enormous area.

And another disaster arrived at the same time: Typhoon Yunya.

Rain mixed with ash falling from the sky. Wet ash weighed far more than dry ash. Buildings beyond the immediate paths of the burning volcanic flows suffered roof collapses. People died even though many communities had escaped the zones scientists feared most.

The warnings had worked, but they couldn't keep the volcano from destroying buildings or stop the storm. This is why saying that everyone was saved would be false.

Even so, the difference was immense. The U.S. Geological Survey later estimated that the evacuations saved between five thousand and twenty thousand lives. Nobody could count those lives directly. It was an estimate of what might have happened if people had stayed.

There was another problem after the explosion. Rain kept lifting loose volcanic debris from the slopes and turning it into lahars—mudflows that could run down river valleys and bury land long after the ash cloud disappeared. Some families who escaped in June still couldn't return to their former homes.

Pinatubo also sent sulfur dioxide high into the atmosphere. Its particles helped cool the planet temporarily by roughly half a degree Celsius. A warning made in one part of the Philippines preceded an eruption whose effects could be measured around the world.

But the most important decision had happened before any of those global measurements were possible. Scientists saw imperfect signs. Officials ordered evacuations. Families left, although the largest explosion was still days away.

They didn't predict an exact minute. They gave people time to get out of the way—and that was the part of the prediction that saved lives.
