Why did the U.S. Navy put fifty sailors on trial for mutiny after hundreds of their shipmates died? And why did it clear their names eighty years later?

In July 1944, the United States was fighting World War Two. At Port Chicago, California, Black enlisted sailors loaded explosives onto ships. White officers supervised them. Training for the dangerous work was inadequate.

On the night of July 17, ammunition exploded at the pier. Two ships were destroyed. Three hundred and twenty people died. The investigation never established the exact cause.

The surviving Black sailors were eventually ordered to load ammunition again. White supervising officers had been granted leave, but the men who handled the explosives faced returning to dangerous work.

On August 9, 258 sailors refused.

Two hundred and eight returned to duty after facing disciplinary pressure. Fifty held out. The Navy charged those fifty with mutiny, one of the gravest offenses in wartime.

A military court convicted all fifty. The others also received punishment for disobeying orders. The sailors were judged before the Navy had even finished its investigation of the explosion.

The sentences were later reduced, but the convictions remained for decades. Civil rights advocates challenged what had happened.

Then, on July 17, 2024—exactly eighty years after the blast—the Navy announced the exoneration of the remaining 256 convicted men. Two convictions had been set aside earlier.

The Navy said their trials had involved serious legal errors, including a lack of meaningful legal counsel.

The explosion's cause is still unknown. But the Navy's treatment of the men who refused to load another ship no longer carries the same official verdict.
