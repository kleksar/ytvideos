A passenger saw a crack in the skin of the plane she was about to board. She did not report it. Twenty minutes into the flight, part of the roof came off.

On April 28, 1988, Aloha Airlines Flight 243 left Hilo for Honolulu, crossing the Hawaiian islands on a routine journey. At twenty-four thousand feet, a section of the Boeing 737's upper fuselage tore away. The opening stretched about eighteen feet along the cabin. A flight attendant, Clarabelle Lansing, was swept out. She was never recovered.

The other people were still inside a plane with a huge part of its roof missing. Captain Robert Schornstheimer took control and began an emergency descent. Instead of continuing to Honolulu, the crew diverted to Maui. The damaged aircraft reached the runway and landed.

Ninety-four people survived. Eight suffered serious injuries. Lansing was the flight's only fatality. This was not a story in which everybody walked away.

Why had the metal failed? The airplane had made almost ninety thousand flights. On those short island hops, its cabin was repeatedly pressurized and depressurized. At an overlapping seam in the fuselage, the bond had deteriorated. Small cracks developed around neighboring rivets and eventually joined. The airline's maintenance inspections had not detected the dangerous damage.

The passenger's observation emerged only after the accident. Investigators did not treat her as the cause. They identified failures in maintenance oversight and inspections, including a mandatory check that had not covered the seam that broke.
