How could an airliner land after losing an eighteen-foot section of its roof? Why did a passenger notice a crack before takeoff when the inspections had not stopped the aircraft? And what did investigators find beneath that apparently ordinary piece of metal?

On April 28, 1988, a Boeing 737 operated by Aloha Airlines had already been making short trips between the Hawaiian islands. Each trip looked routine. The airplane would climb, its cabin would be pressurized, it would land, and then it would do it again. That repeated cycle would become important much later.

The next flight, number 243, left Hilo for Honolulu at 1:25 in the afternoon. Ninety-five people were aboard, including the crew. The destination was another island, not the far side of an ocean.

Then, as the airplane leveled at twenty-four thousand feet, its upper fuselage opened. An eighteen-foot section of cabin structure separated from the plane. The roof above part of the passenger cabin was suddenly gone.

Chief flight attendant Clarabelle Lansing had been standing near the fifth row. She was swept outside and was never found. Passengers and crew were left in a damaged aircraft high over the water. This is the part that turns the story from an astonishing photograph into a question of survival: the airplane still had to fly.

The captain, Robert Schornstheimer, took control from first officer Madeline Tompkins and began an emergency descent. The crew did not try to finish the scheduled journey to Honolulu. They diverted to Kahului Airport on Maui, where a runway was within reach.

The Boeing reached Maui and landed. Ninety-four of the ninety-five people aboard survived. Seven passengers and another flight attendant suffered serious injuries. Lansing was the one person who died; her loss is essential to any account of this landing.

But the landing only answers half the story. A Boeing is designed to withstand repeated changes in cabin pressure. Why had this one developed a failure large enough to remove an entire section of its roof?

The aircraft had accumulated almost ninety thousand flight cycles, despite having fewer flight hours than many long-haul planes. A flight cycle is one takeoff and landing, with the pressurization changes that come with it. The airline's unusually short island routes meant it accumulated cycles faster than a maintenance schedule based heavily on flying hours could properly reflect.

The damaged area contained a lap joint. Imagine the edge of one sheet of metal overlapping another, with adhesive and rows of rivets holding the pieces together. On early versions of this Boeing 737, the adhesive bond was meant to carry much of the load from cabin pressure. But in some aircraft, that bond deteriorated. Once it stopped sharing the load, the rivets and the thin metal around them had to carry more stress.

Small cracks formed around neighboring rivets. They could be difficult to spot separately. The danger was that they could eventually connect, turning several little cracks into one long fracture.

The investigation found that Aloha's maintenance program had failed to detect significant bond separation and fatigue damage. An additional problem lay outside the airline. A federal directive had required inspections at certain joints, but it did not include the location that ultimately tore open, even though Boeing's wider service guidance had addressed it.

There was also a detail investigators learned after the flight. A passenger said she had seen a crack along the aircraft's skin while boarding, near a line of rivets. She had not reported it before departure. That observation was not the official cause of the accident, and the investigation did not transfer responsibility for the maintenance system to a traveler. It showed how far the damage had progressed before the airplane left the ground.

The accident led to stronger attention to aircraft corrosion, repeated flight cycles, inspection quality, and cracks that can grow at multiple places in one structure. The important conclusion was not simply that the pilots managed a remarkable landing. It was that the airplane had been showing evidence of a structural problem which the systems responsible for keeping it safe had failed to catch.

The crew saved a badly damaged aircraft and the people still aboard. The investigation asked why they had ever been put in that position. Its answer began long before takeoff, at a seam that had been cracking one rivet at a time.
