How does an airplane keep flying when a large part of its passenger cabin is suddenly open to the sky? What does a tiny crack beside a rivet have to do with that opening? And why did a safety system built around inspections fail to catch the damage?

The most revealing number in the story of Aloha Airlines Flight 243 is not its altitude. It is the number of times the airplane had flown.

On April 28, 1988, the Boeing 737 was working its regular routes among the Hawaiian islands. Its flights were short. The aircraft would take off, climb, descend, land, and do much the same thing again. Earlier that day it had flown several trips. At the beginning of the day, a routine walkaround in the pre-dawn darkness had revealed nothing unusual. The company did not require a flight-crew walkaround after every subsequent leg.

This was an ordinary airline schedule, and the next trip looked just as ordinary. Flight 243 departed Hilo for Honolulu at 1:25 in the afternoon, with eighty-nine passengers and six crew members aboard.

At the same time, a part of the aircraft's structure had been accumulating damage over many years. No one on the flight knew the full extent of it. The answer would only become clear after the airplane made an emergency landing.

At about 1:46, as the jet leveled at twenty-four thousand feet, an eighteen-foot section of the upper fuselage separated from the aircraft. The roof above part of the forward passenger cabin was gone.

Chief flight attendant Clarabelle Lansing had been standing near the fifth row. The decompression swept her out of the airplane. Her body was never found. The opening left passengers and remaining crew in a cabin that was no longer sealed from the outside air.

That picture is so extraordinary that it almost hides the next question. A section of fuselage had failed. But the pilots still had a controllable aircraft and a runway to find.

First officer Madeline Tompkins had been flying the plane. Captain Robert Schornstheimer took over and began an emergency descent. Honolulu was no longer their destination. They diverted to Kahului Airport on Maui and landed there.

Ninety-four people survived. Eight suffered serious injuries: seven passengers and one flight attendant. Lansing was the only person who died. No account of this flight should make that loss disappear inside the phrase 'miracle landing.'

What had allowed the remainder of the aircraft to reach the runway? The event had destroyed a major section of the upper fuselage, but it had not destroyed the entire aircraft. The crew could still control its flight well enough to descend and land. There is no reason to invent a hidden repair or an impossible maneuver. The documented result is already remarkable.

Yet the greater surprise lay in the investigation. It would not point to one sudden, isolated break in otherwise healthy metal. It would point to many small cracks, years of repeated loads, a failing bond, and a maintenance program that had not found the damage.

Look at the side of a pressurized aircraft as a collection of joined sheets, rather than one continuous shell. Along some longitudinal seams, the edge of an upper sheet overlaps the edge of a lower sheet. That is a lap joint. The particular early Boeing 737 design used adhesive bonding as well as three rows of rivets.

The adhesive was meant to distribute much of the force created when the inside of the cabin was pressurized. It was not just something to stop a rattle. It was a load-bearing part of the joint.

But the manufacturing process for those early adhesive bonds was troublesome. The bond could be weakened by preparation problems and moisture. With time, some areas could separate and become more vulnerable to corrosion.

Now imagine that the adhesive no longer carries the load as intended. The pressure forces do not vanish. They shift to the rivets and to the surrounding thin metal. The countersunk rivet holes in that design concentrated stress. On a repeatedly loaded fuselage, small cracks could develop beside several neighboring rivets.

One tiny crack may be hard to see. Many tiny cracks may appear separate. But when they grow and meet, they can create one long fracture. Engineers call this multiple-site fatigue damage. In this case, it progressed far enough that the structure could no longer safely carry the loads it faced.

The aircraft had accumulated eighty-nine thousand six hundred and eighty flight cycles, but roughly thirty-five thousand five hundred flight hours. Those two numbers describe very different things.

Flight hours tell you how long the airplane has spent in the air. Flight cycles count the repeated takeoffs and landings. Each cycle includes a change in cabin pressure, which makes the skin and its joints expand and contract under load. On long journeys, many hours may pass between one cycle and the next. On short inter-island flights, cycles accumulate quickly.

Aloha's heavy-maintenance schedule was organized largely around flight hours. Its jets could therefore accumulate fatigue-producing pressure cycles at a higher rate than the program adequately accounted for. The official examination concluded that some inspections should have occurred more frequently.

And there was a second problem in how major maintenance was done. A heavy inspection that might ordinarily keep an airplane out of service for a substantial continuous period had been divided into fifty-two separate work packages. Pieces of that work could be performed during overnight checks. Investigators criticized this fragmentation because it made a complete assessment of the airplane's condition harder.

The cracks did not exist in a total vacuum of warning signs. The industry had known for years that early bonded lap joints could deteriorate. Boeing had issued inspection and corrosion guidance. The cold-bond joint design itself had been abandoned on later production airplanes.

In 1987, the Federal Aviation Administration issued a directive requiring inspections of some lap joints. But the compulsory inspection was narrower than Boeing's service information. It focused on the S-4 locations. The section that failed on Flight 243 was at a different location, S-10 on the left side.

This distinction matters. Saying 'the aircraft had been inspected' is not the same as saying 'every relevant weak point had been checked.' A rule may require a real inspection and still leave a different vulnerable seam outside its mandatory scope.

The accident investigation also found deficiencies in how Aloha trained and documented certain nondestructive inspections. Investigators could not establish whether some required inspections had been performed ineffectively or had not been performed at all. They did not need to resolve that uncertainty by inventing a single careless mechanic. The evidence described a wider failure of supervision, training, and inspection.

One more detail emerged after the landing. A passenger said she had seen a lengthwise crack along the fuselage while boarding the flight. It was near the line of rivets where the investigation found damage. She did not tell the crew before departure.

It is tempting to turn that detail into a story about one passenger who could have saved the day. But the official findings did not identify her silence as the cause. Passengers are not the aircraft's maintenance system. The point is more unsettling: structural damage that should have been caught professionally had become visible to someone walking aboard.

The damage was not unique to that single airplane. After the accident, inspections of other Aloha 737s found signs such as bulging skin, damaged paint, and troubled rivets at many lap joints. Two other aircraft were considered beyond economical repair and were sold for parts or scrap. The accident aircraft was also scrapped.

For years, aviation had relied partly on the idea that if a fuselage skin crack grew, surrounding structures would arrest it, leaving a manageable opening rather than catastrophic damage. But several small cracks could grow at once. The protections built around one crack did not guarantee protection against many joining together.

The findings prompted attention to corrosion-control programs, inspection methods, repeated flight cycles, and widespread fatigue damage. The record also exposed a serious oversight gap: the inspection that might have found this exact failing seam was not among the checks the federal directive had made mandatory.

The story has two endings that should be kept together. One is on Maui, where a badly damaged aircraft lands and ninety-four people survive. The other is in the investigation, where the apparent suddenness of the disaster dissolves into years of gradual damage and missed opportunities to detect it.

The crew's decisions mattered after the roof came off. Maintenance and oversight mattered long before that moment. The aircraft could survive one extraordinary emergency, but the people aboard should never have needed that emergency to reveal what was happening along its rivets.
