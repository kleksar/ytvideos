How do six teenagers survive fifteen months on an uninhabited island? And what happens when they finally get home?

In 1965, six schoolboys in Tonga took a boat without permission and set off toward Fiji. They had no engine, no compass, and no map.

On the first night, a storm tore away the sail and rudder. For eight days they drifted without proper food or drinking water. Then they saw a steep green island.

A wave smashed their boat against the rocks. They had reached ʻAta, an island with no one living on it.

At first they caught fish and searched seabird nests for something to eat or drink. Later, climbing inland, they discovered chickens and traces of an abandoned settlement. They made a fire. Keeping it alive became a shared responsibility.

The boys built a shelter, planted food, collected rainwater, and took turns watching for ships. They argued too. When tempers rose, they separated until they could talk again.

One boy broke his leg. The others helped him recover instead of leaving him behind.

Days became months. Then, in September 1966, Australian sailor Peter Warner spotted signs of activity on the island. He approached, found the six boys, and radioed Tonga.

The reply stunned him: their families had believed them dead and held funerals.

But coming home brought one last surprise. The boys were jailed over the boat they had taken. Warner arranged compensation for its owner, helping secure their release.

They had survived a storm, a wreck, and fifteen months alone. The final obstacle was a debt waiting for them at home. And through it all, six people had kept one another alive.
