What happens when six teenagers set out to sea without a compass and disappear for more than a year? How did they stay alive, and why did their rescue not quite end the trouble?

In 1965, six boys from a boarding school in Tonga decided to sail to Fiji. They took a small boat without permission. There was no engine. None of them carried a map or compass. They knew the ocean, but that did not mean they knew where the wind would take them.

Their plan lasted less than a night.

A storm ripped away the sail and damaged the rudder. Now they could neither choose a direction nor make meaningful progress. For eight days the boat drifted, while food and drinkable water ran out.

Eventually, an island appeared. It was steep and green, rising straight out of the sea. The boys didn't sail into a sheltered harbor. A wave hurled the damaged boat against the rocks and broke it apart.

They crawled onto the island of ʻAta. No one came to greet them. It had once been inhabited, but the settlement was abandoned.

Surviving the wreck was only their first problem. Near the shore, the teenagers caught fish and searched seabird nests for eggs. Some of the food they ate was raw. They needed fresh water as badly as food.

Once they had recovered enough strength to climb, they explored the higher ground. There they discovered traces of the old settlement, including a cooking pot, a machete, and chickens. Those objects mattered. They meant the boys no longer had to solve every problem with their bare hands.

They made a fire. Then they faced an entirely different challenge: keeping it burning. A fire that went out could cost them cooked meals, warmth, and a signal for passing ships. They assigned turns to guard it, through the night and into the next day.

The island gradually acquired a routine. They built a shelter with palm leaves and gathered rainwater. They planted a garden, organized food collection, and kept watch for vessels on the horizon.

These were not six characters who agreed about everything. They argued. Mano Totau later explained that when they grew angry, they would go to different parts of the island to cool down. They had to find a way to live together because there was nowhere else to go.

And the island did not stop presenting problems. One boy, Stephen, broke his leg. The others cared for him while he healed. They were not equipped with a hospital. They had one another.

They also made room for things that weren't immediately about survival. With what they could find, the boys built an improvised gym and a badminton court. They even made a guitar. Those activities turned a series of desperate days into something resembling a life.

They still watched the sea.

In September 1966, Australian sailor Peter Warner was passing near ʻAta. He noticed signs that someone was there and brought his boat closer. He found six young people who had disappeared fifteen months earlier.

Warner called Tonga on the radio. The response was extraordinary. The boys had been presumed dead. Their families had already held funerals.

When the six returned, the reunion was real. But so was the theft of the boat that had started their journey. They were jailed. Warner helped arrange payment to the boat's owner, and the boys were released.

Warner later employed some of them at sea. The accidental voyage had created a connection that continued after the rescue.

People often describe a deserted island as a test of what humans become when no one is watching. On ʻAta the important actions were much smaller: one person guarding the fire while another slept, someone collecting water, others helping an injured friend.

Those habits kept six boys alive for fifteen months. And when the captain finally found them, he discovered a group, not six separate survivors.
