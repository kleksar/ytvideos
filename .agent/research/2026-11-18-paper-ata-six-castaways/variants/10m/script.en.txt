How could six schoolboys live for fifteen months on an island where nobody else lived? Why did a storm leave them there in the first place? And when a passing captain finally rescued them, what was waiting back home?

In 1965, the Kingdom of Tonga was a chain of islands in the South Pacific. For young people growing up there, boats were familiar. So was the horizon. But knowing the sea from shore is very different from taking a small boat across hundreds of miles of open water.

Six boys at a boarding school decided to leave. They wanted to sail toward Fiji. They took a boat without the owner's permission and set off without an engine. They had no navigational chart or compass.

They could raise a sail. They could not know what the next night would do to it.

A storm hit. The sail was torn away, and the rudder was damaged. Without those two things, the teenagers could no longer steer toward their destination. They were carried wherever wind and current took the boat.

The first day became another. Their food and drinking water ran short. They tried to endure the heat and the movement of the damaged boat. Eight days passed.

Then they saw land.

An island rose sharply from the water, thick with green vegetation above the rocks. They had not been guided there by a chart. They had drifted to ʻAta, far from where they intended to go.

The approaching surf gave them no gentle landing. A wave pushed the boat onto the rocks, breaking it apart. Now the boys had escaped the open sea, but they had lost their only way to leave.

There were six of them, stranded together. No village was waiting. No lighthouse would call for help. And a tall island is not the same thing as a place where food and water are easy to reach.

Near the shore they worked with what they had. They caught fish. They searched the nests of seabirds for eggs and, in the worst early days, sources of liquid. The survivors later described eating raw fish because they did not yet have a steady fire. For a time, finding enough to eat and drink consumed their attention.

But ʻAta contained evidence of people who had once lived there.

This is an important part of the story. The island was not an untouched fantasy paradise. A Tongan community had lived there until the nineteenth century, when slave raiders took islanders away and the surviving settlement was abandoned. That history was already written into the objects the boys would eventually find. They were surviving in a place with a past, not a place outside human history.

Once strong enough to climb higher, they reached the forested ground above the coast. There they found a pot, a machete, and chickens. They also had access to plants that could help support a garden. These were small discoveries with large consequences.

A pot meant food could be cooked. A blade meant shelter materials could be cut. Chickens and plants made it possible to think beyond the next fish. The island had become difficult but workable.

They made a fire.

Getting a fire going was an achievement. Keeping it going for months was work. If everyone fell asleep and it died, they would have to start again. So the boys divided the responsibility. Someone stayed with it while others rested. It was a practical agreement, not the sort of decision that makes an exciting headline. Yet it changed what they could eat and gave them something visible to any passing ship.

With the fire protected, they built more lasting shelter from leaves and branches. They devised ways to catch and store rainwater, and they established a garden. The work was not glamorous. It had to be done again when the weather changed, when supplies ran down, or when something broke.

They also kept watching the water beyond the island. A ship might appear one morning and pass without noticing a tiny settlement on the heights. Somebody had to be ready to signal.

The teenagers did not stop being teenagers. They improvised a place to play badminton and made simple exercise equipment. They even built a guitar from materials they could gather. The point was not that island life had suddenly become comfortable. It was that a day needed something in it besides collecting food and fearing the next day.

And they argued.

When survivor Mano Totau talked about the experience decades later, he did not claim the boys had lived in perfect harmony. They disagreed and sometimes fought. They learned to separate, walk away from one another, and return when tempers cooled.

On a small island, there was little benefit in winning an argument if that meant losing the person who would help carry water tomorrow. Their way of settling disputes became another survival tool.

The greatest test of that agreement may have come when one boy, Stephen, injured his leg badly enough to break it.

There was no doctor and no boat to bring one. The others supported him while he recovered. Accounts of the experience describe them making a simple splint. They kept helping him with the ordinary demands of living. His recovery was not immediate; meanwhile the island still needed tending.

None of these solutions guaranteed rescue. A working garden could keep them alive, but it could not tell their families where they were. A fire could send a signal, but only if someone happened to look toward the island.

The boys tried to think of ways to leave, including making a raft. The attempt did not carry them home. What they could control was the camp: food, water, fire, shelter, lookout, and how they treated one another.

Meanwhile, far away in Tonga, the missing boys' families had no clear account of what had happened. They had disappeared at sea. Eventually their relatives accepted that they were dead and held funerals.

Months passed. Each season brought the same question back to the island. Would anyone sail close enough to notice six people who were not supposed to be there?

In September 1966, a vessel under Australian sailor Peter Warner passed near ʻAta. He noticed signs of life, including the fire, and steered toward the island rather than continuing past.

For the boys, a ship was no longer only a shape on the horizon. It was turning toward them.

Warner's crew found six young men who said they had been missing for fifteen months. The captain could not simply assume he knew who they were. He contacted Tonga by radio to check their story.

The response confirmed more than their identity. The boys had been presumed dead. Their families had already held funerals. Six people who had disappeared from the world were alive, together, on an island.

Their rescue finally ended the daily work of keeping the camp alive. But it did not erase the choice that had put them at sea.

When they returned to Tonga, the taking of the boat caught up with them. They were jailed. This was a startling turn after so long trying to get home: a rescue at sea followed by detention on land.

Warner stepped in again. He helped arrange a payment to compensate the boat's owner, using a deal connected with a television account of their ordeal. The boys were released. Television crews later documented the story, and surviving footage showed how they had made their island home.

The documentary was not a camera that had watched fifteen months in secret. Much of it was a reconstruction made afterward. That distinction matters: the work of building the camp was real, but the pictures recorded later were not the same as a continuous record of the original days.

Some of the young men later worked with Warner at sea. The rescue had created a lasting relationship between the captain and the people he had found.

There is another detail in what happened afterward. Years later, Warner and Mano were again involved in helping people at sea, according to accounts of Warner's later life. It does not turn their island ordeal into a lesson that every disaster makes heroes. It shows that their connection did not end with the radio call.

For fifteen months, no one on ʻAta could promise the next ship would turn toward the island. No one could promise food would always be there, or that an injured friend would recover quickly.

What the six could decide was whether someone would stay up to protect the fire; whether to plant another crop; whether to stop an argument before it broke the group; whether to help a boy who could not walk.

When the captain approached, he did not find six isolated people competing to survive. He found the small society they had built together. And that is how six missing schoolboys were still alive when a ship finally came.
