How could a rock weighing just twenty-seven pounds trap a man for more than two weeks? And why did the rescue tunnel finally reach him too late?

In January 1925, Floyd Collins was exploring a cave in Kentucky. He hoped to find a passage that visitors could enter from a road nearby.

He crawled through a gap so narrow that one arm had to stretch ahead of him. Then his lantern flickered. He turned back.

On the way out, his foot shifted a rock. It wedged around his ankle, nearly sixty feet below the surface. Collins couldn't get his body into a position to lift it.

When neighbors found him, his brother Homer brought food and water. Volunteers tried to dig near his foot. A rescue team even tried pulling him with a harness, but it hurt him and failed.

Then the cave passage collapsed. The people who could reach Collins no longer had a safe way in.

So workers began digging a separate vertical shaft. They used picks and shovels because heavy drilling might shake more rock loose. Cold rain, hard stone and unstable walls kept slowing them.

Above ground, thousands gathered. Some came to help; others came to watch. Below ground, no one could tell Collins how close the new tunnel was getting.

On February sixteenth, the diggers finally broke into the cave passage. They found Collins dead. He had apparently died days before they reached him.

His ankle was still trapped. The new shaft was already dangerous, so the rescuers left his body there rather than risk more lives.

The tunnel had solved the problem of reaching Floyd Collins. It had not solved the problem of reaching him in time.
