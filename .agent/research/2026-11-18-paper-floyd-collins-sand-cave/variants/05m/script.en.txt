Why did thousands of people gather around a cave to watch one man being rescued? How could a twelve-kilogram rock hold him there? And why did a rescue shaft reach the right place, but fail to save him?

Kentucky, January 1925. Floyd Collins explored caves and hoped to open one to paying visitors. His own family's cave was too far from the main road. Sand Cave seemed better placed, so he went inside to investigate.

The passage narrowed until Collins had to crawl with one arm stretched in front of him, pushing his kerosene lantern. Farther in, the space widened. But the flame started flickering, and he turned around.

As he crawled back, his foot loosened a rock. It dropped against his ankle and pinned it. Collins was about sixty feet below the surface. The rock weighed only twenty-seven pounds. The problem was the space around it: he couldn't turn, stand or get enough leverage to lift it.

His neighbors noticed he hadn't returned. A seventeen-year-old, Jewell Estes, managed to squeeze far enough into the cave to hear him. Soon Floyd's brothers were there. Homer could bring food and water, but no one could simply carry him out.

The first rescue methods looked straightforward from above. Dig around the foot. Slide something under the rock. Pull Collins with a harness.

Inside, they were far harder. The pull with a harness caused pain and failed. Small jacks that might lift the stone either would not fit or kept slipping. Men argued about the safest method while Collins remained fixed in the same position.

A young newspaper reporter, William "Skeets" Miller, crawled down to speak with him. Miller helped in the rescue and brought the story back to readers. Soon the entire country was following a man they couldn't see.

Then, on February fourth, the narrow route began falling in. Rescuers escaped as rocks dropped into the passage. A later attempt to clear the obstruction triggered more falling stone. The only route that had allowed people to bring Floyd supplies had become too dangerous to use.

Now the rescuers had to reach him without using that entrance.

They began digging a separate shaft on February fifth. The plan was to go down from the surface and then cut sideways toward Collins. The workers avoided heavy drilling and explosives for fear of shaking the cave. They used picks and shovels instead.

An engineer initially estimated about thirty hours. But within the first days the diggers encountered mud, loose rock and difficult stone. On February sixth the shaft was only seventeen feet deep. Collins was roughly sixty feet down.

As men dug, the scene above them changed. A crowd of sightseers arrived. Food sellers set up stalls. On one Sunday, an estimated ten thousand people came to the cave. The National Guard controlled the crowds. Newspapers carried rescue reports beside rumors that the whole thing was a hoax.

None of that changed the work underground. The shaft had to be deep enough, its walls supported, and its bottom clear of water.

Cold rain slowed the digging. Snow followed. The workers' hands went numb. When the shaft finally neared the right depth, the sides started shifting and water collected at the bottom. They still needed to dig horizontally to find the original passage.

On February fifteenth they came within a few feet. To the crowd outside, it looked as though they were about to succeed.

The next afternoon the tunnel broke through.

Floyd Collins was there, with his ankle still pinned. He was dead. The people who examined him believed he had died days earlier, before the final breakthrough.

The shaft itself was in danger of collapsing. Rescuers left his body in the cave rather than risk another death. It was recovered later.

Above ground, an enormous effort had finally reached the man everyone had been waiting for. But after the original route collapsed, the rescue became a race between two things: the speed of picks and shovels, and the time a trapped man could survive without help.

The shaft reached the correct passage. It arrived too late.
