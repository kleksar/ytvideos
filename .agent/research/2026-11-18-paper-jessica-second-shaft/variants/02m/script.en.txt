How do you rescue a child trapped twenty-two feet underground, inside a pipe only eight inches wide? And why wasn't reaching her enough to get her out?

On October 14, 1987, eighteen-month-old Jessica McClure was playing in a backyard in Midland, Texas. She slipped through the opening of an abandoned well and became wedged deep below the surface.

Rescuers could hear her crying. They lowered a microphone and later a small camera, but nobody could climb down the narrow pipe. Pulling from above was not an option either.

So workers brought in drilling equipment. They sank a second, wider shaft beside the well, then started cutting sideways through rock toward Jessica.

That sounds simple until you meet the rock. A cement-hard layer called caliche broke drill bits and slowed the men to inches of progress. The child could still be heard. The rescue passage still wasn't wide enough.

When they finally broke through, the team discovered another problem: one of Jessica's legs was pinned in an awkward position. They had to enlarge the opening without bringing the surrounding rock down on her.

Rescuers switched tools, worked carefully around her, and a paramedic eventually freed her from the pipe.

After roughly fifty-eight hours, Jessica came up through the rescue shaft alive. She needed weeks of hospital treatment, including surgery to her injured foot, but she survived.

The entire country had watched a hole in a backyard. What brought Jessica home wasn't one heroic pull. It was the second shaft, the exhausting work through the rock, and the last careful movement beside a child nobody could reach from above.
