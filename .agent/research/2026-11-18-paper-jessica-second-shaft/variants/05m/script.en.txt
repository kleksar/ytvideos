Why would rescuers drill a tunnel below a trapped child instead of straight toward her? Why could they hear her voice for two days without being able to touch her? And what finally changed?

The scene was an ordinary backyard in Midland, Texas. On October 14, 1987, eighteen-month-old Jessica McClure was playing with other children when she fell through the opening of an abandoned water well. The opening was about eight inches across. Jessica came to rest roughly twenty-two feet underground.

She was alive, but the pipe was far too narrow for a rescuer. Above ground, people could hear her cry. A microphone let them listen more closely. A small camera briefly showed her position. There was no room to lower an adult beside her.

The first problem was not lifting Jessica. It was getting another human being close enough to help.

Midland was an oil town. Workers brought a large drill into the yard and began digging a new shaft a few feet from the original well. This one would be wide enough for rescue workers. Instead of simply cutting across at Jessica's level, they went deeper and planned to tunnel upward toward her. Working from below reduced the danger of loose rock dropping directly into her narrow space.

The main obstacle wasn't the depth. It was the ground between the two shafts.

Under that backyard lay caliche, a mixture of hard minerals that could behave almost like concrete. Workers swung heavy drills in a space barely wide enough to turn around. The rock chewed through drill bits. At one point, a worker reported advancing only a couple of inches in an hour.

The microphone was still there. Jessica could be heard crying, talking, and sometimes singing. Crews blew warm air into the well. Doctors considered the danger of dehydration, but also the possibility that feeding her before an examination might cause choking or complicate emergency treatment. The child had to wait while people only a few feet away fought the rock.

Day turned into night, and then another day. Television cameras gathered outside the fence. The rescue became a national live story. Viewers could see the drilling machine and the exhausted workers, but the decisive struggle was happening underground, beyond the camera's view.

On Friday morning, October 16, the tunnel finally met the well. Rescuers used a mirror to see Jessica. Now they could reach toward her.

And still, they couldn't bring her out.

Her right leg was caught at an awkward angle. The opening between the shafts was too tight for a rescuer to turn and free her without risking injury. After all those hours of drilling, they needed more space—precisely where careless drilling could hurt the child.

The crew went back to work with smaller tools, then a high-pressure water drill to widen the passage. The work had to be controlled. Breaking through was only the beginning of the last, most delicate part.

A paramedic, Robert O'Donnell, squeezed into the rescue space and helped work Jessica free. Another rescuer, Steve Forbes, carried her upward through the larger shaft.

After about fifty-eight hours underground, Jessica emerged alive on the evening of October 16. Her face was bandaged, and her injured foot needed prolonged medical treatment. She spent more than a month in hospital. She survived, but not without damage: part of her right foot required surgery.

Millions had followed the operation as a race against time. Its most revealing detail was quieter. The team did not solve the problem by pulling harder. They dug a second route, adapted when the rock defeated their tools, and then slowed down again when they finally reached Jessica.

For two and a half days, the rescue was measured in inches. The last inches mattered most.
