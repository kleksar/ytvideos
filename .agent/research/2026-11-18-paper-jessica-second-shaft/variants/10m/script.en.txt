How can an entire town reach a toddler buried just twenty-two feet beneath a backyard? Why did the rescuers deliberately dig deeper than the child? And after they finally touched her, why did it still take hours to bring her out?

This happened in Midland, Texas, an oil town where heavy drilling equipment was familiar. On Wednesday morning, October 14, 1987, eighteen-month-old Jessica McClure was playing with other children at a family day-care home. She fell through the opening of an abandoned water well.

The opening was only about eight inches wide. Below it, the pipe changed shape, and Jessica came to rest roughly twenty-two feet underground. She was caught in a space much too small for an adult to enter. The rescuers could hear her, but they couldn't simply descend to her level.

For the family and the people arriving in the yard, the first useful fact was that Jessica was alive. They heard cries coming up from the pipe. Rescuers lowered a microphone to hear her breathing and voice. A small camera, the kind used to inspect pipes, gave them a brief look at her position. The vibration from drilling later damaged that camera.

None of those devices could move her. A rope dropped from the top would not free a child wedged sideways against the walls. And there was no room for a paramedic to slide down and work beside her. The team needed a second entrance underground.

Midland's oilfield workers had drilling machinery and experience with difficult ground. A large rig went to work a few feet from the little well, cutting a much wider rescue shaft. This second hole, roughly three feet across, was meant for people and tools, not for the child.

The geometry was more complicated than it looked from the surface. The workers took the new shaft below Jessica's level and planned to connect back upward. If they attacked the old well carelessly from above, loose earth and stones could fall onto the child. Approaching from lower down was a way to control what happened near her.

But they were not drilling through soft garden soil. The underground layer was caliche, a hard mixture of minerals that could resist the tools almost like concrete. The team brought down jackhammers and drills. In the narrow workspace, men took turns operating heavy equipment while others measured their progress.

At one point, a rescuer reported making just two inches of progress in an hour. Bits broke. The rock did not become easier because a child was waiting on the other side. Workers could be within a few feet of Jessica and still have an entire wall of stone between them.

The second problem was time. Jessica was stuck without food or water. Doctors discussed dehydration but did not simply send a bottle down. They were concerned about choking and the possibility of emergency surgery as soon as the child came out. Warm air was directed into the well to help keep her from becoming too cold.

Through the microphone came crying, talking and, at times, singing. Rescue teams listened for those sounds because they were one of the few signs they had of her condition. They were not interviewing a child; she was only eighteen months old. The sounds told them she was still there, still responding.

During Thursday the shaft grew deeper, and the sideways tunnel moved toward the old pipe. The team had measurements, but knowing where Jessica was didn't tell them exactly when the last piece of stone would give way. Public estimates of the rescue time were repeatedly pushed back. Every time crews seemed close, the rock demanded more work.

Above ground, the small backyard had become a national news location. Television crews gathered around it. Some of the most dramatic broadcasts showed people looking at an opening almost too small to see on a screen. Millions could watch the work happening above ground; nobody watching could speed up the drilling underneath.

This is an important difference between following a rescue and carrying one out. The picture looked almost unchanged for hour after hour: a rig, rescue helmets, a fenced yard. Underground, however, each inch changed what the next worker could reach, how a tool could be turned, or whether the passage would be wide enough for shoulders.

On Friday morning, October 16, workers finally broke through to Jessica's well. They held a mirror into the opening and could see her. One of the paramedics reached an arm toward her. What had looked for two days like an impossible distance had suddenly become only inches.

Then came a new obstruction.

Jessica's right leg was trapped in an awkward position. Simply pulling her toward the opening risked making the injury worse. And the hole the team had drilled through the rock still did not give a rescuer enough room to twist, free the leg and maneuver the child safely out.

It was tempting to think the most difficult job had ended when the two tunnels met. In reality, breaking through was not the same as extracting her. The final passage had to be made wider, yet this was the place where uncontrolled force was most dangerous.

Workers resumed drilling with smaller tools, then used a powerful water drill to cut away more rock. The new tool did not make the problem disappear. It let the team remove material in the tight space as carefully as the situation allowed. The work continued for much of the day after that first breakthrough.

Finally, a paramedic named Robert O'Donnell squeezed into the connecting tunnel and worked to release Jessica. Another rescuer, Steve Forbes, was there for the lift to the surface. Jessica was placed on a small support and brought up through the wider shaft that had been drilled beside her well.

It was Friday evening. About fifty-eight hours had passed since the fall. She came out alive.

Cameras recorded a small child with bandages on her face and dirt on her skin, surrounded by rescuers. The country had been watching for two and a half days, but the actual moment meant that the family finally had her back and doctors could now examine injuries that couldn't be fully understood underground.

The story did not end with the cheers. Jessica had been trapped in a position that damaged her right foot. She was dehydrated and needed a long hospital stay. She later lost a small toe and underwent treatment to reconstruct the injured foot. She was discharged more than a month after the rescue and went on to grow up.

That distinction matters. The public headline was that she survived. The full result includes the work required to treat her injuries afterward, and the fact that a child's private recovery continued after the television vans left.

The rescue also became a landmark in television news. National networks interrupted scheduled programs to show the extraction, while twenty-four-hour coverage allowed people far from Midland to keep returning to the same backyard. A 2021 documentary revisited how this prolonged live rescue helped shape viewers' expectations of continuous coverage. It didn't invent twenty-four-hour news, which already existed. It showed what such news could feel like when a single family was at its center.

Look again at the two holes. One was eight inches wide and had taken Jessica underground in seconds. The other took workers nearly two and a half days to create. It was wide enough to let rescuers reach the first, but even that wasn't sufficient until they carefully made the connecting opening larger.

There wasn't one miraculous tool or one person who solved every part. Oilfield drillers opened a route. Medical teams monitored a child they couldn't examine. Workers kept changing tools when rock stopped them. The paramedics completed the extraction only when there was enough room to move her without simply dragging her past the obstruction.

The most difficult part of the rescue wasn't only finding a way down. It was knowing when to stop cutting, when to keep going, and how to make the last few inches safe enough to bring Jessica home.
