How did a rented propeller plane reach the Kremlin, through airspace guarded by Soviet missiles and fighter jets? And why did a fighter pilot see it with his own eyes and let it continue?

On the evening of May 28, 1987, a tiny Cessna was circling central Moscow. Its pilot was looking for somewhere to land. Red Square had crowds and obstacles. A road bridge nearby looked just wide enough, if he could clear the wires above it.

The man at the controls was Mathias Rust, an eighteen-year-old amateur pilot from West Germany. That morning, he had taken off from Helsinki, supposedly bound for Stockholm. A few minutes after leaving Finnish-controlled airspace, he made a hard turn toward the Soviet coast.

Rust later described the flight as a peace mission. The United States and Soviet Union were still locked in the Cold War, and he wanted to deliver an appeal to Mikhail Gorbachev. Whether such a gesture could change anything was another question. Getting there without permission was already a serious gamble.

In Finland, controllers saw the sudden change of course, tried calling him and lost the radar return. When debris was reported offshore, a search for a possible crashed aircraft began. Rust was nowhere near the wreckage. He was crossing the Soviet border.

Soviet operators tracked an unidentified aircraft approaching the coast. Missile crews were alerted, and fighter jets were launched. One pilot spotted an aircraft that resembled a small Soviet plane. Another MiG-23 later moved right up alongside the Cessna.

Rust could see the fighter and its enormous swept wings. The Soviet pilot could see Rust's aircraft. They stayed close briefly, then the MiG departed. The encounter never became an effective instruction to land.

That wasn't the last opportunity to stop him. As the Cessna flew deeper inland, it passed through air-defense sectors that did not share a clear picture. At one point it entered an area with military training flights. A controller treated its radar return as friendly. Later, a different controller mistook it for a search-and-rescue helicopter. The message handed toward Moscow described a Soviet aircraft without a working transponder—not an unauthorized German plane.

The equipment had seen something real. The people in the chain had repeatedly explained it away.

By early evening Rust was over Moscow. He found the Kremlin walls and surveyed his options. Landing inside the Kremlin would conceal the stunt behind its gates. Landing in the middle of Red Square risked the people on the ground. The nearby Bolshoy Moskvoretsky Bridge offered a narrow, astonishing alternative.

He descended between sets of overhead wires, touched down on the bridge and rolled around a car. He then taxied toward St. Basil's Cathedral. People walked up to the plane, asked questions and took photographs. The foreign pilot who had crossed one of the world's most closely guarded borders stood beside an aircraft he had borrowed from a flying club.

The authorities eventually detained him. A Soviet court sentenced Rust to four years for his illegal flight; he was released in August 1988, after roughly fourteen months. The Soviet defense minister and head of air defense were removed shortly after the landing.

Rust never delivered his message to Gorbachev. And this one flight did not, by itself, end the Cold War. It did expose a devastating gap between a powerful defense system and the decisions made by the people operating it.

The bridge landing made the photographs. But the deeper story is what happened long before the bridge: a fighter had pulled up beside the plane, and somewhere along the way, that warning vanished.
