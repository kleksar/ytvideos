A hole in the wrong place flooded a coal mine. Another hole would save the nine men trapped inside.

Pennsylvania, July 2002. Eighteen miners were working underground when a mining machine broke through a wall. Behind it was an abandoned mine full of water.

One crew of nine escaped. The other nine found their way out cut off by the flood.

They climbed to the highest ground they could reach, more than seventy metres below a farm. The water kept rising. They tried to hold it back, then waited in an air pocket.

On the surface, rescuers drilled a narrow hole to find them. When the bit reached the mine, somebody below knocked on the pipe. They had found survivors, but the hole was far too small to lift anyone out.

So a larger drill began cutting an escape shaft. Then its bit broke. As the team worked to free it, pumps fought the rising water and air was sent down to the men.

More than three days after the flood, the larger drill finally broke through. A communication device went down. All nine were alive.

A metal rescue capsule descended on a cable. One man climbed inside and rose to the surface. Then another. Before dawn on July 28, all nine were out.

The investigation found that the miners had been working from an incomplete map of the flooded mine next door. The supposed safe distance had never been real.

The hole that saved them had to be drilled from above. The hole that trapped them began with a wall the map had placed somewhere else.
