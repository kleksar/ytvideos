How do you rescue nine men when you cannot reach the mine entrance? And why did the map put a flooded mine safely out of their path?

In July 2002, eighteen coal miners went to work at Quecreek, Pennsylvania. Their underground passages ran through a coal seam beneath fields and farms. A nearby mine had been abandoned decades earlier. The plan was to leave enough rock between the two workings.

That plan depended on knowing where the old tunnels ended.

Late on July 24, a mining machine cut through the working face. Water burst through. The wall was not separating them from untouched coal. It opened into the flooded abandoned mine.

The eighteen men were in two groups. Nine escaped through rising water, changing routes when passages became impassable. The other nine were too far inside. Water blocked their way to the entrance.

Those nine retreated along the passages to the highest ground they could find. Underground, the roof was low enough that miners often had to crouch. Water reached their refuge, and they tried building a barrier to hold it back.

At one point the men wrote messages for their families. They tied themselves together. But the water eventually stopped advancing. They were still alive in a pocket of air, roughly seventy metres under a farmer's field.

Above them, people were already looking for a way down.

The farmer, Bill Arnold, allowed rescue crews onto his land. Surveyors estimated where the trapped men might be. A relatively narrow drill began cutting down through the ground before dawn on July 25. Its purpose was not escape. It was to find the chamber and provide air.

The narrow drill reached the mine. Rescuers heard knocking through the pipe. Somebody was alive.

It was an extraordinary signal, but it did not tell them whether all nine had survived. The opening was only a few inches wide. A person couldn't climb through it.

Meanwhile, water was still coming into the mine. Crews brought in pumps to bring the level down. They also sent air down the narrow borehole. The miners' survival depended on the water staying below their refuge and on the remaining air being breathable.

Now a much larger drill had to make a shaft wide enough for a rescue capsule. It had to pass through solid ground and reach the correct place, without making the flooding worse.

The drilling continued into July 26. Then the large drill bit broke underground.

For the men below, the noise of the approaching drill stopped. At the surface, workers tried to recover the broken bit and began a second route as a backup. Hours were lost. The rescuers eventually cleared the obstruction and continued.

The shaft did not reach the men that day. It took another night and most of the following day.

On July 27, the large drill finally broke through. Rescuers lowered a small two-way communication device and spoke to the miners. For the first time they could confirm that all nine were alive.

One man, foreman Randy Fogle, had chest pains. He was chosen to come up first.

A narrow metal cage was lowered through the new shaft on a cable. It was designed to carry one miner at a time. After more than seventy-seven hours underground, Fogle was lifted out shortly before one in the morning on July 28.

The capsule went down again. And again. By 2:45 a.m., the ninth man had reached the surface. All nine survived.

The story did not end at the rescue hole.

Investigators found that the available map of the abandoned Harrison mine had failed to show how far its workings actually extended. The planners believed they were hundreds of feet away from the water. In reality, the miners had reached the old mine itself.

After the rescue, investigators even located another old map in a museum's attic. But that map also failed to show the exact room where the breakthrough happened.

The rescue had turned on accurate drilling. The disaster had begun because nobody had an accurate picture of the tunnels already underground.

Nine men came out through a hole made in the right place—after entering danger through a wall their map said should not have been there.
