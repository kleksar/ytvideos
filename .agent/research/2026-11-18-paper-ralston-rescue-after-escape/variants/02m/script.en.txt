How can someone escape a boulder that traps his arm for five days? And why is getting free only half the journey?

In April 2003, Aron Ralston went alone into Bluejohn Canyon in Utah. In one narrow passage, a boulder shifted beneath him. The rock, weighing roughly eight hundred pounds, pinned his right arm against the sandstone wall.

He pushed at the stone. He tried to chip pieces away. He used his climbing gear to make a pulley. Nothing moved it enough.

No one knew his exact route. A day hike became a night in the canyon, then another. On Tuesday, his water ran out. He recorded messages to his family on a small camera.

By Thursday, he believed he could not survive another night. He made the extraordinary decision to amputate his trapped arm with his pocket tool. After five days, he was finally free.

But he wasn't rescued.

Ahead lay a steep descent and miles of canyon. With one arm, exhausted and dehydrated, he rigged his rope and lowered himself some sixty feet. Then he walked.

He eventually encountered a family of hikers, who gave him food and water. A search helicopter found him in the canyon and flew him to hospital.

Days later, rescuers returned with lifting equipment to move the stone and recover his arm.

Ralston survived not when the boulder released him, but when he managed to get out of the canyon and reach other people. The decision that made the headlines was followed by hours of climbing, walking, and help from strangers.
