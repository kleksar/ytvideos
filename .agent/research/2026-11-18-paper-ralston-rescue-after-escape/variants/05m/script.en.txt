How do you leave a canyon when a boulder has pinned your arm for five days? Why didn't the search teams simply find you? And if you finally get loose, how do you reach safety with one hand?

On Saturday, April 26, 2003, Aron Ralston left his truck near the Utah desert, rode his bike toward the canyons, and continued on foot. He was twenty-seven, an experienced solo climber. He intended to return from this trip the same day.

Bluejohn Canyon is a narrow corridor cut through sandstone. In some places, you can touch both walls without stretching your arms. Ralston was moving past a large rock wedged between those walls when it rolled.

He dropped toward the canyon floor. The boulder struck one hand, shifted, and trapped his right arm against the rock wall. It weighed an estimated eight hundred pounds.

Ralston pulled. His hand would not move. He tried to chip away the sandstone with his small tool. He spent hours at it, but the rock was not going to disappear quickly enough. Next, he used climbing equipment to rig a makeshift pulley. The stone barely responded.

This was the first problem. The second was that no one knew precisely where he had gone.

He was used to traveling alone. This time, he had not left anyone his exact route. A search could begin only after people noticed he had failed to return, and the wilderness around him was vast.

On Sunday, Ralston started recording messages on a little video camera. He spoke to family members and left instructions for his possessions. That was not the only thing he did. He continued weighing the possibilities: someone might arrive, the rock might move, or he might have to free himself another way.

At night, the temperature fell. He wore shorts and a T-shirt, used his rope for warmth, and kept his pack close. He remained standing in the narrow gap because of the way the stone held his arm.

By Tuesday morning, his drinking water was gone.

He had climbing equipment but not the one thing that could shift hundreds of pounds of rock. He could hear no search party. And even if rescuers knew he was somewhere in the area, they could not see down into every slot canyon.

On Thursday morning, five days after the accident, Ralston concluded that waiting through another night was no longer possible. He amputated his trapped right arm using his pocket tool. It was an extreme act of self-rescue, not a technique anyone should try to imitate.

The stone was still there. This time, though, Ralston could move.

Now comes the part that a story about the boulder can easily leave out.

He was still in a remote canyon with serious injuries, no water, and a route that included a sheer drop. He secured his climbing rope and descended a wall of roughly sixty feet, working with one arm.

After that, he had to walk. In a later interview, Ralston described several hours of descending, climbing, and hiking before meeting anybody.

He eventually turned a corner and saw three people: a couple and their son, hiking in the canyon. They gave him water and food. For the first time since Saturday, he was no longer alone with the problem.

But the trail out still climbed steeply. In his weakened condition, Ralston doubted he could make it to the vehicles above. The family planned to send word for help.

Then a search helicopter came over the canyon. Rescuers flew him to a hospital, where he received treatment.

The search crews had been alerted on Thursday, the same day he managed to escape. A few days later, another team returned to Bluejohn. They brought hoists and a jack, shifted the boulder, and recovered the arm still trapped beneath it.

Ralston later used a prosthesis and returned to outdoor sports. His rescue wasn't one single moment of impossible courage. It was a series of real obstacles, and the final one required other people.
