How did America's first space station lose its protective covering on the way to orbit? Could a homemade sunshade save it? And why wasn't that enough?

In May 1973, NASA launched Skylab without a crew aboard. Sixty-three seconds after liftoff, part of its protective shield tore away.

The shield had two jobs: keep tiny space debris away and help protect the station from the Sun's heat. Its failure damaged the solar-power system too. One large solar wing was gone. The other was stuck shut.

The workshop reached temperatures that made it difficult to live and work inside. And without the missing electricity, its experiments could not run as planned.

NASA delayed the first crew while engineers prepared emergency repairs. Some designed a shade that folded into a small canister, passed through a scientific airlock, and opened outside the station like an umbrella.

On May 25, Pete Conrad, Paul Weitz, and Joe Kerwin arrived. An early attempt to free the solar wing failed.

Inside Skylab, the crew installed the parasol through that small opening. Temperatures began to fall. The station could now be occupied.

But the new shade couldn't produce electricity. The other repair still waited.

On June 7, Conrad and Kerwin went outside with a long cutting tool. They severed metal debris holding the solar wing. It moved only a little. They had to pull against a stuck hinge.

Then the panel suddenly opened, sending both astronauts tumbling against their safety tethers. They recovered, and the wing unfolded.

Skylab now had the cooling and the power it needed. The crew completed a twenty-eight-day mission. Two more crews later lived and worked there.

The sunshade saved the workshop from heat. The spacewalk saved its work. Together, they turned a damaged launch into a functioning space station.
