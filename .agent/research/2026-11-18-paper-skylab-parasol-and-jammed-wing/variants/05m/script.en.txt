How do you repair a space station that has lost its protection from the Sun? Why did the first repair fit through a hole only eight inches wide? And what problem forced two astronauts outside anyway?

On May 14, 1973, NASA launched Skylab, America's first space station. Nobody was aboard yet. Its first crew was supposed to follow the next day.

Just sixty-three seconds after liftoff, something went wrong. The cylindrical shield around the workshop tore loose. That shield was meant to protect against tiny debris and help keep sunlight from heating the station.

The damage didn't stop there. One of the workshop's two large solar wings was torn away. Metal from the broken shield trapped the other wing against the hull.

Skylab reached orbit, but it had lost the insulation that helped keep it cool and much of the electrical power it needed for science. The station existed. Whether anyone could use it was another question.

NASA held the astronauts on the ground while engineers worked out how to fix a machine already circling Earth.

They needed two different solutions. A new covering could stop the overheating. But nothing about a sunshade would free the solar wing.

For the first problem, engineer Jack Kinzler proposed something that sounded more suited to a garden than a spacecraft: a folding parasol.

Teams made a reflective sheet from layers of nylon, Mylar, and aluminum. It was roughly the size of a small room when opened, yet it had to travel folded inside a narrow case. Rather than send an astronaut across the outside of the station to fasten a giant sheet, they would push it through a scientific airlock and open it from inside.

The design had to be made, sewn, tested, folded, and packed while the station kept orbiting without its crew.

On May 25, Pete Conrad, Paul Weitz, and Joe Kerwin finally launched. When they approached Skylab, they saw the damage for themselves: exposed metal where the shield should have been and the solar wing held partly closed.

They tried a repair before settling in. Weitz leaned out of their Apollo spacecraft with a long tool to reach the trapped wing. The attempt failed. Even docking became difficult, and the crew eventually had to work on the docking equipment before getting a secure connection.

By then their first day had stretched to about twenty-two hours. The overheated workshop was not somewhere to sleep, so the men stayed in their Apollo cabin.

The next day, they checked the station, then brought out the folded parasol.

Imagine feeding a large reflective roof through an opening smaller than a dinner plate. The astronauts pushed the packed shade outward and extended its rods until the fabric spread across the Sun-facing part of the station.

It wasn't perfectly positioned. It didn't need to be. Temperatures started falling, and over the following days the workshop became a place where people could live and work.

That solved half the emergency.

Skylab's scientific equipment required electricity. The station had other solar panels, but the missing and trapped wing left far too little power for all the planned experiments. A cool workshop with no power to use its laboratory was not the mission NASA had built.

Back on Earth, astronauts and engineers practiced another repair underwater. They designed a cutter that could reach a strip of metal holding the remaining solar wing. The tool sat at the end of a pole about twenty-five feet long.

On June 7, Conrad and Kerwin went outside Skylab. Weitz stayed inside to help.

Working in their spacesuits, they assembled the long pole and reached the metal strip. The cutter finally sliced through it.

For a moment, it looked as if that would do the job. The wing moved a little, then stopped. Its hinge was still stuck.

So they attached a rope and pulled against it. When the hinge suddenly gave way, the astronauts were thrown away from their positions. Their safety tethers held them.

They recovered. And the solar wing opened.

Power flowed back into the workshop. Now the two separate repairs had finally made one usable station: a shade to keep it cool and a wing to power its experiments.

Conrad, Weitz, and Kerwin remained in space for twenty-eight days. They studied the Sun, photographed Earth, and monitored what weeks in weightlessness did to their bodies. Two more crews later worked aboard Skylab.

The station hadn't survived because its original design worked perfectly. It survived because people on the ground built a folding roof, and two astronauts finished a repair that the roof alone could never do.
