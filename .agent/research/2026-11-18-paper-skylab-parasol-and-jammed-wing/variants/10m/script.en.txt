What would you do if your space station arrived in orbit with its protective covering torn off? How could engineers send it a replacement roof through an opening only eight inches across? And after that roof worked, what could still prevent the station from doing its job?

Skylab was meant to be America's first home and laboratory in orbit. It was a big cylindrical workshop built from a modified rocket stage, with room for astronauts to spend weeks studying the Sun, Earth, and their own bodies.

On May 14, 1973, a Saturn V rocket carried Skylab into space. There were no astronauts aboard. They would come separately in an Apollo spacecraft.

About sixty-three seconds into the launch, instruments indicated damage. Aerodynamic forces had torn loose the thin shield surrounding Skylab's main workshop.

That shield was not simply a cover to make the station look tidy. It helped protect the hull from tiny particles and, just as importantly now, shielded it from the Sun's heat.

The broken parts struck the workshop's solar-power equipment. One large solar wing was lost. A strip of metal from the wreckage caught the other wing, leaving it partly folded against the body.

The launch vehicle had done its main job: the station was in orbit. But Skylab now had two different failures, and either one could ruin the mission.

Without its thermal shield, sunlight heated the workshop to temperatures unsafe for normal living and work. Food, medicines, and scientific materials were at risk. Without both large solar wings, the station also lacked much of the electricity its experiments needed.

NASA could not simply bring it back to Earth and replace the parts. The workshop was already orbiting overhead.

There were astronauts ready to fly. Instead of launching them immediately, NASA delayed their departure and set teams on the ground to work.

The first challenge was heat. Someone had to put a new barrier between the Sun and a station whose original barrier was gone.

Engineers explored several designs. One possible shade would stretch across poles mounted outside. That meant complex work by astronauts in spacesuits, close to a damaged structure whose exact condition they had yet to see.

Jack Kinzler, a NASA engineer, suggested another approach. Fold an enormous reflective covering so tightly that it could be pushed through one of the station's scientific airlocks. Once outside, telescoping rods would open it, much like a parasol.

It was a remarkably domestic answer to a problem hundreds of kilometers above Earth: make a giant folding sunshade.

The material had to be light enough to travel, reflective enough to stop heating, and strong enough to unfold without tearing. Teams used layers of nylon, Mylar, and aluminum. Workers sewed sections of this reflective fabric while other engineers built rods and tested how they fitted inside a long narrow canister.

Opened, the shade measured roughly twenty-two by twenty-four feet. Packed, it was designed to pass through an eight-inch opening.

That was the clever part. The astronauts would not have to carry the entire sheet around the outside of the station. They could feed the folded package outward from inside and extend it over the Sun-facing surface.

But NASA was preparing more than a shade. It also needed tools to free the solar wing. The two problems were related by the same accident but could not be repaired by the same device.

On May 25, ten days after the originally planned crew launch, commander Pete Conrad, Paul Weitz, and Joe Kerwin finally left Earth.

During their approach to Skylab, the crew made a close inspection from their Apollo spacecraft. The images confirmed the damage. The workshop's exterior was exposed, one wing had disappeared, and the remaining wing was caught by metal debris.

Before even entering their new home, they tried to release it.

Weitz made a stand-up spacewalk from the Apollo spacecraft while Conrad maneuvered close. With a long cutting tool, Weitz tried to reach the obstruction. The position was difficult, and the tool did not solve the problem.

The crew still needed to dock. But their first attempts did not produce a secure connection. They had to work on the docking hardware before they could finally connect Apollo to Skylab.

By the time they finished, their first day had stretched to around twenty-two hours. And they still could not comfortably sleep in the overheated workshop. They stayed in the Apollo cabin.

This is where the story changed. The men had reached their space station. Reaching it did not mean they had a usable place to live.

On the following day, they carefully checked the air and conditions inside. The feared heat-related contamination did not prevent entry, and they moved ahead with the emergency sunshade.

The folded canopy was taken to the scientific airlock. Through a hole narrower than a large dinner plate, they pushed the packed device outward. Then they extended its supporting rods, opening a wide patch of reflective fabric above the station.

The shade did not sit perfectly flat. But it covered enough of the exposed surface.

The temperature began falling. Over the next days, the workshop cooled toward conditions that could support a crew. The first improvised repair had worked.

It is tempting to end here: a station left uninhabitable by a damaged launch gets a new roof and becomes habitable. But it still could not complete the mission for which it was built.

The shade stopped sunlight. It did not generate a single watt of electricity.

Skylab had additional solar panels on its telescope assembly, but the workshop's lost and trapped wings left it short of the power needed for its full scientific program. The astronauts could live there; they could not run everything as intended.

On Earth, a second team studied the photographs, the crew's descriptions, and the thin metal strip that appeared to be holding the remaining wing.

The repair demanded a much longer reach and much better control than the first attempt from Apollo.

Astronaut Rusty Schweickart and other specialists rehearsed procedures in a huge underwater training tank, where buoyancy helped approximate some movements of a spacewalk. They practiced extending a cutting tool toward a metal strap and pulling a heavy structure without being able to stand on the ground.

They built a pole out of sections, about twenty-five feet long when assembled. The cutting head was meant to catch the metal strip, cut it, and leave the wing free to move.

That sounds straightforward until you imagine handling a pole taller than a two-story building while floating outside a space station, in stiff gloves, with no solid ground beneath your feet.

On June 7, nearly two weeks into their mission, Conrad and Kerwin opened the station's airlock. Weitz remained inside.

The two astronauts moved to the trapped solar wing. Kerwin helped assemble the long tool, passing its sections out while Conrad positioned himself near the obstruction.

They brought the cutter around the thin metal strap and worked until it finally parted.

The wing started to open.

Then it stopped.

The metal strap had not been the only problem. The hinge was stuck too. Cutting away the debris had removed one obstacle but exposed another.

The astronauts shifted to the next part of their plan. They used a rope and their own pull to force the hinge to release.

When it gave way, it did not move gently. The sudden release sent both men tumbling from their working positions. Their safety tethers kept them attached to Skylab.

They recovered, returned to the repair, and watched the solar wing unfold.

From inside the station, Weitz could photograph parts of the operation. Conrad had decided not to carry outside cameras because the work was already complicated enough, which is why there is no complete movie of that spacewalk.

What mattered inside Skylab was not the photograph. It was the electrical power.

With the wing deployed, the workshop could run the experiments it had carried into orbit. The repair had taken a three-hour-and-twenty-five-minute spacewalk, but the result changed the rest of the mission.

The crew could now do the work they came to do. They studied the Sun with their telescopes. They photographed Earth and carried out medical experiments to understand how weeks in weightlessness affected people.

Some experiments had been delayed. The astronauts adapted their schedule rather than pretend the first two weeks had gone exactly as planned.

On June 15, Weitz observed a solar flare through the station's instruments. During the mission, the crew also worked with Earth-observation equipment and gathered medical data that later helped plan exercise and meals for other long-duration crews.

On June 22, after twenty-eight days in space, Conrad, Weitz, and Kerwin returned to Earth. Their Apollo capsule splashed down in the Pacific. Skylab remained above them, ready for another crew.

The next crew installed a more permanent sunshade. They stayed for fifty-nine days. The final crew lived aboard for eighty-four. Neither of those missions could have followed the same plan if the first team had left the workshop overheated and short of power.

The accident had never been one simple broken part. It was a missing heat shield, a lost wing, a jammed wing, a shade to manufacture, and a difficult repair that revealed one more jam even after the first obstruction was cut.

And the rescue was never one magical invention. It took sewing and folding on Earth, a narrow opening in the station, repeated attempts, underwater rehearsals, a long cutter, and two astronauts pulling on a hinge that refused to move.

Skylab survived because the people trying to save it treated each new problem as a real problem, not as the end of the story. The folding sunshade made the station habitable. The freed solar wing finally made it a laboratory.
