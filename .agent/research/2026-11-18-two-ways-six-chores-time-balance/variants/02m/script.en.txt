Three chores each. I thought that was a fair way to split six jobs between two people. But on my little planning board, one of us was still working when the other had finished.

I made up six ordinary chores with fixed times, just to test the rule. In the kitchen: dishes take eight minutes, wiping counters takes three, and sorting a shelf takes six. That's seventeen minutes. In the living room: putting away shoes takes two, vacuuming takes five, and folding blankets takes four. That's eleven.

My scoreboard is simple: how many minutes until BOTH people finish? We start together, work equally fast, and never do two jobs at once.

Option A: split by room. I get three kitchen jobs. My helper gets three living-room jobs. Pick A or B before I show the second method.

The helper finishes at minute eleven. I'm still working for six more. Final time: seventeen minutes.

Option B: split by estimated effort, even if that means crossing rooms. I take the eight-minute dishes, the two-minute shoes, and the four-minute blankets. Fourteen minutes. My helper takes the other three, six and five. Also fourteen. Same six jobs. Now we finish together, three minutes earlier.

That looks like a win for B. But I left something out: changing rooms and resetting equipment. If each of us loses four minutes on that move, both B schedules take eighteen minutes. A stays at seventeen. The winner flips.

These are model minutes, not times measured in anyone's home. The real question isn't just how many boxes I tick. It's how long the work and the switching take. So next time I hear the apparently fair plan...
