What if the sensible thing to do with three deadlines is to ignore the earliest one? I have two perfectly reasonable ways to arrange the same morning. One saves two deadlines. The other saves only one. And the answer changes when I move a deadline on the calendar.

I start at nine, with three jobs written on cards. A slide deck needs forty minutes and is due at nine forty. An email needs twenty minutes and is due at nine fifty. A form needs another twenty minutes and is also due at nine fifty.

This is a paper model. The times are made up so I can check the logic. There's one worker—me—no breaks, no switching halfway through, and all three jobs are available at nine. I count one point only when a complete job is finished no later than its deadline. I don't get half a point for an unfinished slide.

I put the cards into two possible orders. A means shortest jobs first. B means earliest deadline first. Before I turn them over, which one would you choose?

A looks almost irresponsible. I leave the nine-forty slides untouched while I write the email. At nine twenty, that's one point. I complete the form at nine forty. Two points, with ten minutes left before either of those short jobs was due.

Now I finally start the slides. They take forty minutes, so the clock reads ten twenty when they're ready. Their deadline was nine forty. My result is two on time, one late.

For B, I return every card to the desk at nine. Nothing about the tasks changes. I begin with the presentation, because its deadline comes first. It is ready at nine forty. One point.

The email finishes at ten, ten minutes after its deadline. The form finishes at ten twenty, thirty minutes late. Two overdue jobs. B scores one out of three.

At first this feels like a trick. But there were eighty minutes of work waiting for me at nine, and two of the deadlines were just fifty minutes away. Doing the big job first used almost the entire useful window for the two smaller ones. The short jobs could both fit before nine fifty if I did them first.

Could I invent a third, magical order that makes all three on time? No. To finish the forty-minute slides by nine forty, I have to start them at nine, with no interruption. Then the other forty minutes of work cannot both be finished by nine fifty. The problem is bigger than a bad choice of queue.

So A wins my announced game: two deadlines rather than one. But it doesn't win every game. Under A, the slides are forty minutes late. Under B, the worst delay is thirty minutes. If the slides are the one thing that absolutely must arrive on time, B could be the sensible order, even though its score is lower.

I try one controlled change. Same three jobs, same durations, same nine o'clock start. I move only the email and form deadlines from nine fifty to ten twenty. Now B completes the slides at nine forty, the email at ten, and the form at ten twenty. Three on time. A still leaves the slides until last and scores two.

There is a clear reason for the reversal. The extra half hour doesn't make me faster. It simply gives the short jobs room to wait behind the presentation. My original deadline was the constraint that made the short-first plan useful.

There is another mistake I could make: call this a real experiment. I haven't timed myself writing slides, and a real email can take longer than I expect. A phone call might interrupt everything. The only result I've measured here is the arithmetic of the model.

What I can take into an actual morning is a better question. First I decide whether I'm trying to finish the greatest number of jobs, protect one important commitment, or limit the worst delay. Then I compare the orders against those deadlines. Without that choice, 'do the shortest thing first' and 'do the urgent thing first' are both incomplete advice.

And this is why, on this imaginary desk, I can get more work submitted on time by letting the earliest deadline wait.
