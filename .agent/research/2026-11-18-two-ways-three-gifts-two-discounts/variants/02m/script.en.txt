Three gifts. Two discounts. One sign promises something free. Which deal actually leaves more money in my pocket?

I'm buying three gifts, priced at twenty-four dollars, twenty-four dollars, and twelve dollars. The regular total is sixty. Both offers apply to all three items, and I'm buying exactly these gifts either way. No extra items, tax, or shipping in this model.

I can take A: twenty-five percent off the entire basket. Or B: buy two, get the cheapest one free. Before I show the totals, which would you choose?

I try A first. A quarter of sixty is fifteen. I pay forty-five dollars.

Now I reset the same basket and try B. The free item isn't one of the twenty-four-dollar gifts. It's the twelve-dollar one. I pay forty-eight dollars. A wins by three dollars.

But the big FREE sign isn't always the loser. I change the three prices to twenty, twenty, and twenty. The basket still costs sixty dollars before any discount. A still costs forty-five. B now removes a twenty-dollar item, so I pay forty. This time B wins by five.

The total price did not change. The cheapest item's share did.

Here's the shortcut I can actually use. With three eligible items, compare the price of the cheapest one with a quarter of the whole basket. More than a quarter? B wins. Less? A wins. Exactly a quarter? They tie.

That's a calculation, not a claim about every store. Some promotions exclude items or use different rules. And if I don't need all three gifts, I must compare only what I would actually buy.

So when another sale appears, I start with the question: how many gifts, and how many discounts?
