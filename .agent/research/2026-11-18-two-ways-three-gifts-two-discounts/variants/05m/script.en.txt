I have three gifts to buy, and two sale signs to choose from. One offers twenty-five percent off. The other promises a free gift. The strange part is that the very same sixty-dollar total can make either sign the winner.

I want to spend the least money on the three things I planned to buy. That's my only score. I'm not measuring shopping time or how excited a discount makes me feel. I'm comparing checkout totals in a simple model, with no tax or shipping. Both promotions cover the same three items, and I cannot combine them.

The first sign, A, takes twenty-five percent off every eligible item. The second, B, says buy two, get one free, with the cheapest eligible item free. The second rule matters. It is a real kind of restriction stores use, but not a promise about every sale.

My three gifts cost twenty-four, twenty-four, and twelve dollars. That's sixty altogether. If the word FREE is doing its job, I'm probably tempted by B. Which would you pick before seeing my receipts?

I start with A. I keep the whole basket, then remove one quarter of sixty dollars. That's fifteen dollars off. The total is forty-five.

I reset the basket, put all three gifts back at their original prices, and use B instead. Two of the items cost twenty-four dollars; the smallest one costs twelve. Under the announced rules, only that twelve-dollar gift is free. I pay forty-eight.

The first answer is simple: A wins by three dollars. But why? A rewards the value of the entire basket. B rewards the value of just one item. A free item can be a smaller discount than a percentage.

Now I make one change that might seem harmless. I still buy three gifts. Their regular total is still sixty dollars. This time each gift costs twenty.

Under A, nothing changes: twenty-five percent of sixty is fifteen, and I pay forty-five. Under B, the free gift is now worth twenty dollars. I pay forty. B wins by five dollars.

I've changed no total, no number of items, and no promotion. Only the spread of prices changed, and the winner flipped.

That gives me a better question than 'Which sign sounds bigger?' I should ask how much the cheapest item contributes to the basket.

I test a third set: twenty-five dollars, twenty dollars, and fifteen dollars. Once again the regular total is sixty. A removes fifteen. B removes the fifteen-dollar item. Both leave a forty-five-dollar bill. It's a tie.

Here's the exact rule behind those three outcomes. Let the full basket total be the number I can see at the register before discounts. The percent offer saves a quarter of that total. The free-item offer saves the price of the cheapest item. I only need to compare those two savings. If the cheapest item is above one quarter of the total, B wins. If it's below, A wins. If they're equal, neither wins.

That is useful because the word free doesn't tell me which price disappears. The eligibility terms do.

There's also a trap I can create for myself. Suppose I want only two twenty-four-dollar gifts. Under A, they cost thirty-six dollars. Under B, I must buy a third eligible gift to activate the offer. If that third gift costs twelve dollars and is free, I still spend forty-eight. I haven't saved money on what I needed; I've carried home an extra thing. For this comparison, the starting list is two needed gifts, not secretly three.

And the cashier might show a different result if the store excludes one item, caps the offer, or won't combine promotions. Some online stores even require me to put the free item in the cart myself. Those rules are inputs, not fine-print decorations. I'm not saying the discounts here are being offered by a particular store today.

I don't need to memorize three receipts. I need to name the amount I care about, read which items qualify, and compare two numbers: a quarter of the eligible basket, and the eligible item that would actually become free.

For my first three gifts, A won. For three equal-priced gifts, B won. For the third set, neither won. And when I only needed two, I shouldn't buy a third simply to feel I got a bargain.

Next time I see two bright sale signs, my first move is not to choose one. It's to make a list of the things I was already buying.
