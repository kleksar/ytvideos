Why did a village have to pay to remove a dangerous hillside after that hillside had already killed its children? And who eventually returned the money?

Aberfan was a coal-mining village in Wales. Above its homes and school stood enormous heaps of mining waste. One heap, called Tip Seven, had been built over an underground spring.

On the morning of October 21, 1966, the saturated tip began moving. By about a quarter past nine, a mass of coal waste had crashed through houses and Pantglas Junior School.

People from the village and beyond dug through the debris. Inside the school, a dinner lady named Nansi Williams shielded five children. They survived. She did not.

The landslide killed 144 people, including 116 children. It was their last school day before the holiday.

An inquiry blamed the National Coal Board, the public organisation responsible for the mine. But the story did not end with its finding.

Other waste heaps still stood above Aberfan. Families wanted them removed. Donations had poured into a disaster fund to help the community recover. Yet in 1968, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds from that fund went towards clearing the remaining tips.

The people who had lost so much were made to contribute to removing the danger.

Decades passed. In 1997, a government minister announced that the contribution should never have been required. The government repaid the original one hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

The tip was gone. The money came back. Neither decision could bring the children home. And that is why the fight over a relief fund belongs in the story of a disaster that began on a hillside.
