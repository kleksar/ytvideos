Why did a Welsh village have to help pay for the removal of the very coal waste that had buried its school? Who was responsible for the collapse? And why did it take thirty years to return the money?

In 1966, the houses of Aberfan stood below the waste heaps of a coal mine. The village depended on mining. But the waste was not being stored safely away from the people who lived there. Seven huge tips had accumulated on the hillside. The newest active heap was known as Tip Seven.

On Friday, October 21, children went to Pantglas Junior School for the final day before their half-term break. Above the school, the ground was already changing.

The tip was sitting over an underground spring. Water weakened the mass of loose mining material. British Geological Survey records show that it had begun to move before seven that morning.

The material did not simply roll down as dry stones. It turned into a fast-moving flow of black spoil. It broke through a water main near a disused canal, crossed a railway embankment, and reached the village around a quarter past nine.

Eighteen houses were destroyed. So was the junior school, along with part of a neighbouring secondary school. One hundred and forty-four people died, including one hundred and sixteen children.

Neighbours, miners, emergency workers and volunteers dug through the wreckage. The last child brought out alive was rescued by eleven that morning.

One small part of that day survived in the testimony of the children. Nansi Williams was collecting school dinner money when the flow struck. She covered five pupils with her body. All five lived; she did not. In 2026, one of those children, Paul Harries, spoke publicly about what she had done.

The rescuers could not solve the question left above the village. Other tips were still on the hill. Had the same danger really gone away?

There had been warnings before the disaster. Local officials had raised fears about coal slurry behind the schools years earlier. The subsequent official tribunal examined those warnings, the condition of the ground and the decisions of the National Coal Board, which ran the mines.

Its conclusion was direct: the blame lay with the Board.

This was not an inquiry into an ordinary mountain that had somehow slipped by chance. The spoil had been deliberately deposited over years. Local warnings had been recorded before the collapse. The question was why the people responsible for the site had failed to understand and act on what water could do to the pile. The survivors were being given an answer to the first question, but not yet an answer to the second: what would happen to the heaps that were still there?

Donations came from across the world. An Aberfan Disaster Fund was set up to support families and the devastated community. But the argument about removing the remaining tips turned into another struggle.

The families wanted a hillside they could live beneath. The fund existed because the hillside had already failed them. Yet in 1968, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds from that fund was contributed to the work of removing the tips.

The money intended to help the people of Aberfan was used to help pay for their safety.

It took until July 1997 for a British government minister to make the admission in Parliament: that contribution should never have been required. He announced a grant returning the same nominal amount, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

The people who had fought to remove the danger had won that part of the battle. The refund came three decades later.

And it was a refund of the original number of pounds, not what that money could have been worth thirty years earlier. Later Welsh funding addressed that loss in value. None of these payments was a price for the people killed. They concerned a separate, specific wrong: donations intended to help a grieving village had been drawn into paying for work the village should not have had to finance.

Aberfan is not just a story about a hillside collapsing in a single morning. It is about the decisions that made the collapse possible, the people who saved lives, and a community that had to keep demanding safety after the cameras left.

And the lesson did not end in 1997. Wales passed a new law on disused mine and quarry tips in 2025, with a dedicated safety authority planned to take responsibility in April 2027.

That law cannot undo October 1966. But the reason for it is written in what the families of Aberfan had to ask for, twice: first, take the danger away. Then, give back the money they should never have paid.
