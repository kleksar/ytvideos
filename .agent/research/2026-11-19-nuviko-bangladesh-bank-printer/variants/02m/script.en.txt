How do you steal eighty-one million dollars from a bank without opening its vault? And why did a broken printer help hide the theft?

In February 2016, Bangladesh's central bank kept part of its foreign reserves in an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Moving that money normally required authenticated payment messages between banks.

The attackers had already broken into computers at Bangladesh Bank. They could send instructions that looked as though the bank itself had authorized them.

On Thursday, February fourth, thirty-five payment orders reached New York. Together, they requested nine hundred and fifty-one million dollars.

Most of those orders were stopped. But five passed through, moving one hundred and one million dollars toward banks in Asia.

Back in Dhaka, the machine that printed records of these transfers wasn't producing its usual reports. Investigators later found malicious software had interfered with the reporting system. Staff struggled to see what had happened while the money was moving.

One transfer, for twenty million dollars, went to Sri Lanka. A misspelled word in the recipient's name prompted questions, and that payment was reversed.

The other eighty-one million went to accounts in the Philippines. Much of the money was moved on through the casino system before it could be frozen.

This wasn't a hack of New York's Federal Reserve computers or of the SWIFT network itself. The criminals had used a compromised bank's trusted access.

In 2018, American prosecutors accused a North Korean-linked hacking conspiracy of the robbery. That was an allegation in a criminal case, not a verdict against everyone involved.

The silence of a printer helped conceal the first hours. The spelling error helped recover one payment. Neither saved the eighty-one million.

A court in Bangladesh ordered the money seized in 2025. But an order was not the same as getting it back. In 2026, investigators were still seeking to finish their report.

The vault never needed to open. The payment instructions were enough.
