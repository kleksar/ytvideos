Why did the biggest attempted bank transfer robbery of 2016 begin with a silent office printer? How did most of the money stay safe while eighty-one million dollars escaped? And what happened when investigators tried to get it back?

Bangladesh Bank is the country's central bank. It holds foreign currency reserves, including dollars in an account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Those dollars aren't stored as bundles of cash in a room in Dhaka. They move when the account holder sends the correct banking instructions.

For international payments, banks use a secure messaging service called SWIFT. That service carries orders; it isn't itself a giant vault full of everyone's money.

Before February 2016, attackers had penetrated computers inside Bangladesh Bank. According to American criminal investigators, their route included deceptive emails that introduced malware. They gained the ability to send apparently legitimate payment instructions from the bank's own systems.

They also needed somewhere to receive the money. Reuters later reported that four accounts at a Philippine bank had been opened months earlier and remained nearly inactive.

Then the orders arrived.

On Thursday, February fourth, thirty-five messages reached the New York Fed. They asked it to move nine hundred and fifty-one million dollars out of Bangladesh's account.

One day's burst of instructions stood out. Some payments were unusually large. Several destinations didn't look like the bank's normal business.

Thirty orders, worth roughly eight hundred and fifty million dollars, were held back. But five payments, totaling one hundred and one million, were processed.

That was the first split in the story: nearly a billion dollars requested, a much smaller sum transferred, and an enormous theft still under way.

Inside Bangladesh Bank, a printer normally produced records of the payment messages. On Friday, staff found it wasn't printing. Reuters reported that malware had interfered with the bank's reporting software, delaying the discovery of the fraudulent activity. When workers eventually recovered the records, they found instructions they hadn't knowingly sent.

There was another delay. Friday and Saturday were the local weekend in Bangladesh. New York's weekend was Saturday and Sunday. Questions had to move across banks and time zones while the funds were already on their way.

Of the five processed transfers, one sent twenty million dollars toward a purported foundation in Sri Lanka. A spelling mistake in the recipient's name drew scrutiny from intermediary banks. That transfer was reversed.

But the typo did not stop the entire robbery. It affected that payment. The other four, totaling eighty-one million dollars, had already gone to accounts at Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation in the Philippines.

The money didn't sit there waiting for a recall. Investigators traced it through the Philippine banking and casino system. Casino transactions made the trail harder to follow, and only part of the eighty-one million was later recovered.

The distinction matters: the Federal Reserve Bank of New York said there was no evidence that its own computers had been breached. Its statement said the instructions passed the normal SWIFT authentication checks. SWIFT's network had not simply been cracked open either. The criminals had used access stolen from a bank trusted to send messages.

In September 2018, United States prosecutors charged a North Korean programmer, alleging that a North Korean-backed group had carried out the Bangladesh theft along with other cyberattacks. That attribution came from an indictment and investigation. A charge is not the same thing as a conviction.

SWIFT announced a customer security programme in May 2016, addressing banks' local security and detection of abnormal payment behaviour. The institution used to convey orders could no longer assume that an authenticated message meant an honest employee had sent it.

The money left a second problem long after the printers were repaired.

In September 2025, a court in Bangladesh ordered confiscation of eighty-one million dollars connected with the Philippine bank. Officials said the order had been sent to the relevant parties. They did not say the money had arrived back in Bangladesh.

By September 2026, the domestic investigation report had been postponed for the ninety-eighth time. A court order and a finished recovery were still two very different things.

The robbery didn't require someone to break into the New York Fed. It required a trusted bank to unknowingly speak with the attacker's voice.

Most of those requests were stopped. One badly spelled instruction was reversed. Four others reached their destination.

And almost ten years later, Bangladesh was still dealing with the cost of those four messages.
