The enormous front of a passenger ferry tore off in a storm. On the bridge, the indicator for its locks could still show green.

The ship was Estonia, crossing the Baltic Sea in September 1994 with almost a thousand people aboard. Its bow had a huge hinged cover. Vehicles drove through an inner ramp behind it, onto a wide car deck.

Heavy waves struck that cover. Its fastenings failed, and the moving cover pulled the ramp open. Water poured across the vehicle deck. It spread sideways, making the ferry lean so sharply that passengers struggled to climb the stairs.

Crew members had reported metallic banging. But the green lamp was linked to the positions of the side locks, not directly to the cover. It could not tell the officers that the enormous steel front was already coming away.

As the ferry leaned further, windows and doors higher up met the sea and broke. Water entered more spaces. People tried to reach the open deck, but the time available was desperately short. Estonia sank. Of the 989 people aboard, only 137 survived.

Years later, footage revealed damage on the side of the wreck. Had something struck the ship? Investigators from three countries surveyed the seabed, recovered the bow ramp, and examined the damage. In December 2025, they concluded that the side opening came from contact with the seabed. The bow failure remained the explanation.

The deeper finding was that weaknesses in the structure had escaped inspection for years. A reassuring signal had never been proof that the bow itself was safe. And the question the accident left behind was painfully simple: what could that green lamp actually see?
