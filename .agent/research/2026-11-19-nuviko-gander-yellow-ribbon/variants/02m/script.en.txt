Where would you put more than six thousand people if they suddenly arrived in a town of ten thousand? And how would you get them home when the skies were closed?

On September 11, 2001, the United States closed its airspace after the terrorist attacks. Passenger jets crossing the Atlantic needed somewhere else to land. Thirty-eight were sent to Gander, a small town in Newfoundland, Canada. They brought roughly 6,600 passengers and crew.

Landing the planes solved only the first problem. Officials still had to work out security, customs and transport. People waited aboard aircraft while arrangements were made. Nobody knew exactly when normal flights could resume.

Then the town became the rescue plan. Schools, churches and community halls became places to sleep. Residents delivered meals, bedding and clean clothes. Local pharmacies helped stranded travelers with prescriptions. Free phones let people contact families they had not been able to reach.

Some officials were initially cautious about inviting unknown passengers into private homes. According to Canada's own account, many locals welcomed them anyway.

The visitors stayed for days. As air travel reopened, the planes eventually departed. But the story did not end at the airport. Passengers kept in touch with their hosts, and some came back to visit. Lufthansa even renamed an aircraft Gander-Halifax in thanks to the communities that helped.

Those thirty-eight aircraft arrived because of an act of terror. What the strangers found on the ground was an answer built from beds, buses, meals and open doors. Gander could not reopen the sky. It could make sure people had somewhere to go while they waited.
