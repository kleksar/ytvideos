How did a town of about ten thousand people look after more than six thousand unexpected visitors? Why couldn't the travelers simply board another flight? And what did the town receive in return?

On September 11, 2001, passenger planes were flying across the Atlantic toward North America. Then terrorists attacked the United States, and American airspace was closed. Aircraft already over the ocean could not simply stop in midair. Canadian authorities told some flights to turn back and sent others toward airports in Canada.

This was Operation Yellow Ribbon. Across Canada, 224 diverted flights brought more than 33,000 passengers. Thirty-eight of those aircraft landed at Gander, Newfoundland. The town had about ten thousand residents. Its airport suddenly held enough unexpected visitors to transform daily life.

An arrival is not the same as a welcome. Planes could be parked on the ground, but their passengers still needed to pass security and customs. Officials were also working out transport and accommodation while the threat of further attacks remained uncertain. The travelers spent long periods waiting inside the aircraft. Even once they could leave, the United States was not ready to receive ordinary flights.

This is where the story changes. The airport could take the planes; the town would have to take the people.

Schools, churches and community halls opened as temporary sleeping places. Volunteers found meals, bedding, clothing and toiletries. Canadian military personnel at Gander helped with equipment, accommodation and transportation. Local pharmacies provided needed prescriptions, while free public phones helped visitors contact family. These were practical answers to real problems. A traveler without luggage could not simply buy several days of clothes from an airplane seat. Someone who needed medication could not wait for a departure time that nobody knew.

But there was another obstacle: trust. Authorities did not know who was on every plane when the emergency began. Gander's mayor, Claude Elliott, later remembered trying to discourage residents from bringing strangers into their homes. Many residents went ahead and invited them anyway. Caution and generosity existed in the same town at the same time.

The people getting off those aircraft were not arriving on holiday. They were watching confusing and frightening reports from a country where some had relatives or friends. A Canadian aviation inspector remembered a briefing at Gander where a young pilot, speaking about the attacks, repeatedly broke down because he had lost friends at the World Trade Center. The task of finding a bed was inseparable from the task of explaining why everyone was stranded.

The visitors remained for several days while the aviation system was slowly restored. Reopening airspace required new security rules as well as airports ready to accept flights. The departure of the thirty-eight planes was therefore not one triumphant moment but the end of a long series of small problems solved by officials, crews and residents.

And then came the part that did not have to happen. Former passengers continued contacting their hosts. Some returned to Newfoundland. Lufthansa renamed an aircraft Gander-Halifax to recognize the help offered in both places. Years later the story became the musical Come From Away, and the September 11 Memorial recorded the continuing friendships and gifts of thanks.

There is no need to pretend that this kindness undid the attacks. It did not. Nor did a small town rescue every displaced passenger in Canada. Many other communities took in diverted flights. But at Gander, the answer to a seemingly impossible logistical problem was thousands of ordinary decisions: a hall unlocked, a bed made, a meal served, a phone made available.

The planes needed a runway. The passengers needed people. For several days, Gander provided both.
