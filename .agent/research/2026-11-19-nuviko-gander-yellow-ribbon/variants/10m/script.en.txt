What happens when a town with about ten thousand residents has to host more than six thousand strangers? How do you move that many people out of parked aircraft when nobody knows whether another attack is coming? And what remains once they finally fly away?

The date was September 11, 2001. Commercial aircraft were crossing the Atlantic toward the United States. Then hijacked planes struck the World Trade Center and the Pentagon, and the United States closed its airspace. A jet halfway across the ocean could not obey an order to stop flying. It needed a runway, fuel and people on the ground who could take responsibility for everyone aboard.

Canada was suddenly part of the emergency. Officials in Ottawa faced hundreds of approaching flights. They ordered some aircraft with enough fuel to return toward Europe, and diverted others into Canadian airports. The system was called Operation Yellow Ribbon. A Transport Canada account records 224 diverted aircraft carrying more than 33,000 passengers. The numbers represent people, but they also represent a chain of tasks that did not exist that morning: keeping track of every plane, checking who was aboard, finding parking, arranging customs and deciding where people would sleep.

Gander, on the island of Newfoundland, had an airport suited to Atlantic aviation. But its town had only about ten thousand residents. Thirty-eight unexpected passenger flights landed there, bringing roughly 6,600 passengers and crew. The airport could put aircraft on the ground. It did not have thousands of hotel rooms waiting for their occupants.

In Ottawa, Canada's transport minister had not even been at his headquarters when the first news arrived. Staff were dealing with an unrelated labor dispute. Once the scale of the attacks became clear, workers abandoned the picket lines and went back to help with the response. The national aviation situation center became the hub for calls between airports, security agencies and the people deciding which flights could move. Those decisions would determine what happened in Gander.

At smaller Canadian airports, the arriving aircraft were parked away from the terminals. Some lined up along available paved space. Officials faced more than a passenger-counting problem. They did not know whether every incoming plane was safe. They also had to meet customs and security requirements. Passengers waited aboard while the authorities prepared to receive them. Meanwhile, rumors and incomplete reports from the United States made it difficult for crews to give reassuring answers. Getting people onto the ground was the first rescue. Getting them off the planes was another.

Then came a question no air traffic controller could answer: where would they all sleep?

The number of visitors was enormous relative to the town. A nearby hotel might have taken a few families. It could not take whole aircraft loads. Public buildings were the next answer. Schools, churches and community halls became temporary dormitories. People brought blankets, bedding, food, clean clothes and toiletries. The Canadian military at Gander supplied logistical and humanitarian assistance, including equipment, accommodation and transport. This was not an airport simply waving passengers through a door. It was a community rebuilding its ordinary spaces for an emergency.

Even a successful first night could have failed the next morning. Travelers had come with the luggage, clothes and medicine they expected to need for an ordinary flight. Their bags were not an emergency kit for several days on an unfamiliar island. The September 11 Memorial records that area pharmacies filled prescriptions without charge. Banks of free public telephones let passengers call home. The same record describes meals, showers and places to rest. Those details matter because an open door without medication or the means to contact family would have solved only part of the problem.

There was also a more uncomfortable question. In the first hours, nobody could simply assume that all the people landing were harmless. Canadian officials had to take the possibility of further hijackings seriously. Gander's mayor, Claude Elliott, later recalled trying to discourage locals from taking unknown passengers into private homes. Many residents disregarded that warning and invited them in anyway. The warning was real, and so was the welcome. Neither should be erased to make the story easier.

At an aviation briefing in Gander, the size of the tragedy became painfully clear. A Canadian civil aviation inspector later recalled a young aircraft commander speaking to other crew members about the attacks. The pilot had lost friends in the World Trade Center and repeatedly broke down while speaking. The people sitting in those parked planes had been physically carried to safety, but some were receiving news of deaths, missing relatives or a homeland in crisis. No pile of blankets could resolve that. Still, a bed, a telephone and somebody willing to listen were things the town could actually provide.

Now imagine the second day. The aircraft are still there. The emergency shelters still need feeding and cleaning. People still need medicine. Their original tickets cannot tell them when they will leave. They need a way to communicate with relatives and with crews. Volunteers cannot treat it like a one-hour welcome party because the system is not reopening to its old schedule on demand. The buildings that normally serve one community must continue serving two groups of people at once.

The national problem also continued. Transport Canada had to design new aviation security arrangements before commercial traffic could resume safely. The officials wrote emergency rules under pressure, coordinating with their American counterparts. A phone number used for aviation questions was mentioned publicly and became overwhelmed with calls. Some came from airlines; others from families trying to find passengers. The difficulty of reopening the sky explains why the emergency in Gander lasted days after the aircraft had safely landed.

Gander was not the only community involved. The operation distributed aircraft among airports across Canada, including Halifax and Vancouver. Other towns and cities also housed travelers. Even within Newfoundland, places beyond Gander faced major unexpected arrivals. Telling this story as if one town handled the whole continent would miss the scale of the operation. Gander is the most memorable window into a much bigger response.

When airspace began reopening, the departures could happen. They were not instantaneous. People who had arrived as anonymous passengers left after days of being someone's guests. The original goal was simply to keep people safe until travel resumed. Nobody needed to promise a lifelong friendship to achieve that. The immediate success was more basic: people had landed, eaten, rested and eventually found a way onward.

But the consequences did continue. Travelers kept in contact with people they met. Some returned to Newfoundland years later. Lufthansa renamed one of its aircraft Gander-Halifax to thank the two places that assisted its passengers. And the story of the stranded travelers and their hosts later inspired the stage musical Come From Away. The National September 11 Memorial recorded acts of gratitude and lasting connections long after the flights left.

A historical account can turn those facts into a sentimental legend if it is careless. Not every traveler felt the same way. Not every host could make the same sacrifice. We do not know what every passenger thought or did. The people who died in the attacks did not come back because strangers helped elsewhere. This was a response to catastrophe, not its undoing.

Yet it answers the questions we began with. The authorities handled the aircraft, security and eventual return to flight. The community handled the daily needs of thousands of people whose plans had collapsed. And what remained was not just a musical or a renamed plane. It was a record of specific choices that strangers made for one another while the world around them had become frighteningly uncertain.

The thirty-eight planes needed an airport. The thousands of people inside them needed a place in someone else's life for a few days. Gander found room.
