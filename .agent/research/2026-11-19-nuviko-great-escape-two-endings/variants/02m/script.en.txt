How did seventy-six Allied airmen get out of a Nazi prison camp in one night—and why did only three reach safety?

In 1944, the prisoners of Stalag Luft Three were living behind fences and guard towers in what their captors hoped was an escape-proof camp. Six hundred men had helped prepare an extraordinary way out.

Their leader, Roger Bushell, organised three tunnels. The first was discovered. A second became a store for supplies. Work continued on the third, called Harry.

They dug roughly nine metres down and more than a hundred metres across. Wood from the prisoners' beds kept the sandy walls upright. They built a little railway to move soil and made forged papers for the men who hoped to escape.

On the night of March twenty-fourth, two hundred prisoners were ready. But the tunnel ended short of the trees, in view of a guard tower. They couldn't simply climb out in a line. Someone in the woods had to signal when the way was clear.

The delay cost them. Before dawn, a guard discovered the exit. Seventy-six men had made it through.

Then the escape changed from a feat of engineering into a manhunt. German authorities recaptured seventy-three. On Hitler's orders, the Gestapo murdered fifty of those recaptured men. Twenty-three survived in captivity. Only three escaped to safety.

Investigators later pursued the killers. The men who dug Harry had proved the camp could be escaped. But the most important part of the story happened after they emerged from the ground.

That is why the famous Great Escape has two endings: the tunnel worked, and fifty prisoners were murdered after it did.
