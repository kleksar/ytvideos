Why would prisoners build three tunnels beneath a camp designed to stop tunnelling? Why did their escape work for seventy-six men, but save only three? And what happened after the guards found the hole?

In March 1944, two hundred Allied airmen were waiting inside Hut 104 at the German prisoner-of-war camp Stalag Luft Three. Their civilian-looking clothes and forged documents were ready. Below the stove, hidden beneath the floor, a tunnel called Harry led towards a forest beyond the fence.

The plan had taken almost a year. The Germans had placed the camp on loose sand and raised the huts so that anyone digging beneath them might be seen. Squadron Leader Roger Bushell organised a much larger operation than one man sneaking out: hundreds of prisoners worked to give as many as two hundred a chance.

They called their tunnels Tom, Dick and Harry. Each needed a deep vertical shaft before it could cross under the fence. The tunnels went down about thirty feet. At that depth, digging became a problem of air, timber and transport, not just earth.

Men stripped wooden boards from their beds to hold the sandy walls upright. They made a rail-and-trolley system to carry excavated sand through a space barely wide enough for one person. Improvised pumps sent air down the passage. Milk tins became part of the ventilation system. This was a construction project carried out inside a guarded prison.

Every bucket of yellow sand also had to disappear. Prisoners concealed it in bags beneath their clothes and scattered small amounts around the compound. Other teams kept watch, made civilian clothing, forged identification papers and prepared maps and compasses for life outside.

Then the Germans found Tom in September 1943. The prisoners abandoned Dick as an escape route and used it for storage. Only Harry remained.

By the night of March twenty-fourth, Harry was ready. Two hundred names were on the list, with places determined by escape experience, language skills, work on the project and a draw. Some had convincing papers and could attempt train journeys. Others faced long routes on foot.

The first man emerged from Harry around half past ten. He found a terrible surprise. The exit stopped short of the protective trees, within sight of a guard tower. A steady procession would have exposed them all.

They improvised a signal. Someone watching from the woods pulled a rope when it was safe for the next man to emerge. Each journey through the tunnel now ended with a wait. An air-raid blackout and other interruptions made progress slower still.

Before dawn, a guard discovered the exit. Those still inside the hut destroyed forged documents and other evidence. Seventy-six men had gone out; the rest could not follow.

Outside, the men scattered across German-controlled Europe. The authorities mounted a huge manhunt. Seventy-three were caught. The three who reached safety were two Norwegian airmen, Per Bergsland and Jens Müller, and a Dutch airman, Bram van der Stok.

The fate of the others explains why this story cannot end at the fence. Twenty-three of the recaptured men remained prisoners. Fifty were murdered by the Gestapo following an order from Hitler. They were not killed in a battle at the tunnel exit.

After the war, investigators pursued those responsible and war-crimes proceedings followed. The breakout entered popular culture as a daring victory of invention over prison walls. Its engineering achievement was real. So was the fate of the men caught afterwards.

The question is not simply whether Harry worked. It did. The harder question is what freedom meant when the men who made it out could still be hunted down.
