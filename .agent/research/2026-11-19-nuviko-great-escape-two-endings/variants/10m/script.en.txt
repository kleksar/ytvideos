What does it take to build a railway beneath a prison camp without the guards finding it? Why did an escape planned for two hundred people let only seventy-six out? And why is the end of this famous escape so different from the ending most people remember?

The answers begin at Stalag Luft Three in German-occupied Europe during the Second World War. The prisoners were Allied airmen. Some had already tried to escape captivity, which made the camp a particularly difficult place to get out of. The huts stood clear of the ground. The earth below was loose, pale sand: hard to support while digging, and easy for a guard to recognise on someone's clothing.

The prisoners still planned a tunnel. Then they planned three.

In 1943, RAF Squadron Leader Roger Bushell, called Big X by the prisoners, organised a mass breakout. The goal was not merely to send the best runner over the fence. Hundreds would help, so that as many as two hundred could make their way into Germany and beyond. A successful departure from the camp was only the beginning. Once outside, each man would need a route, documents, clothing, food and a way to avoid capture.

The tunnels were called Tom, Dick and Harry. They began in separate huts. The men dug shafts down about thirty feet before turning towards the fence. That depth mattered: the deeper passage was harder for the camp's underground detection measures to find, but everything taken from it had to be raised a long way back to the surface.

Imagine the problem of moving a mound of sand without letting anyone see the mound. Every load carried out of the tunnel was evidence. Prisoners concealed sand in bags beneath their trousers and released small amounts while walking around the camp. The ground above looked ordinary only because other men were constantly making the evidence disappear.

At the digging face, the walls could collapse. The prisoners took timber from their own beds and used it for the tunnel supports. The passage was about two feet square, so workers could barely move inside it. They built a small railway and trolley to transport men and sand. More than one person could work on the project, but they could not simply pass each other in the narrow earth.

They also needed air. A long, low passage with men working at the far end could not rely on what drifted in through its entrance. The prisoners made primitive pumping equipment, and empty milk tins helped form ducts to move air along the tunnels. They improvised lighting and found ways to power it. What appeared above ground as a row of huts concealed an underground construction site.

These were not the only teams at work. Lookouts watched the guards. Other men made civilian-looking clothes, forged identity documents, prepared maps and built compasses. Some materials were borrowed or stolen; some came from ordinary prison supplies. A tunnel without all that supporting work would have delivered men straight into hostile territory with little chance of travelling far.

Then, in September 1943, German guards found Tom. The men had already invested months of labour. They could not recover it by simply digging a few feet in another direction. Dick was no longer used as an escape route and instead held materials. The effort concentrated on Harry, the remaining passage.

As winter ended, the plan faced another question: who would leave? Hundreds had worked, yet only two hundred were chosen for the attempt. Some places went to men with escape experience or German-language skills; others reflected contribution to the work, and some were allocated by drawing lots. The plan was to give men a head start into an occupied continent, not a guarantee that every worker would walk to safety.

On the moonless night of March twenty-fourth, 1944, those selected gathered inside Hut 104. They waited above the concealed entrance to Harry. Among them were men who had spent months making the operation possible, and others whose part now was simply to get through the tunnel and start travelling.

The first escapee reached the surface around half past ten. The line had been intended to end beneath the protection of trees. Instead, the exit was several feet short of the woods. A nearby watchtower could see the uncovered stretch. After all that surveying and digging, the last few feet of the journey threatened the entire operation.

They could not emerge as fast as the trolley carried them. Someone beyond the exit watched the guards and used a rope to signal when each man could move. That meant the next man could be ready beneath the ground while the one ahead was still waiting for darkness or a safe moment to cross. The tunnel had worked. The exit now controlled the speed.

The operation was further delayed by an air-raid blackout. The account recorded by later histories also describes a partial collapse. Both problems mattered because a night has a fixed length. Every interruption meant another person would still be inside when daylight arrived.

Before dawn, a guard discovered the escape. The men waiting in the hut began destroying papers and other incriminating supplies. They knew how much evidence the preparations had produced. Only seventy-six had made it out of Harry. The intended two hundred had become seventy-six in a matter of hours.

The story could end there and still be a remarkable piece of engineering: a large hidden tunnel through sand, wood stolen from beds, ventilation made from containers, a trolley railway, false identities and a guarded exit. But that would leave out the part that decided the men's lives.

The escapees scattered. Some intended to use trains; others tried to travel on foot. Crossing German-controlled Europe with fabricated papers was a separate undertaking from crawling a tunnel. Checkpoints, police searches and the difficulty of finding a safe route made the escape far from over.

Roger Bushell was among the men who got out. He and fellow escapee Bernard Scheidhauer were caught days later at a railway station. Two Norwegian airmen, Per Bergsland and Jens Müller, managed to reach safety, as did Dutch airman Bram van der Stok. The other seventy-three were recaptured.

Twenty-three of the captured men survived in detention. Fifty were murdered by the Gestapo on Hitler's orders. The killings were carried out after recapture, away from the tunnel. This was not a case of fifty men falling during a chase across the camp fence. The crime happened when they were already prisoners again.

That distinction matters. Before the breakout, a captured airman could expect to remain a prisoner of war. For fifty of the escapees, that protection was deliberately violated. When news reached Britain, the murders caused outrage. After the war, investigators pursued Gestapo personnel involved in the killings, and some faced war-crimes trials and punishment.

One of the men who had helped prepare the escape, Australian airman Paul Brickhill, had not joined the tunnel procession because of claustrophobia. He survived and later wrote a book about the operation. That book helped make the Great Escape a world-famous story, and a Hollywood film followed in 1963.

The film's motorcycle chase is one of the best-known images associated with the escape, although it was not an episode from these men's actual breakout. The real details need no substitute: prisoners cut up their own beds to shore a tunnel, carried sand out under their clothes, passed forged papers along a line, and waited in darkness for a signal from the trees.

The achievement deserves to be remembered. So do the prisoners who did not make it home. Three got away. Twenty-three were returned to captivity. Fifty were murdered.

The Great Escape was both a successful breakout and a terrible failure of the hope that getting beyond the fence meant safety. The two facts belong in the same story, because leaving the camp and surviving the pursuit turned out to be two very different things.
