How can lighting a second fire save someone from a wildfire? And why did the men running away have almost no chance?

In August 1949, fifteen American smokejumpers parachuted into a remote canyon in Montana. Their job was to stop a forest fire before it grew. A local fire guard joined them. Sixteen firefighters were now on the ground.

The team headed downhill toward the Missouri River, where they hoped to find safer ground. Then the wind shifted. Flames spread into dry grass and cut off the route to the water.

The men turned and ran uphill. Their foreman, Wagner Dodge, ordered them to drop their equipment. The fire was gaining on them faster than they could climb.

Dodge did something that looked like the opposite of escaping. He lit the grass in front of himself. His smaller fire burned away the fuel, leaving a patch of black ground. He called the others toward it, but they continued uphill. The precise reasons they did not join him remain uncertain.

Dodge lay in the burned area as the main fire passed. Two others, Walter Rumsey and Robert Sallee, got through a gap in the rocks and found shelter on the far side of the ridge.

Those three survived. Thirteen firefighters died, including two who reached hospital but succumbed to their burns.

The fire changed how American crews studied fire behavior and trained for sudden danger. Dodge had not defeated the flames. He had removed the grass they needed to keep burning. The men who reached the rocks survived by finding ground with little fuel. In that canyon, the way out was not simply to run faster.
