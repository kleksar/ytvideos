Why would a firefighter deliberately start another fire while being chased by one? Why did only three of sixteen men survive the same hillside? And what did this disaster teach crews about the ground beneath their feet?

On August 5, 1949, a wildfire was reported in Mann Gulch, a steep canyon beside the Missouri River in Montana. The United States Forest Service sent fifteen smokejumpers: men trained to parachute into places too remote for fire engines. The program was young. Its first operational jumps had been made only nine years earlier.

At first the assignment seemed manageable. The crew landed away from the flames and met James Harrison, a local fire guard who had walked in from the river. They began moving down the canyon, looking for a safer position from which to approach the fire.

Then the escape route changed. Wind pushed the blaze into another part of the canyon. Burning material ignited fresh patches of grass ahead of the men. The route to the river was no longer open.

Now all sixteen were moving uphill, away from a fire that was spreading through dry grass on a steep slope. Going uphill was hard enough without carrying firefighting equipment. Their foreman, Wagner Dodge, told them to throw down their tools and run. The flames were closing in.

At this point, the instinctive answer was to reach the ridge. Dodge found a different answer. He stopped, struck a match and set the grass in front of himself on fire.

That sounds disastrous until you look at what a grass fire needs. The flame front moves by igniting fresh fuel. If a small controlled burn consumes that grass first, the larger fire cannot burn the same patch a second time. Dodge was trying to make a temporary refuge from the fuel already beneath his feet.

He called for the men to come into the blackened area. None joined him. The sources do not establish one certain explanation: smoke, noise, speed, separation and unfamiliarity with this maneuver all limit what we can say about their choices. It is not fair to turn the result into a story about men simply refusing to listen.

Dodge went down into the burned patch as the main fire swept across the hillside. He lived.

Meanwhile two smokejumpers, Walter Rumsey and Robert Sallee, had kept climbing. Near the ridge they found a narrow break in the rock wall. They slipped through and got onto the other side, then took refuge among rocks where there was little vegetation for the fire to consume. They survived by a different route.

Thirteen others were overtaken. Two badly burned men lived long enough to reach medical care, but died the following day. In total, thirteen firefighters were killed; only Dodge, Rumsey and Sallee survived.

Investigators later reconstructed the speed of the blowup: the fire was estimated to have crossed roughly three thousand acres in ten minutes. That figure describes the sudden expansion, not the speed of one neat wall of flame. It helps explain why a race uphill was so unlikely to work.

Mann Gulch did not give firefighters a simple rule that they should light fires whenever trapped. Escape fires depend on fuel, wind, terrain, training and time. What this disaster exposed was the danger of treating an open-looking hillside as an escape route without knowing how quickly the fire could move across it.

Afterward, wildfire agencies changed training and invested more heavily in fire-behavior research. The questions were concrete: Where is the safety zone? Is the route still open? How will this slope and this grass react when the wind turns?

Sixteen men entered the canyon to put out a fire. Three got out alive. One survived by burning a patch of grass first; two survived by reaching bare rock. Thirteen lives are why that difference is still taught.
