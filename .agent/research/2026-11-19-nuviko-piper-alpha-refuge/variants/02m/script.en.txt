Why did men on a burning oil platform wait inside for a rescue helicopter? And why did others survive by going into the sea?

On the night of July sixth, 1988, 226 men were aboard Piper Alpha, an oil and gas platform in the North Sea. Most were off duty. The platform was still producing, while maintenance work continued.

One pump had been taken out of service. Its pressure safety valve had also been removed. A temporary metal cover sealed the connection, but the night shift did not have a clear picture of that unfinished job.

Then the other pump stopped.

To keep production running, the crew brought the first pump back into service. An inquiry later concluded that fuel most probably escaped at the temporarily sealed connection. Around ten o'clock, an explosion tore through the platform.

But this was not just one fire. Piper Alpha was linked by pipelines to other installations. Escalating fires damaged its controls and escape routes, while gas from connected facilities fed the disaster.

Some workers gathered in the accommodation area, expecting instructions or helicopter rescue. Smoke and flames made that rescue impossible. Others searched for another way down. Some entered the North Sea and were pulled out by rescue vessels.

Within two hours, the platform was wrecked.

Sixty-one people survived. One hundred and sixty-five men on Piper Alpha died, as did two rescuers: 167 lives in all.

The investigation did not blame a single careless hand. It found failures in communication, safety management and emergency preparation. Its 106 recommendations helped transform offshore safety rules.

The cruel answer to the opening question was that the planned route to safety had stopped working. Many of the men waiting for it were never told to escape by whatever means they could.

On Piper Alpha, the safest place on the plan was no longer safe.
