Why would workers stay inside a burning oil platform instead of escaping? What made the first explosion grow into a disaster that rescuers could not reach? And what did investigators find in an ordinary shift handover?

Piper Alpha stood in the North Sea, far from the Scottish coast. By July 1988 it was producing oil and gas and was connected to other installations by pipelines. On the evening of July sixth, 226 men were aboard. Most were off duty.

The platform had two pumps involved in handling a flammable liquid from the gas operation. One was undergoing maintenance. Its pressure safety valve had been removed, leaving a connection sealed temporarily by a metal cover. That work was not finished when the day shift ended.

This should have been a very clear restriction: do not start the pump until the related work has been completed and the equipment made safe.

But the pump repair and the valve removal were covered by different permits. The critical information about the missing valve was not effectively passed to the night shift.

Around a quarter to ten, the other pump stopped. The crew now needed a replacement if production was to continue. They investigated the pump that had been worked on and brought it back into service, without knowing its safety valve was missing.

The later public inquiry found that, on the balance of probabilities, fuel escaped at the temporary seal. Around ten, it ignited. The first explosion tore through a gas-processing section and damaged vital controls.

Now the problem changed. This was not simply a machine to switch off and repair.

Piper Alpha belonged to a network. Oil and gas lines connected it to other platforms. Fires spread, and further explosions followed as the installation was exposed to fuel from that network. Emergency equipment and routes were damaged. Water-based fire protection did not provide the defence the crew needed.

Workers tried to get to assigned evacuation points. Smoke and fire cut off some approaches. Others moved into the accommodation area. They had been trained to gather and await direction or helicopter evacuation.

Yet the explosion had crippled the command system. Helicopters could not land through the smoke and heat. The accommodation was losing its ability to keep the smoke out.

For a man waiting inside, the absence of an instruction could feel like the absence of a decision. In fact, time was running out.

Some workers made their own way out. They climbed down where possible, or entered the dark, cold North Sea. Rescue boats from nearby vessels pulled survivors from the water, often under terrible conditions. Two men involved in rescue lost their lives.

Within roughly two hours, the towering platform was wrecked. Of 226 men aboard, only 61 survived. One hundred and sixty-five platform workers died. With the two rescuers, the toll was 167.

The inquiry asked a painful question. Could more of the men inside have lived if they had been told to escape by whatever means were available? It concluded the death toll there might have been significantly lower.

It also refused to reduce the cause to the person who restarted a pump. Why was unfinished work not visible to the next shift? Why was a dangerous restart possible? Why did fire protection, emergency communication and planned escape fail when they were needed together?

The inquiry produced 106 recommendations. Companies would be expected to demonstrate how they controlled major hazards, not merely show that rules existed. The regulator's job would change too.

Piper Alpha's first weakness was information that did not survive a shift change. Its last was a rescue plan that did not survive the fire.

The people who got out had to find a different route. Too many of those waiting inside never had that choice explained to them.
