How could a factory fire kill 146 people in a building that was supposed to resist fire? Why did workers on one floor escape while others, just above them, were trapped?

On March 25, 1911, workers at the Triangle Shirtwaist factory in New York were finishing their Saturday shift. They made fashionable blouses. The upper floors held sewing machines, fabric, and scraps of cloth.

A fire broke out on the eighth floor. Workers there began escaping down the stairs. Someone warned the tenth floor, where people could reach the roof.

But the ninth floor received no warning. There was no working factory alarm to tell them what was coming.

By the time the fire reached those workers, one stairway door they tried was locked. The other routes could not carry everyone. Elevator operators made repeated rescue trips, but the elevators eventually stopped. The outside fire escape collapsed.

Firefighters arrived. Their ladders reached only the sixth floor, several stories below the people at the windows.

The fire was over in roughly half an hour. One hundred and forty-six people had died, most of them women and girls.

The factory's owners were prosecuted. They were acquitted: the jury was not convinced that they knew the door was locked at the critical moment. That verdict did not make the exits safe.

New York responded with investigations and new workplace-safety requirements, including stronger fire precautions and ways out.

The central failure was not simply that a fire started. On the ninth floor, the warning came too late, and escape routes failed one after another. The building had walls that stood. The people inside needed doors that opened.
