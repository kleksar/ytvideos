How could a dam kill nearly two thousand people without collapsing? And why did the real danger come from the mountainside, not the concrete wall?

In northern Italy, engineers built the Vajont Dam to hold water for a hydroelectric system. The dam stood in a narrow gorge. High above its reservoir rose Mount Toc.

In 1960, part of that slope slid into the lake. Engineers also recorded cracks and movement on the mountain. So this was not a danger that first appeared on the night of the disaster.

They monitored the slope and adjusted the reservoir level, hoping to keep the movement under control. Tests suggested a water level that could contain the wave from a possible landslide. But those tests assumed the mountain would slide much more slowly than it actually did.

On October ninth, 1963, an enormous mass of rock and earth plunged into the reservoir in a matter of seconds. It forced water up the opposite mountainside and over the dam.

The wall largely survived. The water did not stay behind it.

A violent surge raced down the narrow valley and destroyed Longarone and nearby communities. Italy's Civil Protection Department records 1,917 lives lost.

The answer is not that the concrete suddenly failed. The mountain moved, the water had nowhere safe to go, and the wave passed over a dam that was still standing.

The warnings had been about the slope beside the reservoir. The catastrophe came from the same place.
