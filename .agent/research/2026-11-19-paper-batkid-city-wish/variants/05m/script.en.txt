How did an entire American city become a Batman film set for one day? Why did its police chief call a five-year-old for help? And what happened when the cameras went home?

In 2013, Miles Scott was five. He lived in a small Northern California town and had spent years being treated for leukemia. His treatment had ended and the illness was in remission. When Make-A-Wish asked him to choose something special, he chose a role: he wanted to become Batman.

That request was much bigger than meeting someone in a costume. If Miles was going to be a superhero, he needed a city to save.

The charity started planning a day of staged adventures in San Francisco. Police officers and firefighters agreed to take part. A volunteer named Eric Johnston, who had performed acrobatics and helped with wishes before, would play Batman alongside him.

The plan grew as people shared it. What might have been a small private surprise became a public event. Local officials, businesses and thousands of strangers got involved.

But there was a problem the organizers could not solve with decorations. Miles had to feel he was actually on a mission, not merely attending a party in his honor.

The day before, Eric met him as an instructor at a circus school. The building was full of people dressed as superheroes. They practised tricks together. Miles did not yet know his teacher would be Batman the next morning.

On November fifteenth, Eric knocked on the family's hotel-room door in costume. He gave Miles his own suit. Down in the garage were two Lamborghini sports cars made to look like Batmobiles. The adventure was already underway.

The police chief sent a call for help. A woman needed rescuing beside the city's cable-car tracks. Miles and Batman arrived, and he helped free her from a staged danger.

Then came another mission. At a bank vault, the Riddler was carrying out a pretend robbery. Miles confronted him, and the villain was taken away by police.

The story could have ended there. Instead, while Miles was having lunch, people in Union Square drew his attention to a new emergency: the Penguin had kidnapped Lou Seal, the San Francisco Giants' mascot.

That mission took the pair to the Giants' baseball stadium. There they chased the Penguin and freed the mascot. The team's giant screen then delivered another message, inviting Batkid to City Hall.

All along the route, the people of San Francisco had become more than an audience. They held signs, cheered from sidewalks and played along as though these missions were news breaking in their own city.

And the city itself helped complete the illusion. The San Francisco Chronicle published a special Gotham-themed edition. Then-Mayor Ed Lee welcomed Miles and presented him with a key to the city. Barack Obama sent a short video message of encouragement.

For Miles, it was a whole day of solving crimes that had been invented just for him. For the adults who built it, there was no second take: thousands of moving parts had to work around one child, on one afternoon.

The film set disappeared. The streets returned to normal. The day did not make a medical promise. Miles' leukemia was already in remission when he put on the cape.

But the ending did not stop at the key to the city. Ten years later, Make-A-Wish reported that Miles remained cancer-free, played sports and helped on the family farm. His younger brother had even worn the old costume for Halloween.

His mother Natalie had become a Make-A-Wish volunteer, helping other families plan the kind of day that had once been planned for hers.

That's what the city really made possible. A boy asked to act like a hero. Thousands of people joined the story. And when the costumes were put away, one of the families they had helped went on helping other children.
