What would it take to turn a working city into Gotham for one child? How do you keep a superhero adventure convincing when every villain and every rescue is staged? And what survives of a day like that after the crowds have gone?

In November 2013, five-year-old Miles Scott lived in Northern California with his family. He had spent much of his early childhood undergoing treatment for leukemia. By then the treatment had ended, and the cancer was in remission.

Make-A-Wish, an organization that arranges experiences for children with serious illnesses, asked what he most wanted. Miles loved superheroes, especially Batman. He didn't want a toy or a handshake with a movie star. He wanted to be a crime-fighting superhero himself.

That sounds like the beginning of a child's game. Yet one organization decided to make the game large enough for an entire city to join.

San Francisco was chosen as the stage. There would be calls from police, apparent emergencies, villains, rescues and a final ceremony at City Hall. The danger would be make-believe. Miles' participation, and the people waiting to cheer him on, would be entirely real.

The police department and fire department joined the preparations. The city's baseball team, the San Francisco Giants, got involved too. Volunteers could play frightened citizens, criminals, bystanders and helpers. Roads and locations had to be ready for the performers and for the crowds.

One volunteer was especially important. Eric Johnston was an engineer, an acrobat and a long-time supporter of Make-A-Wish. He would be the adult Batman, a guide who could help Miles without taking over the adventure.

At first Eric expected something small. As the organization found more willing partners, he learned that many more people would be coming than he had imagined.

Word spread through local websites and social media. Support came from people who had never met Miles. Organizers had hoped for a manageable audience. Thousands offered to turn out. Before the day even began, the city's ordinary streets were becoming a kind of theatre.

But a crowded theatre doesn't automatically give its leading actor a good experience. Here the actor was five, had recently completed years of treatment and was not being asked to perform a rehearsed film script.

The people planning the day had to create a story he could follow. Each task needed to feel clear: someone calls, someone needs help, the heroes act, and the city is safer. Eric's job was to stay beside him and pay attention to how he was doing.

Preparation started before the public event. The day before, Eric took Miles to a circus training center. He was dressed as himself, an instructor. Inside, performers in superhero costumes had gathered to help the boy practise tricks.

Miles saw a room full of heroes. Only later, during the big event, did he recognize that the man teaching him at the circus school was the same person wearing Batman's mask.

The following morning, November fifteenth, Eric came to the family's hotel room as Batman. He gave Miles the Batkid suit. Then they went downstairs to a garage holding two Lamborghini sports cars made to resemble Batmobiles.

By now a private wish had become a citywide production. And the first scene had a deadline: Gotham's police chief was calling for assistance.

The heroes took the Batmobile to a cable-car line, where a woman needed rescuing. A pretend explosive device and the actors made a scene of peril, but this was staged for the child. Miles and Batman helped free the woman.

There was another layer to this rescue that the crowd could not necessarily know. The actor playing the woman was Sue Johnston, Eric's wife. They were both part of the team helping a five-year-old navigate his imaginary city.

Next came the bank. The Riddler was inside a vault, carrying out a pretend robbery. Batkid confronted him, and officers took the villain away.

Each scene gave Miles a complete victory, but the day still needed somewhere to go. Rather than bring him directly to a ceremony, the organizers built a second act.

During lunch, people gathered in Union Square. A flash mob drew attention, and news of another crime reached Batkid. The Penguin had kidnapped Lou Seal, the San Francisco Giants' mascot.

A baseball team's mascot is a strange person to rescue in most superhero stories. Here it made perfect sense: the Giants had offered their stadium as a location.

Batman and Batkid followed the Penguin to the ballpark. They pursued him through the public areas and freed the mascot. At the stadium, a message on the giant screen invited the young hero to City Hall.

This sequence matters because it shows why the city couldn't simply rent costumes and be done. Each mission depended on a different real institution doing its part: police, volunteers, cable-car staff, a bank, a baseball club and the people who controlled the next location.

Then there was the audience. Thousands lined the streets, waved signs and cheered. Miles was not looking at a row of hired extras. Those were residents who had decided to give up part of their day to make a little boy's day feel enormous.

The San Francisco Chronicle printed a special Gotham-style edition, turning the invented crimes into front-page news. The real mayor, Ed Lee, took part in the final ceremony. President Barack Obama recorded a brief message of encouragement.

At City Hall the mayor gave Miles a key to the city. For a superhero story, that was the moment after the villain was defeated, when Gotham could finally thank its protector.

But how do you finish a real story like that without making a false promise about the child's health?

You say exactly what happened. Miles had already finished chemotherapy and was in remission before the event. Dressing a city as Gotham did not treat his leukemia. The wish gave him something else: a day with adventures he could choose, victories he could complete, and thousands of people cheering for him rather than waiting for news from a hospital.

His mother described the wish as the end of a chapter for their family. The celebration marked their chance to move forward after years of treatment.

The preparation had not been effortless, either. Eric later explained that the team had only one real chance to carry out the public adventure. There could be no normal dress rehearsal with streets full of spectators. He kept checking how Miles was coping between stops and adjusted his support to the child in front of him.

Those details change the meaning of the cheering crowds. To the public it looked like a spectacular one-day event. For the people closest to Miles it was a carefully made space where he could play without needing to know every backstage complication.

The story continued long after San Francisco returned to normal. The day inspired a feature documentary called Batkid Begins. The Batman costume went into storage. The news cycle moved on.

Ten years later, Make-A-Wish published an update. Miles was still cancer-free. He played team sports at high school and helped on his family's farm, driving a tractor instead of a Batmobile. His younger brother Ben had worn the old superhero costume for Halloween.

The Scott family did not keep the help to themselves. Miles' mother Natalie began volunteering with Make-A-Wish, helping other families plan wishes. She spoke about wanting other children and parents to have a positive end to a difficult chapter, or a new beginning.

There was another connection, too. Years before Batkid, Eric had helped a different child create a video game inspired by his treatment for cancer. The project made him part of the Make-A-Wish community long before he put on Batman's suit.

Much later, Eric himself received treatment for cancer. In a published reflection, he said the children he had known through these wishes, including Miles, were among the people who encouraged him through it. Their relationships had lasted beyond the costumes and the camera crews.

This isn't a claim that every wish works like a miracle, or that everyone who helps will someday be repaid. It's what these people reported about their own lives. A volunteer helped a child play Batman. Years later the child and his family were still part of his world.

One day in 2013, strangers made Gotham City out of San Francisco. They acted in a story whose criminals weren't real. The child at the centre of it was real, and so was the support.

The most lasting part of that story wasn't the Batmobile, the fake bank robbery or even the key to the city. It was the way the people who took part kept carrying something of that day into the years that followed.
