A cruise ship was lying on its side beside an Italian island. Some of its lifeboats were still attached. By then, they were almost impossible to launch.

On January 13, 2012, the Costa Concordia was carrying more than four thousand passengers and crew. Its captain took it close to the island of Giglio for a nighttime sail-by.

The ship struck rocks. Water flooded important compartments, and the power failed.

Passengers were told about an electrical problem. But people on shore were already hearing that something much worse had happened. A passenger's family helped alert the authorities, who contacted the ship.

The evacuation order did not come immediately. The captain gave it more than an hour after the impact.

That lost time mattered. As the ship rolled over, some lifeboats could no longer be lowered from the high side. People had to find other ways off. Local boats, rescue crews and island residents helped bring survivors to safety.

The captain, Francesco Schettino, left while passengers were still aboard. He later disputed the suggestion that he had deliberately abandoned them. A court eventually convicted him of causing the disaster, multiple manslaughter and abandoning the ship.

Thirty-two people died.

You might expect the wreck to have vanished beneath the sea. Instead, it remained on its side beside the island. Removing it took a huge engineering operation that lasted more than two years.

The final lesson is not that there were no boats. It is that the moment to use some of them passed while the ship was still afloat.

Look at the Costa Concordia leaning against the rocks again. The escape boats were right there.
