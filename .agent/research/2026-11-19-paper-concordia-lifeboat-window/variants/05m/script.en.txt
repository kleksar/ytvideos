How could a cruise ship have lifeboats hanging from its sides and still leave people unable to escape? Why did the coast guard hear about the emergency from people on shore? And what happened to the wreck after the passengers were gone?

On January 13, 2012, more than four thousand people were aboard the Costa Concordia, cruising along the Italian coast. Passengers were eating and enjoying their evening. The ship was supposed to continue its voyage, but Captain Francesco Schettino steered closer to the island of Giglio for a nighttime sail-by.

At about 9:45, the hull hit a submerged rock. The collision tore open the side of the ship. Seawater flooded machinery spaces, and electrical power failed.

The first explanation given to passengers focused on an electrical problem. That might sound like something the crew could repair. But the ship had a hole in its hull. Water was entering faster than announcements could solve anything.

Officers working below were already finding flooded compartments. In testimony during the later investigation, one officer described repeatedly reporting the worsening damage to the bridge. What passengers heard and what the ship was experiencing were two very different things.

Then the emergency began unfolding somewhere unexpected: on land. Relatives received alarming calls from the ship. One family contacted the police, and a report reached the coast guard. At about 10:06, officers on shore called the Concordia to ask what was happening.

The people on land were trying to identify an emergency that passengers on the ship had still not been ordered to escape.

The ship was not standing still while decisions were made. Water continued to enter. The vessel's position began to change. And the more it leaned, the less useful some of its rescue equipment would become.

The captain ordered abandonment more than an hour after the collision. The announcement was made at about 10:54. By then, the Concordia was no longer level.

To see why that matters, picture a lifeboat hanging from a ship. Its launching machinery is designed to lower it along the outside of an upright vessel. But when the whole ship rolls, the two sides are no longer equal. One rises high above the water while the other sinks closer to it.

Some boats were lowered successfully. Others, particularly on the elevated side as the list increased, became impossible to launch safely. People had to find other routes off the ship. Coast guard vessels, ferries and local boats moved survivors ashore, and island residents helped those arriving.

The Concordia stopped against rocks near Giglio rather than slipping entirely underwater. That left part of the ship above the sea, but it did not create a level walkway. Passengers still had to move through a tilted vessel to reach the rescuers.

The captain left while people remained aboard. A coast guard officer demanded that he return and help direct the evacuation. Schettino later denied that he had deliberately abandoned the passengers. Other officers and rescue teams continued helping people off.

Thirty-two people died.

The story did not end when the last surviving passengers were brought ashore. The wreck remained on its side, close to the island. It was heavy, unstable and a threat to the surrounding sea. The authorities had to search for missing people, monitor the hull and prevent fuel pollution.

Engineers could not simply pull it free with tugboats. They built underwater supports and attached large external tanks. In September 2013, an operation called parbuckling slowly rotated the hull upright over nineteen hours. Only then could workers see the side that had been underwater.

There was another long wait before the ship could be towed away. On July 23, 2014, two ocean-going tugs began pulling the Concordia toward Genoa. The wreck reached the port four days later, more than two years after the collision.

A criminal trial examined what went wrong. In 2015 Schettino was convicted of causing the shipwreck, multiple manslaughter and abandoning the vessel. Italy's highest court upheld his sixteen-year sentence in 2017.

The mistake was not just hitting a rock. The delay after the impact mattered too. Every minute changed the angle of the decks and the options for getting people away.

That is the unsettling answer to the first question. The lifeboats were there. But for some of them, the chance to launch had already passed.
