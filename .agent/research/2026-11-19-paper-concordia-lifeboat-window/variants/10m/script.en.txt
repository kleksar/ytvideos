How can a cruise ship with lifeboats on both sides end up with passengers unable to board them? Why did the people on shore learn about a serious emergency before the ship's passengers were told to leave? And what does it take to remove a vessel the size of a floating hotel once it has rolled onto the rocks?

The first answer begins on January 13, 2012, just off the Italian island of Giglio.

More than four thousand passengers and crew were aboard the Costa Concordia. The ship was on a Mediterranean cruise, and the evening's meals and entertainment were under way. From the island, its illuminated decks would have been easy to recognize. But it was not supposed to pass so close to the coast.

Captain Francesco Schettino had chosen a route near Giglio for a nighttime sail-by. A cruise ship can make such a passage only if it has enough room to manoeuvre. On this approach, the margin vanished.

At about 9:45 in the evening, the Concordia hit a submerged rock. The impact tore open its hull. Seawater entered the machinery spaces. Electrical power failed. With essential equipment affected, this was not a problem that could be repaired with a quick reset.

For a passenger who had only heard a power failure announced, the emergency was not yet clear. But the people working below had a much different view. During the later investigation, an officer described inspecting flooded spaces and repeatedly reporting how seriously the situation was deteriorating.

The bridge knew water was entering the ship. Still, passengers had not been given the order to abandon it.

At this point, another line of information opened, away from the vessel. People aboard contacted their families. One family called the police, and the report reached the coast guard. According to the recorded chronology of the emergency, officers on shore contacted the Concordia at about 10:06 that night.

Imagine the contrast without inventing anyone's private thoughts. A ship has struck a rock. Water has reached its vital spaces. And the authorities on land are asking the ship what has happened while many passengers have heard only of an electrical fault.

That delay is the central turn of the story. The emergency was not frozen in place while everyone decided what to do. Water kept entering. The ship kept changing position.

The captain gave the order to abandon ship more than an hour after the impact. The public announcement came at about 10:54. By then, even a simple job had become harder: getting a lifeboat down to the water.

A lifeboat is not just a boat placed on a deck. It hangs from launching gear designed to lower it along the outside of a vessel. When a ship is upright, the geometry is predictable. As the Concordia listed, one side of the ship rose farther from the sea, while the other moved closer to it. The boats did not all face the same conditions.

Some lifeboats were launched. Others, particularly from the high side as the list grew, could no longer be lowered safely. The crew could not solve that by finding more life jackets. The problem was the position of the entire ship.

There is a sharp visual contradiction in the pictures of the wreck. Lifeboats are still attached to a vessel that urgently needed to be emptied. Those unused boats are not proof that everyone ignored them. They show how fast ordinary emergency equipment can become difficult to use when a ship is no longer level.

The Concordia came to rest against rocks near Giglio. Part of its enormous hull remained above the water. That prevented the scene from being a simple disappearance beneath the waves, but it did not turn the ship into a safe pier. Decks and passageways now sloped, and passengers still needed a route from the vessel to land.

Rescue boats converged on the area. Ferries and coast guard vessels helped move survivors. People on the island assisted those arriving ashore. The emergency had moved beyond the ship's own equipment and crew.

Meanwhile, the captain left the Concordia while people were still aboard. In a recorded confrontation, a coast guard officer demanded that he return and help coordinate the evacuation. Schettino later disputed the claim that he had deliberately abandoned passengers. Whatever his explanation, people were still being brought off the ship after he left.

Thirty-two people died. The rest of this story must not bury that fact under an argument about the captain's nickname or a spectacular photograph.

Once the evacuation ended, the wreck posed a different danger. It had come to rest on a sloping seabed beside an inhabited island. Authorities had to watch whether the hull moved, search for missing people and protect the surrounding sea from fuel and other contaminants. These were separate problems, and solving one did not automatically solve the others.

Removing the wreck required a plan unlike towing an ordinary damaged ship. The Concordia was lying on its side. Large sections had been submerged, and its weight rested against rocks. Tugboats alone could not simply pull that hull off the coast.

Engineers built underwater supports and attached huge external buoyancy tanks to the ship. The next step was called parbuckling: using a system of cables and controlled forces to rotate the vessel upright. It was a slow, monitored movement. If the structure shifted in the wrong way, the operation could create a new emergency.

In September 2013, the turning operation took nineteen hours. By its end, the hull had rotated roughly sixty-five degrees and was resting on a prepared artificial seabed. The formerly submerged side could finally be examined directly.

But raising the ship was not the end. More flotation equipment and preparation were needed before the Concordia could leave the rocks. In July 2014, it was refloated and towed away from Giglio. On July 23, two ocean-going tugs began pulling it toward Genoa. Four days later, the wreck arrived at the port where it would be dismantled.

The removal had taken more than two years. The search for victims, too, extended long after the first night. Italy's Civil Protection Department records the discovery of the final victim during dismantling in October 2014.

A separate investigation asked who was responsible for the voyage and the response to the collision. In 2015, Schettino was convicted of causing the disaster, multiple manslaughter and abandoning the ship. Italy's highest court upheld a sixteen-year sentence in 2017. The legal outcome did not bring the lost passengers back, and it cannot reduce the entire emergency to one man's actions.

The physical sequence explains something more precise. First, the close passage led to the collision. Then the hull damage and flooding created an emergency. The order to evacuate came after the ship had spent more than an hour deteriorating. As the vessel rolled, part of the equipment intended for escape became unusable. The longer this took, the more difficult the remaining evacuation became.

That is why the image of lifeboats hanging from the Costa Concordia is so unsettling. They were installed for exactly this emergency. Some worked. But by the time people needed others, the ship had changed shape around them.

The story is not that the lifeboats were missing. It is that a chance to use them can disappear while everyone is still aboard.
