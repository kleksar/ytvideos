Could one tiny excavator free a ship that blocked the Suez Canal?

In March 2021, the Ever Given entered Egypt's Suez Canal, the shortcut ships use between Europe and Asia. It was about four hundred metres long. In a narrow, single-lane section, it ran aground sideways. Its bow dug into one bank, and its stern blocked the other side. Nothing could pass.

The photograph that travelled around the world showed an excavator digging at the bow. Next to that enormous hull, it looked like a toy. But this wasn't a job for one digger.

First, crews had to remove the sand gripping the bow. While excavators worked near the bank, dredgers pulled material from under the water. Tugboats tried to turn and pull the ship, but the hull was still stuck fast. Every extra day meant more ships waiting at both ends of the canal.

Then the rescuers gained two advantages. Powerful seagoing tugs joined the smaller boats, and a particularly high tide gave the ship more water beneath it. By the end, crews had dredged roughly thirty thousand cubic metres of sand, and thirteen tugboats had been deployed.

On March twenty-ninth, six days after the grounding, the stern swung back into the channel. The bow finally came free. The Ever Given moved to a lake for inspection, and shipping through the canal resumed that evening.

But the ship itself didn't sail away to deliver its cargo. A compensation dispute kept it in Egypt until July.

So the answer is no. The excavator wasn't pointless; it was one visible part of a much bigger rescue. The ship moved when the digging, the pulling and the tide finally worked together. That famous picture made the whole operation look much simpler.
