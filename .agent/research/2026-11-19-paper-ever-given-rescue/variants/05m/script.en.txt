How does a ship four hundred metres long get stuck in a canal? Why couldn't tugboats simply pull it out? And why did the ship stay in Egypt long after the canal reopened?

On March twenty-third, twenty twenty-one, the Ever Given was heading north through Egypt's Suez Canal. The canal connects the Red Sea to the Mediterranean, saving vessels the long journey around Africa. A ship this big carries thousands of containers, stacked like blocks above the deck. But the most important detail was below the waterline.

The southern stretch of the canal had only one navigable lane. There was no second road around a stranded ship. Strong winds were reported that morning, but wind alone is not an established final explanation for the grounding. A vessel moving through shallow, narrow water is also affected by the water flowing around its hull. The exact sequence and responsibility became disputed.

Whatever combination produced the loss of control, the result was unmistakable. The bow of the Ever Given ran hard into the eastern bank. The ship ended up diagonally across the channel. Its stern sat near the other side. One enormous ship had shut a route used by vessels carrying cargo between continents.

A photograph soon showed a small excavator digging beside the bow. It looked as though someone had brought a child's shovel to move a skyscraper. The image was real, but it hid most of the operation.

The problem wasn't simply the ship's weight. Much of that weight was already supported by the water. The trouble was the part of the bow buried in the bank. Tugboats could pull, but first crews needed to loosen the grip of the soil and give the hull room to move without damaging it.

Excavators dug near shore. Powerful dredgers removed sand and mud from the underwater area beside the bow. Tug crews pulled and pushed while workers checked how the vessel responded. There was no magical single pull. A failed movement could leave the ship in nearly the same position, and every day the line of waiting vessels grew longer.

By March twenty-eighth, hundreds of ships were waiting to pass. Some operators considered the enormous detour around the southern tip of Africa. For the people carrying food, fuel, machinery and consumer goods, being stuck was more than a photograph turned into a meme.

The rescue team also faced a choice. They could try to lighten the ship by taking off containers, but thousands of boxes were stacked high above the water and the necessary equipment would make that a difficult, slow job. Dredging and tugging remained the preferred route.

Then larger seagoing tugs arrived to help the canal's harbour boats. At the same time, a particularly high tide offered a narrow opportunity: extra water under the vessel meant more buoyancy and a better chance to rotate it off the bank. The high tide did not do the work on its own. It made the work already done more effective.

On Monday, March twenty-ninth, the stern began moving decisively away from the bank. The photographs changed: now the giant hull was turning toward the middle of the channel. But the bow was still caught, so early celebrations did not mean the operation was complete. Crews kept working.

By the final release, about thirty thousand cubic metres of sand had been dredged, and eleven harbour tugs plus two stronger seagoing tugs had been deployed. At 3:05 that afternoon, local time, the Ever Given was refloated. It was then taken to the Great Bitter Lake for inspection. Traffic resumed through the canal that evening.

That seems like the end of the story. It wasn't.

Once the waterway was open, Egypt's canal authority and the ship's owner argued over compensation. The authority initially sought hundreds of millions of dollars, and the vessel remained in Egypt under legal detention while the dispute continued. The agreement that finally released the ship in July had undisclosed financial terms. A demand was not the same thing as an amount paid.

The canal was blocked for six days. The ship itself was held for more than three months. And the image that everyone remembered — one excavator against a wall of steel — showed just one corner of a joint operation involving Egyptian crews, international salvors, dredgers, tugboats and the tide. The small machine helped. It was never the whole answer.
