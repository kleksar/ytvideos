How can a ship larger than most city blocks shut one of the world's busiest waterways? What does it take to move that much steel without breaking it? And if the rescue succeeds, why might the ship still be unable to leave?

On the morning of March twenty-third, twenty twenty-one, the Ever Given was travelling north through Egypt's Suez Canal. This canal is a shortcut between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean. For a vessel carrying goods between Asia and Europe, it can save the long voyage around Africa.

The ship was about four hundred metres long and nearly sixty metres wide, carrying stacks of containers above its deck. It was one of the biggest ships using the canal. But the canal is not equally wide everywhere. Parts have been expanded to allow ships to pass in opposite directions. The southern stretch where this voyage went wrong had only one navigable channel.

A large ship entering that stretch needs careful control. Reports described high winds that morning. Water squeezed between a moving hull and a nearby bank can also affect how a ship responds to steering. Investigators and the parties involved later disagreed about responsibility. It would be wrong to turn one possible factor into a settled verdict.

What nobody disputed was where the Ever Given ended up. Its bow ran into the eastern bank. The rest of the hull swung across the channel, bringing the stern close to the opposite side. Traffic stopped in both directions. A ship from another continent could be waiting at one end of the canal while the cargo it needed was just beyond the obstruction.

From the shore came an image that looked almost absurd. A little excavator was digging at the base of a steel wall. That wall was the ship's bow. Online, the photograph became a joke about the smallest machine trying to move the largest problem.

But the rescuers weren't asking that excavator to push the ship. They were trying to solve something much harder: how do you free an enormous floating object when part of it is no longer floating?

A ship's weight is carried by the water beneath it. When its bow bites into land, some of that support disappears, and the soil resists movement. Pull in the wrong way and you may dig the bow deeper or put enormous stress on the hull. So the first task was to remove the material holding it, not simply bring a bigger rope.

Excavators could work close to shore. For the underwater sand and mud, crews used dredgers: machines that move sediment through pipes or scoops and take it away. They cleared the area around the bow and worked to improve the depth beside the hull.

At the same time, tugboats pulled and pushed. A tug's job here was not like towing a broken car along an open road. There was little room to manoeuvre, the ship was still in contact with the ground, and the forces had to be applied in a useful direction. Moving the stern could help turn the long hull, but the buried bow still had to come out.

For days the work seemed painfully slow. The visible excavator was just one part of a coordinated operation by the Egyptian canal authority and professional salvage specialists, including a team from SMIT Salvage. Their decisions had to account for the ship, the banks, the equipment and the very real risk of making the situation harder.

Meanwhile, other captains faced decisions of their own. Stay in the queue and hope the canal opens? Or sail around Africa? The second option adds days of travel and large fuel costs. The first leaves a ship idle without a reliable departure time. By March twenty-eighth, the canal authority was reporting hundreds of vessels waiting to transit.

It's tempting to turn that delay into one enormous dollar figure. Estimates at the time spoke of billions of dollars in trade delayed every day, but delayed cargo value is not the same as goods permanently lost. The real problem was uncertainty spreading along supply chains: factories expecting components, merchants expecting stock, shipping companies trying to keep schedules.

The rescue had a second possible path. The Ever Given carried thousands of containers, many stacked several levels high. If crews removed enough of them, the vessel might sit higher in the water. But unloading a loaded mega-ship at a stranded position would require cranes, suitable equipment and time. A plan that sounds simple on paper could turn a six-day emergency into something much longer.

There was also ballast, the water ships carry to control stability and trim. Adjusting ballast could help the vessel's position, but it could not remove the bank gripping the bow. The main strategy remained to dredge, pull, and use the water level at the right moment.

Tides made that last part essential. Even a modest rise in water level can add buoyant support over a very large hull. A particularly high spring tide was expected around March twenty-ninth. The salvage team could not order it to arrive earlier. They had to get the ship as loose as possible before it came.

And they needed more pulling power. Alongside eleven harbour tugs, two more powerful seagoing tugs were deployed: Alp Guard and Carlo Magno. They added capacity at the moment when the dredging and the tide might finally let the hull move. The answer wasn't a single giant tug any more than it was the little excavator. It was the timing and combination of different tools.

By Sunday, the workers had made progress beneath the surface. On Monday morning, March twenty-ninth, movement became visible. The stern began to swing away from the western side and farther toward the centre of the canal. That long hull acted almost like a lever as it turned.

For a while, people celebrated. But there was an important distinction: changing the angle of the stern did not mean the bow was free. The front of the ship could still be trapped in the eastern bank. There was a chance the movement would stop, leaving salvage teams to dredge more, wait for another tidal window, or consider removing containers.

They kept working through the day. The Suez Canal Authority's dredging crews and the international salvage team were not competing heroes in separate stories. The dredgers were creating space; the tugs were changing the direction of the forces; the tide was helping the hull float. Each part became more effective because the others had done their work.

When the Ever Given finally moved clear, the total sand removed was roughly thirty thousand cubic metres. Thirteen tugs had been deployed, eleven from the harbour fleet and two powerful seagoing vessels. Boskalis, the company behind SMIT Salvage, recorded the successful refloating at 3:05 p.m. local time on March twenty-ninth.

After six days, the canal could reopen. But a released blockage is not necessarily a released ship. The Ever Given was taken to the Great Bitter Lake, a wider body of water inside the canal system, for inspection. Shipping through the canal resumed that evening, and operators began working through the backlog. Clearing a queue of hundreds of ships did not happen the instant the obstructing hull moved.

From the outside, that looked like the natural ending: the ship had moved, other ships could pass, and the world's strangest traffic jam was over.

There was a second trap waiting.

The canal authority wanted compensation for the disruption and the salvage. The Ever Given's Japanese owner and its insurers disputed the size of the claims and the circumstances surrounding responsibility. In April, the ship was detained under an Egyptian court order while negotiations continued. Thousands of containers that had been intended to travel on to Europe remained with it.

The authority initially demanded about nine hundred and sixteen million dollars, later reducing its claim to about five hundred and fifty million. Those were demands, not verified payments. People could report the numbers accurately and still tell the wrong story if they called them the final cost.

In July, an agreement was reached. Its detailed financial terms were not disclosed. On July seventh, the Ever Given was finally allowed to leave Egypt. The waterway had been reopened in March, but the ship had remained there for more than three additional months.

There is an important difference between what this story shows and what it cannot prove. We know how the salvage ended: digging, dredging, tugboats and a favourable tide freed the hull. We know that the subsequent legal dispute delayed the ship. The exact allocation of blame for the original grounding was contested, and the confidential settlement does not reveal one universally agreed price.

That makes the famous excavator photograph more interesting, not less. It showed a real worker doing a necessary piece of the job. It didn't show the dredgers under the surface, the stronger tugs arriving, the rising tide, or the negotiations that followed.

One enormous ship stopped a canal. Many coordinated actions opened it. Then a legal dispute kept the ship from leaving anyway. The first problem was a hull stuck in sand. The second was a bill nobody had agreed to pay.
