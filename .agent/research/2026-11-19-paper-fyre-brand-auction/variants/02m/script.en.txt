How does a luxury festival become a disaster before the music even starts? Why would its founder try to sell tickets to another one? And how could the name of that failed festival still be worth almost a quarter of a million dollars?

In 2017, Fyre Festival promised an exclusive island party in the Bahamas. Its adverts showed beaches, models and expensive accommodation. People bought tickets expecting a carefully organised getaway.

They arrived to find tents, inadequate facilities and no festival as promised. Performances were cancelled. Organisers stopped incoming flights and said they were working on refunds.

This was not just a booking mistake. Founder Billy McFarland had also misled investors. He later pleaded guilty to fraud involving more than twenty-six million dollars in losses and received a six-year prison sentence.

After his release, he announced Fyre Festival Two. The new plan promised a luxury event in Mexico in May 2025, with ticket packages reaching as high as a million dollars.

But officials in the proposed locations disputed whether the event had permission to go ahead. The organisers moved their announced location, then postponed the festival and sent refund notices. No second concert weekend took place as advertised.

Then came a different kind of sale.

In July 2025, McFarland auctioned the Fyre name and its rights on eBay. The final bid was two hundred forty-five thousand three hundred dollars.

The buyer was LimeWire, a revived file-sharing brand. Its new owners said they weren't bringing the festival back. They wanted the name and the internet joke around it.

Fyre had failed to deliver the holiday it sold. Yet the story of that failure had become something someone else was willing to buy.
