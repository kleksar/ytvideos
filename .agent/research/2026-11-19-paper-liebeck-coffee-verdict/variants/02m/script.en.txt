Did a woman really get $2.7 million for spilling coffee on herself? The jury did announce that number. But the famous payout never happened.

In 1992, seventy-nine-year-old Stella Liebeck bought coffee from a McDonald's drive-through in New Mexico. Her grandson parked the car. Stella was in the passenger seat when she held the cup between her knees and tried to remove the lid.

The coffee spilled into her lap. Her sweatpants held the liquid against her skin. She suffered severe burns, spent eight days in hospital, and needed skin grafts.

She initially asked McDonald's for about twenty thousand dollars to help cover her medical costs. The company offered eight hundred.

So the dispute went to court. Jurors learned the restaurant chain served coffee around 180 to 190 degrees Fahrenheit. They also heard about hundreds of earlier burn complaints. McDonald's argued that customers expected hot coffee, and that serious incidents were rare compared with the enormous number of cups sold.

The jury did not pretend Stella had done nothing wrong. It found her twenty percent responsible for the spill.

It awarded two hundred thousand dollars for her injuries, reduced to one hundred and sixty thousand because of her share of fault. Then came the headline: another $2.7 million meant to punish the company's conduct.

But the judge reduced that punitive award to $480,000. Before a final judgment, the parties settled privately. The actual settlement amount was not publicly disclosed.

The injuries were real. The jury's criticism was real. The giant check in the joke was not.
