How did a spilled cup of coffee become a $2.7 million headline? Why did a jury blame the company when the customer was holding the cup? And why did the woman at the center of the case never receive that famous amount?

The answer begins in a parked car in Albuquerque, New Mexico.

It was February 1992. Stella Liebeck was seventy-nine. Her grandson had driven them through McDonald's for breakfast and coffee, then stopped the car. Stella sat in the passenger seat. She placed the cup between her knees and started to remove its lid so she could add cream and sugar.

The cup tipped. Coffee soaked her sweatpants and stayed pressed against her skin. The burns were severe. Her grandson took her for emergency treatment. She spent eight days in hospital and needed skin grafts.

That is the first part of the story the punchline usually leaves out. But a terrible injury alone does not explain why a jury held McDonald's responsible. People know coffee is hot. Stella had handled the cup herself.

At first, this was not a demand for millions. Stella sought roughly twenty thousand dollars to cover medical costs and losses. McDonald's offered eight hundred dollars. Negotiations failed, and the case reached a jury.

Now the dispute changed. The question was no longer simply who had spilled the drink. It was whether the company had sold coffee at a temperature that exposed customers to an unreasonable risk of serious injury.

At trial, jurors heard that McDonald's kept coffee between 180 and 190 degrees Fahrenheit, around 82 to 88 Celsius. They were shown evidence of roughly seven hundred earlier burn complaints. Those complaints did not mean every customer was injured. But they mattered because the company had been warned about the kind of harm a spill could cause.

McDonald's offered a different way of looking at the numbers. It sold an enormous number of cups. Against that total, serious incidents were rare. The company also argued customers expected their coffee to be hot. Jurors had to decide whether a familiar product could nevertheless be unreasonably dangerous at that serving temperature.

This is where the outcome becomes more interesting than the headline.

The jury found that Stella was not blameless. It assigned twenty percent of the responsibility to her. Her basic compensation was two hundred thousand dollars, then reduced to one hundred and sixty thousand. The same jury decided that McDonald's response to the risk deserved a separate punishment.

It set punitive damages at $2.7 million. That figure was tied to about two days of the chain's coffee revenue, not to the price of one cup or a medical bill. It was a jury award, not a payment transferred into Stella's bank account.

The trial judge then cut the punitive portion to $480,000. Both sides still faced the prospect of further legal proceedings. Instead, they reached a confidential settlement before a final judgment. The precise amount was never publicly disclosed.

You can question the jury's figures. You can think a customer should take more care with a hot drink. Those are real disagreements, and the jury itself assigned Stella some fault. But the familiar joke skips the evidence that made the jury consider the company's conduct at all: the severity of the burns, the serving temperature, and the prior complaints.

And it skips the ending. Nobody can honestly point to a public record showing that Stella received $2.7 million.

The case kept travelling through news coverage and jokes. Its most repeatable detail was the largest number. Its most important detail was harder to fit into a headline: an everyday product can be familiar, dangerous in a particular way, and mishandled by a customer at the same time.

The next time you hear the story, there are really two questions. Who spilled the coffee? Stella did. Who was responsible for how dangerously hot it was served? That was the question the jury had to answer. The one thing the headline got badly wrong was what happened after the verdict.
