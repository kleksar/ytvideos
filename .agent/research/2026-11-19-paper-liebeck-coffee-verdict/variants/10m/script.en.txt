What happened in the parked car before the most famous coffee lawsuit in America? What did a jury hear that made it award $2.7 million? And if the verdict was that large, where did the money go?

The short version people remember has three pieces: coffee spilled, a woman sued, and someone won millions. Each piece is connected to a real event. Put together without the missing details, they tell a different story from the one heard in court.

On February 27, 1992, seventy-nine-year-old Stella Liebeck went with her grandson to a McDonald's drive-through in Albuquerque, New Mexico. They bought breakfast and coffee. Her grandson was driving. After collecting the order, he pulled over and parked.

Stella remained in the passenger seat. She wanted to add cream and sugar, but there was no stable place for her cup in the car. She held it between her knees as she started to remove the lid.

The cup tipped. Coffee soaked her sweatpants. Instead of running away immediately, the hot liquid remained trapped in the fabric against her skin. She suffered serious burns, including third-degree burns. Her grandson took her for emergency care. She spent eight days in the hospital and needed skin grafts.

A skin graft is not something needed for a minor splash of warm coffee. Surgeons move healthy skin to an area too badly damaged to heal in the ordinary way. The distinction matters here: the case did not begin because Stella disliked the taste, or because she had briefly touched a hot paper cup. It began with a serious injury.

Still, the spill itself was hers. The car was parked, she was trying to open the lid, and she was holding the cup between her knees. Those facts matter too. Any account that removes them simply turns the story into an advertisement for the opposite side.

After leaving the hospital, Stella faced bills and a prolonged recovery. Before the lawsuit became famous, she tried to settle with McDonald's for around twenty thousand dollars. The company's offer was eight hundred dollars. Negotiation did not resolve the difference.

That brought the case into a courtroom, where the argument took an unexpected turn. The question was not merely whether coffee could burn someone. Everybody involved knew hot coffee could hurt. The argument was about the particular risk created by the way McDonald's chose to serve it.

The chain served coffee at roughly 180 to 190 degrees Fahrenheit. In Celsius, that is around 82 to 88 degrees. At the hotter end, the difference between a spill that hurts and a spill that causes deep tissue injury can be measured in seconds.

The jury heard expert evidence about burns and temperature. If a liquid is much hotter, skin can be seriously injured before a person has time to pull away or remove soaked clothing. Simply saying "coffee is supposed to be hot" does not settle how hot is reasonable.

But there is another side to that argument. Hotter coffee stays hot longer. A restaurant can say customers want a drink that will remain warm while they travel. And McDonald's sold coffee in enormous quantities. A relatively small number of incidents can look very different when divided by all the cups sold.

The company's defense emphasized that scale. Its position was that serious burns were uncommon relative to its business. The jurors were asked to see the spill as a known risk of purchasing hot coffee, rather than a defect that required the chain to change its practice.

Then they heard about previous injuries.

Evidence presented at trial described roughly seven hundred burn complaints that had reached McDonald's before Stella's case. This is an important number, but not because seven hundred people were necessarily burned as badly as she was. The complaints varied. It matters because it showed that spilled coffee causing injury was not a purely unimaginable event the company had encountered for the first time.

The two sides saw those complaints in opposite ways. To the defense, they were a tiny fraction of a huge operation. To Stella's side, they were warnings that had not led to a meaningful reduction in serving temperature.

Notice what the dispute had become. No one was arguing that Stella never touched the cup. The question was whether a company's policy can add a serious, avoidable danger to an ordinary human mistake.

The jury was allowed to divide responsibility. And it did.

For the injuries and losses Stella had suffered, jurors set compensation at two hundred thousand dollars. But they also decided she was twenty percent at fault. That reduced her compensatory amount to one hundred and sixty thousand.

So the familiar retelling that the jury treated her as a completely innocent bystander is wrong. The jurors expressly held her responsible for part of what happened.

The other part of the verdict produced the national headline. Jurors awarded $2.7 million in punitive damages. Those damages are different from compensation. They are meant to punish conduct considered particularly blameworthy and discourage repetition, not calculate what a hospital stay costs.

According to accounts of the trial, that $2.7 million figure was chosen to represent roughly two days of McDonald's coffee sales. It was not a calculation of Stella's medical bills, and it was not a receipt proving that any money had already changed hands.

Even while headlines focused on the biggest number, the legal process continued.

The trial judge reduced the punitive award to $480,000. A jury verdict and the final amount someone takes home are not the same thing. The reduction was not a side note: it changed the headline figure by millions before the dispute was finally settled.

After that, the parties still had legal options. Continuing a case could mean more time, expense, and uncertainty for both sides. Rather than take the dispute all the way through further proceedings, Stella and McDonald's reached a confidential settlement before a final judgment.

The amount they agreed on was not publicly disclosed. Claims that Stella simply pocketed $2.7 million skip over both the judge's reduction and the private agreement. Because the settlement was confidential, it would also be misleading to replace the famous figure with another exact number and pretend it is certain.

There is an even stranger part to this story. The case lasted in public memory not primarily as a debate about serving temperatures, company warnings, or burn treatment, but as a joke about a person who supposedly got rich from an accident she caused.

News reports often condensed the lawsuit to its largest number. Some reports described Stella as driving when she spilled the cup. In fact, her grandson had been driving, and the car was parked. These differences do not automatically establish what verdict was correct. They establish how easily one memorable detail can displace the facts necessary to judge it.

The case then appeared repeatedly in discussions of lawsuits and proposed limits on damages. Critics presented it as proof that juries could impose absurd penalties for everyday mishaps. Defenders pointed to the severity of the injury, the company's temperature policy, and the previous complaints. The PBS documentary account of the case describes how it became a symbol in arguments about changes to civil liability law.

Neither side of that debate needs the story to be distorted.

You can accept that Stella made a mistake and still ask whether the coffee was served at an unreasonable temperature. You can accept that McDonald's knew about previous complaints and still question whether the punitive award was proportionate. You can think juries should have power to punish dangerous practices without believing every large jury verdict should survive unchanged.

Those are three different questions: what caused the injury, how responsibility should be divided, and how large a penalty is fair. A single punchline collapses all three into one.

And there is one more distinction the numbers reveal. The compensation for Stella's injuries was reduced because the jurors blamed her for part of the incident. The additional punishment aimed at the company was separately reduced by the judge. Then a private settlement replaced the public verdict with an amount we cannot verify. Each stage changed what the numbers meant.

The legal case is not a reason to treat every cup of hot coffee as a disaster waiting to happen. It is a reason to distinguish the risk of a familiar product from the severity of a particular accident. Millions of uneventful coffee purchases and one person's serious burns can both be true. So can the customer's mistake and the company's responsibility.

Stella did spill the coffee. Her injuries were serious. The jury did announce $2.7 million in punitive damages, and it did assign her twenty percent of the fault. The judge cut the punitive figure, and the parties settled for an undisclosed amount.

The story that travelled around the world was a verdict with the middle removed. The story that actually reached the court was a question about how much danger a company can put into an ordinary cup. That is what the jury was asked to decide. The giant check in the joke is the part nobody can show you.
