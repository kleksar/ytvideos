Coca-Cola made a sweeter drink that won its taste tests. Then customers started hoarding the one it was meant to replace.

Why would a winning recipe make them do that?

In 1985, Coke was losing ground to Pepsi. The company had tested a new recipe with nearly two hundred thousand people. In blind tastings, the new flavor came out ahead.

So on April twenty-third, Coca-Cola made a bigger decision than changing a flavor. It replaced the original drink Americans had bought for ninety-nine years.

The new can was called Coke. The old recipe was no longer an option.

At first, the company stood behind its results. But shoppers began looking for leftover bottles of the original. Protest groups appeared. People wrote angry letters. The customer hotline filled with calls from people asking for their old Coke back.

Some weren't arguing that the new drink tasted terrible. They were furious that the familiar one had been taken away.

A month into the launch, company staff were working overtime to answer the phones. Coca-Cola still told reporters its tests favored the sweeter formula.

And the protests kept coming.

On July eleventh, just seventy-nine days after introducing the change, Coca-Cola announced the return of the old recipe.

But there was a catch. The original now had to be called Coca-Cola Classic, because the new version was still on sale.

The company had not been wrong that many people liked a sip of the new drink. It had mistaken that answer for permission to remove the old one.

Coca-Cola had tried to win a taste test against Pepsi. Instead, its customers forced it to compete with itself.
