Why did a Japanese soldier need an order to stop fighting in 1974? And why was the man delivering it no longer in the army?

In 1944, intelligence officer Hiroo Onoda was posted to Lubang, an island in the Philippines. His orders included staying in the fight rather than surrendering.

The next year, Japan surrendered. Onoda and three other soldiers remained in the hills.

Leaflets said the war was over. Later, letters and photographs from their families said the same. Onoda's account says he treated these messages as enemy tricks. Whether he truly believed that for every one of those years is disputed.

The group survived by taking food from the island. They also fired on local people and police. This was not a harmless misunderstanding. Filipino families lost relatives.

One soldier gave himself up in 1950. Another died in a gunfight in 1954. Onoda's last companion was killed by police in 1972.

Now alone, Onoda was still armed. Search parties had failed to bring him out.

In February 1974, a young Japanese traveler named Norio Suzuki found him. Onoda said he would stop only if his former commander ordered it.

So Japan located Yoshimi Taniguchi, a former major who had become a bookseller. He returned to Lubang and formally relieved Onoda of his duties in March 1974.

Onoda surrendered. In Japan, he received a public welcome. On Lubang, his departure also meant the end of a long threat to ordinary families.

One piece of the familiar story remains uncertain: we cannot know exactly what Onoda believed. The documented attacks, the deaths, and the order that finally ended them are not uncertain.
