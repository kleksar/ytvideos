How can a soldier keep fighting almost thirty years after his country surrenders? Why would letters from his own family fail to bring him home? And who finally had the authority to make him stop?

In December 1944, a young Japanese intelligence officer, Hiroo Onoda, arrived on Lubang, a Philippine island southwest of Manila. Japan was losing its grip on the Philippines. Onoda's assignment was to conduct guerrilla operations and resist capture rather than surrender.

Within months, American and Filipino forces had retaken the island. Most Japanese troops were gone. Onoda withdrew into the hills with three other men.

Then came news that should have ended their mission. Japan surrendered in August 1945.

Onoda and his companions did not come out. They found leaflets announcing the end of the war, and later received messages urging them to give up their weapons. In Onoda's own telling, the messages looked like an enemy deception.

But the story becomes harder to understand when we follow what happened next.

In 1950, one of the four, Yuichi Akatsu, surrendered to Philippine authorities. He had reached the outside world. The other three remained armed.

In 1952, photographs and letters from their families were dropped over the island. More evidence arrived, and still the men stayed.

They hunted, gathered food and stole rice from the people of Lubang. They also attacked residents and clashed with police. For the families living there, this was not an exotic survival adventure. People were wounded and killed long after the Second World War had ended.

In 1954, Shoichi Shimada was killed in a clash with Philippine troops. Onoda was now down to one companion, Kinshichi Kozuka.

Think about the change in scale. What started as a four-man holdout had become two armed men surviving on an inhabited island. Every year they remained there, another year passed for the people who farmed its land.

Later testimony from islanders gives those years names and faces. Members of the Viaña family described an attack on their father, Emelio, while he was working with his sons. He died from his wounds; one of the boys was also shot. We know this story through later interviews with the family, not through a recording of the attack.

The violence did not end when the outside world stopped expecting a Japanese army to return.

In October 1972, Onoda and Kozuka were involved in an incident around rice crops. Kozuka was shot and killed by local police. Onoda escaped into the forest, alone.

Search efforts followed, but they did not bring him out.

In February 1974, a young traveler from Japan, Norio Suzuki, went looking for him. Somehow Suzuki reached the man whom organized searches had failed to recover. He came back with photographs, and a condition.

Onoda said he would surrender only when given an order by the commander who had assigned him his mission.

That officer was still alive. Yoshimi Taniguchi had left the army and was working as a bookseller.

Taniguchi traveled to Lubang. In March 1974 he formally released Onoda from military duty. After nearly twenty-nine years beyond Japan's surrender, Onoda laid down his weapons.

That is the strange mechanical answer to the question of how the holdout ended: more leaflets were not enough; a former commander brought an explicit order.

But it is not the whole answer to what happened.

Onoda returned to Japan to public attention and a hero's welcome from many. Philippine president Ferdinand Marcos pardoned him. On Lubang, survivors and relatives of people killed during those years had a very different reason to remember his name.

And the familiar explanation — that he simply had no idea the war was over — is contested. Onoda claimed to have distrusted the news. Later researchers and witnesses have challenged how much of that claim should be accepted. We cannot read his mind decades later.

The concrete facts are enough. Four men stayed in 1945. Three were eventually gone. Searchers and family messages failed. Suzuki found the last man, and Taniguchi finally ordered him to stop.

The war had ended for Japan in 1945. For families on Lubang, one part of its violence ended only when that former major came back with an order in 1974.
