Six people are coming over, and I've promised them fries. I can use a small air fryer or a full-size electric oven. Which one gets dinner onto the table sooner?

I call the fryer A and the oven B. You can pick now. I'm not comparing how quickly the first potato is cooked; my finish line is when all six portions are ready. Same frozen potatoes, the same target result, and no running both appliances.

I don't have a camera pointed at a kitchen experiment. I'm building a transparent model from a real consumer test, then changing the serving count. The measured part and my assumptions have to stay separate.

In October 2026, Which? tested 320 grams of frozen chips in both appliances. In its test, the air fryer finished in twenty-three minutes and used 0.287 kilowatt-hours. The electric oven needed thirty-three minutes and 0.863 kilowatt-hours. Those are observations for two portions in those specific machines. They don't tell us what happens when I fill an oven with food for a whole group.

If I'm cooking for two, A has a clear lead: ten minutes less, and substantially less electricity. I can understand why people keep choosing the smaller appliance.

But look at the basket. The model in that test wasn't meant to be piled to the rim. The maker recommended a maximum of 500 grams of chips. I'm putting 320 grams in each round, so I have space for only two of my proposed portions.

Six guests mean three rounds. A takes twenty-three minutes for each round in my simplified calculation. The first pair gets food at twenty-three minutes. The next pair gets theirs at forty-six. The last pair waits until minute sixty-nine.

That is the first trap. A won the single-round test, but it cannot cook all six servings in a single round.

For B, I give my model a different, openly hypothetical input: enough oven space for all 960 grams, spread out on trays, and forty minutes for that larger load. The lab did not measure that forty-minute cook. I need to test it in a real oven before treating it as a fact.

Under those stated rules, B finishes in forty minutes. A takes sixty-nine. The oven wins by twenty-nine minutes. Even before I ask whether the first air-fryer batch has gone cold, the finish line has moved.

What if only four people come? A needs two rounds, or forty-six minutes. For an assumed 640-gram oven load, I set B at thirty-seven. The oven still finishes first. For two, where both times come from the original test, the fryer wins. A smaller meal and a bigger meal have different winners.

Now I change the question without pretending I changed the result. Electricity is a second metric, not a way to rewrite the time winner.

Which? measured 0.287 kilowatt-hours for one air-fryer round. If each extra round uses the same energy, three rounds use 0.861. I assume the single six-portion oven load uses 0.92. In this model, A takes longer but saves a little electricity. That's not a dramatic saving. At a hypothetical electricity rate of roughly twenty-six pence per kilowatt-hour, the difference is around one and a half pence.

Why not just multiply the wattage printed on the machine by the total time? Because heating elements switch on and off as the appliance holds its temperature. Rated power isn't a direct reading of electricity used. For a genuine answer, I'd need a plug-in energy meter and a cooking test with the same food and a comparable finished result.

Then I add two more guests. Eight portions mean four 320-gram rounds for A: ninety-two minutes and 1.148 kilowatt-hours under the repeated-round assumption. For B, my new full-load assumption is forty-three minutes and 0.98 kilowatt-hours. In this particular model, the oven now wins on both time and energy.

The important word is model. A bigger fryer, a dual-basket machine, slower oven heating, or a tray that cannot cook evenly would change those numbers. And the quality of the fries matters. A pile of soggy potatoes is not a fair victory.

So for my six guests, where the objective is to serve everyone together, I'd choose B—provided the full tray really cooks properly. For one or two servings, the measured comparison favors A. I don't need a universal champion. I need the number of people, the capacity, and the metric before I press Start.

And then I hear the doorbell. I've barely cleared the plates before six more people arrive.
