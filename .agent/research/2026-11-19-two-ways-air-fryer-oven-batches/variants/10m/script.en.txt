I've got frozen fries, six hungry guests, and two ways to cook them. A is a compact air fryer. B is my full-size electric oven. I want everybody's food ready at the same time, so that's my finish line: minutes until the last portion is cooked. Which would you choose?

I'm going to keep one condition especially clear. This is a model, not footage of an experiment in my kitchen. I'll use independently measured numbers for a small serving, then state every assumption I make when I increase the quantity. An impressive number from a small appliance doesn't automatically survive a bigger meal.

Here's the real starting point. In a consumer test published in October 2026, Which? cooked 320 grams of frozen chips in an air fryer and an electric oven. The air fryer took twenty-three minutes and consumed 0.287 kilowatt-hours. The oven took thirty-three minutes and consumed 0.863. They were testing specific machines and that particular quantity. I haven't repeated the test myself.

For two portions, A wins my time metric by ten minutes. It also uses about a third of the electricity the oven used in that comparison. If I were eating alone or with one other person, I'd have a good reason to reach for the air fryer. This isn't an argument against the appliance; it's a warning about what I might infer from one attractive result.

Now I put six portions on the counter. Instead of vaguely saying 'a family meal,' I decide what a portion means. In this model, every two portions weigh 320 grams. Six portions weigh 960 grams. The air fryer in the Which? test had a manufacturer recommendation not to exceed 500 grams of chips. So I use one 320-gram load each time, rather than cramming everything into a basket and pretending it'll cook evenly.

That creates a queue. The first air-fryer batch takes twenty-three minutes. I empty the basket and start the second. After forty-six minutes, four people have fries. The last two must wait for a third round, and my clock reaches sixty-nine. That is three separate cooking rounds, not a slow 69-minute program where everybody's food somehow stays fresh.

What about the oven? The same experiment only tells me how long it took to cook 320 grams. It doesn't tell me how long 960 grams spread across trays would take. So I have to make a testable assumption: suppose this particular oven has enough space, and the larger load is properly cooked in forty minutes, including preheating. I can write forty in a model. I cannot claim Which? measured it.

Now the result is forty minutes for B versus sixty-nine for A. The smaller appliance was faster with two servings; the bigger oven is faster with six. I didn't change the finish line or sneak extra help into the oven. I changed only the quantity, then allowed each appliance to use its own stated capacity.

I should also tell you what the model leaves out. At minute twenty-three, the first pair of fries is ready. If I put those on plates, they'll sit while the second and third rounds cook. If I keep them warm, that may consume extra electricity or change how crisp they are. The model's sixty-nine minutes measures when the last batch finishes. It doesn't certify that all six people receive equally hot, crisp fries.

Let's try four portions instead. A needs two twenty-three-minute rounds, or forty-six minutes. For B I assume a 640-gram oven load finishes in thirty-seven minutes. Again, thirty-seven is a hypothetical input. In that scenario the oven already wins by nine minutes. The break comes between the measured single-batch result and an assumed larger load. A viewer shouldn't confuse that threshold with a universal law about every kitchen.

What if I cared about the first serving instead of the last? Then the air fryer hands me the first two portions at minute twenty-three, while the modeled oven takes forty to complete six. A wins the first-plate race even while B wins the everyone-finished race. Those are different metrics. I have to announce which race matters before I see who wins, not change the race after the answer.

Now for the surprise I nearly missed: being faster isn't the same as using less electricity. I can set a second, separate calculation beside the time result. Which? measured 0.287 kilowatt-hours for that single air-fryer test. A kilowatt-hour is energy used over time; it isn't the appliance's wattage sticker. I won't claim that another serving automatically uses precisely the same amount. I'll assume it for a simple repeatable model, and show what depends on that assumption.

Three fryer rounds at 0.287 each come to 0.861 kilowatt-hours. For my single 960-gram oven load, I assume 0.92. So with six portions, the oven is twenty-nine minutes faster, but the fryer uses a little less electricity. This is an intentionally conditional result. No meter has confirmed either appliance's energy use for my larger modeled load.

At an illustrative electricity price of roughly twenty-six pence per kilowatt-hour, the difference between 0.861 and 0.92 is about one and a half pence. That matters because it punctures another expectation. A big change in cooking time need not mean a big change in the bill. I'm comparing electricity for one meal, not promising a household annual saving.

Then two more people join us. Eight portions mean four loads through the fryer. Under the unchanged repeated-round rule, that's ninety-two minutes and 1.148 kilowatt-hours. I give B a new explicitly modeled eight-portion result: forty-three minutes and 0.98 kilowatt-hours, provided all the food really cooks on the available trays. The oven wins both metrics now. It doesn't just catch up with the little fryer; the calculation crosses over.

Notice that I stated the eight-portion oven input rather than pretending to derive it from the two-portion test. An oven could need more time or electricity if air couldn't circulate between full trays. A model that assumes an impossible tray arrangement would be a neat piece of arithmetic and a poor kitchen comparison.

There is another hidden assumption inside A. I used the same energy figure for the first, second, third and fourth rounds. But the first round may include warming the fryer from cold. Later rounds begin with a hot basket and a warm interior. They might use less energy than 0.287 each. Conversely, a different recipe, an opened drawer or a crowded basket could take longer. That means my energy crossover is a question to test, not a historical measurement.

The power ratings on the boxes won't solve that uncertainty. A device marked 1,500 watts isn't necessarily drawing 1,500 watts every second it is cooking. Many heaters cycle as the temperature is maintained. The oven's bigger printed rating can look alarming, but a wattage label alone doesn't tell me the total energy of a cooking session. I need consumption over the whole run, with preheating and the actual food included.

A dual-basket air fryer would change the queue, too. If it could cook four of my portions in one round, six portions could take two rounds instead of three. That sounds quicker. But a larger machine may also use more power, and its cooking programs aren't necessarily identical to my original measured example. I can't borrow the small fryer's 0.287 figure and silently assign it to a double-size appliance.

What would a fair kitchen test look like? I'd weigh out the same amount of the same frozen fries, choose comparable doneness, and record the minutes until every portion is finished. I'd measure energy with equipment appropriate for each circuit, including the oven's installed connection. And I'd repeat the cook, because a single run can be affected by starting temperature or how the food is arranged. Those measurements would replace my invented large-load inputs; they wouldn't automatically match them.

I also need to look at the food. In the independent test, neither appliance delivered perfectly even chips. Some air-fryer chips were underdone, while some oven chips were too crisp. If a loaded oven finishes in forty minutes but leaves one tray pale, it hasn't met the same standard. If I run the fryer in three rounds and the first two go cold, that affects the meal too. Those are practical constraints, not decoration in the final scene.

And I shouldn't mistake the price of one cooking session for the cost of buying a new appliance. If I already own both, today's electricity and time make sense as my immediate comparison. If I have to purchase an air fryer just to save a penny or two on certain meals, that up-front price changes the decision. This model doesn't calculate a payback period because I haven't supplied a purchase price or my actual weekly use.

So my answer is conditional, but it isn't empty. For two portions, the independently tested small batch favors A. In my six-portion model, B reaches the finish line first while A barely wins on energy. At eight portions, with the explicit oven assumptions, B wins both. The lesson isn't 'always use the oven' or 'always buy an air fryer.' It's that a test of one batch answers a question about one batch.

I choose B for tonight's six guests, assuming those full trays really work in my oven. Before I commit to a real energy claim, I would measure my own machines. I start bringing the plates to the table, and the doorbell rings again: six more people have arrived, looking for fries.
