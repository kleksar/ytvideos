Do I answer every message the moment it arrives, or ignore my inbox until I finish an hour of work?

I keep making the same bargain with myself. I'll answer one quick message, then get back to the thing I was doing. The reply might take two minutes. But the important question is what happens between pressing Send and actually working again.

I'm going to test two choices on the same imaginary workday. It isn't footage of a real experiment, and my numbers aren't measurements of anybody's concentration. I want a transparent comparison that I can change without pretending I know exactly how your brain works.

Here's the starting position. I have one task needing sixty minutes of uninterrupted effort. Four ordinary messages arrive at minutes ten, twenty, thirty-five and fifty. Reading and answering each costs two minutes. If a message pulls me away while I'm working, getting my place back takes three additional minutes. I chose three minutes for the example. It could be more, less, or zero for you.

My first method is to answer as soon as the alert arrives.

At minute ten, I've completed ten minutes of the task. I answer until twelve. Then I spend minutes twelve through fifteen getting back into it. At minute twenty, another message comes. The second reply runs to twenty-two, and I'm ready to concentrate again at twenty-five.

Notice what's happening. The clock is moving even when I'm not adding to the finished work. After two messages I've done fifteen minutes of the task, but twenty-five minutes have passed.

The third arrives at thirty-five. I answer until thirty-seven, recover until forty, and keep working. The fourth comes at fifty. Another reply, another return. I finally sit down with no more alerts at minute fifty-five. I've only completed thirty-five of the sixty work minutes.

The task ends at minute eighty. I answered everyone quickly, but I finished my own work twenty minutes later than its uninterrupted finish time.

Now I rewind the exact same clock. Same four arrival times. Same task. Same two-minute replies. No bonus efficiency because I'm suddenly in a good mood.

This time my phone is silent for the task. I work from zero to sixty. Then I answer the four messages in order: the first is done at sixty-two, the second at sixty-four, the third at sixty-six, and the fourth at sixty-eight.

Method A finishes everything at eighty. Method B at sixty-eight. If my goal is simply to clear the task and the replies, B wins by twelve minutes. That saving is the four three-minute return costs. It is not eight minutes of magically faster typing.

But look at the first sender. With A, they get an answer at minute twelve. With B, they wait until minute sixty-two. I didn't make the delay disappear. I moved it onto the people waiting for me.

What if the first message is about an appointment that needs confirmation before minute twenty-five? Now I have an actual deadline. The starting schedule and amount of work stay exactly the same; only that message's latest acceptable reply time changes.

A passes that test at minute twelve. B fails it at sixty-two. If missing that deadline is unacceptable, the twelve-minute saving can't make B the winning method. This is why I have to choose the rule of the comparison before I see the stopwatch.

Here's another useful change. Suppose returning to work costs no extra time at all. I can jump straight from sending a reply back into the task. A now spends sixty minutes working and eight replying, and finishes everything at sixty-eight. B also finishes everything at sixty-eight. The big total-time win has vanished. B still protects the hour-long task from interruptions, but it hasn't shortened the combined workload.

And if the return cost is six minutes instead of three, the four interruptions add twenty-four minutes. A ends at ninety-two while B still ends at sixty-eight, assuming all replies can wait. None of those figures is a claim about how long people normally lose to a notification. They follow from the rules we picked.

So I can finally answer the original choice without pretending it's a productivity law. When all four messages are genuinely non-urgent and switching back takes time, I prefer to reply after the work. When one message has a real deadline, I must allow for it before protecting my focus block.

The useful question isn't just, "Which timeline ends first?" It's, "Whose clock am I trying not to miss?"
