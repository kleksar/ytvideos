Two twelve-inch pizzas for nineteen dollars, or one eighteen-inch pizza for twenty. I'm at the order screen, and the cheaper pair looks like the obvious choice.

But I'm not comparing the number of boxes. I want the most pizza for each dollar. So I make the two options fair: same style, same thickness, same toppings, and perfectly round pizzas. These are example menu prices, not prices I measured in a restaurant.

I call the big pizza A and the two smaller ones B. B gives me two full circles. That looks like more food until I put them side by side at the correct scale.

One twelve-inch pizza covers about 113 square inches. Two cover 226. The eighteen-inch pizza covers 254. One box holds about twelve and a half percent more surface than both smaller pizzas together.

Here's why: eighteen inches describes the width, not the amount of pizza. The area grows with the square of that width.

Now I bring the prices back. A gives me about 12.7 square inches per dollar. B gives me about 11.9. The twenty-dollar pizza is actually the better value in this example, even though it costs a dollar more.

Then I change one thing. The two-pizza deal drops from nineteen dollars to seventeen. Now B gives me about 13.3 square inches per dollar. It wins. The crossover is roughly seventeen dollars and seventy-eight cents for the pair.

I'm not pretending a real pizza is perfectly uniform. Crust, thickness, different toppings, and what I can actually eat all matter. I'm testing surface area per dollar, not taste or weight.

At the original prices, I choose the single large pizza. At seventeen dollars for the pair, I choose two. I empty my cart to check the offer one more time, and there are my two choices again.
