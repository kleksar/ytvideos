The menu on my phone offers two twelve-inch pizzas for nineteen dollars. One eighteen-inch pizza costs twenty. I need dinner, not a geometry lesson, so I almost choose the cheaper pair.

Then I notice the number that actually matters: diameter. Two boxes might mean more slices, but do they give me more food for my money? I'm going to compare the same kind of round pizza, with the same thickness and toppings. All the menu prices in this story are examples. I haven't bought or weighed these pizzas.

I label the single large pizza A and the pair B. Before I count anything, I look at the circles. B has two centres and two sets of crust. A has one giant circle. If I put two twelve-inch rulers next to an eighteen-inch ruler, twenty-four beats eighteen. But that isn't what a diameter measures.

I take one twelve-inch pizza. Its radius is six inches, the distance from the middle to the edge. The area is about 113 square inches. Two of those make 226. The eighteen-inch pizza has a nine-inch radius, so its area is about 254.

I have my first surprise. A single large pizza covers about twenty-eight more square inches than the pair. Relative to B, that's an extra twelve and a half percent. The big one is only fifty percent wider than one small one, yet its area is more than double the area of a single small pizza. When I increase the diameter, I expand the entire circle, not just a thin strip across the middle.

That still doesn't tell me what to order. B costs nineteen dollars, and A costs twenty. I care about how much pizza each dollar buys. I divide the areas by the prices. A gives me roughly 12.7 square inches per dollar. B gives me 11.9.

At this menu, I win with A. I pay one dollar more and receive more than enough additional area to make up for it. But I'm close to pressing Order when a discount appears for the two smaller pizzas.

The pair now costs seventeen dollars. I keep the sizes and every other assumption fixed. B's value rises to 13.3 square inches per dollar. A stays at 12.7. The cheaper pair has finally won, and I didn't have to change the pizza itself.

I can find the exact turning point. Since B has eight ninths of A's area, it must cost less than eight ninths of A's twenty-dollar price to be better value. Eight ninths of twenty is about seventeen dollars and seventy-eight cents. Above that price for the pair, A wins on area per dollar. Below it, B wins. Right on the boundary, they're tied by this metric.

Then I look at the edges. A pizza isn't covered in cheese all the way to the rim. Suppose both sizes have a one-inch band of plain crust, consistently measured in from the edge. In that simplified picture, A has about 201 square inches inside the crust. Both small pizzas together have only about 157. Because the smaller pizzas have more edge for their area, the large one has an even bigger advantage in topping-covered surface. That doesn't prove a real kitchen adds toppings uniformly; I'm testing another stated assumption.

Now I have a problem that the calculator can't settle. What if I need two different flavours? What if one pizza is thicker, or the party won't eat the leftovers? An area calculation doesn't measure taste, pizza weight, or what my guests will actually finish.

For the original choice, though, I know my rule: same style, same thickness, and the most total surface per dollar. Under those conditions I select the twenty-dollar eighteen-inch pizza. With the seventeen-dollar pair offer, I change my order to B.

I clear the cart, because I want to test whether I can spot that difference without being fooled by the number of boxes. The menu returns to the same two choices.
