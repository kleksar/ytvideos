I have four lunches to pack, and the method that finishes them faster makes the first person wait a hundred seconds longer. I want to understand that trade without guessing about what really happens in a kitchen.

The everyday scene is familiar. Four identical sandwiches, four wrappers, one person preparing them. A friend needs to leave soon with lunch; the other three sandwiches only have to be ready when the bags are collected. If I look at the total order, I want the fourth sandwich finished as soon as possible. If I look at the person waiting, the first completed sandwich matters more. Those are two different promises, and I can test both with the same inputs.

Here are my two methods. A is one-piece flow: I take a portion of bread, add its filling, wrap that sandwich completely, and only then begin another. B is batching: I prepare the bread for all four, add all four fillings, and finally wrap them. Before I calculate anything, choose the one you think will win the race to finish every lunch. Then ask yourself whether you made the same choice for someone who needs only the first one.

This is a deliberately simplified model, not footage of me cooking or a real stopwatch experiment. I fix three jobs per sandwich: ten seconds preparing the bread, fifteen seconds adding filling, and five seconds wrapping. That is thirty seconds of direct work per sandwich. I also assume I lose six seconds whenever I move between the bread, filling and wrapping stations. B needs twenty-five extra seconds before starting to lay out and label its batch trays. A uses the stations as they are. One worker, no parallel hands, same ingredients and packaging. The model does not include waiting for a stove, washing equipment, food temperature or different recipes.

I draw two timelines. Both start with all four sandwiches unmade. Every movement and setup cost is counted, including the return to the bread station between sandwiches in A. I measure first-ready time and all-ready time, with all-ready time declared as the main winner metric.

Under A, sandwich one takes thirty seconds of work and twelve seconds of switching: bread to filling, filling to wrapping. It is complete at forty-two seconds. I can pass it over immediately. But to begin sandwich two, I switch back to bread for six seconds. Then I spend another forty-two seconds doing bread, filling and wrapping. The second is complete at ninety seconds. The third finishes at one hundred and thirty-eight, and the fourth at one hundred and eighty-six.

The pattern matters more than the final number. The direct work for four sandwiches is always a hundred and twenty seconds. A adds eleven station changes, for sixty-six seconds. Two changes within each sandwich give me eight. Returning from wrapping to bread between the four sandwiches adds another three. Eight plus three is eleven, and one hundred and twenty plus sixty-six is one hundred and eighty-six.

For B, I reset everything. I first spend twenty-five seconds arranging four labeled trays. Four bread portions require forty seconds. I switch once, spending six seconds, and work on four fillings for sixty. I switch a second time for six seconds and wrap four sandwiches for twenty. I finish at one hundred and fifty-seven seconds.

The comparison is unambiguous under the assumptions: A takes one hundred and eighty-six seconds to finish all four; B takes one hundred and fifty-seven. Batch processing saves twenty-nine seconds. The food itself took the same one hundred and twenty seconds to assemble. The difference came only from switching and initial setup.

Now I look at the first sandwich. B's first completed lunch waits behind all four breads and all four fillings. The trays use twenty-five seconds; bread takes forty; the first station change takes six; filling takes sixty; the second change takes six; and the first wrapper takes five. The sum is one hundred and forty-two seconds.

A handed over its first lunch at forty-two seconds. If I only cared about the person waiting at my counter, choosing B would have cost a full hundred seconds of extra waiting. Yet at the instant B finishes its entire order, A still has one sandwich unwrapped. It is not that one timeline has a hidden mistake. They describe different deadlines.

What happens if my order shrinks? I keep every parameter fixed, including B's twenty-five-second setup. With one sandwich, A needs forty-two seconds, while B needs sixty-seven. The batch technique has no work to consolidate, so the setup is wasted. With two sandwiches, A needs ninety seconds and B ninety-seven: A still wins. With three, however, A climbs to one hundred and thirty-eight seconds while B only needs one hundred and twenty-seven. Three sandwiches are enough to repay the cost of preparing those trays. Four makes the advantage twenty-nine seconds.

The turning point is not a lucky number selected after seeing the answer. I can express each method with the same formula. For n sandwiches, direct work is thirty n seconds. A makes three n minus one station changes. At six seconds per change, its total is forty-eight n minus six. B makes just two changes but pays twenty-five seconds of setup, so it takes thirty n plus thirty-seven. At n equals two, A is faster. At n equals three, B is faster. I can check those statements against the individual timelines, without adjusting the rules.

Now I stretch the order to eight sandwiches, still one worker and the same assumptions. A takes three hundred and seventy-eight seconds. B takes two hundred and seventy-seven. B has saved a hundred and one seconds by not cycling repeatedly through the stations. But the first batch sandwich is not ready until two hundred and forty-two seconds. A still has its first at forty-two. Larger batches magnify both sides of the trade: better final throughput, longer delay before the first item comes out.

That still does not make B a law of nature. I run one sensitivity test, changing just one assumption: a station change costs one second instead of six. The bread, filling, wrapping and the twenty-five-second tray setup stay fixed.

With four sandwiches, A now spends one hundred and twenty seconds doing the work and just eleven seconds switching. It finishes in one hundred and thirty-one. B uses the same one hundred and twenty seconds, two seconds switching and twenty-five preparing trays. It finishes in one hundred and forty-seven. The winner flips. At four sandwiches, the convenience of moving between stations has erased the batch advantage. In this new model it takes ten sandwiches for batching to pull ahead again, by only two seconds.

I want to avoid a tempting mistake here. None of these numbers are a prediction that your lunch takes a hundred and fifty-seven seconds. The six-second station changes and the twenty-five-second setup are chosen inputs for a thought experiment. Your counter may be small enough that moving takes almost no time. You might make four different sandwiches, so keeping the fillings separate is more important than reducing movements. A single tray may be enough, or labels may already be printed. Hygiene and ingredient handling can add requirements this stripped-down clock does not include. I have not measured any of that.

There's also a difference between a complete order and a person being served. An assembly line can be efficient at finishing all the work and still be an unpleasant way to help the first person in a queue. For a waiting person, a shorter first-ready time is a real benefit; for four lunches leaving together, it isn't. Neither concern cancels the other.

I could imagine a third, hybrid approach: finish one urgent sandwich, then batch the remaining three. That might be a useful experiment later, but it is not one of these two methods, and I won't invent a winning time for it here. Today's A/B comparison stays on its original rules.

So what have I actually learned? I can't label a process efficient until I decide what I need from it. A protects the first person, and with small orders or cheap station changes it may also finish everything first. B can beat repeated station changes when there is enough work to share its setup cost. For these four identical sandwiches and the six-second switching model, B gets the entire order out twenty-nine seconds sooner; A gets lunch into one person's hands one hundred seconds sooner.

When I am packing lunches again, I'll look at the person waiting and the bags that must leave together. Those are two different finish lines. That's why the method that loses at the very end can still be the one I choose first.
