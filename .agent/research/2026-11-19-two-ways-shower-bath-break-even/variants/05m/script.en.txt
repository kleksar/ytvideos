If I want to save water, should I take a shower instead of a bath? How long can that shower last before the answer changes? And would a different showerhead change it again?

I normally reach for the shower without thinking. A bath looks like a whole tub of water. A shower sends the water straight down the drain. It feels as if the smaller-looking option must be cheaper. But a drain doesn't tell me how much has passed through it.

So I set up a comparison I can check with a calculator. I am not claiming to have measured a real bathroom. I give myself one bathroom, one person, and the same goal: finish washing. The scoreboard measures water drawn, not comfort, cleaning quality, gas, electricity, or time spent getting ready.

Method A is the shower. Method B is a bath that takes thirty US gallons to fill. That bath volume is a published household benchmark, but in my model it is a fixed input, not a claim about every tub.

For the shower, I choose a head flowing at two and a half gallons per minute. That is a standard reference rate, not a secret measurement of my own shower. I also choose an eight-minute wash. Now I can let each method run from the same starting point.

A goes first. At the end of eight minutes, the shower has used twenty gallons. B fills the tub once and uses thirty. A wins by ten. If I stopped here, I could make a tidy little video saying showers always save water.

But I know exactly which assumption made A win: I turned the water off after eight minutes.

I reset both sides. Same thirty-gallon bath, same showerhead, same one-person wash. This time I leave the shower running for fifteen minutes. The shower drains away thirty-seven and a half gallons. B still uses thirty. The method I thought was saving water has lost by seven and a half gallons.

There was no surprise leak. I simply ran the same tap longer. The bathtub is a fixed amount in this model; the shower keeps adding water for every minute it runs.

That gives me a better question than "bath or shower?" I want to find the precise moment the leader changes.

At two and a half gallons a minute, the shower catches the thirty-gallon bath at twelve minutes. Below twelve, A uses less. At exactly twelve, it is a draw. Above twelve, B uses less. Neither method is the permanent winner.

But perhaps I have been unfair to the shower. Modern efficient showerheads are rated for lower flow, and the WaterSense label, for example, sets a maximum of two gallons per minute.

I change only that one number, reset both methods, and keep my fifteen-minute shower. Now A uses thirty gallons, exactly the same as the bath. What looked like a loss turns into a tie. At ten minutes with this head, the shower would use twenty gallons and beat the bath again.

Then I notice something about B. I had treated the bathtub as if thirty gallons were unavoidable. It isn't. The level I choose matters too.

For a separate model round, I keep the original two-and-a-half-gallon showerhead but allow a shallower, twenty-four-gallon fill. Eight minutes in the shower still uses twenty, so A wins. Ten minutes uses twenty-five, so B wins. The crossover is now nine minutes and thirty-six seconds. The bathtub hasn't become magically efficient; I have changed how much water I put in it.

Four comparisons, and there isn't one honest slogan that covers them all.

If I want an answer for my actual home, I have to measure two things: the amount I fill my tub, and the flow from my showerhead. I could time how long it takes to fill a marked container and convert that to water per minute, then note how long the shower actually runs. If I pause the water to soap up, I should count the running time, not the total time in the bathroom.

These numbers tell me only about volume. They do not tell me which feels better, what the heated water costs, or which one cleans better. Those are different tests.

I started with a choice between two fixtures. I ended up with a rule I can use: compare a fixed fill with a running tap. My shower wins only while the water going through it stays below the amount I would have put in the bath.
