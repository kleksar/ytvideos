Is a shower always the lower-water choice? Where is the exact point at which a bath becomes better? And what would I need to know before using that answer in my own home?

I have always thought of a bath as an enormous amount of water sitting in one place. A shower looks smaller. The water disappears down the drain almost as soon as it falls, so it is easy for me to stop noticing it. But the drain is not a measuring cup. Water that disappears has still passed through the meter.

I want to compare two ways of doing the same everyday job: getting washed. A is a shower, B is a bath. I'm building a transparent model here, not pretending I ran a real experiment or tested how clean either method leaves me.

I have to decide what winning means before seeing a result. My metric is the total volume of water drawn from the supply for one wash. I won't award points for speed, warmth, relaxation, electricity, or how well soap rinses off. Those things may matter in real life; this comparison doesn't measure them.

To make the first round reproducible, I write down two inputs. B is a bath filled with thirty US gallons. That's about a hundred and fourteen litres. A uses a showerhead delivering two and a half gallons each minute. Both are plausible published reference figures, not measurements of my own bathroom. They're numbers I've deliberately chosen for the model.

Both sides start with one person and the same task. I give A eight minutes with the water running. B fills its tub once. B needs thirty gallons, while A uses two and a half times eight: twenty gallons. If I only cared about this result, I would be finished. A saved ten gallons.

This is why the familiar advice to take a shower instead of a bath often makes sense. Portland's municipal water guidance describes an average shower as about eight minutes and seventeen gallons, compared with roughly thirty gallons for an average bath. That is a general observation, not a measurement of my imaginary bathroom. My modeled shower happens to use twenty because I chose a different flow.

Now I try to break my own result. I leave everything alone except one thing: the time A keeps running.

At ten minutes, A reaches twenty-five gallons. Still ahead. At twelve, it's thirty. A draw. At fifteen, the shower has used thirty-seven and a half gallons. I haven't upgraded the bath, bought anything, or increased its fill. And yet B now uses seven and a half gallons less than A.

The reason is visible without a complicated chart. B is a fixed fill. Sitting in it longer doesn't automatically add more water. A is a running tap. Every extra minute sends another two and a half gallons down the drain.

I can calculate the precise boundary: thirty divided by two and a half equals twelve minutes. Below twelve, A draws less water. Above twelve, B draws less. At twelve, neither wins. This is an answer, but only for the conditions I wrote down.

I could make a serious mistake by repeating the twelve-minute rule in every bathroom. My own bathtub might be smaller or larger. I might fill it halfway. So I reset the model and change only the amount in B.

This time B uses twenty-four gallons for a shallower fill. I'm not claiming every shallow bath needs twenty-four gallons. It is an explicit new assumption. At eight minutes, the original shower uses twenty, so A still wins, but by only four. At ten minutes, A uses twenty-five, and B wins by one.

The new crossover is twenty-four divided by two and a half: nine point six minutes, or nine minutes and thirty-six seconds. Nothing dramatic happened inside the bathtub. The result shifted because I put less water in it.

Then I give the shower its own improvement. The US EPA's WaterSense label limits showerhead flow to no more than two gallons a minute. I choose that exact flow as another model input, and restore B to its original thirty-gallon fill.

Fifteen minutes under A now takes thirty gallons. That's a tie. At twelve minutes it takes twenty-four, so A wins by six. The boundary against the thirty-gallon bath is now fifteen minutes, not twelve.

But the label doesn't guarantee victory. If I compare this same two-gallon showerhead against the shallow twenty-four-gallon bath instead, the boundary goes back to twelve minutes. Same showerhead, different fill level, different conclusion.

At this point I notice something else I nearly hid. What counts as a fifteen-minute shower? If I spend fifteen minutes in the bathroom but turn the water off while shampooing, the actual running time might be much shorter.

Suppose it flows for only eight of those fifteen minutes. At the original two-and-a-half-gallon rate, I use twenty gallons, not thirty-seven and a half. The result changes without changing the fixture or how long I was getting ready. The water bill doesn't care how long I stood on the bathroom mat. It cares how long water flowed.

And what about the cold water I let run while waiting for the shower to warm? Water sent down the drain before the wash is still water drawn from the supply.

For a separate sensitivity test, I assume two gallons go down the drain during warm-up, then the original shower runs for eight minutes. The total is twenty-two gallons, not twenty. Against a thirty-gallon bath, A still wins. But the break-even running time becomes eleven point two minutes, because two gallons have already been used before the main wash.

I must be equally fair to B. Filling a bath can waste water while someone adjusts its temperature too. I cannot add a penalty to A and assume B always avoids it. This is a hypothetical model variation, not an observation about which bathroom really wastes more during warm-up.

Even repeated use changes the scale without changing the logic. One hundred original eight-minute showers consume two thousand gallons under these fixed assumptions. One hundred thirty-gallon baths use three thousand. A difference of one thousand. Now repeat one hundred fifteen-minute showers at the original flow: that is three thousand seven hundred and fifty gallons. The direction of the difference reverses. The multiplication is certain; the idea that a person repeats exactly the same routine a hundred times is only an assumption.

How would I find the numbers for my actual home? I could catch water from the shower in a marked container for a known amount of time and turn the result into flow per minute. If a container held one and a quarter gallons after thirty seconds, that would mean a steady rate of two and a half gallons per minute. This is an example of a possible measurement, not a measurement I secretly made.

I would also count the minutes the water actually runs, including warm-up and rinsing, instead of timing my whole bathroom visit. For the tub I'd need to know the volume at the level I actually fill, rather than its advertised capacity to the brim. Calibrated fill marks or a reliable meter could provide that input. Until I have it, even a perfect shower measurement can't decide the comparison.

One last limit matters. Less water is not automatically the same as less heating energy, less money, or a more comfortable wash. Water temperature, heating equipment, utility rates, and personal needs are outside this single-metric model. I'd need additional inputs and perhaps a different test to answer those questions.

I began thinking I had to choose between two fixtures. I ended up comparing a fixed volume in a tub with a flow multiplied by running time. No fixture owns the winning label. If I want to use less water, I first check how much I fill the bath, how fast the shower flows, and how long I leave it on.
