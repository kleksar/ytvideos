I needed five hundred grams of berries for dessert. Two boxes were on the shelf. The larger one was cheaper per kilo. Yet buying it would cost me more.

I'm not measuring a real supermarket. I'm testing two shopping rules with fixed prices.

A is a small, 250-gram box for €1.80. B is a large, 750-gram box for €4.20. They're the same berries, and I can buy more than one box.

Before I show the bill, choose a side. Would you take the cheaper-looking small boxes, or the big box with the lower price per kilo?

The labels tell me A costs €7.20 per kilo, while B costs just €5.60. So B really is cheaper for each gram. That part isn't a trick.

But I only need five hundred grams. With A, I buy two boxes and pay €3.60. With B, I must buy a whole large box and pay €4.20. The supposedly better unit price costs sixty cents more at checkout, and I have 250 grams left over.

Now I change just one thing: I need 750 grams. Three small boxes cost €5.40. One large box still costs €4.20. B wins by €1.20.

And there's another surprise. At one thousand grams, four small boxes cost €7.20, while two large boxes cost €8.40. A wins again.

This is a model, not proof about every store. If I can use the leftovers, or mix box sizes, the choice can change.
