My recipe calls for five hundred grams of berries. The bigger box is cheaper per kilogram. Does that mean I should buy it?

I'm testing an imaginary shelf, not reporting prices from a real supermarket. Both boxes contain the same berries. A is 250 grams for €1.80. B is 750 grams for €4.20. I can take several sealed boxes.

B really is cheaper by weight: €5.60 a kilo instead of €7.20. But I want to know what I'll pay at checkout. I'll test two rules: buy only A, or buy only B. I need enough for the recipe, and I have to pay for whole boxes.

At five hundred grams, I take two A boxes and pay €3.60. With B, I have to buy a whole 750-gram box for €4.20. That's sixty cents more, even though its price per kilogram is lower.

Now I invite more people over. The recipe grows to 750 grams. Three A boxes cost €5.40. One B costs €4.20. This time B wins by €1.20.

So has the bigger box taken the lead for good? I test one kilogram. Four A boxes cost €7.20. One B isn't enough; two cost €8.40. A wins again. The price per gram didn't change. The number of whole boxes did.

But I've left out one option: mixing the sizes. For that same kilogram, I can take one A and one B. That's exactly a kilogram for €6.00, cheaper than either all-small or all-large basket.

So A and B were two shopping rules, not the only possible purchases. This model doesn't tell me what real shops charge. And if I use the leftovers tomorrow, I may value them differently.

For today's dessert, I count the whole boxes, including combinations, before declaring a winner. When I face the shelf again, I don't start with the big discount label. First, I check the recipe.
