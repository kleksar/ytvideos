The bigger box was cheaper per kilo. I bought it to save money on dessert. In my test, that decision would have raised the bill.

Here's the everyday problem I want to solve: I need an exact amount of berries, but the shop only sells sealed boxes. Does the lower unit price always mean I'll spend less?

This is an illustrated calculation, not footage or measured prices from a real store. Both choices use the same berries and the same shelf. The small box holds 250 grams and costs €1.80. The large box holds 750 grams and costs €4.20. I can buy several boxes of one size. For the head-to-head test, I won't mix sizes, and unused berries have no value for tonight's dessert.

I'll judge each method by a single number: the cash I have to pay to get at least the quantity I need.

Method A is to fill my basket with small boxes. Method B is to fill it with large boxes, trusting the cheaper price per kilo. Pick A or B before I open the imaginary checkout.

First I check the shelf labels. Four small boxes make a kilogram and cost €7.20. A large box works out to €5.60 per kilogram. So B has a genuine discount per gram. If I use every berry, that discount matters.

But I don't need a kilogram. My recipe calls for 500 grams.

With A, I put two small boxes in the basket. That's exactly 500 grams, and the bill is €3.60. With B, I cannot buy two-thirds of a sealed box. I take one large box, pay €4.20, and carry home 250 grams I didn't need. A wins by sixty cents.

The trap isn't a false price label. It's the gap between the quantity on the shelf and the quantity on my shopping list.

Now I invite more people. I reset the same shelf and the same prices; nothing else changes. The recipe needs 750 grams.

A takes three small boxes: €5.40. B takes one large box: €4.20. This time the bulk option wins by €1.20. I haven't changed the relative unit prices at all. I've only changed how much I need.

At this point it's tempting to declare that a larger recipe makes B the permanent winner. So I try a full kilogram.

Four small boxes cost €7.20, exactly the quantity required. But one large box isn't enough. Two large boxes cost €8.40 and bring 1.5 kilograms home. Now A wins again by €1.20.

It's not a neat dividing line where large packs become better forever. The bill jumps whenever the required quantity crosses the capacity of a box. Those jumps happen at different points for A and B.

To see the difference, I look at two more shopping lists. At 1.25 kilograms, five small boxes cost €9. Two large boxes still cost €8.40, so B comes back by sixty cents. At 1.5 kilograms, A costs €10.80 and B stays at €8.40. B has a bigger lead.

The winner depends on the exact amount, not just whether I'm shopping for one person or a crowd.

There's an important catch to the experiment. I forced myself to choose only small boxes or only large ones so the two rules could be compared honestly. A real shopper could combine them. At one kilogram, one large and one small box cost just €6. That's cheaper than either pure method.

So A and B aren't the two best possible baskets. They're two deliberately simple rules. I can test them; I shouldn't pretend one is a universal shopping strategy.

Leftovers also change the question. If I know I'll eat the spare berries tomorrow, some of that extra purchase is useful. If they spoil, the extra weight doesn't save me anything. Neither outcome is measured here.

My conclusion is narrower, but more useful: a low unit price helps compare the contents of packages. It does not, by itself, tell me what tonight's recipe will cost. For that, I need the package sizes, whole-box counts, and total at checkout.

I can still use the price-per-kilo label. I just don't let it make the decision before I read how much I actually need.
