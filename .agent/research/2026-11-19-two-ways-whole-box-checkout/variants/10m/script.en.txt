I'm standing in front of two imaginary boxes of berries, trying to spend less on dessert. The larger box has the better price per kilogram. That sounds like the sensible choice. But with one shopping list it raises my bill; with another list it saves me money; and with a third it loses again.

I want to know why the same discount can produce three different answers. And I want a rule I could actually use before reaching the checkout.

This is a model, not a hidden-camera experiment or a record of actual supermarket prices. I'm inventing two prices so I can show every step. Both boxes contain identical berries. Both are always available, I can buy several, and I'm buying for one planned use. There are no promotions, membership fees, or delivery charges in the model.

Method A means I buy only small boxes: 250 grams for €1.80 each. Method B means I buy only large boxes: 750 grams for €4.20 each. I won't mix the sizes until after I've judged A against B.

Here's my scorecard. I need at least a certain weight. I pay for whole sealed boxes. The winner is whichever method gets me enough berries for less cash today. Extra berries don't count as a reward in that scorecard. That's a deliberately narrow measure, because I want to separate the price of buying dessert from the future value of leftovers.

Which side would you choose? A, the smaller boxes with a lower sticker price, or B, the larger boxes with the better price per kilogram?

The first calculation almost makes the answer look obvious. A kilogram of berries would cost €7.20 if every gram were bought at the small-box rate. The large-box rate is €5.60 per kilogram. So B is genuinely cheaper per gram. I don't need to accuse the label of lying. Instead, I need to ask what happens when I can't purchase the exact number of grams I need.

My first recipe calls for 500 grams. A gives me exactly that with two boxes: €1.80 plus €1.80, or €3.60. B gives me only one possible minimum purchase: a whole 750-gram box for €4.20. I get more berries, but I hand over sixty cents more.

Under my stated measure, A wins. If I have exactly €4 available today, I can make dessert with A but not B. The lower unit price does nothing for a purchase I can't afford at the till.

Before moving on, I check that the comparison is fair. I haven't changed the berries, invented a sale, or made the large box disappear. Both routes solve the same 500-gram requirement. The only difference is which size of sealed box I allow myself to buy.

Now imagine another person joins me for dessert. I reset the basket. The requirement rises to 750 grams, while the prices and everything else stay fixed.

A needs three small boxes. That's €5.40. B needs one large box for €4.20. B wins, and it saves €1.20. Here the larger box matches my need exactly, so its lower unit price turns into a smaller total bill.

It feels as though I have found the cutoff. Five hundred grams: choose A. Seven hundred and fifty: choose B. Perhaps every larger shopping list should choose B too.

I test that guess with a full kilogram. Four small boxes cost €7.20. But one large box contains only 750 grams. B must buy a second, even though I need only another 250 grams. The big-box basket now costs €8.40 and weighs 1.5 kilograms.

A wins again by €1.20. The surprise is not that the bulk discount vanished. It never vanished. What changed was that I crossed the boundary of a large box. The next big box added €4.20 to my bill at once, while a small box would add only €1.80.

That's the mechanism behind the story. The price per gram is a smooth comparison. My cash bill is not smooth, because boxes are indivisible. It moves in steps. And the small and large steps don't occur at the same quantities.

I keep expanding the shopping list, not to make up a new race, but to see whether the first three outcomes were a coincidence.

At 1.25 kilograms, A needs five small boxes and costs €9. B still needs only two large boxes, costing €8.40. B wins by sixty cents. For 1.5 kilograms, A needs six boxes and costs €10.80. B remains at €8.40, so its lead grows to €2.40. Both methods buy exactly the required amount in this case.

At 1.75 kilograms something different happens. A buys seven small boxes for €12.60. B needs a third large box, which also makes €12.60. It's a tie, though B supplies two kilograms and a quarter rather than exactly 1.75. If I only care about today's bill, they are equally priced solutions. If spare berries matter, that's a separate question.

At two kilograms, A needs eight boxes, still €14.40. B still takes three large boxes for €12.60. B wins once more.

I can describe all these cases without guessing at a winner. For A, I divide the required grams by 250, round up to a whole box, then multiply by €1.80. For B, I divide by 750, round up, and multiply by €4.20. Rounding up matters. Rounding to the nearest number could give me less food than my recipe calls for.

This is also why simply comparing prices per kilogram isn't enough for my particular question. Unit price says how much the product costs at a common scale. It doesn't say how many sealed packages I must pay for to meet my exact requirement.

But there's a flaw in my little contest that I don't want to hide. Why did I force myself to use only one box size? A real shelf would normally let me buy both.

I return to the one-kilogram recipe, where A costs €7.20 and B costs €8.40. I put one 750-gram box and one 250-gram box in a third basket. Together they make exactly a kilogram for €6. That beats A by €1.20 and B by €2.40.

So my A-versus-B winner wasn't necessarily the cheapest possible shopping basket. A and B are two common shortcuts: always take the smaller pack, or always take the lower unit-price pack. If I allow a combination, I must search for the cheapest mixture instead. The mixed basket is a useful check on our conclusion, not a secret third method that was quietly allowed to compete from the beginning.

There's also a bigger question. What does it mean for the spare 250 or 500 grams to have no value?

For a single recipe tonight, measuring today's cash is perfectly legitimate. I still have to pay the full box price. But if I reliably use the rest tomorrow, then the extra berries haven't been wasted. My first shopping list might have a different objective: cost over two meals, not just the cash required for the first dessert.

And if I won't use them before they spoil, a low price per kilogram can be especially misleading. I don't get a saving from grams that end up in the bin. I haven't measured spoilage or predicted how long berries will last, so I cannot declare the big pack better or worse on that basis.

Even the budget changes how I interpret the model. With only €4 in my pocket and a 500-gram need, A works and B is impossible. With €4.20 and a 750-gram need, B works and A doesn't. A method that fails a cash constraint isn't a runner-up; it isn't an available solution.

None of this means the unit-price label is useless. I still need it to identify a genuine discount, especially when I know I'll consume everything and have enough cash for either option. It's just answering a different question from the one on my shopping list.

I began with a label that said €5.60 per kilogram and assumed it settled the matter. Now I check four things in order: what weight I need, which whole boxes meet it, what each possible basket costs, and whether any leftovers will actually be useful. If I can mix sizes, I check that combination too.

In this model, B really has the lower price per gram. A still wins at 500 grams and one kilogram. B wins at 750 grams and 1.5 kilograms. Neither sentence is a contradiction.

The best answer isn't a favourite box. It's the cheapest basket for the amount I can use. The moment my shopping list changes, I have to count again.
