Other ships sent lifeboats to an ocean liner that already had enough for everyone.

On the night of July 25, 1956, the Italian liner Andrea Doria was approaching New York with 1,706 passengers and crew. Fog covered the Atlantic. Another liner, the Stockholm, was heading the opposite way. Both ships had radar. But their crews interpreted the encounter differently.

When they finally saw each other through the fog, there was no time to avoid the collision.

Stockholm's reinforced bow tore into Andrea Doria's side. The Italian liner began leaning sharply to the right.

Now the lifeboat plan broke down. Boats on the high, left side could not be launched. Boats on the low side had to be lowered into the sea before people could board. The ship had places in lifeboats for everyone, but much of that capacity was suddenly out of reach.

The crew called for help—and specifically for more boats.

A cargo ship called Cape Ann arrived first. Its crew launched two lifeboats and took aboard 129 survivors. Stockholm, damaged but still afloat, began taking people from the ship it had struck.

Then the French liner Île de France turned back from its journey to New York. It launched thirteen lifeboats. With help from other boats, 753 survivors were brought aboard.

By morning, 1,660 people from Andrea Doria had survived. Forty-six had died in the collision. Five sailors also died aboard Stockholm.

Andrea Doria finally sank after staying afloat through the night. Its safety equipment had not vanished; the ship had tilted so far that people could not use it as intended.
