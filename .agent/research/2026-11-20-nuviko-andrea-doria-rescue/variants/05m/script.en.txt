How can a passenger ship carry enough lifeboats for everyone and still run out of usable boats? Why did the vessel that struck it become one of its rescuers? And what made a French ocean liner turn around in the middle of the Atlantic?

In July 1956, Andrea Doria was finishing a voyage from Italy to New York. There were 1,706 people aboard, including passengers and crew. Some were vacationers. Others were immigrants arriving in America with their possessions. The ship itself was a symbol of Italy's recovery after the Second World War.

Late on July 25, dense fog lay off the coast of Nantucket. Andrea Doria was heading west. The Swedish liner Stockholm was heading east. Both had radar, so this was not simply a case of two ships moving blindly through the mist.

The crews read the approaching situation differently. On Andrea Doria, officers expected the other vessel to pass on their right. An officer on Stockholm anticipated a different passing arrangement. He changed course believing he was making more room.

By the time the ships appeared to each other, their paths were already crossing. Stockholm's reinforced bow struck deep into Andrea Doria's right side.

The collision killed people in cabins near the impact and damaged the Swedish ship's bow. But Stockholm remained afloat. Andrea Doria began to heel sharply to starboard—its right side.

And that tilt created a second emergency.

Andrea Doria had lifeboat capacity for everyone. In an ordinary evacuation, crew members would lower the boats while passengers boarded. But now the boats on the raised left side could not be brought into use. On the submerged right side, even the available boats had to be lowered into the water before people could enter them.

The ship was sinking with hundreds of lifeboat places that existed on paper but could not be reached.

Captain Piero Calamai sent an SOS and asked nearby vessels to send their own lifeboats. The question was no longer just whether help would arrive. It was whether enough small boats could move people away before the list grew worse.

The cargo ship Cape Ann was the first relief vessel to arrive. It had come about fifteen miles through the fog. Its crew launched two boats and began carrying people from Andrea Doria. On one trip, a boat brought forty survivors to Cape Ann. Two doctors among those rescued could treat injured people using the cargo ship's medical supplies.

Then another difficulty appeared. Cape Ann drifted away while its lifeboats were busy. The crew had to bring the ship close again without interrupting the rescue.

A troop transport, Pvt. William H. Thomas, also arrived. It launched two motor lifeboats and eventually took aboard 159 survivors. Stockholm's crew established that their damaged ship could remain afloat and started receiving people too. The vessel that had caused the collision became part of the rescue.

But these ships and their limited boats were struggling to handle the numbers.

The French liner Île de France was already on its way toward New York. Its captain turned the large ship around and brought it to the scene. The crew launched thirteen lifeboats. These made repeated journeys, assisted by boats from other vessels, until 753 survivors had been brought aboard the French liner.

The rescue had changed from a handful of small ships reaching a wreck to a coordinated operation, with one large liner able to receive people arriving from different boats.

Andrea Doria stayed above water long enough for that operation to work. By the following morning, 1,660 of its 1,706 passengers and crew had survived. Forty-six had died aboard Andrea Doria, and five more on Stockholm. The loss of life was real, even amid the remarkable rescue.

Shortly after ten in the morning on July 26, Andrea Doria finally sank. News cameras documented the ship's last moments. But the most important result had already happened during the night: thousands of movements between decks, lifeboats and rescue ships had brought nearly everyone still alive out of danger.

The lesson is not that radar was useless, or that a large ship simply lacked boats. Radar did not make two different interpretations of the encounter agree. And lifeboats fixed to a heavily listing ship were not the same as lifeboats people could board.

What saved the survivors was a chain of decisions made beyond the damaged liner: ships answered the call, small boats kept returning, and a large passenger liner changed course to take them aboard.
