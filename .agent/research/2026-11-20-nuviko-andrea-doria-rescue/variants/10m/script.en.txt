Why did a ship with lifeboat places for all 1,706 people aboard have to ask strangers to send more boats? What happened when the ship it collided with became one of its rescuers? And why did a liner already steaming toward New York turn around in the night?

By the morning of July 26, 1956, people on the American coast were watching an Italian ocean liner tip farther into the Atlantic. Cameras followed the last hours of Andrea Doria. But the fate of the people who had been aboard was being decided before those photographs were taken, during an overnight rescue conducted by ships that had never planned to meet.

Andrea Doria was one of Italy's great new passenger ships. Launched in 1951 and put into service on the Genoa-to-New York route in 1953, it carried tourists as well as immigrants crossing the Atlantic. Some passengers had all their possessions with them. For postwar Italy, the ship represented an expensive, visible return to international travel.

On its final voyage there were 1,706 passengers and crew under Captain Piero Calamai. The ship was approaching New York after crossing from Italy. The Swedish liner Stockholm was heading the other way, toward Europe. Near Nantucket, dense fog reduced visibility.

Radar was available on both bridges. That detail matters, because this was not a collision between ships that had never detected one another. At about a quarter to eleven on July 25, Andrea Doria's radar reported another vessel roughly seventeen nautical miles away.

The Italian officers expected the two ships to pass with Stockholm on their right, or starboard, side. Stockholm's officer reached a different conclusion about how the vessels would pass. Believing he was increasing the separation, he ordered a change of course to starboard.

For the people on either bridge, each calculation implied that the vessels had space to pass. Taken together, their courses were leading them toward the same part of the ocean.

Shortly after eleven, the vessels emerged into one another's view. Corrections came too late. Stockholm's reinforced bow, built for icy northern waters, drove into Andrea Doria's right side. It opened a large breach in the Italian hull. People in cabins near the impact were killed.

Stockholm's bow was badly damaged, and five of its sailors died. But the Swedish liner remained afloat. Andrea Doria took on water and began leaning heavily to starboard.

The first emergency was damage from the collision. The second was what that damage did to the evacuation equipment.

An ocean liner is supposed to have enough lifeboat places for its passengers and crew. Andrea Doria did. Yet the ship's steep list raised the port, or left, side so far that those lifeboats could not be launched. Boats on the lower starboard side were not an ordinary answer either. They had to be lowered into the sea before people could safely board.

Imagine the difference between seeing a lifeboat attached to the deck and actually reaching it from a ship that is tipping over. The capacity written in the safety plan was still there. The physical route into much of that capacity was gone.

Within half an hour of the collision, Calamai ordered people to abandon ship. He also sent an SOS. His request went beyond asking for another ship to stand by. Andrea Doria needed other vessels to bring their lifeboats.

The first relief vessel to arrive was not another enormous ocean liner. It was a cargo ship called Cape Ann.

Cape Ann had been sailing toward New York from Europe. It received the distress call about fifteen miles from the scene and made its way through dangerous fog. It approached close enough to lower two lifeboats. Those boats were smaller than the liner they were trying to save, but they could do something the liner's own equipment could not: travel to people and bring them out.

One of Cape Ann's first trips carried forty survivors. Some had broken bones or other injuries. Two doctors were among the people who came aboard, and they could use the cargo ship's medical supplies to care for others.

Even this straightforward rescue developed a complication. Cape Ann was not carrying a full load of cargo, and it could not maneuver normally while its lifeboats were out. The ship drifted away from the damaged liner. Its crew brought the boats along and moved the ship back toward the scene so they could keep working.

That is a small but important part of the story. Answering an SOS did not mean one heroic arrival and a finished rescue. A ship had to hold its position, send its boats, receive survivors, and begin again.

Other vessels joined in.

The troop transport Pvt. William H. Thomas was carrying American military personnel and dependents toward New York. After receiving the SOS, it reached the scene within two hours. Its crew launched two motor lifeboats. Across repeated trips the transport took aboard 159 survivors. Later, its radio operators helped coordinate traffic among the rescue vessels, continuing until the Coast Guard took over that responsibility the next morning.

And there was a more uncomfortable participant in the operation.

Stockholm was still there. Its bow had been crushed in the collision and five people aboard had died. But its crew established that it could still remain afloat. The Swedish liner began taking survivors from the Italian ship. It eventually received hundreds of them.

The vessel that struck Andrea Doria was also a vessel helping save its passengers. That did not erase the collision or settle who was responsible. It was simply a consequence of which ships were present and able to help.

Yet the problem was not solved. Small boats were moving between ships. People still waited aboard a heavily listing liner. The rescuers needed a large place to receive them and enough boats to sustain the shuttle.

That larger vessel was Île de France.

The French liner was sailing toward its home port of New York. It had already passed the area when the distress call arrived. Rather than continue its voyage, its captain changed course and headed back toward Andrea Doria.

A turning ocean liner is not a small object changing direction. There were passengers aboard the French ship, its own schedule, and a substantial distance to cover. What is documented is the decision and the action: the liner came back.

When Île de France reached the scene, its crew launched thirteen lifeboats. Those boats traveled repeatedly between the sinking vessel and the French liner. Boats belonging to other ships assisted too. By the end, 753 survivors had been brought aboard Île de France.

This changed the scale of the rescue. Early boats had been bringing people to whatever vessel was near enough. Now a large liner could receive many transfers, while smaller craft ferried survivors from Andrea Doria and between ships. Its size was useful not because it could tow Andrea Doria to safety, but because it provided room and working boats while Andrea Doria still remained above water.

Cape Ann's boats also began delivering survivors to the French liner. Once Cape Ann's own boats were back aboard, it proceeded toward New York carrying 129 survivors, nine seriously injured. That figure is recorded in the US Maritime Administration's account of the operation.

The troop transport, meanwhile, did more than run its boats. By directing radio traffic until the Coast Guard arrived, it helped the different parts of the rescue work together. No one ship had all the equipment, room and access needed by itself.

The collision occurred after eleven at night. Andrea Doria did not sink immediately. It remained afloat for about eleven hours. That time was the difference between a ship disappearing before boats could reach it and a night in which people could be carried away in repeated journeys.

Near the end, Captain Calamai was still aboard his damaged vessel. According to the Noble Maritime Collection's account, his officers persuaded him to leave. A Navy escort, Edward H. Allen, took him and other crew members aboard.

Just after ten in the morning on July 26, Andrea Doria finally disappeared beneath the Atlantic. Aircraft and boats had brought cameras close enough to record the sinking, and the images made the loss of the ship visible to people who had never been near Nantucket.

What photographs could not capture in one frame was the chain of work that preceded it: two cargo-ship boats going back and forth, a transport's motor launches and radio traffic, the damaged Stockholm receiving survivors, the thirteen boats of Île de France, and people moving between vessels throughout the night.

The human outcome was two truths at once. Forty-six people aboard Andrea Doria died, along with five aboard Stockholm. Those deaths were not undone by the rescue. But 1,660 of the 1,706 people on Andrea Doria survived, because the ship remained afloat long enough and other crews reached them.

The collision also shows a limit of radar. Each bridge had detected another vessel, yet the officers interpreted the situation differently. Simply possessing an instrument did not make their assessments match. It would be wrong to turn that into a claim that radar itself caused the crash, or to invent a final verdict about individual blame.

And the rescue shows another limit: lifeboat capacity is only useful if the boats can be reached, launched and boarded when the ship is damaged. Andrea Doria was equipped to carry everyone safely, but its list made much of its evacuation system unusable precisely when it was needed.

A catastrophe is often described by the ship that is lost. In this case, the decisive actions also belonged to vessels that stayed afloat. Their crews repeatedly sent boats into the gap between a sinking liner and a safe deck.

Andrea Doria was gone by daylight. Most of its people were not. The outcome depended on work and decisions made by strangers who were supposed to be sailing somewhere else.
