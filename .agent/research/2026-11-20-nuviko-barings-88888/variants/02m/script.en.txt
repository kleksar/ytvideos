While Nick Leeson's hidden losses grew, his bank kept sending cash to Singapore.

In February 1995, Barings was more than two hundred and thirty years old. It was one of Britain's best-known merchant banks. Leeson worked at its futures business in Singapore. His approved job was to exploit small price differences between two exchanges, buying in one market and selling in the other.

But he was also making bets on which way Japanese markets would move. When those bets lost money, he didn't report the full damage. Losses went into an account numbered 88888.

Here's the extraordinary part. As head of the Singapore operation, Leeson could influence both the trading and the records used to check it. London saw what looked like profits, while the hidden deficit grew.

By the end of 1994, losses of more than two hundred million pounds had not been recognised. An internal audit had already warned about weak controls. In January, the Singapore exchange asked questions about account 88888. The problem was not stopped.

Then Japanese markets moved against Leeson's positions. Barings continued transferring money to meet demands from the exchange. The hidden losses surged. By late February they stood at about eight hundred and twenty-seven million pounds, more than twice the bank's recorded capital.

On February twenty-sixth, administrators took over Barings. The historic banking business was subsequently sold to ING for one pound. Leeson was later sentenced to prison in Singapore.

He hadn't secretly stolen eight hundred million pounds into a private account. He had concealed unauthorised trading losses. And the bank's own checks had failed to reveal them in time.

Long before Barings collapsed, it had been repeating one disastrous habit.
