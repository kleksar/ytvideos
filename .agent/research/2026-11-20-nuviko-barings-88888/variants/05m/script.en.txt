How can a bank survive for more than two centuries, then be destroyed by trades made in one overseas office? And why would it keep sending money to the person making those trades?

In 1992, Nick Leeson went to Singapore to work for Barings, a British bank with roots stretching back to the eighteenth century. Its new futures business bought and sold contracts on Asian exchanges. In ordinary language, the authorised strategy was supposed to take advantage of small price differences. If one exchange was offering a contract a little cheaper than another, the bank could buy here and sell there. Management regarded that as a relatively low-risk business.

Leeson took a different kind of position. He bet on where Japanese markets would go. That meant the bank could lose real money when prices moved the other way. And when losses appeared, he had somewhere to put them.

The account was numbered 88888.

That account is the centre of this story, but not because five eights were a magic password. It was a place in the records where unauthorised losses could be hidden from the numbers reaching London. Leeson also ran the Singapore business. He could influence the trading side and the staff who recorded and checked trades. The independent barrier that should have separated those jobs barely existed.

For a while the London office saw an extraordinary performer. Leeson's apparently successful operation generated a large share of the group's derivatives revenue. But high reported profits came with an awkward question: why did a supposedly low-risk business need such large transfers of cash?

Those transfers were not a reward paid into Leeson's pocket. Futures exchanges require money to support open trading positions. As those positions became larger and riskier, Barings companies sent funds to Singapore so the trades could stay open.

By the end of 1994, more than two hundred million pounds of losses were absent from the bank's recognised accounts. Its draft figures showed a pre-tax profit of one hundred and two million pounds. The numbers told two different stories about the same business.

And warning signs had already surfaced. An internal audit in the summer of 1994 identified control weaknesses. Management did not fix them in time. In January 1995, the Singapore exchange wrote to question account 88888 and its funding. Within Barings, instructions were also issued to reduce positions. The dangerous trades continued.

Then, on January seventeenth, an earthquake devastated Kobe in Japan. Japanese financial markets fell, and Leeson's positions were exposed to that movement. He added to his bets on recovery instead of making the hidden losses visible. The earthquake did not invent the problem. The secret account and the failures of supervision were already there.

For a time in early February, some losses were recovered. That matters: this was not a straight line from one losing trade to the end. Each apparent recovery made it possible to keep gambling. But in the final two weeks of February the markets turned against the positions, and losses grew at frightening speed.

By late February, the unreported trading losses were about eight hundred and twenty-seven million pounds. Including the cost of closing those positions, the total eventually reached roughly nine hundred and twenty-seven million. The group's recorded capital at the end of 1994 had been around three hundred and fifty million.

On February twenty-sixth, administrators took control of Barings. A bank with more than two hundred and thirty years of history could not absorb what had happened in Singapore. Its banking business was subsequently sold to ING for just one pound. Leeson was arrested and later sentenced in Singapore to six and a half years in prison.

The easy version is that one reckless trader destroyed an ancient bank. He did make the unauthorised bets and conceal their losses. But the inquiry found something larger: one man could place the bets, influence the checks, report impressive profits, and receive more funds despite repeated warnings.

The disaster wasn't just that someone had found a way to hide a loss. It was that the system kept financing the loss while treating its own warning signs as somebody else's problem.
