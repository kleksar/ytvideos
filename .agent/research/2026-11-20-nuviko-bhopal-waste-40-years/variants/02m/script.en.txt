In 2025, trucks carried hundreds of tonnes of hazardous waste away from a factory in India. The disaster that made the factory infamous had happened forty years earlier.

Why did the material remain there for so long? And what actually happened on the night of the leak?

In December 1984, people lived close to a pesticide plant in the city of Bhopal. Inside, a storage tank held methyl isocyanate: a chemical used to make pesticides, and extremely dangerous when released.

Late on December second, operators noticed the pressure in a tank rising. Water had entered the chemical and triggered a reaction that produced heat and pressure. Investigators later found that the exact route the water took into the tank was uncertain.

Then the tank's relief valve opened. Poisonous gas escaped and drifted toward nearby homes.

There were supposed to be layers of protection. But the flare system, designed to burn off escaping gas, had been out of service. The refrigeration system had been shut down. And an attempt to use the gas scrubber failed because it was not operational.

Hospitals were overwhelmed, without essential information about the chemical they were treating. Thousands died. The precise death toll remains disputed.

The gas leak stopped. The consequences did not.

A compensation settlement came in 1989. Claims and payments continued for decades. Meanwhile, hazardous material remained at the former factory.

In January 2025, authorities moved 337 tonnes of stored waste to a disposal site. By June, that batch had been incinerated. Officials described the operation as controlled; campaigners argued that much broader contamination still needed attention.

That waste was not the same as the gas that escaped in 1984. Burning it could not reverse the injuries or answer every question about polluted ground and water.

The trucks finally left the factory. But Bhopal's story was never only about what could be loaded onto them.
