Why would a chemical factory still be sending hazardous waste away forty years after its worst night? What failed when the gas escaped? And why does removing one pile of waste not mean the disaster is over?

To answer that, we need to go back to Bhopal, India, in December 1984.

The Union Carbide India pesticide plant stood close to residential areas. One of its storage tanks contained methyl isocyanate, or MIC. This was not an ordinary spill hazard. If released, the chemical could harm people far beyond the plant's fence.

Late on December second, an operator noticed that the pressure inside a tank was higher than normal. Around the same time, workers reported a leak near the system intended to clean vented gas. The pressure was still inside the tank's operating range. But soon it rose rapidly.

Something had entered the stored chemical: water. It triggered a heat-producing reaction. The tank grew hotter; pressure built until its relief valve opened.

That was the moment a problem inside the plant became a disaster outside it.

A cloud of toxic gas moved toward nearby housing. Families had no way to inspect a pressure gauge or identify which substance was coming through the air. At hospitals, doctors were suddenly facing large numbers of patients without adequate information about the gas involved.

And this was not a system with no safeguards. The plant had equipment intended to stop or limit a release. The flare system could burn escaping gas, but it had been out of service for months. Refrigeration, intended to keep the stored chemical cold, had been shut down. Operators tried to use the vent gas scrubber, but it was not in operational mode.

These failures matter more than any single dramatic image of the escaping cloud. The tank's pressure was one part of the accident. The missing barriers made its consequences far worse.

Investigators identified water entering the tank as the trigger, but the exact route it took was not firmly established. A confident story about one particular open valve would claim more than this evidence supports.

Thousands died, and many more were injured. Counting the long-term deaths is more difficult than stating a single number. Even the British safety authority's accident study warns that an exact toll is uncertain.

When the airborne release ended, the response moved onto another timescale.

India created a system to process claims. In 1989, Union Carbide deposited 470 million US dollars under a Supreme Court-directed settlement. Distribution of compensation began in the following years, and additional payments continued long afterward. These dates do not mean everyone agreed the compensation was adequate.

There was another physical problem. Hazardous material remained at the abandoned factory. Some of it was stored and identifiable; disputes also continued about contamination of the wider site and surrounding area. A batch in containers is not the same thing as all polluted soil or groundwater.

A court order finally drove a new removal operation. On the first days of January 2025, twelve containers carried 337 tonnes of waste to a treatment facility in Pithampur.

The journey did not end the argument. Nearby residents protested the plan to burn the material. Authorities said emissions would be monitored and safety procedures followed. Environmental campaigners argued that removing these containers addressed only a fraction of the site's legacy.

By June 30, officials reported that all 337 tonnes had been incinerated. Burning the waste produced ash and other residue, which still required secure handling and disposal.

It would be misleading to say nothing changed in forty years. The court process, payments, removal, and incineration were real actions. It would be just as misleading to say the cleanup settled everything.

The emergency in 1984 was a gas cloud. The argument in 2025 concerned stored waste, the remaining environment, and who was responsible for dealing with it. Those are connected chapters, not the same material drifting through the city.

And that's the answer to the opening question. The trucks could take away the material packed into them. They could not carry away forty years of illness, disputed accountability, or the unanswered question of what else remained.
