The Eastland rolled onto its side beside a Chicago pier. Eight hundred and forty-four people died, though the ship had not even reached Lake Michigan.

It was July 24, 1915. Workers from Western Electric and their families were boarding for a company picnic. The boat was supposed to take them across the lake. Instead, its decks filled with passengers while it was still in the Chicago River.

The ship began leaning. At first it straightened. Then it leaned again. The crew tried moving people and adjusting water in the ballast tanks, which were meant to help keep the ship upright. But the list grew worse.

At about half past seven, dishes and furniture slid across the decks. Water entered through openings along the lower side. The Eastland rolled over and settled in shallow water, almost against the shore.

From the exposed upper side, some people could climb onto the hull and escape without getting wet. Below them, the same movement turned stairways into traps. Furniture and passengers were thrown across the rooms. Water came in before people could find a way out.

That is why the depth of the river tells only half the story. It was roughly twenty feet deep. But people were not standing in the river, free to swim to the pier. Many were pinned inside an overturned ship.

People on shore rushed to help. Rescuers pulled survivors from the water and later cut openings into the hull. A Western Electric nurse named Helen Repa helped organize care for the injured. Yet no lifeboat was launched in the moments before the capsize.

The Eastland had carried life-saving equipment. It also had a history of instability, and changes to its upper decks had made the safety question harder, not simpler. Investigators would argue over responsibility.

The picnic never began. Hundreds of families lost someone only a short distance from dry land.

The people on the pier could see the ship. The question they could not answer quickly enough was how to reach everyone trapped inside it.
