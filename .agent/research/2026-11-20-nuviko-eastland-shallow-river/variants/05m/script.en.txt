How could a ship kill 844 people in water barely twenty feet deep? Why did some passengers escape without even getting wet? And why did all its lifeboats remain unused?

On a rainy Saturday in July 1915, thousands of people gathered beside the Chicago River. They worked for Western Electric, the company that made telephone equipment. For many families, the annual trip across Lake Michigan was a rare day away from work.

The Eastland was the first excursion steamer scheduled to leave. Parents brought their children. Co-workers found places on the decks. Some passengers moved indoors to get out of the rain.

The crew counted the people boarding. The ship was licensed to carry 2,500 passengers, plus its crew. By shortly after seven, it was full.

Then the ship leaned toward one bank. It came upright, leaned the other way, and straightened again. From the pier this might have looked like ordinary movement on a crowded boat. But the Eastland had behaved badly before.

Years earlier, it had listed sharply with passengers aboard. The vessel had been altered several times, increasing its capacity. It also relied on ballast tanks below the decks to help control its balance.

Now the crew was trying to correct another list. Water was ordered into tanks on the opposite side. For several minutes, it did not flow as intended. Passengers were asked to move across the deck. The boat did not settle.

At about 7:28, the Eastland leaned so far that furniture began to move by itself. A piano slid across a room. A refrigerator overturned. Water poured through low openings.

Within minutes the ship was lying on its side in the Chicago River. It had barely moved away from its berth. Its bow ended up just nineteen feet from the wharf.

Here is the part that makes the photographs so difficult to understand. The exposed upper side was close enough for some people to walk across the hull toward safety. They never had to swim.

But anyone caught in the rooms below faced a different disaster. The floor had become a wall. Tables, chairs and people slid toward the same side. The staircases that should have led upward were blocked by bodies and debris. As water entered the cabins, the ordinary exits became unusable.

The river itself was not deep. Being trapped inside the hull was what made escape impossible for so many.

People on the dock jumped in or threw whatever might float. Nearby crews joined the rescue. Divers entered dark compartments. Workers later cut openings in the metal to reach those still inside.

Helen Repa was a nurse employed by Western Electric. She heard the commotion while travelling through Chicago and reached the scene. At the hospital she helped obtain blankets, food and transportation for the injured. These were not tasks anyone had planned for the company's picnic.

One question remained: why couldn't the passengers simply get into the lifeboats?

There was no time. The Eastland rolled over so quickly that none of the boats or rafts was launched. Life jackets were not handed out before the capsize. The equipment existed, but the disaster unfolded before it could be used.

There was a second, more troubling question. After the Titanic sank in 1912, authorities had pushed for more lifeboats and rafts. The Eastland carried extra life-saving equipment high on its decks. Its added weight raised concerns about a vessel already known to be unstable. Exactly how each factor contributed to the final capsize has been disputed; the story cannot honestly be reduced to a single lifeboat causing it.

The death toll was 844. Many victims were young workers and members of their families. The legal proceedings continued long after the overturned ship had been removed. They examined the ship's condition, its operators and its history, but no verdict could restore the people lost.

The cruel contrast was visible from shore. For those who reached the upper hull, the dock was almost within walking distance. For those trapped beneath it, those same few feet might as well have been open sea.

The Eastland never reached the lake. That was not enough to save its passengers.
