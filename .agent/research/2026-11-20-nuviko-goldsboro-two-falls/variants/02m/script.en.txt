One nuclear bomb opened its parachute above North Carolina. The second fell straight into the ground.

Both came from the same American bomber, a B-52 that broke apart in January 1961. The plane was trying to return to its base after trouble developed in its right wing. Three of its eight crew members died in the crash.

The bomber was carrying two powerful Mark 39 hydrogen bombs. Neither produced a nuclear explosion. But investigators discovered that the two weapons had followed very different paths.

As the aircraft tore apart, the connections that normally kept the first bomb safe were pulled. Its parachute opened. That slowed the fall, but it also allowed other parts of its normal arming sequence to operate.

The bomb landed largely intact. A control switch had remained on SAFE. The laboratory investigation found that this setting prevented detonation.

The second weapon had no open parachute. It struck the earth at speed and broke apart. Its fall did not give the same arming sequence time to run. There was no nuclear explosion from that bomb either.

Recovery teams searched the wreckage and dug for buried pieces. Some uranium-bearing components were left underground, where the government still restricts digging. North Carolina has reported no contamination in its periodic groundwater testing.

These were not two bombs that simply failed to explode on impact. One had already passed several steps towards arming; the other had not followed the same sequence.

And when investigators tried to understand why, they began with the detail you could see from the sky: the parachute.
