From beneath a bed in a Serbian farmhouse, American gunner Clare Musgrove watched a visitor's boots cross the room. He couldn't tell who the man was, or whether he was looking for the hidden airman.

In 1944, Musgrove's B-24 was hit over an oil refinery in Romania. Nine crewmen who parachuted close together were captured. Musgrove landed separately: he needed longer to climb out of the bomber's cramped ball turret.

Two women and two boys tending sheep led him to a village. A family fed him. When someone knocked, the host led Musgrove into a bedroom and motioned for him to hide beneath the bed. Later another man walked through the house and left without finding him. Musgrove later said the boots looked German; he never identified that visitor.

The family had saved him for a night, but there was still no way home. Villagers and fighters under Draža Mihailović sheltered other stranded Allied airmen, providing food and hiding places while German forces searched for downed crews.

American intelligence officers planned to fly the men out. But there was no airfield. Near Pranjani, villagers and airmen cleared stones, filled holes and levelled a meadow on a hill. Their C-47 transports had to land, load passengers and take off on that short strip.

On August ninth, one pilot came in too far down the field and went around. Other aircraft landed. Forty-eight men flew out that night. Daylight flights with fighter escorts followed. Later airlifts carried hundreds to safety.

The pilots could use that runway because people had built it. The airmen could reach it because families had kept them hidden. One of those decisions had been made when a stranger came to a farmhouse door.
