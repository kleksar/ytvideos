How do you land a transport plane on a meadow behind enemy lines? What happens if the pilot runs out of ground before the aircraft can stop? And how do hundreds of stranded airmen stay hidden long enough to try?

In the summer of 1944, American bombers were flying from bases in southern Italy to strike oil refineries in Romania. To reach those targets and return, crews crossed occupied Yugoslavia. Damaged aircraft did not always get home.

One B-24 lost engines after being hit over the target. Its pilot ordered the crew to bail out. The Germans captured nine men who came down together. But ball-turret gunner Clare Musgrove got free of his cramped position late and landed far from the others. He was alone, in a country whose language he could not speak.

He found two women and two boys with a flock of sheep. They led him to a village and a family gave him food. When a visitor arrived, Musgrove's host moved him into a bedroom and told him to crawl beneath the bed. Musgrove later recalled seeing only the man's boots as he searched the house. The visitor left without finding him.

It was a rescue, but only for that night.

Serbian villagers and royalist Chetnik fighters under Draža Mihailović were protecting scattered Allied airmen. Some had spent months in barns and farmhouses. German forces pursued downed crews, and the people sheltering them took real risks. The men could not simply walk across the front lines to safety.

American intelligence officers now faced a different problem. They knew groups of airmen were gathering near Pranjani, but there was no Allied airfield for them. The Office of Strategic Services sent a team led by George Musulin, including radio operator Arthur Jibilian, to organise an evacuation.

Their solution was a meadow on a hill.

The ground was littered with stones and holes. Villagers, fighters and airmen hauled material with ox-drawn carts, moved rocks and levelled the surface. In about nine days they made a narrow landing strip more than half a kilometre long. There were trees at one end and a drop at the other. A loaded C-47 would need space both to stop and to take off again.

On the night of August ninth, people gathered beside that strip and watched for the first transport.

One aircraft came in too far down the field. Its pilot abandoned the landing and climbed away. The next plane touched down, but ran frighteningly close to the end before turning. More aircraft managed to land. Wounded and sick airmen were given priority.

Then came the second problem. The aircraft had to leave with passengers aboard. The pilots accelerated toward the edge of the hill and lifted off. Forty-eight men escaped that night.

The organisers decided that more night landings were too dangerous. Daylight would make the strip easier to see, but it would also expose the operation. A German garrison and airfield were not far away.

The next morning, twelve more C-47 transports came in, protected by Allied fighters. Men climbed aboard in groups and flew back toward Italy. More evacuation flights followed later in August.

The story did not end with that first meadow. As fighting shifted, the rescuers used other improvised landing fields and continued flying people out through December. The total is reported differently in wartime and later records, but the central fact is not in doubt: hundreds escaped by aircraft from territory controlled by the enemy.

The flight crews deserve credit for those difficult landings. So do the intelligence officers who organised them. Yet the entire operation depended on something the aircraft could not provide. Before a runway existed, families had fed and hidden the men who would eventually use it.

For Musgrove, that chain of decisions began not with a C-47 in a mountain meadow, but with a farmhouse door opening and someone making room for a stranger.
