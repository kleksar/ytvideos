Why would American transport pilots land on a farm field inside German-occupied territory? How could they lift hundreds of stranded airmen out without an airport? And who kept those men alive before the first rescue flight was even planned?

In 1944, the United States was attacking oil facilities in Romania. American bombers flew from southern Italy, crossed occupied Yugoslavia, reached their targets and tried to make the long journey back. The Germans defended those facilities fiercely. Damaged aircraft sometimes broke apart or ran out of power over the mountains.

A gunner called Clare Musgrove was aboard one of those bombers, a B-24. His station was a cramped ball-shaped turret hanging beneath the aircraft. He could not simply stand up, put on a parachute and walk to an exit. The turret had to be turned into the right position before he could climb out.

Anti-aircraft fire disabled engines. The pilot ordered everyone to jump. Musgrove had to work his way out of the turret first. The rest of the crew parachuted down together and nine were captured. Musgrove landed miles away.

That difference saved him from immediate capture, but now he was a lone American in occupied Serbia with no local language and no clear escape route.

He noticed a group of sheep and approached the people tending them: two women and two boys. They could not talk to each other, but the group eventually led him toward a village. A family brought him inside and shared their food.

During the meal, someone knocked at the door. His host spoke to the visitor, then took Musgrove into a bedroom and signalled for him to crawl under the bed. Later another man entered and walked around the house. Musgrove later said he could see only the man's boots, which looked German to him. The guest went away without finding the hidden airman.

It is important not to turn that memory into a claim that a known German officer personally searched him. Musgrove could not identify the visitor. What we know is that the family concealed him and the man left.

The next day, two local fighters took Musgrove away from the farmhouse. They also spoke no English. He walked with them for days until someone was able to explain that other Allied airmen were gathering in the area.

These were not isolated acts of kindness. Across the hills, Serbian families and fighters from the royalist Chetnik movement under Draža Mihailović were sheltering men who had come down from Allied aircraft. German patrols pursued parachutes. Hiding a foreign airman could endanger the people of a village. And a hiding place could keep someone alive only as long as the food, secrecy and local protection lasted.

Hundreds of airmen were becoming too many for scattered farms to support indefinitely.

The American Office of Strategic Services, the wartime intelligence organisation, learned where the men were. George Vujnovich helped develop a plan: instead of trying to move everyone overland through enemy territory, bring transport aircraft into the hills.

The plan had a difficult missing piece. There was no suitable Allied runway.

A small OSS team, led by George Musulin and including radio operator Arthur Jibilian, went into Serbia. The radio mattered: aircraft could not be summoned to a random meadow without coordination. The team needed a known location, a usable surface, an agreed pickup, and a way to tell the air crews when the field was ready.

Near the village of Pranjani was a hilltop meadow called Galovica. It was reasonably flat, but that did not make it an airfield. Boulders, holes and soft ground could wreck a landing. A forest stood at one end; the opposite end fell away. Hills beyond the strip added another obstacle.

Serbian villagers, local fighters and stranded airmen worked together. Stones were removed. Holes were filled. Ox carts brought sand and rock up from a streambed. The work took about nine days. The resulting strip was roughly six hundred and forty metres long and forty-five metres wide in the detailed account preserved by Smithsonian Air & Space.

That length created a hard limit. The plane chosen was a C-47, a twin-engine transport. Every one of those aircraft had to land, turn, load passengers and accelerate back into the air without much room for error.

The night of August ninth became the first test.

People gathered by the makeshift field. Fires and flares helped mark the landing area. An approaching C-47 touched down too far along the strip. Rather than force it to stop, the pilot applied power and came around again. Another aircraft landed and turned near the end. It was close, but the transport was still able to fly.

Four aircraft ultimately took passengers out that night. The sickest men were loaded first. There was no elaborate passenger terminal, just a hilltop and transport aircraft waiting to take them toward Italy.

A takeoff carried its own danger. A plane with people aboard had to gather speed along the same strip that had barely accommodated its landing. The first C-47 ran toward the end and dropped below the edge of the hill before climbing away. From the field, the aircraft disappeared for a moment and then came back into view.

By morning, forty-eight men had escaped.

That was a victory, but it did not solve the whole problem. Many more were still waiting, and the OSS team decided that repeated night approaches in mountainous terrain were too risky.

Daylight offered a clearer runway. It also meant a much more visible target. A German garrison and airfield were nearby, with fighters that might respond if the evacuation was detected.

The next morning brought twelve more C-47s accompanied by Allied fighter aircraft. Some escorts attacked German aircraft on the ground while the transports landed and loaded passengers. Men filled the benches along the cargo holds. One plane departed, then another.

More flights came in mid-August. The airmen had often arrived in Serbia with almost nothing. Some gave footwear or flying equipment to the villagers who had protected them. The farewell was not only between military units. It was between people who had shared farmhouses and meals in a dangerous place.

Yet the operation could not simply run like an ordinary airport for the rest of the war. Serbia was also a battleground between rival Yugoslav movements. The Chetniks who helped hide these airmen were aligned with Mihailović; the communist Partisans followed Josip Broz Tito. The wider military and political record of these groups is contested. The actions of families in one rescue operation cannot settle that larger history.

Political tension affected the rescue itself. After two ill Chetnik men were transported to Italy for treatment, Musulin was replaced by another OSS officer, Nick Lalich. But the airlift continued. The men on the ground still needed to get out.

As the military situation changed, the rescuers used other improvised strips. Flights continued through the autumn. On December twenty-seventh, two C-47s made one of the final pickups from a frozen field. It had to be firm enough to take the weight of the aircraft.

How many people were saved? You'll hear the number five hundred and twelve in many commemorations and later accounts. Other flight-record reconstructions give four hundred and seventeen. Those tallies use different surviving records, so quoting either as an undisputed precise total would hide the disagreement.

What does not depend on a single disputed number is the scale: hundreds of Allied personnel were carried to safety from occupied territory. The airlift had succeeded without losing a transport during the operation.

After the war, public recognition of the people who sheltered the airmen became entangled with Yugoslav politics. Mihailović was executed by the new government in 1946. The United States later awarded him a posthumous decoration, and rescued airmen continued to speak about the villagers who had protected them. Those statements document their gratitude; they do not erase the war's broader controversies.

There is a quieter ending to this story. One of the rescued men, Ray Weber, received a letter from a Serbian family in 1955. The letter recalled the time he had stayed with them and asked how he was. Weber had it translated and kept the original among his wartime things.

The aircraft took him out of the country. Eleven years later, a piece of paper crossed the distance in the opposite direction.

That is why the secret runway matters. It was not only an extraordinary landing ground. It was the visible final step of a rescue that had begun at farmhouse doors, when people decided to shelter strangers long before they knew whether any plane would ever come.
