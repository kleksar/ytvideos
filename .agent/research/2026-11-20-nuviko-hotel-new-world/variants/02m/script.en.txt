A woman was trapped beneath a collapsed six-storey hotel in Singapore. Firefighters reached her by digging through the wreckage instead of lifting the concrete above her.

On March 15, 1986, the Hotel New World building fell in less than a minute. It had a hotel upstairs, a nightclub, and a bank. Fifty people were caught in the wreckage.

Rescuers arrived and began clearing debris. But the remaining slabs could shift. Moving a heavy piece to free one person might crush another.

Some of the people who knew how to work below ground were already in Singapore. They were building its new underground railway. Tunnelling specialists joined the rescue, and firefighters who had trained in those tunnels put their skills to work.

One group heard a woman named Helen Tan. Three firefighters later described digging a passage six to eight metres long with their hands and emergency tools. Dust filled the narrow space. A basin still blocked their way. They broke it apart with a small hammer.

Nearly midnight, Helen came out alive. She was the ninth person rescued that first day.

The work continued. Sound detectors helped teams listen for survivors. After more than three days beneath the rubble, Chua Kim Choo became the last person pulled out alive.

Seventeen people survived. Thirty-three died.

An inquiry then examined why the hotel had fallen. It found seriously inadequate structural design, poor construction, and warning signs that had gone unaddressed. The concrete had been carrying loads that its supports were not properly designed to bear.

The rescuers could not undo those failures. They could only answer the urgent question the collapse left them: how do you reach a person when the weight above them is too dangerous to lift?
