How do you rescue people when the building above them has become the thing that might kill them? Why did a six-storey hotel collapse in the middle of an ordinary Saturday? And what could investigators learn from the cracks people had noticed years before?

In March 1986, Hotel New World stood at the corner of Owen Road and Serangoon Road in Singapore. The building had been completed in 1971. A bank occupied the ground floor, a nightclub was above it, and hotel rooms occupied the higher floors.

On Saturday, March 15, the entire structure came down in less than a minute.

A hotel, a bank, and a nightclub were suddenly compressed into a mass of concrete and steel. Fifty people were trapped. Those outside could see the wreckage. They could not see how many were still alive beneath it.

Emergency crews reached the site quickly. More than five hundred people would eventually take part in the operation. They began with the problem every collapse presents: to reach someone inside, you must move what has fallen on top of them.

But here the obvious method could make matters worse. Large pieces of debris remained unstable. Pulling out the wrong slab might bring another one down into the space where a survivor was breathing.

The rescue therefore changed direction. Singapore was constructing its underground railway. Tunnelling specialists from the railway project joined the teams at the hotel. Firefighters who had trained for emergencies in railway tunnels were there too.

Instead of relying only on lifting rubble from above, crews cut and dug passages into the wreckage.

On the first day, three firefighters heard cries from a woman named Helen Tan. In a later interview they remembered having little more than emergency tools and their hands. The tunnel they dug was between six and eight metres long. It was so narrow that they had to take turns crawling through it.

They still had to reach Helen herself. A broken basin blocked the route. The men chipped away at it with a small hammer until there was a way through.

Close to midnight, she emerged wrapped in a blanket. Helen was the ninth person rescued that day.

That could have been the ending of one rescue story. At the hotel, it was only the beginning of a much longer operation.

The teams had to work around heavy slabs, narrow gaps, and dust. They used sensitive listening equipment to search for signs of life. Other rescuers, including tunnellers from abroad, helped them work beneath the fallen structure. The machinery that might clear a site quickly was not necessarily the machinery that could bring a trapped person out alive.

On March 17, contemporary news reports described rescuers reaching bank employees and other survivors through tunnels. The sound of voices had directed teams towards people they could not see.

And there was still someone below.

Chua Kim Choo remained trapped for more than three days. The rescuers finally brought her out on March 18. She was the last person recovered alive.

Seventeen people survived the collapse. Thirty-three did not.

Now a different question faced Singapore. Why had a building that stood for fifteen years suddenly fallen?

Investigators considered different possibilities, including nearby railway construction. The inquiry did not find the railway responsible. Its findings pointed instead to the building itself.

The structural design was seriously inadequate. Some columns and other supports had been assigned loads they could not safely carry. The workmanship and concrete quality were also criticised. Additional equipment and exterior finishes had put more weight onto the structure, while reports of cracking had not produced the response that the damage required.

These were not defects created by the rescue. They were problems that had existed before anyone heard a voice in the rubble.

The inquiry led to a sharper focus on qualified design, responsible construction, and inspections. The emergency also shaped Singapore's rescue services, which were reorganised in the years that followed.

There were two struggles at Hotel New World. The first was to reach people without bringing more concrete down on them. The second was to understand why the concrete had been able to fall at all.

The first saved seventeen lives. The second exposed warnings that should have been taken seriously while the building was still standing.
