How could a hotel in a busy Singapore neighbourhood collapse in less than a minute, without an earthquake? How did rescue teams reach people buried beneath six floors of concrete? And why did the investigation end up looking at the very warnings that had appeared years before the disaster?

The building was called Lian Yak. Most people knew it by the business on its upper floors: Hotel New World. At street level there was a bank. Above that, a nightclub. People could enter for entirely different reasons and still find themselves inside the same six-storey structure.

It had been there since 1971. By March 1986, the city around it was changing. Work was under way on Singapore's new underground railway. The hotel was still operating.

On Saturday morning, March 15, the building came down. Witnesses and contemporary reports describe a collapse so rapid that the entire structure was gone in less than a minute.

Fifty people were trapped beneath the ruins. From the street, the scale of the damage was obvious. What was happening inside the pile of concrete was not.

The first rescue teams began the urgent job of finding a way through. But imagine trying to remove a ceiling that is now resting on somebody else's ceiling. A large slab might be holding up a pocket of space. Move it without knowing what lies beneath, and that space could disappear.

The first fire engines arrived eight minutes after the collapse. Across the road, a piano shop became an improvised command centre. The first problem was not merely how to shift concrete, but which slabs rescuers could move without crushing the people beneath them.

Trying to cut and lift debris from above had not worked well. The roads around the site were crowded too. Rescue leaders turned to tunnels beneath the wreckage. Specialists who knew how to dig underground happened to be working nearby.

They were helping to build Singapore's railway. Experienced tunnelling specialists, including foreign engineers working on the project, joined the rescue. Firefighters who had trained in the railway tunnels also had knowledge that now mattered in the ruins of a hotel.

The same railway project would later be examined by investigators as a possible cause of the collapse. At the rescue site, however, its people were already helping find the living.

On that first Saturday, three young firemen followed cries coming from the debris. The woman they were trying to reach was Helen Tan.

Decades later, Jimmy Tan, Mohd Salleh Ali and Isnin Ghani described what it took to reach her. They did not have a spacious access passage or a machine that could simply cut a safe doorway. They worked with their bare hands and emergency tools, advancing through a tunnel six to eight metres long.

They had to take turns crawling. The space was narrow and dusty. The men did not know everything that remained suspended above their path.

Then, close to the end, they found another obstruction. A basin stood between their tunnel and Helen. The rescuers chipped away at it with a small hammer.

Just before midnight, Helen was brought out wrapped in a blanket. She was the ninth person saved on the first day.

The operation did not stop. More than five hundred rescue personnel would be involved, with support from police, civil defence, the military, firefighters and tunnelling specialists. Crews searched for spaces where someone might still be breathing. Sensitive sound detectors helped them hear voices or movement through layers they could not see through.

More equipment arrived: high-pressure water cutters that could cut concrete with less vibration, cranes, and sensitive devices for hearing the trapped. None could tell rescuers for certain what would happen when the next slab moved.

On Monday, March 17, a news report written during the operation described five people rescued after more than forty-eight hours. Two worked at the bank. One was found beneath its heavy steel safe door. Later that day, sensitive microphones helped rescuers locate another three people. They were brought out through a tunnel just seventeen inches wide. The report's survivor count was provisional, not the final tally.

A threatened rainstorm created another problem. Water might flood the narrow new tunnels. Crews laid tarpaulins over the wreckage and stood pumps ready while they kept digging. Hearing a person beneath the slabs was only the start: there had to be a safe way to bring that person out.

The following day, rescuers brought out Chua Kim Choo, who had spent more than three days trapped. She was the last person rescued alive. The operation continued afterwards to search and recover those who had died.

The final count was seventeen survivors and thirty-three dead.

By Wednesday morning, further sound checks found no signs of life. The search for living survivors ended, though the work to recover the dead continued. Seventeen people had been saved and thirty-three had died. Investigators now needed to understand why the building had fallen.

Some early reports mentioned a possible explosion. Others pointed to the new underground railway, whose construction work was taking place in the district. Investigators studied the alternatives. They did not find that the railway had brought down the hotel.

Investigators examined recovered columns, beams and construction materials. A four-member commission was appointed on March 22. It would take evidence from 117 witnesses and scrutinise the people and decisions behind the design, construction and upkeep.

A building does not only support the people who enter it. Its columns must also hold the weight of its own floors, walls and roof. Engineers call these permanent weights dead loads. The changing weight of people and furnishings is a different kind of load.

The 1987 inquiry, later summarised by a British structural-safety committee, found that the permanent weights had been underestimated and that numerous structural members were seriously under-designed. Some columns were operating with safety factors below one. In simple terms, the design did not give those supports enough capacity for the loads they had to carry.

It also emerged that the structural design had been prepared by people without the necessary qualifications, and the building had gone up with virtually no professional supervision. The real columns and concrete therefore mattered as much as the calculations in a file.

The building had gained weight over time. Water tanks, heaters and air-conditioning equipment were installed on the roof, while exterior finishes added roughly fifty tonnes. Those loads increased the pressure on a structure whose original design was already inadequate.

The warning signs formed a separate timeline. Cracks appeared in hotel walls from the mid-1970s, and damaged columns were reported later. On the evening before the collapse, a nightclub door jammed and a mirror fixed to a column cracked. By the next morning, hotel staff found doors that would no longer close. Workers propped up a cracked column with timber. These details were reconstructed later from inquiry evidence; none had led to an effective repair of the structure.

The structural-safety report described what happened as the damage progressed. Cracks spread. Some columns deformed, which shifted more weight onto neighbouring supports. Those columns then had to carry loads for which they too were not designed. Eventually the failures spread through the structure.

To someone in the street, the hotel disappeared in less than a minute. Inside it, doors had stopped fitting their frames and someone had tried to hold a damaged column up with timber. The warnings had been visible before the full cause was known.

That left a sharp contrast. Skills from the underground railway helped rescuers find people inside the fallen hotel. But the inquiry traced the collapse to design and construction decisions made long before the rescue.

The commission recommended professional structural checks every five years for buildings open to the public, closer scrutiny of calculations, and qualified supervision on construction sites. Singapore later tightened building oversight. Its fire and civil-defence services were brought together in 1989 to improve coordination during major emergencies.

Helen Tan had been saved because three firefighters dug by hand and broke the basin blocking their passage. Chua Kim Choo came out after eighty-three hours. Thirty-three other people had not survived. The rescue teams could still change the outcome for anyone they managed to reach.

Years before, cracked walls and columns had offered a different opportunity to act. On the morning the building fell, a damaged column was already being held up with timber. By the time those first fire engines arrived, that chance had gone.
