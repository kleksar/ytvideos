Mount St. Helens exploded sideways.

On May eighteenth, 1980, an earthquake shook the volcano in Washington State. Its northern slope collapsed. And what came next didn't wait for the enormous ash cloud people associate with an eruption.

For two months, scientists had watched the mountain change shape. Small earthquakes began in March. Steam explosions opened a crater. The north side pushed outward, about two metres every day. Something was growing inside the mountain, without breaking through the surface.

At 8:32 that Sunday morning, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake struck. The swollen flank gave way. A gigantic landslide carried part of the mountain down toward the valley.

That landslide released pressure on hot rock and water trapped inside the volcano. The sudden release drove an explosion through the opening in its side.

Hot ash, gas and rock swept north at more than 480 kilometres an hour. Trees were flattened across a huge area. Fifty-seven people were killed or remained missing.

Only then did the enormous vertical ash column become the familiar image of the disaster. It rose more than twenty-four kilometres into the sky. The eruption continued for hours.

But the ground had changed as well. Debris blocked the outlet of Spirit Lake. Water began rising behind that new barrier, creating a flood hazard that engineers later had to control.

So the lesson wasn't simply to look for smoke over a summit. The bulging slope had been telling part of the story all along.

If the side of a volcano is moving outward, what happens when the wall holding everything inside suddenly collapses?
