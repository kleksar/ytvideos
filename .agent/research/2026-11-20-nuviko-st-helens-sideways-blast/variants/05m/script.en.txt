How can a volcano destroy a forest before the towering eruption cloud appears? Why would a mountain swell sideways for weeks? And what happens to a lake after the disaster seems to be over?

In the spring of 1980, Mount St. Helens in Washington State began to move. Small earthquakes started in March. On March twenty-seventh, explosions of steam broke through the summit and opened a crater. It was the volcano's first eruption in more than a century.

Scientists watched the crater widen. They counted more earthquakes. But the most revealing change was on the north face of the mountain: it was expanding outward.

By mid-May, the bulge had pushed about 140 metres beyond its earlier position. Its growth was roughly two metres a day. Magma was forcing its way into the mountain, forming a mass of hot rock beneath the surface, but it hadn't broken through.

That wasn't a countdown clock. Measurements could show the deformation without predicting the precise moment or full scale of what would happen. The danger was no longer confined to the summit.

On the morning of May eighteenth, at 8:32, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake struck. The swollen north side and part of the summit collapsed together.

The landslide was immense. It carried about two and a half cubic kilometres of rock and debris, most of it west along the North Fork Toutle River valley. A mountain had begun to fall apart.

But the slide did something even more dangerous. The missing rock had been holding down a body of very hot, pressurized material inside the volcano. Take that weight away suddenly, and the pressure drops.

Water flashed into steam. Gas and hot material expanded. The eruption burst through the opening left by the collapsing northern wall.

Mount St. Helens exploded sideways.

The blast overtook the falling debris and accelerated to at least 480 kilometres an hour. Within minutes, it devastated a broad fan-shaped area north of the mountain. Close to the summit, virtually no trees remained. Farther away, entire stands lay flattened in the direction of the blast. At the outer edge, trees still stood, but had been scorched.

This was not simply ash drifting out of the sky. The most destructive first blow came almost horizontally across the land.

Fifty-seven people were killed or remained missing. Among them was US Geological Survey volcanologist David Johnston, working at an observation post north of the mountain. Calling the area dangerous doesn't mean the scientists knew precisely how far that sideways explosion would reach.

Less than fifteen minutes after the collapse, material was rising into an eruption column more than twenty-four kilometres high. The main explosive phase lasted about nine hours. Prevailing winds carried ash far to the east; parts of Washington were plunged into darkness.

The towering cloud became the image many people remembered. Yet a separate chain of damage had already spread through the valleys.

Hot debris melted snow and ice. Water mixed with loose volcanic rock and soil to form fast-moving mudflows, called lahars. The largest of these grew as it travelled down the Toutle River system, damaging roads, bridges and homes many kilometres away.

Then there was Spirit Lake, just north of the volcano.

The landslide had blocked its natural outlet and raised its water surface. Rain and melting snow kept adding water. A disaster that had taken minutes to begin now left a slower problem: the lake could eventually overtop its unstable natural dam and send floodwater downstream.

Temporary pumping began in 1982. Engineers later bored a tunnel through bedrock to provide a controlled outlet. It opened in 1985.

The mountain was quieter by then, though it had not simply returned to its old shape. Smaller eruptions followed, and a new lava dome grew inside the crater.

The important distinction is the order. First the mountain's side moved. Then it collapsed. The collapse released pressure. The horizontal blast hit the forest. The huge ash column followed, while rivers and lakes inherited hazards of their own.

Watching a volcano means more than watching its summit. Sometimes its changing shape is the warning, and the direction of the first explosion is the whole story.
