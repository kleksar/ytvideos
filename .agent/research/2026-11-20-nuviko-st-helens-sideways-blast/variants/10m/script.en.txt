Why did the deadliest part of the Mount St. Helens eruption travel sideways instead of straight into the sky? Why had scientists spent weeks watching a slope that seemed to be growing? And why did a lake threaten to cause another disaster long after the initial explosion?

To understand the morning of May eighteenth, 1980, we have to begin with a mountain that was already changing shape.

Mount St. Helens stands in Washington State, in the Cascade volcanic range. Before 1980, its summit formed a familiar, almost symmetrical cone. The trouble began with small earthquakes in March. On March twenty-seventh, steam explosions broke through the ice and rock near the summit. A crater formed. Within a week it had become much wider, and new cracks crossed the upper mountain.

The steam explosions mattered. They showed that heat and pressure were disturbing the mountain's shallow interior. But an even more important clue appeared on its northern flank.

The slope was expanding outward, nearly horizontally. By May seventeenth, it had pushed some 140 metres beyond its previous position. During much of this period the bulge grew by roughly two metres a day. This was a shape change large enough to measure repeatedly, not a distant tremor that could be interpreted in several ways.

Scientists understood that magma had intruded into the mountain. It was forming a concealed body of hot material — a cryptodome — rather than erupting freely at the surface. But knowing that the mountain was deforming did not provide the exact moment when the slope would fail, or show exactly how far any blast might travel.

By May seventeenth, more than ten thousand earthquakes had shaken the volcano since the unrest began. Small eruptions had stopped and started again. There was plenty of evidence that the volcano was active, but no simple clock counting down to a particular outcome.

Then, at 8:32 on Sunday morning, May eighteenth, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake occurred.

Almost immediately, part of the summit and the bulging northern flank began sliding away. The landslide was so large that it became the biggest debris avalanche recorded in historic time. Around two and a half cubic kilometres of rock and soil moved, sweeping through the landscape. Much of the material continued west along the North Fork Toutle River valley for more than twenty kilometres.

If you had looked only at the landslide, you might have thought you were seeing the main disaster. In fact, the landslide was also removing the lid from something under enormous pressure.

The north flank had been holding down a mass of very hot rock and pressurized water. Once the rocks above it disappeared, pressure fell suddenly. Water could flash into steam. Gases and hot material expanded through the opening left in the side of the mountain. The collapse and the blast happened within the same rapid sequence.

And this is the crucial difference between Mount St. Helens and the picture many of us have of a volcano.

Mount St. Helens exploded sideways.

The hot cloud overtook the moving landslide and reached at least 480 kilometres an hour. Rather than rising neatly out of the summit, much of the initial destructive force swept across the ground to the north.

The pattern left behind told investigators which way it had travelled. Near the volcano, trees were stripped away almost entirely. Farther out, whole stands of forest lay flattened. At the edge of the blast zone, trees remained standing but had been scorched by hot gases. The devastated fan covered roughly 600 square kilometres.

The destruction was not a uniform circle around the summit. It was a broad sector pointing away from the collapsed side of the mountain. A person looking at the plume above the crater could miss the more immediate danger on the ground.

Fifty-seven people died or remained missing in the disaster. One was the US Geological Survey volcanologist David Johnston, who had been working at an observation post north of the volcano. He was among the specialists closely studying its activity. That detail matters because this was not merely a case of nobody monitoring the mountain. People were watching it and still could not eliminate the danger.

Within fifteen minutes the eruption column had reached more than twenty-four kilometres above the volcano. A separate, enormous vertical eruption followed the collapse and blast. The main explosive phase continued for about nine hours.

Wind carried ash east. In some towns far away, daylight disappeared beneath the dust. More than five hundred million tons of ash were distributed across large parts of the United States. The fine material eventually traveled around the world.

Those images of a mountain crowned by a huge dark column became famous. But by the time that column dominated the photographs, the side of the volcano had already torn away and the first blast had already changed the forest.

And the sequence wasn't finished.

Parts of the hot material crossed snow and glacier ice. Meltwater and groundwater mixed with loose volcanic debris. They formed dense moving slurries known as lahars: flows that could carry rock, mud and tree trunks down the river valleys.

The largest lahar in the North Fork Toutle system grew as it travelled. It cut into fresh deposits, collected more material and damaged bridges and homes. By around midnight, the flood of volcanic debris had reached the Cowlitz River, roughly eighty kilometres from the mountain.

This was a different mechanism from the blast. A horizontal explosion could devastate a forest in minutes. A river full of mud and debris could keep spreading the consequences long afterward.

North of the volcano, the landslide also ran into the area around Spirit Lake. The lake's natural outlet was blocked. Its water surface rose, and a new dam made of loose volcanic debris stood where the old drainage had been.

The eruption was over. But rain and snowmelt continued feeding water into that lake. With no normal outlet, the rising level presented a separate risk: if water overtopped the blockage and broke through it, a flood could rush downstream.

Engineers could not solve that problem by waiting for the volcano to calm down. In 1982, temporary pumping was installed to regulate the lake. A longer-term project bored through bedrock, creating a controlled outlet away from the unstable debris dam. The tunnel opened in 1985.

Other drainages changed too. Debris blocked Coldwater Creek and South Fork Castle Creek, forming new lakes. Spillways had to be built to keep their levels from rising dangerously. The eruption had redrawn not just the mountain but part of its drainage system.

Meanwhile the volcano did not simply go back to being a quiet cone. Smaller explosive episodes occurred through the summer and autumn of 1980. Later, magma formed a new lava dome inside the crater. The summit now faced north through a broad opening where the old wall had stood.

The difference between before and after could be seen from the air, on maps, and in the courses of rivers. A new volcanic landscape was being studied in detail, including the slow recovery of vegetation in ground that the blast had stripped almost bare.

There was also a difference in what scientists had to watch. Earthquakes remain important, and ash clouds are dangerous. But swelling ground, unstable slopes, pressure within a volcano, and the possibility of a sideways blast cannot be understood by imagining every eruption as a vertical fountain.

The evidence from March to May had shown a bulge growing. The earthquake accompanied the failure of that wall. The landslide released the pressure. The sideways explosion devastated the north. The high ash column followed. And the dislocated rock and water left engineers dealing with hazards years later.

Mount St. Helens was one disaster with several linked consequences, each moving at a different speed.

Its lesson isn't that scientists had no warning, or that every volcano will behave this way. It's that a warning can be visible without revealing exactly how a disaster will unfold. The mountain's changing shape was a crucial part of the story, and the first deadly force didn't rise into the sky. It moved across the land.
