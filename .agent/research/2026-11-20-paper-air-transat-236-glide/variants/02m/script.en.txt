How does a passenger jet cross sixty-five nautical miles with both engines stopped? And why did a warning about uneven fuel make the problem worse?

On August 24, 2001, an Air Transat Airbus A330 was flying from Toronto to Lisbon. There were 306 people aboard. Far out over the Atlantic, the pilots noticed that the fuel in one wing was running lower than in the other.

They opened a valve to balance the tanks. Fuel from the left side could now feed the right engine.

But the right engine had a leak. And the crew did not yet know it.

A fuel pipe had cracked after an engine replacement. Fuel had been escaping for almost an hour before the imbalance warning appeared. Opening that valve let more fuel reach the leak.

The pilots diverted toward Lajes, an airfield on an island in the Azores. They were still over the ocean when the right engine stopped. Thirteen minutes later, the left engine stopped too.

With no engine thrust, the plane became a glider. Emergency systems kept enough flight controls working. Air traffic controllers guided the pilots toward the runway.

The aircraft was high on approach. The captain turned it through a full circle and made further turns to lose altitude. Then the jet came in fast, struck the runway hard and stopped on damaged wheels.

Everyone survived. Some passengers and crew were injured during the evacuation.

Investigators later traced the leak to mismatched pipes fitted during the engine change. They also found that the crew had followed a fuel-imbalance response without identifying the leak. A procedure for leaking fuel might have kept an engine running.

The remarkable landing saved everyone aboard. The investigation revealed how the plane had reached that point: a damaged pipe, a misleading problem, and fuel sent in exactly the wrong direction.
