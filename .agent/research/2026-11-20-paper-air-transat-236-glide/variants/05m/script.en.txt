What happens when a plane still has fuel on its instruments, but the fuel is leaving through a hole? How can a routine correction make that loss worse? And how do you land a full-size passenger jet with no engines?

At the end of August 2001, Air Transat Flight 236 was crossing the Atlantic from Toronto to Lisbon. The aircraft was an Airbus A330, carrying 293 passengers and thirteen crew members. Nothing about the planned journey called for an emergency landing on a small island.

A week earlier, technicians had replaced the plane's right engine. Two tubes from different equipment configurations ended up beside each other. A hydraulic tube rubbed against a fuel tube. During the flight, the fuel tube cracked.

The fuel began escaping at 4:38 in the morning, according to the later investigation. For a long time, the pilots had no clear indication of a leak.

Instead, the first strange readings concerned engine oil. The right engine showed an unusually low oil temperature and other unexpected values. The crew checked manuals and spoke to maintenance staff on the radio. The instruments were puzzling, but the engine kept working.

Then, at 5:33, a different warning appeared: there was more fuel in the left wing than in the right.

That looked like an imbalance. The pilots opened the crossfeed valve and switched off the right-side fuel pumps, allowing fuel from the left tanks to supply the right engine.

It was the wrong response to a problem they could not see. The right engine was leaking. Now fuel from the healthier side could flow toward that leak too.

The amount of fuel aboard dropped much faster than expected. The crew checked the calculations. Cabin attendants looked outside for signs of a leak, but saw none. The pilots decided to divert to Lajes, on Terceira Island in the Azores.

By then, getting to an airport had become a race against the falling fuel quantity.

At 6:13, the right engine stopped. The aircraft was still far from the island. The crew declared an emergency. At 6:26, the left engine stopped as well.

Both engines were now silent. The airplane was still airborne, but it could no longer maintain height with engine thrust. It had to trade altitude for distance.

Emergency equipment supplied electricity and hydraulic power to essential flight controls. The pilots had fewer systems than normal, but they could still steer the aircraft.

Air traffic controllers at Lajes provided radar guidance. The runway was visible in the night, and the weather was good. That mattered. A successful glide could still end badly if the aircraft reached the island at the wrong height.

The captain reached the approach too high. He flew a complete circle and then made additional turns to shed altitude without an engine-driven descent. Each turn used up distance and height that could never be recovered with extra thrust.

At 6:45, the A330 reached runway 33 at high speed. It landed hard, bounced, struck the surface again and rolled on damaged wheels. Airport rescue crews put out small fires near the landing gear.

The passengers were evacuated. All 306 people survived, although several were hurt in the evacuation, including two passengers seriously.

Afterward, investigators worked backward through the flight. They found the broken fuel pipe beside the hydraulic line. The engine replacement had mixed components that were not intended to be installed together.

They also examined the pilots' decisions. The imbalance warning had not told the crew directly that fuel was leaking. Yet once the fuel quantities began falling abnormally fast, the separate fuel-leak procedure was critical. It had not been carried out.

The investigation concluded that this failure was a key reason the aircraft exhausted its fuel. It also found that the crew's handling of the powerless approach and landing had been highly effective.

Both findings can be true. An error in understanding the problem helped bring the aircraft to the edge of disaster. Then precise flying kept it from going over.

Flight 236 reached the runway because it had just enough height left to glide there. It lost both engines because a cracked pipe had drained fuel that the crew was still trying to balance.
