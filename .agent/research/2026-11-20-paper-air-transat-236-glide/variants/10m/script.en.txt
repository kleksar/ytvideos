Why did a long-range passenger jet run out of fuel in the middle of the Atlantic? Why did a warning intended to help the pilots lead to more fuel being lost? And how could an aircraft weighing well over a hundred tonnes reach a runway without engine thrust?

In August 2001, Flight 236 was supposed to be an ordinary crossing. An Air Transat Airbus A330 left Toronto for Lisbon with 293 passengers and thirteen crew. The route stretched over the North Atlantic, where a problem can leave an aircraft a long way from the nearest usable runway.

The flight's first important event had taken place days earlier, on the ground.

Technicians had noticed metal particles in the oil system of the right engine. Air Transat decided to replace that engine. Its own spare was unavailable, so it used a replacement supplied by Rolls-Royce.

The engine was not arranged exactly like the one being removed. Some of its hydraulic and fuel lines belonged to an earlier configuration. During the replacement, technicians fitted a newer fuel tube alongside an older hydraulic tube.

Those parts were meant to be changed as a matched set. The hydraulic tube and fuel tube now lay too close together.

The technicians obtained clearance during installation. But when hydraulic pressure was applied, the line moved back toward its natural position. It touched the fuel tube. Over repeated flights, vibration wore at the contact point.

The aircraft passed its checks and went back into service. Nothing in the departure that night announced that a small mechanical contact would dominate the flight.

Flight 236 took off with more fuel than the regulatory minimum. The first hours passed normally.

At 4:38 in the morning, according to the later analysis of the flight data, the fuel tube in the right engine began leaking. It was a line in the engine, not a hole in one of the wing tanks.

A pilot looking at the tanks could see amounts of fuel. The problem was that those amounts were being drained through a route the crew had not identified.

The first signs did not look like a fuel leak. Around 5:03, the crew noticed odd oil readings on the right engine. The oil temperature was lower than expected, the pressure higher, and the quantity abnormal.

The pilots examined their manuals and contacted company maintenance staff by radio. The readings were confusing. They remained within operating limits, so there was no obvious engine failure to explain them.

Investigators later found the connection. Fuel escaping through the damaged line passed through a heat exchanger and affected the engine oil temperature and pressure. The instrument clues were real, but the underlying cause was not apparent in the cockpit.

Meanwhile the fuel system was doing something the pilots had not requested to stop. It transferred fuel out of a smaller tank in the tail toward the wings as part of its normal operation.

On this flight, that additional fuel passed toward the leaking side. Investigators concluded that this transfer delayed the imbalance warning by about fifteen minutes.

At 5:33, the warning finally appeared. The quantities in the left and right wings no longer matched.

For the crew, the immediate problem looked like a difference between two tanks. A familiar response was to balance them. The pilots opened the crossfeed valve and turned off the right-side fuel pumps, so the right engine could draw fuel from the left tanks.

If one wing simply contained less fuel than the other, that would be a reasonable direction to investigate.

But this was not simply unequal fuel. The fuel line in the right engine was broken.

The new configuration meant fuel from the left wing could feed the right engine, and from there escape through the same damaged line. The attempted correction made the consequences of the hidden leak worse.

The crew saw that the total fuel on board was much lower than planned. They checked the original fuel figures for a mistake. The numbers did not explain what they were watching.

They even asked cabin attendants to look outside for signs of fuel escaping. Cabin lights were dimmed for the inspection. No visible leak was found.

This mattered because the crew still lacked a clear picture. They had an engine with strange oil readings, a wing-tank imbalance, and fuel disappearing faster than the flight plan predicted. Those pieces fit together in the investigation. They had not yet fitted together in the cockpit.

At 5:45, the pilots made a vital decision: divert to Lajes Airport on Terceira, one of the Azores islands.

The aircraft turned toward the island. The destination had changed from Lisbon to an emergency airfield surrounded by water.

The fuel continued falling. The crew altered the pumps again while trying to understand where it had gone. They also prepared for the possibility that the airplane would not reach the runway.

At 6:13, the right engine stopped through fuel exhaustion.

The pilots still had one engine, but the problem was now visible in a much more serious form. Thirteen minutes later, at 6:26, the left engine stopped too. The airplane was about sixty-five nautical miles from Lajes.

An Airbus A330 without engine thrust does not fall straight down. Its wings can still generate lift as the aircraft moves forward. But speed and altitude are now finite resources: descent supplies the energy that engines normally provide.

The jet became a very large glider.

With both engines stopped, the normal electrical and hydraulic systems could not continue as before. A small emergency turbine driven by the airflow supplied enough power for essential controls. That did not restore the airplane to ordinary operation. Several functions and protections were unavailable.

The cabin crew had already begun preparing passengers for an emergency landing, possibly on the sea. Life jackets and safety instructions mattered because reaching an island was not yet guaranteed.

The pilots had to manage three things at once: the aircraft's remaining height, its distance to Lajes, and the speed needed to keep the wings flying.

Air traffic controllers provided radar vectors to guide them. The night was clear enough for a visual approach, which gave the crew a runway to aim for rather than only instruments to follow.

The next surprise was that the plane approached the airport too high.

Ordinarily a pilot could adjust the descent using engine thrust. Flight 236 had no thrust left to add. If the aircraft descended too far before reaching the runway, it could not climb back on engine power. If it arrived too high, it could overshoot the airport.

The captain, Robert Piché, made a complete turn to lose height. He then flew further S-shaped turns on approach. The turns changed the height and distance available until the airplane could reach the runway.

The crew lowered the landing gear and prepared to stop a heavy aircraft with degraded braking systems. On this landing, there would be no routine second attempt with engines pushed forward.

At 6:45, the A330 reached runway 33 at roughly 200 knots, faster than an ordinary landing. It touched down hard, bounced and struck the runway again.

The airplane rolled a long way before stopping. Its wheels and landing gear were damaged. Small fires developed around the left main wheels, and waiting airport crews quickly extinguished them.

Passengers evacuated down the emergency slides. The evacuation took roughly ninety seconds. Two passengers suffered serious injuries, and other passengers and cabin crew members were also hurt.

But nobody died. All 306 people aboard had reached land.

That landing was the dramatic end of the flight, but it was not the end of the investigation.

Investigators examined the broken tube, the engine replacement, the fuel system and the crew's decisions. The hydraulic tube had been installed too close to a fuel tube from a different configuration. The contact produced the fracture that started the leak.

They also asked why the available signs had failed to produce a diagnosis.

The fuel imbalance advisory itself did not announce a fuel leak or require immediate action. The captain carried out a balancing response from memory. A warning to consider the separate leak procedure was not acted on.

Investigators concluded that failure to use the fuel-leak procedure was a key factor leading to the exhaustion of the aircraft's fuel.

They calculated that other responses, including keeping the crossfeed closed or correctly applying the leak procedure, would have preserved fuel for the approach to Lajes. Those were reconstruction results, not actions the crew actually performed.

At the same time, the report praised the handling of the engines-out approach. The captain had not been trained for a powered-off A330 landing of this kind. The investigators described the crew's actions during that final phase as highly effective.

The investigation therefore gave two different answers to two different questions.

Why did the airplane lose both engines? Because a fuel tube fractured, and the leak went unidentified while fuel was managed as if the problem were only an imbalance.

Why did everyone survive? Because the diversion began before the fuel was completely gone, the remaining height and emergency controls made a glide possible, and the pilots brought the aircraft onto the runway with help from the controllers and cabin crew.

The recommendations covered maintenance control, leak detection, procedures and training. They reflected a problem that had crossed several layers of protection before the aircraft reached the Atlantic.

On Flight 236, the decisive mistake was not made at the moment the engines stopped. It began with two tubes that should not have been fitted together. And it grew when the crew tried to balance fuel that was already escaping.
