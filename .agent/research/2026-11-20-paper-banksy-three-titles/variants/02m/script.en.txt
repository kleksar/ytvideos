At Sotheby's in London, a painting worth a million pounds started shredding itself.

It was October 2018. The picture was Banksy's Girl with Balloon: a small girl reaching towards a red, heart-shaped balloon. It was sitting in a heavy frame, ready for auction.

The final price was £1,042,000, including fees. The auctioneer brought down the hammer.

Then an alarm sounded. The picture slid down through a slot beneath its frame. A hidden machine was cutting the bottom of the canvas into hanging strips.

But it stopped halfway. The balloon was still visible above the shredded section. Banksy later released a rehearsal video suggesting the entire picture had been meant to pass through.

So what had the buyer actually purchased? A ruined painting? Or a new one?

Banksy's authentication office, Pest Control, certified the altered piece. Its name changed from Girl with Balloon to Love is in the Bin. And the collector still completed the purchase at the agreed price.

The strips weren't repaired. They became part of the work. The picture even spent time on display at museums in Germany.

Three years later it returned to the same London auction house. This time the estimate was between four and six million pounds.

After ten minutes of bidding, the price reached £18,582,000, including fees. Almost eighteen times the previous sale price.

And there was one more change. When the piece appeared at an exhibition in South Korea in 2023, its catalogue called it Girl Without Balloon.

One frame. A partly shredded painting. Three names. Two extraordinary prices.

The machine had been designed to destroy an image. Instead, the moment it switched on became the image's most valuable history.

And it all began just as an auctioneer finished a sale.
