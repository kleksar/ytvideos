Why would anyone pay a million pounds for a painting that had just been cut into strips? And how could the same damaged object sell again for almost eighteen times that price? The answer starts with a picture almost anyone could recognize, even without knowing the artist.

Banksy's Girl with Balloon shows a child reaching towards a red balloon shaped like a heart. The image first appeared as a stencil on a London wall and became one of the anonymous British artist's best-known designs.

In October 2018, a canvas version arrived at Sotheby's in London. It hung inside an unusually substantial, ornate frame. Banksy had supplied the frame, but the people bidding were there for the picture inside it.

The bidding ended at £1,042,000, including the buyer's fees. The auctioneer's hammer came down. The sale was supposed to be over.

An alarm sounded. The canvas began sliding out of the bottom of its frame, entering a concealed cutting mechanism. By the time it stopped, the lower half hung in narrow strips. The small red balloon was still visible above them.

The buyer hadn't bid for a bundle of strips. And the audience hadn't been told there was a machine hidden in the frame.

Banksy soon posted a recording that appeared to show the shredder being built into the frame. A later video showed a remote control being pressed during the auction. It also showed a rehearsal in which a test picture went all the way through.

That last detail changes the story. The final artwork was only partly shredded. The video suggested that total destruction, not a neatly divided image, had been the plan. We cannot independently establish why the machine stopped.

Now the difficult question belonged to the auction house and its customer. Sotheby's said it had not known about the stunt. A successful bid had been made for Girl with Balloon. But the object on the wall no longer matched the picture the customer had just won.

For the next part, the artist's authentication body mattered more than the cutter. Pest Control, which authenticates Banksy's work, certified the altered object. It gave the piece a new identity and a new name: Love is in the Bin.

The frame and the hanging strips were no longer things to hide or remove. They were part of what made this particular work unique.

After discussions following the auction, the anonymous buyer agreed to go ahead with the purchase at the original price. The sale was not reversed. She received the half-shredded picture.

And that gave the story a second life. The work was shown at Museum Frieder Burda in Baden-Baden in early 2019, then at the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart. The peculiar shape of the picture—the intact top and the strips below—was now its defining feature.

In 2021, the same piece returned to Sotheby's in London. Its published estimate was four to six million pounds. That was already several times the earlier purchase price, before the auction even began.

Nine bidders competed for roughly ten minutes. The final amount, including fees, was £18,582,000. Nearly eighteen times the 2018 sale price, and a record for Banksy at that time.

But the story did not stop with the money. The machine remained inside the frame, according to Sotheby's catalogue, though it had been decommissioned. The mechanism that once threatened the work was now documented as part of the work itself.

Then came a quiet third act. When it appeared in a South Korean exhibition in 2023, the catalogue used another title: Girl Without Balloon. A 2024 report in The Art Newspaper said its date had also been changed to 2021. Banksy's authentication body did not publicly explain the decision.

So there were three names attached to the same physical object: Girl with Balloon, Love is in the Bin, and Girl Without Balloon.

The price jump is real. The sale records are real. But neither tells us exactly why the next buyer valued it so highly, or proves that Banksy intended to multiply its market price. Those are interpretations.

What we do know is stranger than a simple prank. In 2018, a machine changed the picture. The buyer still paid. The art world authenticated the altered object, exhibited it, and sold it again.

A painting can lose half its canvas without losing its identity. This one went further: the missing half became the very thing people remembered.

The auctioneer had said the sale was over. For this picture, that was when the next story started.
