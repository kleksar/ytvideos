Can cutting a painting in half create a new work of art? Why would its owner keep paying for it? And how can one physical canvas end up with three different titles and three different dates? These aren't imaginary questions. They follow a picture that once sold in a London auction room.

The picture was by Banksy, an artist famous for street images and for keeping his identity out of public view. One of his most recognizable designs shows a child reaching towards a red, heart-shaped balloon. It first appeared as a stencil on a wall in east London.

That image matters because it is simple enough to recognize at a glance. The figure is small. The balloon carries the only strong color. Even if you have never visited a gallery, you understand what has been placed just beyond the child's hand.

But there is a difference between recognizing an image and owning a particular object. A design can appear on walls, prints, posters and canvases. What came to auction in London was a specific, unique canvas—not every version of Girl with Balloon that Banksy had ever made.

The canvas was mounted on a board. Around it sat a large, elaborate frame supplied by the artist. In October 2018, Sotheby's offered this object for sale. Its catalogue still presented it as Girl with Balloon and attached an earlier date to the work.

For the bidders, the image was familiar. The frame was unusual, but its real purpose was not announced to them.

On 5 October, bids reached £1,042,000, counting the buyer's premium. That is the fee an auction house adds to the winning bid. The auctioneer brought down the hammer. A successful collector now had a purchase to complete.

And then, while the room was still looking at the picture, a sound came from the frame.

An alarm was heard. The canvas moved downwards. A hidden cutting device beneath the image drew part of the picture through it. Long, narrow strips began hanging below the bottom edge.

The movement stopped partway through. Above the strips, part of the girl and the red balloon remained visible.

Just imagine the practical problem without inventing anyone's feelings. The buyer had agreed to purchase a framed picture. The object offered on the wall was now physically different from the one that had received the winning bid. Someone had to decide whether that change cancelled the deal.

Sotheby's said it had not known about the mechanism. Banksy later released footage that appeared to show it being installed inside the frame. In another video, a hand pressed a button on a remote-control device during the auction.

That video supplied a second surprise. It included a rehearsal in which a test picture travelled completely through the shredder. The real auction piece hadn't done that.

The footage suggests that full shredding was intended. But it does not independently prove why the actual mechanism stopped halfway through. Calling the result a carefully planned half-and-half design would go further than the evidence allows.

The result, however, was plain to see. A picture once dominated by a little girl and a red balloon now had two very different forms in the same frame: the surviving image above, and strips of canvas below.

The artist's next move took place away from the machine.

Banksy's authentication body is called Pest Control. It exists to confirm whether works presented as his are genuine. Authentication matters in a market where the artist is anonymous and images can be copied. A familiar drawing on a piece of paper isn't automatically the same thing as an authenticated original.

Pest Control recognized the altered picture and issued new documentation. Its title changed to Love is in the Bin. Sotheby's treated the transformation itself as a work by Banksy, completed during the 2018 auction.

There is an important difference here. The auction house wasn't claiming that someone had carefully repaired the ruined canvas. It was saying that the particular combination of intact image, hanging strips and frame formed the work in its new state.

That still left the buyer's decision. Following discussions with the auction house, the anonymous European collector agreed to complete the purchase for the original £1.042 million price, including fees.

The sale had not fallen apart. And the strips were not swept away as waste.

The public could later see the result. From February to March 2019, Love is in the Bin was displayed at Museum Frieder Burda in Baden-Baden, Germany. It was then shown at Stuttgart's Staatsgalerie, on loan from March 2019 into February 2020.

Museums did not turn the strips back into a conventional rectangular painting. They displayed the transformed object. By now, if you saw a photograph of the work, you were likely to recognize the loose lengths of canvas before anything else.

That was the first strange consequence. The thing that might once have made an artwork unacceptable to a buyer had become its defining feature.

The second came three years after the London auction.

In September 2021, Sotheby's announced that the same object would return to sale. It was still called Love is in the Bin. The auction house expected it to fetch between four and six million pounds.

Notice the change. Those numbers were not the result of a new act of painting. They were estimates for the same physical object that had been partly cut in 2018, now with a documented history.

Its auction catalogue is unusually revealing. Sotheby's described it as a unique work in spray paint and acrylic on canvas, mounted on board. The frame was explicitly part of it. And the remote-controlled shredding mechanism, though decommissioned, was still inside.

The very device that had threatened the canvas was listed as a surviving component of the artwork.

The catalogue also recorded a chain of ownership: a gift from the artist to a private collection, the October 2018 Sotheby's auction, and acquisition at that sale by the owner now offering it again.

It listed the museum exhibitions too. Rather than being an anonymous damaged picture, the object now came with a provenance—a documented path through owners, an auction, authentication and public display.

None of that tells us what the next buyer was thinking. It does explain what could be established about the object: it was the same unique piece, carrying the visible evidence of the event that had made headlines.

On 14 October 2021, it came back to the Sotheby's room in London. Nine bidders pursued it for around ten minutes.

The final sale price, including fees, was £18,582,000.

Compare that with £1,042,000 in 2018. The later sale was roughly seventeen point eight times the earlier one. At the time, it set a new auction record for Banksy.

The difference is astonishing. But it is not proof that shredding any picture will make it valuable, or that Banksy had forecast an eighteenfold return. We have two recorded prices for one already-famous artist's unique work. That is not a general rule about damaged paintings.

And it wasn't the last time the work changed.

The 2018 sale had called it Girl with Balloon. After the incident, the 2021 catalogue called it Love is in the Bin and described it as completed in 2018.

Then, at a 2023 exhibition in South Korea, the catalogue carried a further title: Girl Without Balloon.

The Art Newspaper reported this in January 2024. It also reported that the work had been given the date 2021. A Sotheby's representative said Banksy's authentication body had not explained the change.

So the frame and the strips were still the same physical object, yet its recorded identity had moved again. The latest title asked you to look for what was absent rather than what survived.

There is a caution here. Catalogues and certificates can describe a work under different titles or completion dates. Those descriptions are part of its documented art-market history; they do not mean three separate canvases were created in three separate years.

The question of exactly what Banksy hoped would happen inside the frame also remains open beyond the footage and public statements. The auction house says it wasn't involved. The footage suggests a more complete shredding. Neither settles every theory about the stunt.

But we don't need to settle those theories to see the outcome.

First, a sale produced a damaged object. Then authentication established a new work under a new name. Museums displayed it. A second auction set an enormous new price. And a later exhibition introduced yet another name.

The most surprising part isn't merely that a famous artist put a shredder in a frame. It's that the attempted destruction didn't finish the picture's story. It created a new chapter that could be documented, exhibited and sold.

At the end of the first auction, the hammer had said one thing: this transaction is complete.

The frame had something else to say.
