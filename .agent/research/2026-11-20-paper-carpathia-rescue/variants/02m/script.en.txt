The rescue ship found lifeboats where Titanic should have been.

In April 1912, the passenger liner Titanic struck an iceberg in the North Atlantic. Another ship, Carpathia, was dozens of miles away when its wireless operator, Harold Cottam, picked up the distress call. He was getting ready to stop work for the night.

Cottam took the message to the bridge. The captain, Arthur Rostron, ordered Carpathia to turn around. He asked twice whether the signal really came from Titanic. Then he set a course straight toward the reported position.

That choice was dangerous. Titanic had just struck ice, so Rostron knew the same water could threaten his own ship. He ordered more lookouts forward and pushed Carpathia as fast as he could.

He did something else while the engines worked. He turned his ordinary passenger ship into a floating rescue station. Doctors were assigned to different dining rooms. Stewards brought blankets, hot drinks and food. The crew prepared lifeboats, ropes and ways to lift injured people aboard.

The preparations kept moving. Titanic did not. At about 2:20 in the morning, it sank.

Carpathia reached the area around four. Its first lifeboat was taken alongside shortly afterward. For hours, the crew helped survivors up from small boats in the freezing Atlantic. By half past eight, more than seven hundred people had been brought aboard.

Rostron turned Carpathia toward New York. The ship that had been sailing away from America now carried the people who could still be rescued from Titanic.

His crew had prepared to receive an enormous liner's passengers. When they finally reached its last reported position, what was left for them to find?
