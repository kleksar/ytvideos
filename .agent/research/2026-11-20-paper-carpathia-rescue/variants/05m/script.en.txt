Why did a captain turn his passenger ship toward the ice that had just destroyed Titanic? And how do you prepare to rescue hundreds of people when you don't even know whether their ship will still be afloat?

Late on April 14, 1912, Titanic struck an iceberg in the North Atlantic. Her wireless operators sent distress calls. One of them reached a much smaller passenger ship called Carpathia, on its way from New York toward Europe.

Carpathia's operator, Harold Cottam, was about to finish work for the night. He had removed his coat but still had his headphones on. A message from Titanic came through. He took it up to the bridge, where officers went to wake Captain Arthur Rostron.

Rostron had only recently gone to bed. He ordered the ship around. Then, according to his later testimony, he asked Cottam twice whether he was absolutely certain the signal really was from Titanic.

It was. The position lay about fifty-eight miles away.

Rostron knew one thing about that position: Titanic had hit an iceberg there. Following her distress signal meant taking his own passengers into the same dangerous water. He called for the engines to run at full speed and put additional eyes on the lookout. Officers watched from the bridge, the crow's nest and the bow.

But here is the strange part of his decision. Rostron could not simply arrive, tie up beside Titanic, and open a door. If the liner had to abandon ship, its passengers would be scattered among small lifeboats. Some might be injured. Children could not be expected to climb a ship's side on a rope ladder.

So the preparation began long before Carpathia reached the scene.

Rostron assigned doctors to three different dining rooms. Stewards laid out hot drinks, food and blankets. The first officer prepared Carpathia's own lifeboats for use if needed. Gangways were opened. Ropes and tackle were made ready. The captain's orders included chairs and canvas bags that could help lift sick people and children up from boats alongside.

He also had to make space without endangering or frightening the people already travelling on Carpathia. Passengers and crew were directed away from the working areas; rooms and cabins could be given up to the expected arrivals.

All of this took place while the ship pushed through an ocean where the lookout might spot more ice at any moment.

As Carpathia approached, Rostron ordered rockets to signal that help was coming. Titanic had already sunk at about 2:20 in the morning. The rescue ship had not reached her.

Around four, Carpathia arrived near the reported position. Instead of a huge ocean liner, there were lifeboats. The first was brought alongside at about ten past four.

Now those preparations had a job to do. People had to be helped from boats that moved beside a high ship's hull, then brought inside, warmed, fed and counted. A single boat did not solve the problem. Another followed, and then another. The task continued through the morning.

By around half past eight, the people who could be recovered from Titanic's lifeboats were aboard: more than seven hundred survivors. Many more had died before Carpathia arrived. There is no honest version of this story in which a faster engine saves everyone.

There was another practical decision. Carpathia had been heading east, with passengers bound for ports in Europe. Rostron turned her back toward New York with Titanic's survivors. The wireless room now had to handle a different emergency: reports, names, messages to families, and other ships asking what had happened.

The United States Senate later singled out Rostron's preparations, particularly his medical arrangements, ready boats and extra lookouts. Those were not details added to make the story heroic. They were instructions he had issued before he knew what he would find.

And that is the part of Titanic's rescue that tends to disappear behind the sinking. Carpathia could not reach a ship that no longer existed. Its crew arrived with the equipment, space and people needed to turn the remaining lifeboats into a rescue.
