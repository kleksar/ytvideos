In 1932, Australian soldiers set up machine guns to stop birds from destroying wheat fields. The birds kept running. So the soldiers tried chasing them with a truck.

Those birds were emus: tall, flightless, and fast. Thousands had moved into Western Australia's wheat country during a drought. They ate crops, damaged fences, and left gaps for rabbits. Farmers were already struggling with falling wheat prices.

The government sent a small military team under Major Gwynydd Meredith. They brought Lewis machine guns and thousands of rounds. They expected to shoot at groups of birds.

But the emus did not stay in groups. When the men approached, the birds scattered across the fields. A machine gun could fire quickly, but first it needed a target that stayed in sight.

The soldiers tried another approach. They put a gun on a truck and pursued the birds. The ground was rough. The vehicle could not keep up, and the gun was too difficult to aim while bouncing.

The operation was suspended, then restarted when farmers asked for help again. Meredith later reported hundreds of emus killed, but the totals were disputed. The fields still needed protection.

This became famous as Australia's war against the emus. It was never a declared war, and the birds did not fight soldiers. They simply kept moving.

The longer-term answer was not a faster gun. It was fencing that kept birds away from wheat.

And that is why a truck carrying a machine gun ended up chasing birds across a field in 1932.
