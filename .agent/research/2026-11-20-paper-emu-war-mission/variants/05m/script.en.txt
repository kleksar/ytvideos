How do you stop thousands of birds from eating a wheat harvest? In 1932, Australian authorities tried soldiers with machine guns. When that failed, the men even took a gun onto a moving truck.

The strange part is not just that the birds escaped. It is that the people who needed help had good reasons to ask for it.

In Western Australia, farmers had been encouraged to settle and grow wheat. Some had served in the First World War. Then the Depression pushed wheat prices down, while drought made the land harder to work. Families had invested in fields that were no longer bringing in enough money.

Now emus were moving through the wheat country in large numbers, looking for food and water. An emu is a tall, flightless Australian bird with powerful running legs. A flock could consume crops and push through fences. The damaged fences created another problem: rabbits could get through the openings.

The farmers asked for protection. The Defence Minister approved military assistance, and a small group under Major Gwynydd Meredith reached the Campion district in November 1932. They carried Lewis machine guns and thousands of rounds of ammunition. A film cameraman came along too.

If the birds remained in one place, automatic fire might have removed many of them quickly. That was the expectation.

On the ground, the plan met a different kind of target. As the soldiers approached a group, the emus separated and ran. Instead of one concentrated flock, there were birds scattered across a wide field. There were fewer targets in front of each gun, and those targets did not remain there.

A machine gun can fire many rounds in a few seconds. It cannot make a fast-moving animal stop long enough to aim at it.

The men tried to turn mobility into an advantage. One Lewis gun was mounted on a truck. Now the soldiers could chase the birds instead of waiting for them.

But an Australian wheat field was not a smooth road. The truck bounced over uneven ground, struggled to keep pace, and the man on the gun could not aim reliably. The attempted solution added movement without adding control.

Press coverage made the whole operation sound ridiculous. There was a serious farming problem behind the jokes, but the early results did not justify the effort. The first campaign was suspended.

Then the farmers made another request. Their crops were still exposed; a few days of military action had not solved that. So the operation resumed later in November. This time there were more reported kills, yet the birds remained a problem across the region.

Meredith eventually claimed nearly a thousand emus killed in the campaign, along with additional birds that had supposedly died after being wounded. The figures have been disputed. They are not a reliable measurement of how much farmland had been protected.

That distinction matters. The mission was not to win a contest in marksmanship. It was to keep emus out of wheat fields. Even killing hundreds would not necessarily stop other migrating birds from arriving.

By December, the military effort ended. Farmers continued to seek assistance in later years. Bounties were used, but the more durable way to protect fields was to keep the birds out with exclusion fencing.

The phrase Great Emu War makes it sound as though birds defeated an army in battle. No such battle happened. It was a pest-control operation that struggled against a moving, dispersed population.

The soldiers brought a weapon designed to concentrate fire. The emus survived by doing the opposite: spreading out, moving fast, and refusing to stay where the guns were pointing.

And once the government chose that weapon, the next idea almost wrote itself: put it on a truck and chase the birds.
