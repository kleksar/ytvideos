Why would a government send machine guns into a field to stop birds? And why would it send them back after the first attempt had already failed?

In 1932, the Australian military was drawn into a problem that began years before the first shot. The outcome became a national joke. But if you look at what the farmers needed and how the birds behaved, the joke hides a much more interesting mistake.

Start with a wheat farm in Western Australia. The people working it were not simply choosing an easy life in the countryside. Government-backed settlement programs had encouraged new farmers to clear and cultivate land. Some were veterans of the First World War who had been offered a chance to build a life after military service.

Then came the Great Depression. The price a farmer could get for wheat fell sharply. A poor harvest was becoming more dangerous to a family's finances, not less. Drought added another strain. A farm could have a field of wheat and still have trouble surviving.

During the dry conditions, large numbers of emus moved toward the wheat country in search of water and food. An emu cannot fly. It can, however, cover ground on long, strong legs, and it does not need to follow a road.

The birds ate grain and damaged crops. When they pushed through fences, they also opened gaps that rabbits could use. Rabbits caused their own damage. A farmer who repaired a fence might have to do it again after the next group passed through.

The scale mattered. Contemporary and later accounts describe tens of thousands of emus in the affected districts. You could chase a few birds away from one field, but the others did not know that field was supposed to be safe tomorrow.

Farmers appealed for help. The Australian Defence Minister, George Pearce, authorized military assistance, and Major Gwynydd Meredith led a small party into the Campion area. Their equipment included Lewis machine guns and a supply of thousands of rounds. A cameraman was present to record the expedition.

The machines were not ridiculous objects. Lewis guns had a serious military purpose. In the right setting, automatic fire could strike a concentrated target much faster than one person firing a rifle. The problem was the assumption that a flock in the distance would remain a flock long enough to shoot at.

The operation began in November 1932. The men approached birds on open country. As soon as the birds sensed danger, the group broke apart. Instead of a mass of animals, the gunners faced separated targets moving in different directions.

Imagine looking through the sight of a gun. There is a bird ahead. You begin to track it, but it turns. Another bird is now farther away. The first disappears behind uneven ground. The gun can fire quickly. Your opportunity to aim is brief.

That is an illustration of the difficulty, not a reconstruction of any particular soldier's thoughts. What the historical reporting establishes is that birds scattered and the gunners struggled to hit them efficiently.

There was another limitation. Even a successful shot would not protect the fence on the next farm. The operation was attempting to reduce a wide, mobile population one encounter at a time. The birds could leave the area before soldiers arrived, then come back after they moved on.

The army did not stop after the initial disappointment. Its next attempt seems like an answer from a straightforward action story: put a gun on a truck. If the birds would not wait, the soldiers would follow.

The vehicle moved across rough agricultural land, not a paved road. It bounced. The gunner was shaken. The emus could run and veer across terrain the truck could not negotiate in the same way. The chase failed to provide the stable firing position the weapon required.

At this point the spectacle became news. Soldiers with machine guns had been sent against large birds, and the birds were still running through wheat. Parliamentary and newspaper jokes soon followed. Later versions of the story describe an army defeated by an enemy it could not even catch.

But there were real costs behind the laughter. Farmers were watching grain disappear during a depression. Soldiers were spending time and ammunition. The animals being shot were not an opposing army, but wild birds moving through their normal environment.

The initial operation was stopped. That might sound like the end of the story. It was not the end of the farm problem.

The birds were still there, and the farmers asked for help again. If the response had solved the damage, there would have been little reason to make that request. The government authorized a renewed effort, and Meredith's team went back into the field.

Later accounts describe more successful shooting during the second period. Meredith's final reported figure was 986 emus killed using 9,860 rounds, along with an estimate of other wounded birds that died later. Historians have challenged those numbers, especially the unverified deaths after wounding. We cannot treat every claimed kill as an independently established fact.

There is another issue even if the totals were exact. What would a number of dead birds tell us about next season's wheat? Almost nothing on its own. Farmers did not ask the government to produce an impressive statistic. They asked to be able to grow and sell a crop.

A culling operation and a protective barrier answer different questions. A gun can remove a bird at one location. A fence can keep many birds away from a particular location, as long as it is designed, placed, and maintained well enough. The number of rounds fired no longer has to track the number of birds arriving.

That did not mean fencing instantly solved every problem. It cost money and labour, and Western Australia had enormous distances to cover. Bounties and other control measures continued. In later years, farmers again requested military help. The historical record does not support the neat idea that the entire emu problem ended when one officer left the district.

It also does not support the image of a formal war between Australia and the emus. There was no declaration of war, no bird army, and no human casualties in combat with birds. The phrase Great Emu War was a mocking name for military pest control.

The animals were not following clever orders. Their natural behaviour happened to frustrate the plan. They scattered when approached, ran fast, and did not concentrate where the gunners wanted them. That is enough to explain why this looked like a military defeat.

One detail is especially revealing. A historical film record held by the State Library of Western Australia describes soldiers with guns in the fields and men following an emu by car. It records the event as the management of troublesome wildlife, not an invented battle scene. The archival footage has separate rights restrictions; it is evidence for the story, not material we can freely put into a new video.

An archive held by the State Records Office also records contemporary opposition to the shooting. Conservationists protested the method. The disagreement did not disappear just because the headlines turned the birds into a comic enemy.

So the event had several different outcomes. The soldiers did shoot birds. The farmers' underlying need for protection remained. The method was widely mocked. And the longer-term answer increasingly involved barriers separating crops from wildlife.

This is why the usual one-line version, 'Australia lost a war to birds,' is funny but incomplete. It makes the army's embarrassment the whole story and leaves out the farmers who had asked for help in the first place.

The closer you look, the less this resembles a battle and the more it resembles an engineering problem with the wrong tool. A fast-firing gun solves the problem of delivering bullets to a target. It does not solve the problem of keeping thousands of moving animals outside a huge patchwork of wheat fields.

The emus never had to defeat a machine gun. They only had to be somewhere else when it fired.

And that brings us back to the strangest scene of all: a truck crossing a wheat field with a gun on top, trying to catch a bird that did not know it was in a war.
