In 1945, a Dutch painter faced a strange accusation. Investigators believed that he had helped sell a priceless Vermeer painting to a senior Nazi leader. His defense was that the painting was not a Vermeer at all.

He said he had painted it himself.

The man was Han van Meegeren. Eight years earlier, a Rotterdam museum had celebrated another canvas, The Supper at Emmaus, as an extraordinary rediscovered work by Johannes Vermeer. Van Meegeren had made that picture too.

His trick went beyond copying brushstrokes. Old oil paintings have hardened paint and tiny cracks. Van Meegeren mixed a modern resin into his pigments, heated the finished surface, and created convincing cracks. The materials helped the painting pass tests that buyers trusted at the time.

Then the war changed what his deception meant. A different fake, Christ with the Adulteress, passed through an art dealer into Hermann Göring's collection. When Allied investigators found it after the war, they traced the sale back to Van Meegeren. They suspected he had helped transfer a Dutch national treasure to the Nazis.

Now admitting fraud was part of his defense. But why should anyone believe the confession of someone who had already deceived experts?

He painted another supposed Vermeer while witnesses watched. A photograph from 1945 records him working on it. Examination of his paintings and materials supported his account.

The accusation about selling a genuine Vermeer fell away. In 1947, he was convicted of forgery and fraud instead and sentenced to a year in prison. He died before serving the sentence.

His confession did not make the fraud harmless. It changed what investigators could prove. A painter who had spent years convincing the world his pictures were genuine finally had to convince it that they were fake.
