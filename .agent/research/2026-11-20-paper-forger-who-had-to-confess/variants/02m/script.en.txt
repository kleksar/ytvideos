In 1947, Dutch painter Han van Meegeren received a one-year prison sentence for forging masterpieces. The strange part is that he had been trying to prove his own fraud.

In 1937, a Rotterdam museum celebrated The Supper at Emmaus as a lost Vermeer. But the supposedly centuries-old picture had been painted by Van Meegeren.

He invented a religious scene that appeared to fill a gap in Vermeer's work. He mixed modern resin into his paint, hardened it with heat, and added age-like cracks. The museum accepted the picture.

During World War II, another fake Vermeer passed through a dealer into Hermann Göring's collection. After the war, Allied investigators traced the sale to Van Meegeren. They suspected him of helping sell a genuine Dutch treasure to a Nazi leader.

He confessed: he had made that painting, too. But how could anyone believe a man who had already deceived experts?

In 1945, he painted a new Vermeer-like scene while witnesses watched. A surviving photograph shows him at work. Examination of his paintings and materials supported the confession.

The supposed national treasure was a forgery. That defeated the allegation that he had sold Göring a genuine Vermeer. It did not excuse the deception: buyers and a museum had paid for another artist's name.

Once investigators accepted who had really painted the pictures, the question before the Dutch court changed. What penalty should the proven fraud carry?
