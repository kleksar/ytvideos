How can a painting be so convincing that a museum calls it a masterpiece, yet its maker later begs the police to call it a fake? And why would admitting a fraud protect him from a more serious accusation?

In the Netherlands, the name Johannes Vermeer carried enormous weight. He had painted relatively few pictures in the seventeenth century. Finding another one would mean that art historians had a new piece of a very small puzzle.

In 1937, a canvas called The Supper at Emmaus appeared and was hailed as just such a discovery. Museum Boijmans in Rotterdam acquired it as an exceptional Vermeer. The painter credited on the wall was a man who had been dead for centuries.

But the man who had actually made the picture was alive. His name was Han van Meegeren.

He understood what experts hoped to find. Rather than merely copy one of Vermeer's familiar domestic interiors, he offered a religious scene that could be explained as an early, little-known phase of the master's career. The picture appeared to fill a gap, and that made an unlikely attribution sound plausible.

Getting the style right was only half the problem. A seventeenth-century painting should not feel like one made last week. Paint hardens over time. Cracks form on old surfaces. Van Meegeren mixed a modern resin into his paint and heated the surface to make it hard. He then produced cracks that looked like age. An exhibition devoted to art forgery later documented how these techniques could defeat the alcohol-and-needle checks used at the time.

A museum had bought the painting. Experts had praised it. The deception was paying off.

During the Second World War, Van Meegeren made other paintings masquerading as Dutch masters. One, Christ with the Adulteress, went through an art dealer and ended up in Hermann Göring's collection.

Then Germany lost the war.

Allied investigators tracing art in Nazi hands found the supposed Vermeer and followed the sale back to Van Meegeren. To them, the living Dutch painter appeared to be connected to the transfer of one of his country's cultural treasures to a Nazi leader. That was a very different problem from fooling a collector.

He gave them a startling answer: the painting was his own work. It was a forgery.

But that answer created another obstacle. If the man had been dishonest about who painted the work, why trust him now? Even the museum's celebrated Vermeer was still being treated as genuine. An ordinary confession could be another lie.

So Van Meegeren agreed to demonstrate his ability. In 1945, he worked on a new painting in a Vermeer-like style while witnesses observed him. A surviving photograph in the Rijksmuseum shows him at the easel. Its contemporary caption says the point was to demonstrate how he had made the celebrated Emmaus painting.

The demonstration mattered, but it was not a magic trick that settled everything on its own. Examination of the disputed pictures and their materials also supported the account. The very modern substances that helped paint imitate age became clues that it could not be centuries old.

The case changed direction. The claim that he had sold a genuine Vermeer to Göring was dropped. In 1947, a Dutch court convicted Van Meegeren of forgery and fraud and sentenced him to one year in prison. He died before serving it.

There is a tempting way to tell this as the tale of a brilliant artist who outsmarted the Nazis. It leaves out the people he cheated, and it makes a criminal defense sound like a moral victory. The record supports something stranger and more limited.

He had persuaded experts to trust the age of his paint, the gaps in their knowledge, and the name they wanted to see on a canvas. When his own freedom was at stake, he needed those same experts to reverse their verdict.

Today, the Rotterdam museum lists The Supper at Emmaus under its real maker's name: Han van Meegeren. The painting did not vanish when its attribution changed. What vanished was the claim that it had been painted by Vermeer.

For years, Van Meegeren sold a false answer to the question, 'Who made this?' When investigators finally asked him again, the truthful answer was the one he had most reason to fear giving.
