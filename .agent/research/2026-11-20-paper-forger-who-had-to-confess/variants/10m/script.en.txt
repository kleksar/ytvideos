Why would a painter spend years making the world believe his work was centuries old, then ask the police to prove the opposite? How did a respected museum become part of his story? And why did the fall of Nazi Germany turn an art forgery into an entirely different kind of case?

Begin with a painting, not a courtroom. The Supper at Emmaus shows a religious encounter at a table. In 1937 it was presented as a newly discovered work by Johannes Vermeer, a Dutch painter who had died in the seventeenth century. A major Rotterdam museum acquired it as an extraordinary find.

For a collector, this was more than another attractive canvas. Vermeer had left a relatively small body of known paintings. A newly authenticated one would alter what scholars thought they knew about his career.

There was also a gap that made this particular discovery persuasive. Most people associate Vermeer with carefully lit domestic interiors. This canvas offered something different: a religious scene. Supporters could describe it as evidence of an early phase of his work, a period that had not been firmly documented. The difference from familiar paintings could be explained as a discovery instead of treated as a warning.

One artist knew exactly how useful that explanation could be. His name was Han van Meegeren. He had painted the picture himself.

The Dutch museum still holds the work. Its catalog now identifies him as the maker, though the historical story of the acquisition and the formal accession record use different dates. The important point is undisputed: the museum once presented his work as an exceptional Vermeer and did not learn the truth until after the war.

Van Meegeren did not need to reproduce an existing picture. He needed to create a painting that viewers could imagine Vermeer might once have made. He studied the expectations of collectors and art historians. An unusual canvas became convincing because it looked like an answer to a question they already had.

But an old-looking subject was not enough. The physical painting had to look old as well.

Oil paint changes over decades and centuries. A newly painted canvas and a centuries-old one can respond differently to a needle or a solvent. Examiners at the time used simple alcohol and needle checks to distinguish fresh paint from hardened old paint. Van Meegeren built his method around those tests.

He mixed a resin associated with modern plastics into his pigments. Heating helped the surface harden. He then made the surface crack in patterns that resembled age. An exhibition organized by International Arts and Artists later explained why this treatment could defeat precisely the checks that buyers relied upon.

That is a crucial distinction. He had not developed a material that would look ancient under every test imaginable. He had worked out how to fool the tests being used by the people standing in front of his pictures.

The painted scene, its claimed place in Vermeer's career, and the feel of its surface all told the same false story. The art world accepted it. The museum acquired it. The fame attached to the supposed Vermeer made the deception valuable.

Several other pictures followed. Van Meegeren offered works that appeared to belong to famous seventeenth-century Dutch painters, not just Vermeer. Some reached prominent collections. One went much further into history than any painting in a museum.

During the occupation of the Netherlands, a work called Christ with the Adulteress passed through the dealer Alois Miedl. It was acquired by Hermann Göring, one of the most powerful men in Nazi Germany. Van Meegeren's own role in that chain would become decisive after the war.

When the Allies defeated Nazi Germany in 1945, they were also tracing works of art that had passed through Nazi hands. Investigators found a supposed Vermeer in Göring's collection. Following the transaction led them back to the painter in the Netherlands.

Think about what that meant to the investigators. A Dutchman seemed to be involved in transferring a rare Dutch masterpiece to a Nazi leader. From their perspective, they were following the path of a national treasure. Whether a crime of collaboration had occurred mattered much more than whether a collector had overpaid for a picture.

Van Meegeren now faced an extraordinary problem of his own making. He had built his fortune on convincing people that other artists made his pictures. The accusation confronting him was serious because those pictures had been accepted as authentic.

So he confessed to the forgery.

The supposed Vermeer sold into Göring's collection, he said, had not been painted by Vermeer. Neither had The Supper at Emmaus and other works he identified. He had painted them.

Imagine being the investigator listening to that. A man connected to a suspect art deal announces that the supposedly valuable object at its center is a fake. His explanation would reduce the allegation that he had sold a genuine national treasure. But it was also exactly the sort of claim an experienced deceiver might make to escape a charge.

The problem was not simply whether Van Meegeren could paint. It was whether he could account for the extraordinary objects experts already believed in. A confession was a starting point, not a substitute for evidence.

Van Meegeren therefore set out to demonstrate the craft. In 1945 he painted another religious scene in a Vermeer-like style, titled Jesus among the Doctors, while witnesses observed him. The painting was his own new composition, not a copy of a known Vermeer.

We can see the demonstration in a contemporary photograph held by the Rijksmuseum. Van Meegeren is at work at an easel in Amsterdam. The period caption explains that he wanted to prove he had made the celebrated Emmaus picture. It also mentions old canvas, special brushes, and paints supplied for the exercise.

This is not a story in which he painted a perfect new fake in a few minutes and everyone immediately surrendered. The demonstration took work, and it did not stand alone. The attribution of the disputed paintings also depended on examining how they had been made.

Investigators and specialists had reason to look beyond style. Old masters had no access to materials invented centuries after their deaths. The resin that helped Van Meegeren imitate hardened old paint was a twentieth-century substance. Once scientists understood his method, the apparent age of the surface became a source of evidence against authenticity.

The details of the cracking mattered as well. A painting does not become old simply because someone has drawn cracks on it. The relationship between paint, its underlying layers, and the material inside the cracks can reveal how the surface was treated. Later scientific investigations of paintings associated with Van Meegeren also found synthetic resin in the paint.

This turned the logic of the case around. The technical method that had allowed him to sell modern pictures as old ones could help establish that they were modern. The objects that had once supported his reputation now supported his admission of forgery.

The suspected sale of a genuine Vermeer to Göring could no longer be maintained on the same basis. The case against Van Meegeren shifted from the transfer of a genuine national treasure to the deception involved in selling a fake.

In 1947 a court in Amsterdam found him guilty of forgery and fraud and sentenced him to one year in prison. He died before he served the sentence.

An easy ending would call this a victory for the man who fooled the Nazis. It would even turn him into a hero for having tricked a powerful Nazi collector. But that confuses the collapse of one accusation with the disappearance of wrongdoing. The people who purchased and authenticated his paintings had been deceived. The fact that he could defend himself by admitting forgery did not make the original fraud honest.

It is also too easy to blame the entire story on foolish experts. Their mistake had several parts. They were dealing with a small and incomplete record of Vermeer's work. The fake appeared to supply a missing chapter. Its physical surface had been engineered to survive the tests then in use. Each part reinforced the others.

There was a further irony. The painting did not become worthless as a historical object when the label changed. The Supper at Emmaus remained a real canvas, painted by a real person in the twentieth century. It became evidence of how an attribution was made, accepted, defended and finally overturned.

Museum Boijmans still lists it in the collection, not as a lost masterpiece by Vermeer but as a work by Han van Meegeren. The museum no longer needs to hide the mistake. The wrong identification is part of the object's history.

And that distinction is the final answer. Van Meegeren was not innocent because he confessed. He avoided being treated as the seller of an authentic Vermeer by proving that the supposed masterpiece was one of his own forgeries. The truth saved him from one allegation while confirming another.

For years, people paid for the name they believed stood behind the paint. In 1945, the one man who knew the actual name had to persuade investigators to stop believing the lie he had sold them.
