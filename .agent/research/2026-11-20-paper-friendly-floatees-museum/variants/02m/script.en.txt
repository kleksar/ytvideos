A yellow bath duck washed onto an Alaskan beach. Today, a duck from that same spill is catalogued in a British science museum.

It wasn't released for an experiment. On January 10, 1992, a storm knocked twelve shipping containers into the North Pacific. One held roughly twenty-nine thousand plastic bath toys. And here's the first surprise: most of them weren't ducks. There were also turtles, frogs and beavers.

They floated away from the ship. Ten months later, people near Sitka, Alaska, began finding the toys on their beaches.

For most people, that is rubbish to pick up. For oceanographers Curtis Ebbesmeyer and James Ingraham, it was a rare opportunity. The toys had escaped from a known location on a known date. Each reliable beach report could be compared with the route predicted by their computer model.

They had already studied shoes lost from another cargo ship. Now the bathtub animals were helping test where the surface water—and the wind pushing objects above it—might carry floating debris.

But no duck had a tracker. The scientists couldn't follow one toy across every wave. Stories about toys crossing the Arctic into Britain are much harder to prove than a specimen found in Alaska.

The Science Museum has one of those Alaskan ducks in its oceanography collection. Its label records a real object, not merely a line drawn on a map.

So a shipment that never reached its customers gave researchers something the toy maker never intended: evidence about the sea. And the strangest museum object in that story is also where it begins: a yellow bath duck on an Alaskan beach.
