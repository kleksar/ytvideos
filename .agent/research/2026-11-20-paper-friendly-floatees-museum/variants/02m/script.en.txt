Near Sitka, Alaska, a beachcomber picked up a yellow bath duck washed ashore. That small discovery gave oceanographers something a computer couldn't make: a real arrival point.

Ten months earlier, on January 10, 1992, a storm knocked twelve containers off a cargo ship in the North Pacific. One held about 29,000 bath toys: ducks, frogs, turtles and beavers. They weren't all yellow ducks.

Researchers Curtis Ebbesmeyer and James Ingraham knew where the shipment was lost. They were already studying how floating objects move. Two years earlier, lost sneakers had offered another accidental test.

Now a computer model suggested where the toys might drift. But would the ocean carry them there?

People began reporting toys on beaches near Sitka. The scientists could compare those real landing places with simulated routes. The toys had no trackers. A recovered duck couldn't reveal every wave it had crossed, or prove the dramatic stories about toys reaching Britain through Arctic ice.

What the reports could provide was a check on the model: a real arrival, not just a curve drawn on a map.

Britain's Science Museum now holds a genuine yellow duck from that spill, found near Sitka. It's catalogued under oceanography, even though it never measured a single current.

The scientists could calculate a possible route before any toy appeared. But to test that calculation, they still needed someone on the coast to find what the sea had delivered.
