Why is a plastic bath duck displayed in a science museum? How could it help researchers without a battery or a tracking chip? And what can that little toy never tell us?

The answer starts in the North Pacific, in January 1992. A cargo ship was travelling from Hong Kong toward Tacoma, Washington. During a storm near the International Date Line, twelve large containers went overboard. One held about twenty-nine thousand plastic bath toys.

The familiar version of this story says twenty-nine thousand rubber ducks fell into the ocean. That's not quite right. The shipment included yellow ducks, green frogs, blue turtles and red beavers. The total was close to twenty-nine thousand, not the duck count.

Picture the containers gone, the ship continuing its voyage, and the toys scattered across an enormous stretch of water. No scientist had planned to release them. Nobody had attached a sensor. For months, their destination was unknown.

Then people near Sitka, Alaska, began finding them on the shore. It took roughly ten months after the spill for the first reported toys to reach that coast.

A beachcomber holding a faded toy might seem an unlikely research assistant. But to oceanographers Curtis Ebbesmeyer and James Ingraham, one recovered toy could answer a very specific question. If a floating object starts in one known place at one known time, does a computer model send it toward the beach where it was actually found?

The men were not starting from zero. In 1990, another cargo accident had scattered thousands of athletic shoes across the Pacific. Ebbesmeyer and Ingraham studied those recoveries too. A lone shoe on a beach could be more useful than a tidy theory if its origin was identifiable.

The toys offered another test. A computer simulation could estimate how currents carried objects across the North Pacific. Wind mattered as well: a plastic toy above the surface doesn't behave exactly like a drop of water below it. The model could suggest a broad route; a dated beach report provided something real against which to check it.

That distinction is the heart of the story. The toys did not discover an ocean current by themselves. They supplied observations for researchers to compare with calculations they already had.

The reports also raised a second problem. The entire batch did not arrive on the same afternoon. Objects drifting from a common accident can take different routes, become stranded, or circulate for years. Finding one today doesn't tell you where all the others went.

And it doesn't tell you the complete journey of that one toy. No bath duck carried GPS. To reconstruct a route, researchers needed a starting point, a plausible model and somebody willing to record a landing place.

That is why the spectacular claim about toys travelling through Arctic ice and turning up in the Atlantic needs care. It is a possible route discussed in later accounts; identifying an individual weathered duck as a definite survivor of that exact journey is a different and harder task.

There is, however, something solid enough to hold. Britain's Science Museum catalogues an actual yellow Friendly Floatee found near Sitka. Its record lists the object under oceanography. The museum also holds examples of the other animals from the spill.

The museum didn't acquire an expensive instrument. It kept an ordinary toy whose starting location, recovery and use in research give it scientific meaning.

And there is an uncomfortable ending to the adventure. These toys were useful to scientists because plastic can stay afloat and travel a very long way. But that also means the cargo didn't simply disappear. A bright, funny-looking duck on a map is still plastic that entered the sea by accident.

So what did this remarkable fleet actually teach us? Not that every lost object can be traced, or that ducks can map every current. It showed how thousands of accidental drifters, a computer model and people checking beaches could reveal something about the movement of the ocean.

The proof is not a perfect map of a toy's entire voyage. It is a small yellow duck, found in Alaska, now filed in a science museum.
