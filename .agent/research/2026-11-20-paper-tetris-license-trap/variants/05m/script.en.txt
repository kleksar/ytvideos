Why did a man bring a Tetris cartridge into a Soviet office and come out questioning the rights to his own product? How did Nintendo's new handheld become part of the argument? And why would a court stop another publisher from selling a game that was already in stores?

The story begins in 1984. Programmer Alexey Pajitnov created Tetris in the Soviet Union: shapes made of four squares fell into a narrow space, and completed horizontal lines disappeared. The idea was simple enough to explain in seconds. Staying ahead of the falling pieces was much harder.

Copies found their way beyond Soviet computers. Western businesses began arranging publishing deals. But the programmer wasn't the person free to sell those rights internationally. A Soviet organization called Elorg controlled commercial licensing.

That distinction mattered more as the game spread.

In 1988, Henk Rogers discovered Tetris at a trade show in Las Vegas. He already developed and published games in Japan. Rogers later said he kept returning to the Tetris demonstration. He thought this little puzzle could reach a much larger audience.

He acquired what he understood to be Japanese publishing rights through other companies and prepared a cartridge version for Nintendo's Famicom home console. This was a serious investment, not a casual trial. In later interviews, Rogers recalled roughly two hundred thousand cartridges being prepared and family property helping secure the financing.

Then came Nintendo's new machine. The Game Boy was a portable system with a small monochrome screen. It didn't need a spectacular action game to show what it could do. Tetris fitted the display and was easy to play in short sessions.

Rogers wanted to get that version licensed. In 1989, he went to Moscow to negotiate with Elorg.

He brought his Japanese cartridge.

According to his recollection, officials told him they had never given permission for a home-console version of Tetris. He had assumed the businesses that sold him those rights could legally do so. Suddenly, the paperwork behind his existing product looked uncertain.

One word explains much of the trouble: computer.

An earlier agreement covered computers. That did not automatically cover a dedicated games console plugged into a television. And a handheld console was another market again. If a company had only been given computer rights, it couldn't necessarily pass console rights to the next company.

Rogers now had two problems. He wanted a deal for Game Boy, but his Famicom cartridges might already rest on a faulty chain of licenses. He also faced rivals. Robert Stein, who had helped bring Tetris to Western publishers, and businesses connected to British publisher Robert Maxwell were pursuing rights of their own.

Elorg did not have to treat everybody's paperwork as equal. Its officials could decide what the original agreements covered and who would receive new permissions.

Rogers negotiated directly with the Soviet rights holder. He secured the handheld arrangement for Nintendo. Nintendo obtained separate rights for home consoles. It was the direct connection to the owner of those rights, rather than the apparent promise of a reseller, that mattered.

The dispute wasn't over yet. In America, Atari Games, through its Tengen label, had released a Tetris cartridge for the Nintendo Entertainment System. It believed earlier agreements authorized the release. Nintendo disagreed.

The two sides went to court. Atari argued in part that a game console counted as a computer under the old license. The judge rejected that interpretation. In June 1989, a preliminary injunction stopped further distribution of Tengen's version. The larger lawsuit continued, but Tengen could no longer keep selling its cartridge on that claim.

Nintendo's Game Boy version had a different future. Tetris accompanied the handheld's launch that year and became closely associated with it. People did not need to understand Soviet export contracts to play the falling-block puzzle. Yet those contracts had decided who could put the game in their hands.

Pajitnov, who made Tetris in the first place, did not become rich overnight when it reached the Game Boy. The Soviet control of the rights meant he initially missed out on the commercial returns. Years later he and Rogers would become business partners.

For one company, Tetris became a cartridge whose sales were stopped by a judge. For Nintendo, it became a game millions knew on a small handheld screen.

And for Rogers, the hardest part of getting a new license was discovering how uncertain his old one had been.
