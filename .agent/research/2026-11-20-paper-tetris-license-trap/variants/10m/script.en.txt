How did a puzzle made on a Soviet computer lead to a dispute over cartridges already being produced in Japan? Why did several companies believe they owned the right to sell the same game? And what did a court decide that shaped Tetris on Nintendo machines?

In 1984, Alexey Pajitnov worked at a computing centre in Moscow. He built a puzzle with falling shapes, each formed from four squares. Rotate them, move them sideways, and fill horizontal lines. The lines vanish. When the pieces pile up to the top, the game ends. There was no character to rescue and no map to explore. The pleasure came from mastering a small set of rules that kept getting harder.

He made a game, but he did not create an international publishing company.

That difference matters. Under the Soviet system, software created in a state institution was not simply private property for the programmer to license around the world. A government export organization called Elorg controlled commercial contracts for Soviet software. Pajitnov could explain how his game worked. He could not personally sell every international console license.

Copies of Tetris nevertheless found their way from computer to computer and, eventually, outside the Soviet Union. A businessman named Robert Stein saw the opportunity to distribute it in the West. Publishers such as Mirrorsoft and Spectrum HoloByte released versions for personal computers. Each new agreement depended on what an earlier agreement had actually allowed.

Think about the machines involved. A home computer was sold with a keyboard and could run many programs. A console was designed primarily for games and used cartridges. An arcade machine stood in a public gaming hall. A handheld was small enough to carry away. The same Tetris rules could work on all four, but a commercial contract could give one company permission for only one category.

The problem was not obvious while the deals looked like a chain of normal business transactions. It became obvious when different businesses tried to claim the same market.

Henk Rogers was an experienced game maker living in Japan. He had worked on The Black Onyx, an early successful Japanese role-playing game, and knew the market for Nintendo's Famicom. At a Las Vegas trade show in 1988, he tried Tetris and kept returning to it. He later said he had never been so pulled back to a demonstration.

The puzzle needed neither cinematic graphics nor a complex story. Rogers saw the potential for a game that a newcomer could start immediately and keep learning. He arranged publishing rights he believed covered Japan, but the authorization reached him through other companies, not from Elorg directly.

His company prepared a Tetris cartridge for the Famicom. Rogers later recalled a large commitment: about two hundred thousand cartridges, around two million dollars of manufacturing exposure, with his in-laws' property supporting the financing. That account comes from him years afterward, not from a factory inventory inspected for this script. It is enough to explain the stakes without inventing private thoughts.

A publisher considering a product can still walk away. A publisher who has commissioned a huge number of physical cartridges cannot simply turn the idea off.

Then Nintendo was preparing a different kind of product: the Game Boy. Its screen was monochrome and far less elaborate than a television game. But Tetris only needed recognizable shapes and clear controls. Rogers saw the chance to put the game on a machine people could carry.

He understood that rights for a home console were not necessarily rights for a handheld. So in 1989, he travelled to Moscow to negotiate a new agreement with Elorg.

He also carried something that represented a problem he had not expected: a Japanese Tetris cartridge from his existing business.

According to Rogers's later account, the officials looking at the cartridge told him Elorg had not given permission for a home-console version. The publisher had bought what he believed to be legitimate rights from another business. But if the earlier company did not possess those rights, it could not give them to him.

This was not a dispute over whether the game was real or whether Rogers had paid money to someone. It was a dispute over whether the seller actually had the authority to license that kind of machine.

The key word in the earlier agreement was computer.

At the time, many people used that word broadly. Technically, a game console contains a computer. But the contract could still mean a personal computer sold for general use, not a dedicated games machine. Those separate markets were valuable to different publishers. A firm with permission to publish on personal computers might not own the console market, however confident its contracts sounded.

The portable Game Boy created another separate category. Even a publisher with legitimate rights on one Nintendo system could not simply assume it controlled every future Nintendo device.

Now Rogers had two linked problems. He wanted to obtain handheld rights for Nintendo. He also had to understand whether the license supporting his existing Famicom cartridges was as strong as he had believed.

Other parties had arrived with their own interests. Robert Stein had been one of the men bringing Tetris toward Western publishers. Businesses associated with British publisher Robert Maxwell also had claims and hopes for further licenses. Elorg was examining royalty payments, the wording of old documents, and what earlier deals had actually conveyed.

None of this required every person involved to be deliberately lying. Some businesspeople believed that their chains of agreements were valid. But multiple sincere beliefs cannot create a missing legal permission.

Rogers chose to deal directly with Elorg. That was a practical change of strategy. Instead of asking yet another reseller for assurance, he went to the source of the commercial rights. And because the Game Boy was a new device, the negotiations could specify it as a distinct market rather than rely on a vague word from an older agreement.

He also met Alexey Pajitnov. The creator knew the game as a puzzle and a program, not just an entry in a contract. Rogers was a game designer as well as a publisher. Their acquaintance later grew into a friendship and business partnership. But meeting the programmer did not, by itself, transfer the Soviet government's licensing rights or pay Pajitnov a fortune.

The agreements that emerged gave Rogers the handheld rights to support Nintendo's Game Boy edition. Nintendo also obtained the rights for home consoles from the Soviet side. Those were separate legal needs solved with direct deals.

That sounds like the ending, but another business already had its own cartridges in the shops.

Atari Games, operating its Tengen publishing label, had prepared a version of Tetris for the Nintendo Entertainment System in the United States. Tengen believed it had the right to sell it through an older chain of licensing. Players could buy the cartridge and enjoy the puzzle. The disagreement was about who had permission to distribute it on that platform.

Nintendo claimed that its agreement with Elorg gave it the relevant home-console rights. Atari's side argued that its earlier license covered the Nintendo machine because a game console could be classified as a computer.

That argument moved from conference rooms to a federal courtroom.

In June 1989, the judge rejected the interpretation that those computer rights extended to Nintendo's home video-game console. A preliminary injunction stopped further distribution of the Tengen version. The full legal battle had other stages, but the immediate consequence was concrete: an already released cartridge could no longer be sold on the basis of those claimed rights.

The ruling did not reach into every home and erase every copy that had already been purchased. Surviving Tengen cartridges still exist. It determined who could keep distributing the game commercially. That is a different thing from saying the game itself was banned.

Now compare what happened to the two cartridge businesses. Rogers brought a Famicom cartridge to Elorg and discovered his earlier paperwork might be defective. Atari's Tengen label brought a cartridge to the American market and saw a judge stop its distribution. Both stories turned on what the earlier contracts actually said. They did not have identical legal outcomes, and should not be mixed together.

The Game Boy story went another way. In 1989, Tetris accompanied the launch of Nintendo's handheld in major markets. People could play its familiar falling shapes wherever the portable console went. For a product with modest graphics and simple controls, the design was a remarkably good fit.

That success belonged to several different actors. Pajitnov created the original rules and working game. Other programmers adapted and spread it. Western publishers exposed new players to it. Nintendo built and promoted the portable hardware. Rogers pursued the commercial rights. Elorg controlled the permissions that made those business decisions possible.

It would be inaccurate to say a single meeting invented Tetris or that Nintendo discovered an unknown Soviet game entirely on its own. By 1989, many people were already playing it. The Moscow negotiations mattered because they changed who was legally allowed to manufacture and distribute particular editions.

They also did not immediately turn Pajitnov into a wealthy owner of the international brand. Soviet control of the game's licensing meant he initially did not share in the commercial returns in the way a private Western publisher might. In 1991, he moved to the United States. In 1996, he and Rogers formed the partnership behind the company that would manage the Tetris brand.

That is a longer human result than the immediate victory in court. A programmer and a publisher from very different systems ended up working together, years after they first met because of a game made from seven simple shapes.

The story has since been adapted into a 2023 movie with car chases and Cold War dangers. Rogers has said openly that some of those screen events did not happen. The real story does not need an invented pursuit. It has a shipment already under way, uncertain contracts, direct negotiation, rival claims and a judge ruling against an existing product.

The first question was who could publish a puzzle. The decisive question was narrower and more costly: who could publish it on each kind of device?

For one company, the answer meant a Tetris cartridge that could no longer be distributed. For Nintendo, it meant a handheld carrying one of the most recognizable games in history. The falling shapes were the same. The rights behind them were not.
