What would make me buy an air fryer when I already have a working oven? How much would I save each time, and how many dinners would it take to earn the price back?

I want to cook the same small portion of frozen chips, over and over, without changing the food. I can pay for a new air fryer, or keep using the electric oven I already own. Before choosing A or B, I'm fixing the score: total pounds paid for buying a machine and running it. Faster cooking is useful, but it isn't my money score.

Here's the catch. An air fryer can genuinely use much less electricity and still cost me more money overall.

I haven't run a kitchen experiment. I'm borrowing a published laboratory comparison and building a simple purchase-cost model on top. In that comparison, both appliances cooked 320 grams of frozen chips. The researchers included preheating when it was needed. The air fryer took twenty-three minutes and used 0.287 kilowatt-hours. The built-in oven took thirty-three minutes and used 0.863.

I expected the small hot-air basket to have an advantage. But the size of the difference surprised me: the air fryer used almost exactly one third of the electricity and finished ten minutes sooner. On a single dinner, A seems like a straightforward win.

Then I look at the shopping receipt. My oven is already paid for. In this scenario, I must buy A first, for thirty pounds. That price is an explicit assumption, not the verified price of the particular fryer the laboratory tested. I'm also assuming that a thirty-pound model would match its energy use. A real purchase could behave differently.

To keep the arithmetic honest, I pick an illustrative tariff of twenty-six pence per kilowatt-hour. The oven costs about twenty-two pence in electricity for one batch; the fryer costs about seven and a half. That saves A almost fifteen pence per batch.

But saving fifteen pence does not erase a thirty-pound purchase in one evening. I put two columns on my imaginary kitchen counter. A begins at minus thirty pounds before any food cooks. B begins at zero. Each identical batch moves A about fifteen pence closer.

After fifty batches, B is still more than twenty-two pounds cheaper in total. After a hundred, B is still ahead by roughly fifteen. At two hundred, it's almost a dead heat. Batch two hundred and one is the first one that puts the thirty-pound fryer ahead in this model.

That's the moment I was actually looking for. A wins the energy test from the first plate. B wins the spending test until I've cooked about two hundred plates.

Now I change one input without pretending to redo the laboratory experiment: how often I make that same portion. Twice a week makes two hundred batches take close to two years. Every day reaches the crossover much sooner. Once a month may never repay the machine during the period I keep it.

Another input matters even more: the price tag. With the same measured energy difference, a hundred-pound fryer needs about six hundred and sixty-eight batches to overtake my existing oven. A two-hundred-and-eighty-five-pound fryer needs roughly nineteen hundred. These are hypothetical calculations, not evidence that such models consume the same electricity.

There is also a trap in making the portion bigger. The laboratory's fryer had a recommended limit of five hundred grams of chips in its basket. If I need enough food for a large family, I may have to cook repeated batches. The oven can hold more, but we don't have a matched laboratory measurement of that larger oven load. I cannot award B a bulk-cooking victory using the energy figure for the smaller meal.

And the chips themselves? Both appliances produced some unevenly cooked pieces. So I won't quietly turn an electricity comparison into a claim that one always tastes better.

I end up with a choice, not a universal appliance champion. If I already own an oven and cook this little portion rarely, B keeps more money in my pocket. If I cook it frequently and buy a suitable affordable air fryer, A can eventually catch up while saving energy and time per batch.

The electricity winner was easy to name. The money winner depended on a question I hadn't asked at the start: how many times would I really use the thing?
