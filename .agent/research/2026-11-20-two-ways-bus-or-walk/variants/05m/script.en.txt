I'm at a bus stop with one ordinary problem. I can wait here, or I can walk to the place I'm going. Which choice gets me there first?

Let me put the two routes on the same map. On foot, it's twenty minutes from this stop to the door. If I ride the bus, the driving part is ten minutes. In this illustration there is no walk from the bus to the building, no transfer, and no traffic surprise. I start both clocks at the same instant.

Before I reveal anything, pick A: stay at the stop. Or B: start walking. The only score is minutes until I reach that door.

The arrival board says the bus comes in five minutes. I can already see why A looks tempting, but the result has to include the part everyone forgets: standing still. Five minutes waiting, then ten minutes riding. Fifteen in all. Walking needs twenty. A wins by five minutes.

I could stop there and announce that buses beat walking. That would be a terrible rule.

I change only one thing in the drawing: the wait grows from five minutes to fifteen. I don't lengthen the walking route or slow the vehicle. A now takes twenty-five minutes. B still takes twenty. This time the person who walked away from the stop arrives five minutes earlier.

What actually flipped? Not the bus's speed. Not my walking speed. The number on the arrival board.

Once I'm on board, the bus makes a twenty-minute walk into a ten-minute ride. That means it has a ten-minute advantage to spend. Every minute I stand at the stop uses up one minute of that advantage. At a wait of nine minutes, the bus arrives one minute ahead. At eleven, walking arrives one minute ahead. At ten, they reach the door together.

Now comes a less obvious question. If the times tie, is the choice really a tie for me?

A published questionnaire gave 391 people a similar choice. They were told the walk takes twenty minutes and the bus ride takes ten. When the wait was five minutes, about 78 percent chose the bus. With a fifteen-minute wait, about 86 percent chose walking. But when both journeys took twenty minutes, roughly 63 percent chose walking.

Those were choices in a questionnaire, not races recorded on a street. And the percentages don't tell me why any individual answered that way. Still, the middle result matters: equal arrival times don't guarantee equal preferences. I might prefer moving to standing still. Someone else might choose the seat.

There is another trap. The board is a prediction. If it says five minutes but the bus takes fifteen, my earlier winner disappears. And if the route takes twelve minutes on a bad day, the break-even wait changes from ten minutes to eight. I should check what is actually included in the time shown.

For this model, I have not pretended to measure a real city's traffic. I fixed the route and changed one input, then checked the arithmetic. It doesn't prove that walking beats buses or that buses beat walking. It tells me which number I need before deciding.

In the first case I wait. In the second I walk. When the two clocks tie, the fastest method no longer answers the whole personal question.

I can take that rule to a real stop, but only after checking the route, the arrival estimate, and whether I can comfortably walk it. And at the next stop, I still face the same choice.

Should I wait for this bus, or walk?
