My freezer is twenty degrees below zero. That sounds like the obvious place to put a warm can. But I have two choices: the freezer, or a bowl of ice, water, and salt. Which one actually gets the drink cold first?

I am not pretending I ran this test. A researcher at SINTEF published measurements using a can filled with water. The can started at twenty-one degrees Celsius. The finish line was five degrees inside the can, measured with a sensor.

Here is why I would have picked the freezer. Its air was minus twenty. The ordinary ice bath was around zero. Surely colder means faster.

The results overturn that guess. In the freezer, the small can needed about sixty minutes to reach five degrees. Sitting in plain ice water, it needed ten. And in salty ice water, it took only two and a half minutes.

So my two choices are not even close. The salted bath was twenty-four times faster than the freezer in that particular test.

The trick is not that salt somehow makes the drink instantly colder. Salt lowers the freezing point, allowing an icy mixture to get below zero. More importantly, the water surrounds the metal can and pulls heat away far more efficiently than still air. The researcher recorded a bath around minus sixteen degrees with the salt added.

But this is not a guarantee for every kitchen. The test used water inside a small can, a particular amount of salt and ice, and a specified starting temperature. I cannot promise the same clock time for a big plastic bottle.

If I have ice and want one small can cold now, I choose the bowl. If I have no ice, the freezer is still an option, just not the quickest one in these measurements.

And I know exactly which instinct I'd fall for again without that stopwatch.
