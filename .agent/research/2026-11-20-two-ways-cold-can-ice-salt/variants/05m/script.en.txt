What would I use to chill a warm can: a freezer at minus twenty degrees, or a bowl of ice water? The freezer sounds colder. But temperature is not the same thing as speed. And when salt enters the bowl, the gap becomes almost absurd.

I want to compare two methods against one finish line: how long until the centre of a small can reaches five degrees Celsius? I have not tested this in my kitchen. I am walking through a published home experiment by Kristian Solheim Thinn, a researcher at SINTEF in Norway. Keeping that distinction matters, because a neat animation can make a model or someone else's measurement look like my own.

First, the starting conditions. The experiment used a 0.33-litre can filled with water, not a branded soda. A temperature sensor measured the liquid inside. Each method began with the can at twenty-one degrees. That lets me compare the time to one destination instead of comparing one slightly cool can with another that starts near the fridge.

My choice A is the freezer. Its air was around minus twenty degrees, so I expect it to take the heat out quickly. Choice B is the bowl. The researcher tried ordinary ice water, around zero, and an ice-and-salt mixture that reached roughly minus sixteen. Those are two settings for my bowl method, not two unrelated stories.

Before seeing the salt result, something surprising already happens. The plain ice bath gets the water inside the can down to five degrees in about ten minutes. The freezer needs an hour. The bowl wins without any special ingredient. If my picture of cooling is simply 'pick the lowest number on the thermometer,' that picture has already failed.

Now add salt to the ice bath. In the reported setup, the salty mixture used five litres of water and 1.25 kilograms of salt. This is a sizeable test bath, not a pinch of salt dropped into a coffee mug. The measured bath was about minus sixteen degrees. And the can crossed the five-degree line in two and a half minutes.

That is twenty-four times as quick as the freezer in this one test, and four times as quick as the ordinary ice bath. It also means the can is already at the target before the freezer has made much progress on its sixty-minute journey. The answer to my A-or-B choice is B, by a remarkable margin, provided I actually have the ice and salt available.

How can a minus-sixteen bath beat minus-twenty air? The freezer's air may be colder, but the can is mostly surrounded by gas. A water bath puts liquid into contact with the metal across much of its surface. Heat can leave the can much faster. The salt also changes the freezing point, so ice and liquid water can coexist at a temperature below zero while the ice melts and takes in energy. The colder bath and the more effective contact work together.

I could stop at the dramatic result and call it a universal hack. But a good comparison needs to survive a change of circumstances. The researcher also tested a one-and-a-half-litre bottle. In that larger container, an ordinary ice bath took roughly twenty to twenty-five minutes to reach five degrees, while the freezer took about ninety minutes. The article did not report a matching salt-bath time for that large bottle. I cannot paste the small can's two-and-a-half-minute result onto it.

There is another practical choice: what counts as 'cold enough'? In the report, the small can in salty ice water reached nine degrees in about two minutes. Reaching five took a little longer. If I only need a pleasantly cool drink rather than the coldest possible one, I should decide the target first. Otherwise I can make any method look fast by lowering the finish line after the result arrives.

And there is a cost hidden in the bowl. It requires ice, water, salt, a container large enough for contact, and cleanup afterwards. The freezer asks much less of me if I am not in a hurry. My metric here is time to five degrees, not electricity use, ingredient cost, or convenience; I have no measured winner for those other metrics.

The result is useful because it corrects the exact mistake I would have made. I used to look for the coldest place. Now I look for the place that removes heat from the can most effectively. For one small warm can and an ice supply, the published test points decisively to the salty bath. If all I have is a freezer, I will use it and give it time.

My winner is not the bowl in every imaginable situation. My winner is the bowl for this starting can, this finish line, and this measured comparison. That is a much better answer than a miracle trick that supposedly works everywhere.
