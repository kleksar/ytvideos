Suppose I want a cold can of something to drink, but all I have is a can at room temperature. I could slide it into a freezer at minus twenty degrees Celsius. Or I could prepare a bowl filled with ice, water and salt. Which would get the liquid in the centre of the can down to five degrees first? And why might the method with the warmer surroundings win?

Those are two different questions. The first gives me a useful decision in the kitchen. The second forces me to rethink what 'cold' actually does. I am going to answer both using a published set of measurements, not a test that I secretly performed for this video. The distinction between someone's measured result and my illustrated retelling is part of making the comparison fair.

The experiment was described by Kristian Solheim Thinn, a researcher at SINTEF in Norway. He placed temperature sensors into containers full of water and compared several cooling environments. A 0.33-litre can and a 1.5-litre bottle were both included in his work. The smaller can is my main subject, because it is the one with a reported result for salty ice water.

Before I start either timer, I need a rule that cannot change. Every small-can test starts with the water at twenty-one degrees Celsius. The sensor sits inside the liquid. I stop the timer at five degrees. That way, I am not comparing freezer air temperature with ice temperature and calling the colder one the winner. I am comparing time to the same temperature inside the thing I actually want to drink.

My first option, A, is the freezer. In the published experiment its air was around minus twenty. I know why I would choose it: I can put the can inside, shut the door and forget it for a while. There is no mixing, no bowl to wash and no ice to gather. Its temperature looks impressive, too. If I were asked to guess using only the numbers, I would probably put my money on minus twenty.

Option B is a water bath surrounding the can. Start with ordinary ice and water, near zero degrees. This is not yet the special salt trick. It is simply water touching much of the metal surface instead of air. I can imagine a timer running beside each container, but those clocks belong to the researcher's measurements; no camera footage or stopwatch of mine exists.

Here comes the first reversal. The ordinary ice bath takes around ten minutes for the small can to reach five degrees. The freezer takes about sixty. Even though the freezer air is much colder than the ice bath, the ordinary bath finishes roughly fifty minutes earlier. That tells me something more useful than 'water is cold': a comparison of cooling speed depends on how the surroundings take heat away, not just on their starting temperature.

Now I make the second change to option B. The researcher added salt to ice water. For that particular setup, the article describes five litres of water and 1.25 kilograms of salt. It is a substantial quantity. If I tell someone to throw a tiny pinch into a bowl and promise the exact same result, I will be pretending that the experimental setup does not matter.

The salty bath reached about minus sixteen degrees. So it still was not colder than the minus-twenty freezer air in this comparison. Yet it brought the water inside the small can to five degrees in about two and a half minutes. The freezer took sixty. The ordinary ice bath took ten. I have three numbers now, but only two main choices: the freezer versus an ice-water bowl, with salt showing what a change inside the bowl can do.

Look at the effect on my decision. At the start I might have waited an hour with option A. With option B, the reported salted setup passes my finish line within a few minutes. Divide sixty by two and a half and I get twenty-four. That is a factor of twenty-four in this particular experiment, not a promise that my freezer is always exactly twenty-four times slower than every ice bath.

If I stop here, I have a life hack. But I still do not understand why it works. The freezer is cold air. The can gives its heat to the air next to it, and heat must keep moving away. How quickly that happens depends on more than the thermometer's lowest reading. Air generally transfers heat away from a metal surface much less effectively than a liquid bath in close contact with it.

The water bath surrounds the can, including more of its curved sides. The liquid can accept heat from the metal directly, and movement in the water redistributes that energy. Think about placing a hand in cool air and then in cool water. I would not need them to feel identical simply because a thermometer gave a similar number. That analogy is about heat-transfer behaviour, not an invitation to use dangerously cold water on skin.

Salt supplies the second part of the explanation. Pure water and ice sit near zero degrees under ordinary conditions. Dissolved salt lowers the temperature at which the mixture freezes. When some ice melts in salty water, it can absorb energy, and a mixture of ice and liquid can remain below zero. In this experiment the reported bath was around minus sixteen. That gives the surroundings an additional temperature difference while the water keeps effective contact with the can.

The Australian National University has an educational experiment on this same mechanism. Its instructions ask people to compare ice with ice plus salt and to measure the change rather than simply declare the method superior. The university explains freezing-point depression and heat transfer. It does not supply the precise two-and-a-half-minute result from SINTEF; those are different sources, with different experiments, and I will not splice them into one fictional laboratory session.

One subtle point matters when I turn research into animation. The temperature sensor in SINTEF's setup was inside a can full of water. The experiment did not claim to measure the centre of my favourite soda with its own sugar and dissolved gas. A real soft drink can behave somewhat differently, and its packaging and starting temperature may differ. The small-can numbers are a measured example of the mechanism, not a guaranteed timer for every drink on a supermarket shelf.

I also need to be careful about changing container size. The researcher tried a larger bottle, holding one and a half litres. For that bottle, plain ice water reached five degrees in roughly twenty to twenty-five minutes. The freezer took about an hour and a half. So a water bath still beat the freezer under those tested conditions, but neither number is the same as for the small metal can.

What about salty water around that big bottle? The published table does not give that result. I can reasonably hypothesize that the temperature and heat contact would matter, but I cannot write an exact finish time and present it as a measurement. Plastic transfers heat differently from thin metal, and the bottle holds far more liquid. Rather than fill my ten-minute story with invented precision, I should leave that cell in the scoreboard blank.

Now change my target rather than the container. Five degrees is not the only possible definition of a cold drink. SINTEF also gives times for nine degrees. For the small can in salty ice water, the report puts nine degrees at about two minutes. That is already a usable endpoint for someone who does not demand a drink as cold as the five-degree benchmark. The winner is still the bath in this test, but the finish line moves closer.

The refrigerator is another reminder that a low number outside does not tell the whole story. In the reported small-can test the fridge needed about two and a half hours to reach nine degrees, and about ten hours to reach five. I am not going to compare those numbers with my two-minute bath and announce the refrigerator is defective. A fridge is designed to maintain food at a stable low temperature efficiently, not necessarily to chill one freshly warm can as fast as a stirred bath.

There is a temptation to replace a bowl of salt with a different shortcut. Maybe I could wrap the can in wet paper and put it in the freezer. The SINTEF researcher said he tested that idea in his setup and found essentially identical cooling time to the unwrapped can. That is useful as a counterexample to a confident-looking internet trick. It is still only the result of that particular test, not proof that wet wrapping never helps in any airflow or humidity.

I could also try moving the can gently in the bath to reduce warm layers of liquid near the surface. The researcher mentions shaking as a way to distribute temperature more efficiently, and ANU suggests movement as a variable for further experiment. But I do not have a timed, comparable number for the exact movement I am illustrating, so I will not award it another made-up victory. If the can is carbonated, I would avoid carelessly shaking it before opening it.

Imagine I want to repeat the comparison at home. I would use two matching unopened containers of the same drink, brought to the same starting temperature. I would choose one endpoint and one thermometer method before the clock starts. I would record the size and amount of ice, water and salt, because an amount of salt without enough ice does not magically recreate the measured bath. I would run each condition more than once if I wanted a reliable estimate rather than a single anecdote.

And I would not change the success metric mid-test. There are at least three reasonable scores: time until a drink reaches five degrees, the total effort and materials used, and how conveniently I can cool several drinks at once. I only have data from this experiment for the first. A bowl can use salt and consume ice, while the freezer is already available in many kitchens. I cannot name a universal winner for electricity use, money or cleanup based on these cooling times alone.

The same caution applies to the most dramatic comparison. When I say twenty-four times faster, I mean sixty minutes divided by two and a half minutes to reach the same internal target for the specific small water-filled can. I am not claiming twenty-four times less electricity, twenty-four times colder liquid, or twenty-four times better taste. Those would be entirely different experiments.

The best part of the result is not the speed record. It is the change to my original mental shortcut. I thought the surrounding temperature settled the question. But for an object to cool, its heat must get out. A minus-twenty box full of air is not automatically the fastest exit for that heat. A somewhat warmer bath can win because it stays in much better thermal contact with the container.

So when I need one small can cold now and I already have enough ice, I would choose the bowl with water and salt. I know what target produced the headline result, who measured it and how large the test can was. If I have no ice, or if I care more about convenience than speed, the freezer remains reasonable. My answer is conditional, which makes it useful.

What would I do if I forgot all those conditions and chose by the lowest number on the thermostat alone? I would put the can straight into the freezer. That would bring me right back to the mistake this comparison was built to catch.
