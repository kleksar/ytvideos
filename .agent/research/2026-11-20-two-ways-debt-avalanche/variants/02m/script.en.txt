I erased one debt in three months. The other method made me wait until month eleven for my first finished bill. So why did I choose the slower-looking method?

I made a model with three bills: six hundred dollars at five percent interest, twenty-four hundred at twenty-nine percent, and four thousand at sixteen percent.

I can pay four hundred dollars every month. Two hundred and five covers the three minimums, so I can choose where the remaining hundred and ninety-five goes.

A is the snowball: pay extra toward the smallest debt. B is the avalanche: pay extra toward the highest interest rate. I keep the same budget, pay every minimum, and add interest monthly. This is a fictional calculation, not my actual bank account.

With A, that tiny six-hundred-dollar bill disappears in month three. I remove its envelope. Two debts left.

With B, the small envelope stays. I attack the twenty-nine-percent bill instead. It takes until month eleven to close my first account.

Which would you choose if you could see only those envelopes?

Now I reveal the cost. Both methods clear all three bills in month twenty-one. Snowball charges me one thousand two hundred fifty-six dollars and ninety-three cents in interest. Avalanche charges one thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars and ninety-six cents. B saves one hundred seventeen dollars and ninety-seven cents.

The reason is simple: while A chased an easy win, the expensive account kept charging interest. B reduced that balance earlier.

Real credit cards may calculate interest daily, change minimums, or add fees. And the feeling of a quick victory can help some people keep paying; my spreadsheet cannot measure that.

My chosen score was total interest, so B wins. But if I measured the first visible victory, A wins by eight months. And the thought that still tempts me is exactly the one that fooled me at the start.
