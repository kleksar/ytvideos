Would I rather get rid of one bill quickly, or spend less money paying off all my bills? I tried both choices in the same fictional experiment. One made me feel successful eight months earlier. The other saved money.

I put three envelopes on a table. The small one says six hundred dollars at five percent annual interest. The middle one says twenty-four hundred dollars at twenty-nine percent. The largest says four thousand dollars at sixteen percent. Total debt: seven thousand dollars.

I can pay four hundred dollars a month. Each bill has a minimum: thirty, seventy-five and one hundred dollars. Those minimums use two hundred and five. The extra hundred and ninety-five is my decision. Whenever I finish a bill, I keep the total payment at four hundred and redirect the freed-up amount. No new purchases or skipped payments.

I name the strategies before I see a winner. In A, the snowball, I aim extra payments at the smallest remaining balance. In B, the avalanche, I aim at the highest rate. And I choose the score in advance: total interest until all balances reach zero. I'll also record the month in which my first whole bill disappears.

My simulation adds interest to each balance once a month, at the annual rate divided by twelve, rounds to cents, then sends payments to the accounts. Real lenders may use different rules. I'm not claiming this happened to me.

A gets off to a fantastic start. Its smallest envelope receives two hundred and twenty-five dollars in the first month: the thirty-dollar minimum and every spare dollar. After interest and payment, only three hundred seventy-seven dollars and fifty cents remain on that bill.

Three months in, the small envelope is gone. One entire problem has vanished from my table. It feels as though I must be ahead.

But look at the middle envelope. Because I made only minimum payments on it, the twenty-nine-percent debt is still about two thousand two hundred seventy-seven dollars after three months. That rate is doing expensive work in the background.

With B, I leave the small bill alone except for its minimum. I send the extra hundred and ninety-five to the twenty-nine-percent bill. After one month, that costly balance is two thousand one hundred eighty-eight dollars. Under A it would be two thousand three hundred eighty-three dollars. Both plans spent exactly four hundred.

At month three the difference on that high-rate bill has widened. B owes about seventeen hundred forty-nine dollars there. A owes about twenty-two hundred seventy-seven. More than five hundred dollars of high-interest balance separates the two methods.

Yet B has not removed a single envelope. That first complete payoff only arrives in month eleven, when the twenty-nine-percent debt finally disappears. A crossed out a bill in month three. So if the score were completed accounts by month three, A would crush B.

Now I let both plans run to the end. A clears the high-rate debt in month twelve and the last debt in month twenty-one. B also finishes the entire set in month twenty-one. That's a calendar tie, but not a money tie.

Under A, the bills generate one thousand two hundred fifty-six dollars and ninety-three cents in interest. Under B, they generate one thousand one hundred thirty-eight dollars and ninety-six cents. B costs one hundred seventeen dollars and ninety-seven cents less. I don't artificially pay another full four hundred dollars after the balance is gone; the final payment is simply smaller.

Why does B win on cost? Each extra dollar removed from the twenty-nine-percent bill stops more future interest than a dollar removed from the five-percent bill. A didn't fail to pay its minimums. It chose where to spend its discretionary money.

There is a human complication. Crossing off a small bill early can be motivating. Research on repayment behavior has found such small victories can support persistence in some settings. That doesn't prove every snowball user sticks to a plan better, or that a particular person will make the same choice.

I also can't transfer this model directly to a real card statement. Rates, fees, daily interest, and minimum payment rules may all differ. Missing a required payment could matter more than either strategy.

So my scoreboard has two honest answers. For the fewest interest dollars, I choose B. For the earliest completely cleared envelope, I choose A. I began by cheering as one envelope vanished. The surprise was discovering what the other two cost while I was cheering.
