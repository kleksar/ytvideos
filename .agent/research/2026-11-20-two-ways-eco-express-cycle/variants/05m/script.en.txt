Does a dishwasher that runs for three and a half hours cost less to use than a one-hour cycle? And if so, am I trading away clean dishes just to save electricity?

I'm looking at two choices for one ordinary load. Option A is Express, an hour. Option B is Eco, three hours and thirty-five minutes. I'll compare electricity first, then water, then what the word "better" actually means. I'm using published laboratory and consumer-test figures, not pretending I've measured a dishwasher myself.

My example is one specific Bosch model, SMS4EMI06G. That detail matters: an Eco button on another machine can have very different numbers.

The manufacturer's program table lists 1.3 kilowatt-hours for Express and 0.645 for Eco. Express finishes two hours and thirty-five minutes earlier, but uses almost double the electricity. By the metric I declared, Eco wins.

That feels backwards. A three-hour machine seems like it should run up a bigger bill than one that stops after sixty minutes.

But an hour on the clock isn't an hour at one fixed electrical power. Some parts of a dishwasher cycle require relatively little electricity. Heating water can require much more. On this model, Express is a hotter, time-optimised program. Eco has a lower wash temperature and more time to work. That's the mechanism behind the apparent contradiction, not proof that every long cycle is cheap.

I can also compare water, with no assumptions about a utility bill. Express is listed at 10.5 litres per cycle; Eco at nine. The difference is one and a half litres. Here, the slower option uses less of both resources.

Now I want the result to be useful rather than just a pair of surprising numbers. Suppose I run twenty loads in a month and pay a hypothetical twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour. The energy difference is 0.655 kilowatt-hours per load. Multiply that by twenty and my illustrative saving is $3.28 a month. I haven't looked up anyone's local tariff, and the actual bill would depend on the machine and cycles used. The point is that a big percentage difference doesn't always create a huge cash saving for one household.

At this stage, I could announce Eco as the winner. But I haven't told you whether the dishes come out clean.

The manufacturer's table reports water, energy and time; it does not give me a head-to-head cleaning score for these two programs. I can't invent that missing result.

So I turn to a different source, and keep its limits visible. Consumer organisation Which? tested main, quick and eco programs on three big-brand dishwashers in a 2024 snapshot. On average, the main program scored eighty-three percent for cleaning. Eco scored seventy-nine, and quick scored seventy-four. Their drying results also differed.

That tells me there may be a trade-off between resource use and cleaning. It does not tell me the cleaning score of either program on my Bosch example. Those were different machines in a small comparison.

It also changes my choice. If I need to reuse the plates in an hour, a cheaper three-and-a-half-hour program can't meet my deadline. Express wins on availability, even though it loses on electricity. But if I'm loading the dishwasher after dinner and don't need the plates straight away, I can give Eco the time.

One more button makes the story less obvious. The same Bosch lists a Silence program lasting four hours. It uses 1.05 kilowatt-hours, more than Eco's 0.645. That mode is designed around reduced noise, not winning an energy contest. A longer duration alone tells me almost nothing.

My result is limited but clear. For this model and these published consumption figures, Eco beats Express on electricity and water. Express beats Eco on speed. Cleaning quality for this exact pairing is unverified.

For my next ordinary load, I would choose Eco if the plates can wait. If I need them in an hour, I'd choose Express and accept its higher listed energy use. The decision isn't hidden in the timer; it depends on what I need the cycle to do.
