Which gas station is cheaper by the time I get home?

I see two prices. The station near my house charges a dollar eighty per litre. The station across town charges a dollar sixty-five. Fifteen cents less sounds like an easy win. But that second station is much farther away.

I'll compare them in a simple model, not pretend I've driven this route. I start at home and want to come back with forty more litres in my tank than I had at the start. The same car uses eight litres per hundred kilometres. Both stations have the same fuel, and there are no queues or discounts.

Method A is two kilometres from home, so four kilometres there and back. Method B is twelve kilometres away, so twenty-four kilometres for the round trip.

Pick A or B. I'm measuring money paid for the same amount of fuel left at home, not just the price on the sign.

At A, the journey burns point three two litres. To finish forty litres ahead, I buy forty point three two. The bill is seventy-two dollars and fifty-eight cents.

At B, the drive burns one point nine two litres. I must buy forty-one point nine two litres to reach that same final tank level. The bill is sixty-nine dollars and seventeen cents.

B wins, but only by three dollars and forty-one cents. Most of the apparent discount disappears on the road.

Now I change only the amount I need. Suppose I want ten extra litres at home. The roads and prices stay exactly the same. A costs eighteen dollars and fifty-eight cents. B costs nineteen dollars and sixty-seven cents.

The cheaper pump just became the more expensive trip. B needs a bigger fill-up to pay for its detour. Under these exact assumptions, that changeover is around seventeen point three litres.

And I've not counted my extra driving time. Before I turn the wheel toward a bargain, I need to ask the question again.
