Two gas stations have almost the same product, but their prices are different. If I drive farther for cheaper fuel, how much do I really save? And could the cheapest sign actually cost me more?

I have a normal problem: the car needs fuel before the next week of driving. Close to home there's a station at one dollar eighty a litre. Across town the same fuel is one dollar sixty-five. My first instinct is to take the discount. The question is whether the drive will eat it.

I'll test A, the nearby station, against B, the cheaper station. This is an illustrated calculation, not footage of a trip I made. I want the same outcome whichever route I choose: when I get back home, the tank must contain forty litres more than it did when I left.

Here are the fixed conditions. My model car burns eight litres per hundred kilometres. A is two kilometres away. B is twelve. I start at the same house, take one return trip, and use the same car. There are no queues, tolls, memberships or differences in fuel quality. I assume the tank has room for the required purchase.

Which one would you pick? I'm scoring actual money spent to reach the same fuel level back at home. I'll look at my time after I settle the fuel bill.

Let's drive to A on paper. The station is two kilometres out and two back: four kilometres. At eight litres per hundred, that trip burns zero point three two litres. I don't buy just forty. I buy forty point three two, because the return drive will use some of what I bought. Multiply by a dollar eighty and the till shows seventy-two dollars and fifty-seven point six cents, rounded to seventy-two fifty-eight.

Now B. It's twelve kilometres out and twelve back. Twenty-four kilometres use one point nine two litres. To arrive home forty litres ahead, I buy forty-one point nine two. At a dollar sixty-five, that costs sixty-nine dollars and sixteen point eight cents, or sixty-nine seventeen.

The lower sign wins this forty-litre test. But look at the margin: three dollars and forty-one cents. If I had just multiplied forty litres by the fifteen-cent discount, I'd expect six dollars. The extra trip took away more than two and a half dollars of that apparent advantage.

And here's where the story changes. I may not need a big refill. I could just need ten litres to get through the next few days. Everything else stays fixed, including the roads and the car.

For A, ten point three two litres at a dollar eighty costs eighteen dollars and fifty-eight cents. For B, eleven point nine two at a dollar sixty-five costs nineteen dollars and sixty-seven cents. This time the cheaper pump loses by one dollar and nine cents.

I can draw the tipping point. Every extra litre I actually want at home gives B another fifteen cents of advantage. But the longer route starts B two dollars and fifty-nine point two cents behind on the trip itself. Divide that disadvantage by fifteen cents and I get seventeen point two eight litres.

Below that amount, A costs less. Above it, B costs less. At the crossing the bills are equal, apart from rounding. The sign didn't change. My need did.

One other cost isn't on those receipts: time. Suppose B adds twenty minutes to the journey compared with A. For the forty-litre result, I'd be spending twenty minutes to keep three dollars and forty-one cents. That's roughly ten dollars an hour if I translate the saving into a rate, not a wage anyone paid me.

If I value those minutes more than the saving, A can be my personal choice while B is still the cash winner. And if B happens to lie on a route I'm already taking, its extra distance could shrink; I'd have to run the calculation again rather than charge it for a trip I was going to make anyway.

There isn't one universal winner. With these prices, distances and fuel use, B wins a forty-litre refill, while A wins a ten-litre top-up. The useful trick is to count the road and equalize what ends up in my tank. Then I can decide whether the remaining saving is worth the drive.
