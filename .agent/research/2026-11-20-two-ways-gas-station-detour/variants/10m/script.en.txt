Two signs are tempting me. One says one dollar eighty a litre. The other says one dollar sixty-five. The cheap sign is farther away. I could take the discount, or I could be paying for it before I even reach the pump.

Here's the everyday decision I want to settle. I need petrol, I have two stations, and I want to come home with the same amount of fuel whichever one I use. The surprising part is that both options can win without either station changing its price. All I have to change is the size of my refill.

I'll build one transparent model. I'm not showing a road test, and these are invented example prices, not today's prices in any city. My car's assumed consumption is eight litres per hundred kilometres. I start at home with enough fuel to reach either station, and there's enough empty tank space to buy what the model requires. No traffic delays, toll roads, queueing, rewards cards or differences in petrol quality. I'm comparing the cash spent first, and treating time as a separate choice.

A is the close station, two kilometres from home. Its return journey is four kilometres, and it sells fuel at a dollar eighty per litre. B is the far station, twelve kilometres from home. Its return journey is twenty-four kilometres, and it charges a dollar sixty-five.

Imagine I need to increase the fuel in my tank by forty litres, measured after I get back home. Which would you choose? Before I calculate, pick A or B.

It's tempting to say B saves fifteen cents on forty litres: six dollars. But that comparison leaves something unequal. If I buy exactly forty litres at each pump, I won't have forty extra litres at home. I spend some fuel getting there and back, and B spends much more. I'd have more fuel left after A's shorter trip. That's not a fair end state.

So I draw the journey out and back before adding the receipts. At A, four kilometres at eight litres per hundred uses point three two litres. To be forty litres up when I return, I buy forty point three two. At a dollar eighty, the precise bill is seventy-two dollars and fifty-seven point six cents. That's seventy-two fifty-eight at the checkout.

B is twenty-four kilometres round trip. That uses one point nine two litres. To arrive home with the same forty-litre gain, I need forty-one point nine two at the distant pump. At a dollar sixty-five, I pay sixty-nine dollars and sixteen point eight cents, or sixty-nine seventeen.

B wins the cash contest, but the difference is three dollars and forty point eight cents, rounded to three forty-one. The posted discount suggested six dollars. The extra travel reduced that to a little over half. It didn't erase it.

That answer sounds final until I change my plan. I don't always fill a large amount. On another day I only need ten additional litres for short errands. The stations, roads, fuel use and prices remain fixed.

A now needs to sell me ten point three two litres. That bill is eighteen dollars and fifty-seven point six cents, or eighteen fifty-eight. B must sell me eleven point nine two, at a cost of nineteen dollars and sixty-six point eight cents, or nineteen sixty-seven.

The winner flips. On the small refill the cheaper sign costs one dollar and nine cents more to achieve the same final tank level. No dishonest price board. No secret membership. The distance did the damage.

Why does the answer flip? The discount increases with every litre I need, while the detour stays the same length. Every extra litre of desired fuel gives B another fifteen cents in price advantage. But the extra fuel I must buy for B's longer route creates an initial handicap.

I can calculate that handicap exactly. B's route burns one point nine two litres, charged at a dollar sixty-five when I replace it: three dollars and sixteen point eight cents. A's route burns point three two litres, charged at a dollar eighty: fifty-seven point six cents. The difference between those two trip components is two dollars and fifty-nine point two cents.

That initial disadvantage is offset by the fifteen-cent-per-litre savings. Two dollars and fifty-nine point two divided by point fifteen gives seventeen point two eight litres. That's the break-even amount I want left over at home. At a ten-litre goal A wins. At forty B wins. At precisely seventeen point two eight, the exact costs match, before the display rounds to cents.

Now the real test is whether that boundary is useful beyond a neat number. One reason it's useful is that I can move it when the car changes. The road distance is the same, but not every car burns eight litres per hundred.

Suppose a more economical model burns six. The near trip uses point two four litres and the far trip uses one point four four. Under the original prices the crossover drops to twelve point nine six litres. The distant discount can pay for the journey with a smaller refill. At a forty-litre goal, A costs seventy-two dollars and forty-three cents, while B costs sixty-eight dollars and thirty-eight. B saves four dollars and six cents after rounding.

Now suppose a thirstier car uses twelve litres per hundred. The near route consumes point four eight and the far route two point eight eight. The crossover rises to twenty-five point nine two litres. For the same forty-litre gain, A costs seventy-two dollars and eighty-six cents, while B costs seventy dollars and seventy-five. B still wins, but only by two dollars and eleven cents. This isn't a claim about any specific real vehicle. These are three assumptions applied to one route.

The second lever is the difference on the signs. Keep my original eight-litre car, but suppose the distant price only drops to one dollar seventy-five. The near price stays one eighty. The discount is five cents, not fifteen.

At a forty-litre target, A's bill is still seventy-two fifty-eight. B is forty-one point nine two litres times one seventy-five, which is seventy-three dollars and thirty-six cents. The distant station loses by seventy-eight cents. The price gap was too small to buy back the detour. The corresponding crossover climbs to fifty-five point six eight litres, possibly more than I can safely fit in my actual tank. I shouldn't recommend a refill a real car cannot hold.

If the distant station instead charges one dollar fifty, B wins much more easily. For a forty-litre gain, its bill is sixty-two dollars and eighty-eight cents versus A's seventy-two fifty-eight. The difference is nine dollars and seventy cents. At a ten-litre goal, B is seventeen eighty-eight and A eighteen fifty-eight; even the small refill now favours the trip. Again, these are different hypothetical signs, not a report about actual fuel prices.

The third lever is the route itself. So far I've treated both options as special trips from home. If I'm already driving past B to visit a friend, B may add almost no kilometres. Then charging the whole twenty-four kilometres to the fuel stop would be misleading. My comparison should include only the journey caused by choosing that station. On the other hand, a detour off a regular commute can still add kilometres even when the station looks close on a map. What matters is the change in the whole route, not the straight-line distance between two signs.

And there's a separate cost the pump can't show: my time. Suppose B adds twenty minutes compared with A. The forty-litre case saves three dollars and forty-one cents in fuel spending. To earn that much saving I'd be giving up a third of an hour. That's about ten dollars and twenty-two cents per extra hour of travel, if I express the saving as a rate. It isn't income, and it doesn't prove what my time is worth.

If I decide that twenty minutes of my evening is worth more than three dollars and forty-one cents, I can personally choose A, even while admitting B won the fuel-only calculation. Traffic or queues could increase that time; I haven't measured either. Tyre wear and vehicle depreciation are also outside the cash-at-pump model. Including them could make the far choice less attractive.

There are two other catches before turning a model into instructions. I need enough petrol to reach either station in the first place. And I need sufficient tank capacity to replace the fuel burned on the trip while still achieving the target amount. A forty-litre net gain on B means buying forty-one point nine two litres; some starting tank levels and cars cannot accommodate that purchase. In that case the scenario is infeasible for the car, not a reason to ignore the extra fuel.

The calculation also assumes a constant eight litres per hundred over both routes. In reality a short city trip and a longer highway trip may have different fuel use. A model using route-specific consumption would replace the one common rate with two measured or defensibly estimated rates. I'd check my real trip and car before taking any of these cents as a promise.

So what's the payoff for me? The cheap sign can be the right answer at forty litres, the wrong answer at ten, and an even worse answer after I value my time. The comparison only works when I hold the starting point and final fuel level fixed, count the kilometres each choice causes, and state which costs I care about.

Next time I see a bargain across town, I won't start with the sign. I'll start with the trip I must make and the amount I actually need. Only then do those two prices mean something.
