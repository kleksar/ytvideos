Do I leave the heating at twenty degrees all day, or turn it down while I'm at work and warm the house up again?

I leave at eight. Both versions start in the same warm house. Outside, it's five degrees. I want the rooms back at twenty when I return at four. My measure is the heat the system has to deliver over those eight hours.

In A, I keep the thermostat at twenty. The house keeps leaking heat to the colder outdoors, and the heater keeps replacing it.

In B, I lower the thermostat to sixteen. For the first few hours, the house cools without heating at all. Then it holds sixteen. Before I return, the heater starts again. That final warm-up counts; I'm not hiding it.

I haven't tested a real house. This is a simple calculation with identical insulation, thermal mass and heating power on both sides.

The result: A needs 19.2 kilowatt-hours of delivered heat. B needs 15.64. By four o'clock, both rooms are warm again.

So even after paying back the heat needed for the warm-up, B uses 3.56 kilowatt-hours less in this model. The reason is simple: a cooler room loses heat more slowly.

That isn't a promised saving on my bill. A real house has changing weather, people and heating equipment. A heat pump can even use inefficient electric backup during a rushed warm-up, changing the electricity result.

For the modeled conventional heater, I choose B. I let an empty house cool, but schedule it to be comfortable before I walk through the door. If I have a heat pump, I check its setback guidance instead of copying the same schedule.
