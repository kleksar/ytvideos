I'm comparing two ways to clean the same dirty dishes. One uses two basins in my sink. The other uses a dishwasher. Which creates less climate pollution?

This isn't footage of an experiment I performed. I'm following a published American life-cycle study, with the same number of place settings and the same ten-year period.

For method A, I wash in warm, soapy water, then rinse in a second basin of cool water. I don't leave the tap running.

For method B, I fill the dishwasher, scrape off food instead of pre-rinsing, select a normal cycle, and leave heated drying off.

Before I reveal the result, choose A or B. I'm measuring greenhouse-gas emissions over the full life cycle, not how quickly the dishes become clean.

In that study's recommended-practice scenarios, A comes to about 1,610 kilograms of carbon-dioxide equivalent over ten years. B comes to about 1,960.

The careful hand-washing method wins this particular carbon comparison by 350 kilograms, roughly eighteen percent. That surprised me, because an efficient dishwasher usually beats people who wash under a constantly running tap.

But here is the catch. If I abandon the two-basin method and wash under running water, I'm no longer testing the winning method. The study's typical hand-washing scenario actually has much higher emissions than typical machine washing.

And the machine gives me something that isn't on this carbon scoreboard: my time back. The carbon winner isn't automatically the winner for my evening.

So before I choose a method for my next load, I have to decide what I'm trying to save.
