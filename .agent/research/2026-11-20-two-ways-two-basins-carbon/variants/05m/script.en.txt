I have a sink full of dirty dishes and two ways to deal with it. I could use two basins, or I could load a dishwasher. If my goal is the smallest carbon footprint, which one should I choose?

I haven't washed a hundred plates on camera or measured a machine myself. I'm comparing two clearly specified scenarios in a 2020 University of Michigan life-cycle study. The researchers didn't just count the water leaving a tap. They considered equipment, use, energy and other impacts over time.

The common answer seems easy: the dishwasher. It keeps reusing a relatively small quantity of water instead of letting clean water flow straight down the drain. And if I stand at the sink with the tap running, that is usually true.

But that isn't the manual method I'm testing.

For A, I put warm soapy water in one basin. I wash the dishes there, then rinse them in a second basin of cooler water. The tap isn't running continuously. For B, I scrape the dishes rather than pre-rinse them, fill the dishwasher, use its normal program and skip the heated-dry option.

Those are two recommended techniques applied to the same sort of dish load. I will keep the study's scenario fixed: four loads of eight place settings each week, for ten years. My main score is kilograms of carbon-dioxide equivalent across the life cycle. Not money, not cleaning speed, and not the litres used by one particular machine.

Which side do you think is lower?

Here is the first useful clue. In the study, ordinary hand washing was much worse than ordinary machine washing. The reported ten-year estimates were 5,620 kilograms for typical manual habits and 2,090 for typical machine habits. That comparison helps explain why the dishwasher has such a strong reputation.

Now I change the question from typical habits to the two specific recommended methods I announced at the start. The hand-washing total falls dramatically when I stop running the tap and use two basins.

The study's recommended manual method produces an estimated 1,610 kilograms over ten years. Its recommended machine method produces 1,960. A, the careful two-basin method, is lower by 350 kilograms, about eighteen percent relative to B.

The machine didn't suddenly become inefficient. I changed what the person at the sink was doing. That's why a slogan like 'machines always use less' misses the most interesting part of the comparison.

There is another surprise. The result would look different if I chose time rather than emissions as the main score.

Suppose I take thirty-five minutes longer to do each load by hand. That is an illustration, not a measurement of these exact two households. At four loads a week, the difference adds up to roughly 1,200 hours over ten years. I might happily choose the dishwasher to get those hours back even if its modelled carbon total is higher.

And I still can't treat the 350 kilograms as a universal number. The study models a typical American household. Water heating, local electricity, how full I load the machine, and whether I secretly pre-rinse the dishes can all move the result. The researchers also used a small-scale study of real household behaviours. It's not a trial of every dishwasher or every kitchen.

So I have two honest results, not one miraculous cleaning trick. With the two specified recommended methods and this life-cycle carbon metric, A wins. With the same dirty dishes and a time-saving goal, B can be the better personal choice.

Next time I wash up, I'm not asking simply, 'hand or machine?' I'm asking what behaviour and what finish line I actually mean.
