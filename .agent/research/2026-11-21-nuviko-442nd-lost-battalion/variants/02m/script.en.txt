The men sent to rescue an American battalion in 1944 had barely been given time to rest.

In the Vosges mountains of France, roughly 275 U.S. soldiers had pushed down a wooded ridge. German forces moved behind them and cut off their route back. The trapped unit became known as the Lost Battalion.

Other American troops tried to reach it and were turned back. Supplies dropped from the air often fell outside the surrounded position. Inside the pocket, ammunition, food, and medical supplies were running low.

Then the Army called in the 100th Battalion and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. Most of their infantrymen were Japanese Americans. Back in the United States, the government was still holding many Japanese American families in incarceration camps.

On October 27, the rescuers advanced into the forest before dawn. The trees were so dense and the darkness so complete that men held the packs of the soldiers in front of them to stay together.

German machine guns and mortars blocked the narrow ridges. When one position fell, another stood farther along. On October 29, the leading companies fought through a heavily defended hill, but the trapped men were still beyond reach.

The following afternoon, a patrol from Company I reached the surrounded battalion. Men from the 100th Battalion made contact from another direction.

Two hundred and eleven soldiers were rescued.

The price was enormous. An account by the 100th Battalion's historical organization estimates fifty-four rescuers killed and about three hundred wounded during the five-day rescue. Those are casualties, not all deaths.

The Army had saved the trapped men. But the soldiers who broke through had paid dearly, and the fighting did not simply stop when the rescue was complete.

That is why, when we tell the story of the men who finally came out, we have to return to the men who were sent in.
