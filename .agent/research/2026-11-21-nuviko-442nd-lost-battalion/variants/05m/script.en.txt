How did two American units end up fighting their way toward each other through the same forest? Why was the battalion sent to the rescue already exhausted? And why is the final number of men saved only part of this story?

In October 1944, the U.S. Army was pushing through the Vosges mountains in eastern France. Thick woods, narrow ridges, freezing rain, and German artillery made each stretch of ground difficult to take.

One American regiment had been fighting for days around the towns of Bruyères and Biffontaine. Its soldiers finally received a short break: dry clothes, hot food, a chance to sleep.

Then another American battalion advanced too far along a wooded ridge. German troops cut in behind it. Around 275 men of the 141st Infantry Regiment were trapped, with no safe road back.

Other attempts to break through failed. Aircraft tried to deliver supplies, but much of what they dropped landed beyond the surrounded soldiers. The men holding the ridge had wounded comrades and too little food, water, and ammunition.

On October 26, the regiment that had just been allowed to rest was called back. The 100th Infantry Battalion and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team were ordered to reach the trapped soldiers.

This was not an ordinary detail in the history of the U.S. Army. The unit was made up predominantly of Japanese American soldiers. At home, many families of Japanese ancestry were still imprisoned by their own government without individual charges.

Before dawn on October 27, men in the advancing battalions walked through darkness so complete they had to hold the packs of the soldiers ahead of them. German mortars and machine guns were waiting along the ridges.

That afternoon, German troops counterattacked the 442nd's Third Battalion with infantry and a tank. Private Matsuichi Yogi moved forward with a bazooka and disabled the tank. The counterattack failed, but the rescuers could not keep moving in the darkness. Yogi was killed two days later.

The next morning, the 100th Battalion followed retreating German soldiers toward a hollow between two slopes. Artillery and mortar fire struck the hollow and broke up the attack. On another part of the front, German machine guns stopped Company K. Sergeant Gordon Yamashiro moved forward, located enemy positions, and helped open a gap. He was killed while covering his men's advance.

The troops had made some ground. They still had not reached the trapped battalion.

On October 29, the route tightened into a narrow ridge. Mines blocked the approach. The slopes were too steep for an easy move around the German positions. Tanks and artillery helped, but the leading companies were repeatedly pinned down.

Eventually men from Companies I and K advanced from tree to tree under fire and broke the position. Barney Hajiro was among those who pushed through its machine-gun defenses. Another battalion fought for a hill on the left flank, protecting the wider advance.

By evening, the gap had narrowed. But a final roadblock still stood between the rescue force and the surrounded Americans.

On October 30, artillery struck that roadblock. Company I drove out its defenders. At about two in the afternoon, a patrol led by Technical Sergeant Takashi Senzaki reached the outer positions of the Lost Battalion. Minutes later, a platoon from Company B of the 100th Battalion arrived from another direction.

The isolated men were no longer alone. Two hundred and eleven survived to be rescued.

It would be easy to stop the story there. But the operation had cost the rescuers heavily. The 100th Battalion's historical organization estimates fifty-four killed and roughly three hundred wounded in the five-day rescue. Some accounts use larger figures, but those include different spans of fighting in the Vosges. Casualties means killed and wounded together, not everyone killed.

And the surviving rescue troops did not all go home or even return to a long rest. On October 31, the 100th and Third Battalions were assigned more positions to hold or attack.

There was a ceremony after the war. In July 1946, President Harry Truman told the 442nd that they had fought both an enemy and prejudice. That was a remarkable acknowledgment from the country that had incarcerated so many Japanese Americans during the same war.

The battalion trapped on the ridge was rescued. The harder question is what that rescue had demanded of the men sent to reach it. Their answer was written into the ground they crossed, one defended position at a time.
