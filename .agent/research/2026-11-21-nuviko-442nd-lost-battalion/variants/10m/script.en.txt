What does it take to rescue soldiers when the road to them is itself held by the enemy? How did a regiment already worn down by two weeks of fighting manage to get through? And why does a rescue celebrated by the United States also expose a deep contradiction in the country that ordered it?

The story begins in eastern France, in October 1944. American forces were pushing through the Vosges mountains. On a map the distances between nearby towns looked manageable. On the ground, narrow ridges and thick pine forests broke that map into a succession of obstacles.

Rain soaked clothing and turned roads into mud. Fog shortened visibility. German troops placed roadblocks at narrow approaches, covered them with machine guns, and used artillery against men moving beneath the trees. Shells striking the upper branches sent fragments into the ground below.

The 100th Infantry Battalion and the 442nd Regimental Combat Team had arrived in France after fighting in Italy. Their infantrymen were predominantly Japanese Americans. The 100th had its own identity and combat history, but it fought alongside the larger 442nd team.

During the battles for Bruyères and Biffontaine, the men pushed through defended hills and villages for days. The regimental history describes soaked equipment, little sleep, and mounting exhaustion. When the 100th and Third Battalions were relieved on October 24, the chance to get dry clothes, hot food, and a few hours of real sleep mattered enormously.

Meanwhile, another American unit was moving farther east. The First Battalion of the 141st Infantry Regiment, part of the 36th Division, had advanced down a wooded ridge. German forces worked around its rear. The men could still defend their position, but they could no longer reach the rest of the American line.

They were only a few miles away from friendly forces. That distance was deceptive.

Other American battalions tried to break the surrounding positions and were driven back. The trapped troops were ordered to fight their own way out, but that failed too. They had wounded men, dwindling ammunition, and no secure route for food or water. Aircraft dropped supplies; some reached the pocket, but much landed elsewhere, including in territory held by German troops.

Now came the order to the 442nd: go into that forest and open a way to the Lost Battalion.

There was something startling about the men chosen to carry it out. At the same time they served in American uniform, many Americans of Japanese ancestry had been forcibly removed from their West Coast homes and held in government camps. The soldiers of the 100th and 442nd came from different backgrounds, including Hawaii and the mainland; it would be wrong to describe them all as former camp prisoners. But the contradiction between military service abroad and confinement at home was real.

The Second Battalion of the 442nd had already returned to the line on October 26 to protect a flank. The 100th and Third Battalions received orders to lead the direct drive to the surrounded unit the following day.

At four in the morning on October 27, those soldiers set out in darkness. According to a wartime regimental history, the men walked in column holding on to the packs ahead of them because they could not see one another. By afternoon three battalions were deployed across the sector, with different responsibilities on the flanks and in the middle.

The first day of the direct push ended short of the trapped men. German infantry counterattacked the Third Battalion, supported by a tank and a half-track. One tank drove close enough to threaten a company position. Private First Class Matsuichi Yogi advanced with a bazooka, struck the tank, and helped break the attack. When darkness returned, the Americans did not pursue. They could not keep their formations together safely. Yogi would be killed in action two days later.

On October 28, the 100th Battalion encountered another problem. German soldiers withdrew from a position. The Americans followed them toward a hollow between slopes. Then the hollow came under a heavy artillery and mortar barrage. The attack broke apart.

Farther across the ridge, Company K faced a defended roadblock and machine-gun positions. Staff Sergeant Gordon Yamashiro moved forward to identify and suppress the guns. His actions helped open a gap, but he was killed while covering his squad. After supporting artillery fire, the company could advance again.

The 100th and Third Battalions had gained only a limited distance. There were more hills to cross. The men in the Lost Battalion were still isolated.

By October 29, the situation inside the pocket had grown worse. Air deliveries had brought some food and ammunition, yet water and medical care remained desperately short. Wounded soldiers could not simply be carried down the mountain while German troops blocked every exit.

The rescuers now confronted a narrow ridge with a mined approach. Steep slopes left little room to move around the defenses. German machine-gun positions covered one another, so a move against one brought fire from another.

The 100th tried to work around a flank. For the Third Battalion, there was no easy route. Artillery fire and tanks offered support but did not remove the obstacles. Companies I and K were repeatedly pinned down.

Then the assault moved forward. The regimental account describes soldiers getting up in ones and twos, closing the distance from tree to tree as others followed. It does not claim that a single dramatic command started everything; the account says no one knew precisely what set off the last attack.

Private Barney Hajiro was among the men pushing against the machine-gun defenses. Staff Sergeant Fujio Miyamoto went forward after being wounded and attacked a gun position that had held his company back. Their actions helped break the German position. Many of the men around them were killed or wounded.

At the same time the Second Battalion fought for Hill 617 on the left. A visible attack from one direction drew attention, while other companies worked around to take the crest. The rescue was not just one line of infantry moving straight ahead. The flanks also had to remain secure, or the men pressing toward the pocket might be surrounded themselves.

By the evening of October 29, the leading battalions were reportedly within about seven hundred yards of the trapped soldiers. That sounds like an easy walk. Yet a fortified roadblock still occupied the route. Under fire, yards and minutes did not behave the way they do on an ordinary road.

On October 30, the American artillery fired on the remaining defenses. Company I, supported by Company K, fought past a mined roadblock. At about two in the afternoon a patrol from Company I led by Technical Sergeant Takashi Senzaki reached the outer positions of the Lost Battalion.

A few minutes later, men from Company B of the 100th Battalion made contact from the other side. Two approaches had finally met in the forest.

The surviving trapped soldiers could now be reached and brought back through the American line. Of the roughly 275 surrounded, 211 survived the ordeal and were rescued.

The regimental history describes a scene without much celebration. The soldiers had been fighting for days, and the relief force had taken terrible losses. Their job was not finished just because they had reached the men. The position still needed to be held.

Counting the cost is more complicated than repeating one famous figure. The 100th Battalion's historical organization estimates fifty-four killed in action and approximately three hundred wounded during the five-day rescue. Other accounts give much higher casualty totals. Some are describing the wider Vosges campaign or a different span of days; even when authors use the word rescue, the accounting periods are not always equivalent. The essential distinction is clear: casualties include wounded people, not just those who died.

The story also continued after October 30. The next day the 100th and Third Battalions received orders to move into and defend other positions. The regiment stayed in the mountains, fighting, holding lines, and losing more men before finally being relieved in November. The rescue had solved one urgent problem; it had not ended the campaign.

And what of the other struggle running through the story? The men of the 442nd had been defending American soldiers in France while their own families and communities faced suspicion and unequal treatment in the United States.

In July 1946, President Harry Truman greeted members of the unit and acknowledged that they had fought prejudice as well as an enemy. The government would not end segregation in the armed forces by executive order until 1948. A speech did not erase the wartime incarceration of Japanese Americans.

None of that changes the fact that the rescue succeeded. It changes what success meant for the people who carried it out.

A battalion disappeared behind German lines. Two hundred and eleven soldiers came back. Between those two facts lies a forest crossed by men who were exhausted before the mission began, and who kept moving through positions that had already stopped others.

That is the story behind the number on the rescue memorial: not only how many soldiers were saved, but who had to fight their way through to save them.
