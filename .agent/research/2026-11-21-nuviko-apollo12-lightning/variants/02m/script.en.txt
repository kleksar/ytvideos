Lightning struck Apollo 12 just thirty-six seconds after launch. The rocket kept rising, but inside the spacecraft warning lights came on across the panel.

It was November 1969. Three astronauts were heading for the second human landing on the Moon. Before they had even left the clouds, all three fuel cells disconnected from the spacecraft's electrical buses. Backup batteries carried the load.

Sixteen seconds later came another electrical shock. The spacecraft's guidance display was lost, and in Houston the data from Apollo 12 had turned into meaningless numbers.

Yet the giant Saturn V was still flying normally. It had its own guidance system. The trouble was in the spacecraft it was carrying.

Flight controller John Aaron recognized the strange numbers. He had seen a similar pattern during an earlier test. He called for the signal-conditioning equipment to be switched to its auxiliary power setting.

The message had to be repeated. Then astronaut Alan Bean found the little-used switch and moved it.

Houston could read the instruments again. Now the team could see which fuel cells had disconnected and guide the crew through reconnecting them. The rocket reached Earth orbit, where the astronauts checked their spacecraft before continuing.

Five days after launch, Apollo 12 landed near the unmanned Surveyor 3 probe on the Moon. Its three astronauts eventually returned safely to Earth.

The switch didn't restart the rocket; the rocket had never stopped. It restored the information needed to diagnose the electrical failure. Later, investigators concluded that the ascending rocket itself had triggered the lightning.

It began with an enormous rocket climbing through an electrically charged cloud.
