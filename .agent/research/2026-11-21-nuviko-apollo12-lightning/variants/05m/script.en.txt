How can a Moon rocket keep flying when its spacecraft suddenly loses electrical power? Why did the people in Mission Control see nonsense instead of readings? And why did a switch hardly anyone talked about become the most important switch of the launch?

On November 14, 1969, Apollo 12 was lifting off from Florida under low gray clouds. Commander Pete Conrad, Richard Gordon, and Alan Bean were heading for the Moon. Their job wasn't simply to land there again. They were supposed to land close enough to an unmanned probe, Surveyor 3, to visit it on foot.

The Saturn V climbed into the clouds. Thirty-six and a half seconds after liftoff, lightning discharged through the vehicle.

Inside the Apollo spacecraft, warning lights appeared all over the instrument panel. All three fuel cells that normally provided its electricity disconnected from the main circuits. The batteries meant to help power the capsule during its return to Earth took over the load.

The crew were still climbing at speed. Sixteen seconds after the first strike, another discharge disturbed their guidance instruments. The display that told them the spacecraft's orientation was no longer trustworthy.

In Houston, flight controllers had a problem of their own. The measurements coming down from the spacecraft were garbled. They couldn't use the numbers to understand what had failed.

There was one crucial distinction. The Saturn V rocket had its own guidance system, separate from the spacecraft's instruments. The booster was still steering normally. A problem that looked like a complete loss of control wasn't actually stopping the launch vehicle.

But there was no good reason to keep flying toward the Moon with an electrical system nobody could properly diagnose.

John Aaron was monitoring the spacecraft's electrical systems in Mission Control. He noticed something recognizable in the nonsense on his screen. During an earlier test, he'd seen much the same kind of data when the voltage supplying a device called the Signal Conditioning Equipment fell.

That equipment converted sensor readings into usable data for the ground. Its normal power supply wasn't coping with the low voltage. Aaron remembered a second setting that would power it differently.

He asked for SCE to AUX.

Even the instruction needed clarification. Mission Control repeated it to the spacecraft. Alan Bean, responsible for much of the crew's electrical monitoring, knew the switch and moved it.

At about ninety-eight seconds after liftoff, Houston's telemetry returned.

This didn't magically reconnect the fuel cells. It gave the controllers their eyes back. They could now see the disconnected power sources and tell the astronauts what to reset. The first two cells were back online roughly two minutes and twenty-four seconds after launch; the third followed shortly afterward.

And through all of it, the Saturn V kept flying.

Apollo 12 reached its planned parking orbit around Earth. There, the crew and the ground teams checked the systems before committing to the next engine burn, the one that would send them toward the Moon. They also had to restore their spacecraft's navigation reference.

The lightning had damaged some outside measuring sensors, but the checks found no reason to abandon the voyage. Apollo 12 set off for the Moon.

On November 19, Conrad and Bean landed about 160 meters from Surveyor 3. They walked to the probe and brought parts of it back for examination. Gordon had remained in lunar orbit. All three eventually came home.

The mission had passed two very different tests: surviving an electrical failure during ascent, and landing close to a chosen spot on another world.

Investigators then looked at the weather. The rocket hadn't simply been unlucky enough to pass through a naturally occurring bolt. Its passage through electrically charged clouds had helped trigger the discharges. NASA changed its launch-weather restrictions afterward.

So the overlooked switch wasn't the thing that kept a giant rocket in the air. A separate guidance system had already done that. The switch restored the information needed to fix the spacecraft riding on top.

And the entire chain of trouble began with a rocket climbing through a cloud that could produce lightning.
