Why did a rocket bound for the Moon lose its electrical readings less than a minute after launch? How did one controller recognize a fault when his screens showed nonsense? And what had to happen before the crew could leave Earth orbit?

Apollo 12 was supposed to demonstrate something more precise than the first Moon landing. In July 1969, Apollo 11 had reached the lunar surface. The next mission would try to land within walking distance of an unmanned probe called Surveyor 3. That machine had sat in the Ocean of Storms since 1967. Astronauts would be able to inspect equipment that had spent more than two years on another world.

On November 14, commander Pete Conrad, Richard Gordon, and Alan Bean were strapped into their Apollo spacecraft on top of a Saturn V in Florida. The weather was cloudy, with showers. Under the launch rules at that time, it did not require a cancellation.

The rocket lifted off at eleven twenty-two that morning. For the first half minute, the climb appeared normal. Then an electrical discharge passed through the vehicle.

Thirty-six and a half seconds after liftoff, warning lights appeared throughout the Apollo cockpit. All three of the spacecraft's fuel cells disconnected from the main electrical buses. These cells were supposed to generate most of the power needed by the command and service modules.

The spacecraft was not completely without electricity. Two batteries, intended primarily to power the capsule during its later return through Earth's atmosphere, were already connected and took over the load. But the astronauts couldn't complete a mission to the Moon on that backup supply alone.

At the same time, Houston lost useful measurements from the spacecraft. The numbers on the flight controllers' displays were scrambled. They couldn't see what had failed or decide which readings were still reliable.

At fifty-two seconds, another discharge disturbed the spacecraft's guidance reference. Pete Conrad and the others now faced a forest of warning lights and an attitude indicator that could no longer be trusted.

Imagine trying to diagnose an electrical failure while the machine you're trying to repair is accelerating away from you. The astronauts could report warnings over the radio, but the controllers on Earth weren't getting the measurements needed to identify their cause.

There was one crucial piece of good news, although it wasn't obvious from the troubled displays. The Saturn V rocket had its own guidance equipment. Trouble inside the Apollo spacecraft didn't mean the giant booster had forgotten where to fly.

The booster continued along its planned route. That separation gave the team time. It did not prove the spacecraft was safe for the rest of the journey.

At Mission Control, John Aaron monitored the spacecraft's electrical and related systems. His screen was wrong in a particular way. During an earlier test, he had seen a similar pattern when a drop in voltage made another system send back unusable data.

The device involved was called Signal Conditioning Equipment, or SCE. Sensors across the spacecraft produced many different electrical signals. The SCE converted them into a form that could be transmitted and read back on Earth. If it wasn't working, a healthy system might look broken, while a truly broken system could be impossible to identify.

The equipment's normal power supply wasn't coping with the lower voltage. But the SCE had an auxiliary setting. Aaron wanted the crew to select that instead.

His call was short: SCE to AUX.

That instruction did not instantly solve the problem. It went through Mission Control and had to be repeated over the radio. Conrad was not immediately sure which switch was meant. Alan Bean, who handled much of the spacecraft's electrical monitoring, recognized it on his panel.

He moved the switch.

At roughly one minute and thirty-eight seconds after launch, Houston's telemetry became useful again. This change restored signal processing, not electrical generation. That difference mattered. Aaron could now see the fuel cells were disconnected, so the ground team could recommend specific actions instead of guessing.

The crew reconnected the first two fuel cells about two minutes and twenty-four seconds after launch. The third came back at about two minutes and fifty-one seconds. The main electrical buses returned to normal voltage.

And throughout all of this, the Saturn V kept flying.

Its stages separated on schedule. Apollo 12 reached a temporary parking orbit around Earth less than twelve minutes after liftoff.

Reaching orbit, however, wasn't the same as being cleared to fly to the Moon. The Saturn V had brought the crew this far on its own guidance. Now the astronauts had to trust the navigation and electrical systems inside Apollo itself.

The second discharge had disrupted the spacecraft's primary navigation platform. During ascent the crew had shut down the unstable instrument. A separate backup attitude system had continued to provide information, but the primary system still needed to be restored before the next maneuvers.

In Earth orbit, the astronauts used sightings of stars and updated information from the ground to realign the navigation platform. A pointer moving on a panel wasn't proof that its direction was right. They needed a reference outside the spacecraft before they could trust it.

Other evidence of the strike remained. Nine specialized measuring sensors on the service module had stopped working. Some monitored temperatures on the spacecraft's exterior. Those were real losses, but different from losing the fuel cells that generated electricity or the instruments needed for navigation. A dead reading and a dead system could look equally alarming until the team established which was which.

For a few minutes after launch, the challenge had been to get usable data. Now the controllers had to decide what the recovered data said about the spacecraft's condition. They and the crew checked its electrical equipment and other systems before committing to the next maneuver.

Only after those checks did Mission Control clear the flight to continue. The Saturn V's third stage fired for a second time. Instead of circling Earth, Apollo 12 was now heading for the Moon.

That engine burn didn't end the inspection. The lunar module was still attached to the rocket stage. Richard Gordon separated the command and service module, turned it around, guided it back toward the lander, docked, and pulled it free. During the coast toward the Moon, the crew also inspected the lunar module to check for damage from the lightning strikes.

The little switch Bean moved during launch had restored the ground's measurements of the command and service module. It had not established that every piece of a two-spacecraft expedition was unharmed. Checking the lander was another necessary step.

Five days after launch, the original destination came into view. Gordon stayed in lunar orbit. Conrad and Bean descended in the lunar module toward the Ocean of Storms. They were not trying merely to land somewhere safe. Their target was Surveyor 3, an unmanned probe that had stood there since 1967.

They used recognizable craters during their approach, and Conrad made the final corrections. On November 19, their lander touched down roughly 160 meters from the probe. The astronauts could walk to it, examine it, and remove parts to bring home for study. After the lightning, the repairs and the repeated checks, the mission had still achieved the precision landing it was meant to demonstrate.

The two men returned to Gordon in lunar orbit. All three arrived home on November 24. The lightning had not prevented them from completing the flight. But getting home didn't establish that the original launch conditions were safe.

The first minute of their flight was not forgotten. Investigators examined how lightning had reached a rocket ascending through clouds. Their conclusion was stranger than a simple story of bad luck in a storm.

The moving launch vehicle and its long exhaust plume had helped trigger the electrical discharges in the charged atmosphere. The rocket hadn't merely flown beneath an existing bolt. Its own presence had helped create the discharge.

NASA tightened its launch-weather restrictions afterward, paying closer attention to hazardous electric fields.

On launch day, the clouds had met the weather rules in force at the time. After the investigation, those rules became stricter. The crew had recovered the systems they could diagnose. NASA also had to change its understanding of what conditions could create the discharge.

The rocket's separate guidance had allowed Apollo 12 to keep climbing. But surviving lightning the vehicle itself had helped trigger was no reason to send another crew through the same kind of charged cloud.
