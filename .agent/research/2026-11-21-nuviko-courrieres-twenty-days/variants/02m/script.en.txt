Thirteen miners came out of a French coal mine twenty days after an explosion that had killed more than a thousand people.

On March 10, 1906, a coal-dust explosion tore through connected mine workings near Lens. Families gathered at the shafts. Some workers escaped early. Many never came back.

Deep underground, miner César Danglot heard a distant blast. At first, he thought rock had fallen somewhere else. Then another worker arrived with news that the air had become so bad his horse could barely move.

Danglot and others tried to reach a shaft. They found dead colleagues along their route. Gas stopped them from going farther. When a companion collapsed, the men waved their coats to move breathable air toward him.

They followed pipes through the dark, hoping the route would lead them out. Parts of the pipes were broken. One passage was blocked by fallen earth. They tried clearing it with their hands.

Above ground, rescue teams struggled with fires, rubble and dangerous gases. German miners arrived with breathing equipment. As time passed, the chances of finding anyone alive seemed to shrink, and the suspension of searches angered families.

But on March 30, thirteen men reached the surface at shaft two. Among them were a father and his teenage son. Their return made the abandoned hope painfully real.

On April 4, another survivor, Auguste Berthon, came out alive.

The explosion had taken 1,099 lives. Those who returned could not undo that loss. They did show how wrong it was to assume the darkness below had already told its whole story.

Because after twenty days, one question still had an answer: who was left alive underground?
