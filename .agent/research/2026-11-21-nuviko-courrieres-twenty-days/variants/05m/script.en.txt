How could thirteen miners survive for twenty days beneath a devastated coalfield? Why did their return cause anger as well as relief? And what happened when another man appeared even later?

In northern France, coal came from a network of connected underground workings. In March 1906, the Courrières mining company operated several shafts near the town of Lens. Men and boys worked below, while families above depended on the wages they brought home.

Shortly after half past six on the morning of March 10, an explosion of coal dust swept through three connected mine areas. The blast devastated the workings. Some miners escaped in the first hours. Others died underground. The eventual official death toll was 1,099.

At first, a miner named César Danglot did not even know what had happened.

Danglot later described hearing a distant detonation while working underground. He thought it might be a routine fall of rock. His crew went back to their work. Then a horse driver came from another section with a warning: the air there had become so foul that his horse could hardly move.

The men headed for a route toward the surface. Two bodies lay along the way. They changed direction, looking for another passage.

A route leading toward a shaft seemed possible until the air grew too dangerous. In Danglot's account, a companion struggled to keep walking. Other miners waved their coats to move air toward him. They had to stop and recover in darkness.

They tried a different clue: compressed-air pipes running along the passage. Following the pipes might lead them toward equipment, and perhaps a way out. But the explosion had broken sections of the system.

Then fallen earth blocked the gallery. They began clearing it by hand. Danglot described an obstacle stretching roughly sixty or seventy metres. Clearing it was exhausting work, and it did not open a simple route home.

Above them, rescue crews were facing their own obstacles. Rubble had closed passages. Fire and poisonous gases made other routes impassable. Men volunteered to enter the mine. Specialist rescuers from Germany arrived with breathing equipment two days after the explosion. They helped the wider rescue effort, but it would be misleading to credit them with personally finding the thirteen men who emerged later.

Days went by. Funerals began. There were families waiting for news that did not come. A suspension of searches was received by many miners as abandonment. Anger grew alongside grief, and strikes spread through the coalfield.

By March 30, nobody could honestly promise that any of the missing were still alive.

Then thirteen men appeared at shaft two in Billy-Montigny. They had spent twenty days below ground. Among them were Charles Pruvost and his teenage son Anselme. César Danglot was also in the group.

Their survival was real; the neat heroic version came later. Accounts of their ordeal describe makeshift food, water found underground, and repeated efforts to navigate blocked passages. The precise experience was not identical for every survivor. Even Danglot's later first-person account is a recollection, not a recording from the mine.

The return of the thirteen was an answer to one question, and the beginning of another. If men were alive after twenty days, had the search ended too soon?

Four days later, on April 4, Auguste Berthon came out alive as well. He had been underground even longer.

That made fourteen late survivors, not fourteen thousand saved. The catastrophe still killed 1,099 people. No story of endurance should make that number disappear.

But what happened at shaft two changed the meaning of the days before it. The families who had protested the end of the search were not simply refusing to accept a loss. The mine had held living men long after many above believed the chance had passed.

And after twenty days of silence, the next news from underground was not a list of names. It was thirteen men coming home.
