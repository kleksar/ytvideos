One sailor was pulled alive from an abandoned yacht. The men who had climbed into a life raft hours earlier thought he had died. So why had they left him there?

The answer begins in August 1979, with a race across the Celtic Sea. The Fastnet fleet included 303 yachts. One of the smaller boats was Grimalkin, with six experienced sailors aboard. Its skipper was David Sheahan. His seventeen-year-old son Matthew was part of the crew, along with Nick Ward, Gerry Winks, Mike Doyle and Dave Wheeler.

At first this was a yacht race. Then the weather changed. Waves struck from behind, rolled the boat onto its side and, more than once, overturned it completely. Matthew later described being dragged underwater by his safety harness. The same line that kept him attached to the yacht could also trap him beneath it.

By dawn, Nick and Gerry were in serious trouble. Cold, exhausted and battered, they were struggling to stay conscious. Around six in the morning, David and Matthew went below to send a distress call. A nearby yacht helped relay the message. While David was at the chart table, another wave rolled Grimalkin. Unsecured stores shot through the cabin. A tin of food struck David in the head. His son and the others brought him onto deck, badly injured.

The radio aerial broke. Their flares were difficult to use in the water and wind. A patrol aircraft passed overhead, but the crew still faced the storm. Then another wave turned the boat over. Matthew was trapped below the hull with his harness line holding him underwater. He struggled free of his life jacket and surfaced. Dave Wheeler emerged nearby. The yacht righted itself, suddenly throwing Matthew back on board.

Nick and Gerry were lying motionless in the flooded cockpit. Matthew could see his father's body in the sea. Two other men had launched the life raft. They asked whether Matthew was coming. He did.

That decision became the most disputed moment of the whole story. Matthew says the two men in the cockpit appeared dead, were tangled among ropes and were beyond the crew's strength to move. Mike believed the damaged yacht was going down. Nick later wrote about waking up on board and realising he had been left behind. These are different accounts of a few minutes in which no one had a complete view.

About two hours after leaving Grimalkin, Matthew and the other two were lifted from their raft by a Royal Navy helicopter. Matthew immediately identified their boat and said there were still people aboard. The crew could not go back at once because of fuel limits. They promised to pass the position to the rescue operation.

Another helicopter eventually found the dismasted yacht. Gerry had died. Nick was alive. He had endured hours aboard a wreck that was still floating. The vessel itself was later found and towed into an Irish port.

There was a second reversal after the rescue. The question was no longer simply whether a life raft could save three men. It was whether leaving the yacht had been necessary. Matthew defended the choice and rejected accounts suggesting the three had knowingly abandoned living crewmates. Nick's own experience made the decision look very different. An inquest found Gerry had drowned and, according to Matthew's account of its findings, was already dead when the raft left. That does not erase Nick's survival or the disagreement.

Fifteen Fastnet competitors died during the storm. Lifeboat crews and military helicopters carried out an enormous rescue operation. Procedures for offshore sailing would change, but no later rule could tell those six men what would happen next on that Tuesday morning.

The yacht survived. Nick survived. Matthew's father and Gerry did not. And the three men in the raft were left with a question that was much harder than knowing which vessel stayed afloat: what could anyone reasonably have known before the rescue?
