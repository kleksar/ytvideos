A rescue helicopter approached a damaged yacht in the Celtic Sea and found someone nobody expected to be alive. Nick Ward had been left behind by crewmates who believed he was dead. The yacht had been abandoned hours earlier. Yet here he was, still aboard. Why had the other sailors gone, and how had the boat survived when they thought it was sinking?

To understand their decision, go back to the start of the Fastnet Race in August 1979. Three hundred and three yachts set off on a demanding offshore course. The route took crews across the English Channel, out into the Celtic Sea, around Fastnet Rock near Ireland and back toward Plymouth. This was a serious competition, but not an expedition expecting a disaster. Many of the crews knew one another and had spent the season preparing their boats. The smallest craft were especially exposed when the weather changed.

One of those yachts was Grimalkin, a thirty-foot racing boat. There were six men aboard: owner and skipper David Sheahan, his seventeen-year-old son Matthew, Nick Ward, Gerry Winks, Mike Doyle and Dave Wheeler. Matthew later wrote a detailed account of the storm. Nick wrote about it too. Their stories overlap on the central events but not on every judgement about what happened, and that difference matters.

The weather deteriorated rapidly on the night of August thirteenth. The sea did not simply rise and fall beneath the hull. Breaking waves knocked boats sideways, swept across decks and rolled some vessels right over. On Grimalkin, one violent wave followed another. At one point a breaking sea pushed the stern high and drove the bow into water ahead. The boat turned end over end. Matthew was dragged underwater by his safety harness. He reached the surface, fought his way back on board and found that this was not the final knockdown.

Racers relied on harness lines to stay connected to their boats in heavy weather. That safety measure had a terrifying second side. A line could hold a sailor beneath an overturned hull, where he had little room to breathe or move. On a small yacht, the distinction between being secured to the boat and trapped by it could disappear within seconds. Matthew would discover this again later, at the worst possible moment.

For hours the crew kept going in the flooded boat. Nick and Gerry were losing strength. Gerry, in particular, was suffering from the cold and finding it hard to remain conscious. The men debated whether to abandon Grimalkin and whether a distress call was justified. They did not agree immediately. David had prepared carefully for the race, even rehearsing the procedure for calling Mayday. But leaving the vessel for a small inflatable raft had not been a central part of their preparation.

Around six in the morning, David and Matthew went below to send the distress message. A yacht called Morningtown helped relay it toward the coast radio service. That gave them a route to help, but it also put father and son inside a cabin where the usual idea of shelter no longer applied. The boat was being thrown about so violently that things once kept safely on bunks and shelves were flying around the interior.

Another breaking wave hit. Grimalkin rolled through a complete circle. When she righted, David was slumped over the chart table. Matthew's later account says a loose tin of food had struck his father's head. The blow left a deep injury. The crew hauled David onto deck and tried to treat him. Matthew held his father's head while the storm continued around them. It was the last time he remembered getting a clear response from him.

The latest roll had broken the radio aerial. Some distress flares had been washed away, and the remainder were difficult to fire correctly as waves swept over the deck. Matthew remembered seeing a Nimrod patrol aircraft pass overhead and drop green flares. Those signs showed that people were looking, but they did not yet put a rescuer beside the boat. Grimalkin was disabled, her crew exhausted and her skipper badly hurt.

Then came another strike. Matthew was thrown into the sea and found himself under the upturned yacht. His harness line passed beneath a guardrail, leaving too little length to lift his head clear of the water. He later described trying to free himself while taking occasional breaths as the hull moved. The thing intended to stop him being swept away was now preventing him from surfacing.

Matthew managed to remove his inflated life jacket and work at the harness beneath it. Eventually he got his head above water. Dave Wheeler surfaced nearby. Then the hull began to move. Grimalkin righted with such force that Matthew was pulled back into the cockpit. He landed among the other sailors.

Nick and Gerry were not moving. Matthew recalled Nick's blue face and Gerry's injury. He also saw his father's body in the sea. The distinction between someone who had drowned, someone who was unconscious and someone who might still recover was almost impossible for the shaken survivors to establish in those minutes. That uncertainty does not make the outcome less devastating. It explains why the later arguments could never be settled merely by showing what was discovered afterward.

Mike and Dave had launched the life raft. They asked Matthew whether he was coming. According to Matthew's account, the two motionless men were caught in ropes, and the survivors could not lift them. Mike believed the battered yacht was about to go down. Matthew joined the raft. Three men escaped the boat; Nick and Gerry remained on her. David was lost at sea.

The raft did not offer instant safety. It was tossed and overturned in the remaining waves. Matthew later described the experience as terrifying in its own right. The choice the men had made did not reveal itself as a good decision the moment they left the yacht. They had exchanged one uncontrolled danger for another, and from the raft they still could not know what had happened to their friends.

At about half past ten that morning, a Royal Navy Sea King helicopter lifted Matthew, Mike and Dave from the raft. Matthew told the crew the name Grimalkin and insisted that they return for those still aboard. The helicopter had already been flying in a rescue operation larger than its crew had expected. It lacked the fuel for an immediate second search and headed toward shore, promising to pass on the position.

The task facing the rescuers was enormous. Boats were scattered across a violent sea. There were sailors in the water, overturned life rafts and vessels with damaged rigs. Even locating a low hull amid breaking waves was difficult. According to the Royal National Lifeboat Institution's contemporary account, its lifeboats spent more than 186 hours at sea during Fastnet rescue and attempted rescue operations. RNLI crews directly saved sixty lives; military and other rescue helicopters also carried dozens of people to safety. These counts describe different services and should not simply be added together.

On Grimalkin, Nick Ward regained consciousness. His later account describes the horror of realising that the other three surviving men had gone, and of remaining with Gerry Winks in a broken yacht. Gerry did not survive. Nick did. He waited through hours of exposure until a helicopter eventually reached the vessel that evening. A rescue crewman had to work among the lines and wreckage to recover those aboard. That detail matters because moving two men out of the same tangle had been beyond the strength of the crew earlier.

The news created two stories of survival. In one, a seventeen-year-old had escaped an upside-down yacht, lost his father, got into a life raft and then spent hours telling rescuers to return. In the other, a man left for dead had awakened on a ruined boat and endured until the helicopter arrived. Nick's book, and Matthew's later response, made the disagreement public. Neither man's ordeal disappears because the other lived.

There was also a bitter twist. Grimalkin herself did not sink. The yacht was eventually located afloat and towed into Waterford in Ireland. People could now look at the boat and ask why the crew had left her. But that view from dry land was available only after the storm. It did not restore the radio, revive the injured skipper or tell the men how long the hull would remain above water.

Matthew rejected later accounts suggesting the three men had calmly chosen to abandon living crewmates. He argued that in those minutes Nick and Gerry appeared dead and could not be moved. He also stressed the urgency with which he requested another rescue. Nick had his own direct experience of waking alone, with Gerry still aboard, and his recollection of that day was understandably different. The dispute should not be turned into an invented confession or a simple verdict from the narrator.

At an inquest after the disaster, evidence addressed Gerry's death and the efforts to remove him. Matthew later wrote that the coroner's view was that Gerry had already died by the time the raft was launched. Nick's account remains a separate testimony, and the timing of those final moments is not something a dramatic reenactment can prove. What is certain is the outcome: David Sheahan and Gerry Winks died; Matthew, Mike, Dave and Nick survived.

Across the Fastnet fleet, fifteen competitors lost their lives. Rescue crews worked through conditions that made ordinary procedures hard to follow. The disaster prompted changes in offshore racing safety, including better attention to equipment and communication. Those lessons are important, but they are not a substitute for the human story. A regulation written later cannot give the men in Grimalkin's cockpit knowledge they did not have that morning.

The final contrast is almost unbearable. A life raft carried three men away from a boat that remained afloat. On that boat, a fourth survivor waited for a helicopter. Two men died. The choice has been argued about for decades, partly because survival tells us what happened, not what people could have known before it happened. That is the real question left by Grimalkin: how do you judge a decision when the only evidence that it could have ended differently arrives hours too late?
