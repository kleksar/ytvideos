A cargo ship hits a space station. Air starts escaping. And three people have to decide which part of their home they can afford to lose.

On June 25, 1997, the Russian space station Mir was home to two cosmonauts and American astronaut Michael Foale. Its unmanned supply ship, Progress, was making a practice approach under remote control.

The commander could see through a camera on the cargo ship. But the television picture made it hard to judge how quickly the distance was closing.

Progress struck a solar panel, then the Spektr module. The crew heard air escaping. A pressure reading was dropping. If the leak reached the rest of the station, all three might have to abandon Mir.

They knew which compartment was damaged. But the hatch was blocked by cables carrying electricity from Spektr's solar panels.

Foale and Aleksandr Lazutkin hurried to disconnect and clear those cables so they could close the hatch. Spektr emptied into space. The rest of Mir kept its air.

Then came the cost. Cutting the electrical connections deprived the station of a huge share of its power. The impact had also sent Mir spinning, leaving the remaining solar panels badly positioned for sunlight.

With normal instruments unavailable, Foale watched the stars move past a window and helped estimate the spin. The crew and controllers used that information to regain some control.

Weeks later, new crew members worked inside the depressurized module in spacesuits to reconnect some of the power lines. Spektr stayed sealed off.

No one on Mir died in the collision. They saved the station, but not every room in it.

And that was the terrible choice waiting inside a station with a hole in its wall.
