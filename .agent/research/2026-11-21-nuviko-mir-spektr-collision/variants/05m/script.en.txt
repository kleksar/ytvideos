How can you close a hole in a space station when electrical cables run through the very hatch you need to shut? And what happens when saving the air means cutting off much of the station's power?

In June 1997, three people were living aboard Mir: Russian commander Vasily Tsibliyev, flight engineer Aleksandr Lazutkin, and NASA astronaut Michael Foale. The station was assembled from compartments joined in orbit. One of them, Spektr, contained much of Foale's work and his sleeping quarters. Its solar arrays also supplied a large share of Mir's electricity.

The crisis began not with an approaching asteroid, but with a routine-looking test using a spacecraft that had already delivered supplies.

The unmanned Progress M-34 had been sent away from Mir and was coming back for a practice docking. Instead of relying entirely on automatic guidance, the commander was controlling it remotely while watching a television image transmitted by a camera on the cargo ship.

He could see the station. What he could not judge reliably from that screen was the closing speed. As Progress approached, it became apparent that it was moving too fast. Braking rockets were fired, but they could not prevent the impact.

Progress hit one of Spektr's solar arrays and then struck the module itself. A piece of Mir's outer wall had been breached.

There was no dramatic explosion inside. The crew heard air hissing away, and the pressure began to fall. On Earth, a damaged room might mean a repair call. In orbit, it meant their entire supply of breathable air was in danger.

The emergency ship, Soyuz, was still attached to Mir. Abandoning the station was possible. But first the crew tried to stop the leak.

Foale and Lazutkin went to the passage leading to Spektr. The obstruction was painfully ordinary: bundles of electrical cables ran straight through the doorway. Spektr's solar panels were feeding power to the rest of Mir, and those wires prevented the hatch cover from closing.

There was no time to preserve the whole arrangement. The men disconnected and cleared the cables. Then they shut the hatch. Pressure in the remaining rooms stabilized. Spektr, on the other side, lost its atmosphere.

That single action saved the crew's air. It also created the next emergency.

With Spektr isolated, the station could no longer use much of the electricity generated by that module's solar panels. The collision had also put Mir into a slow spin. Its remaining panels were no longer positioned consistently toward the Sun. Equipment began shutting down.

Imagine saving a house from flooding by sealing off a room, only to discover that the room contained much of the electrical supply. Now add the fact that the whole house is turning in space.

Foale found an unexpectedly simple way to help. With normal instruments unavailable, he watched stars drift past a window and estimated how quickly the station was rotating. He passed information to the crew and mission control. Using spacecraft thrusters, they began to bring Mir's motion back under control. It was not an instant or perfect repair. But they could keep working.

The damage did not disappear when the immediate danger passed. The crew lived with reduced power and lost access to Spektr. Foale's belongings and some experiments remained behind the closed hatch. Later that summer, replacement cosmonauts entered the depressurized module in spacesuits to reconnect electrical lines through a modified hatch arrangement. They recovered part of the lost power without reopening Spektr to the station's air.

In September, Foale and a Russian crewmate inspected the damaged exterior during a spacewalk. The station remained inhabited. Foale returned to Earth in October.

This was not a story about patching a puncture with a clever gadget. The hole was never simply repaired and the compartment was never restored as a normal room. The crew survived because they recognized which part of the station had to be sacrificed first. They closed a door before they could fix the lights.

A collision that could have forced an evacuation instead became a long recovery. The station survived; Spektr remained sealed away.
