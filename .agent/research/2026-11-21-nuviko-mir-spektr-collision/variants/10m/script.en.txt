How do you stop the air escaping from an orbiting space station when the damaged room cannot be sealed without disconnecting its electrical supply? How do you steady that station after a collision starts it spinning? And what does a crew do when the emergency is over but their home is still broken?

The answer begins on June 25, 1997. The Russian station Mir was flying around Earth with three people aboard: commander Vasily Tsibliyev, flight engineer Aleksandr Lazutkin and American astronaut Michael Foale.

Mir did not resemble one enormous spacecraft built in a factory. Its rooms had arrived separately, launched over a period of years, then joined together in orbit. One was called Spektr. It housed scientific equipment, gave Foale a place to sleep, and carried solar panels that produced a major part of the station's power.

That arrangement had a hidden weakness. Electricity from Spektr reached the other rooms through cables running across the passage between them. The cables were useful every ordinary day. They would be in exactly the wrong place on the day the hatch had to close.

The flight that led to the accident was a test. An unmanned Progress cargo ship had already brought supplies to Mir. Engineers wanted to try a manual docking system, with the commander aboard the station piloting the freighter from a distance. A remote camera sent him a picture from the approaching ship.

The idea had a practical attraction. Automated docking equipment was expensive. But the camera picture came without something a pilot desperately needs during an approach: a reliable sense of how fast the target is getting closer.

Through the screen, Tsibliyev could make out Mir against the Earth below it. Clouds made the picture harder to interpret. As the freighter came toward the station, the changing image did not clearly communicate its speed. By the time it became obvious that Progress was moving too fast, there was little distance left in which to slow it.

The braking rockets fired. They were too late.

The cargo ship struck a solar array on Spektr. Then it hit the module itself. The impact damaged an array and a radiator and breached the hull. Suddenly, the station's problem was not docking. It was keeping breathable air inside.

There were alarms. Air could be heard escaping. The crew felt the pressure change. A falling gauge gave them a warning more useful than any dramatic view through a window: a compartment was leaking into space.

In an ordinary building, closing a door prevents smoke or water from reaching the next room. On Mir it could preserve the atmosphere needed for everyone to stay conscious. But the station was designed to function with the compartments connected, not with a damaged one abandoned.

The men had an escape route. A Soyuz capsule was docked to Mir, ready to carry a crew back to Earth if they had to leave. They did not immediately take it. They tried to save the station while there was still enough time.

Foale and Lazutkin approached the passage to Spektr. The hatch cover could not be put into place because power and other cables occupied the opening. Those cables were not a minor inconvenience. They were bringing electricity from the module's panels to the rest of the orbital complex.

Keeping that electricity flowing meant leaving the opening unsealed. Leaving it unsealed meant the air would keep escaping. The crew could not have both things.

The men worked through the connections, disconnecting and clearing wires. One of the cables presented particular difficulty. They had to free a cover that was normally secured out of the way. This was not a tidy repair on a workbench. It was an emergency rearrangement of the station's interior while its pressure was falling.

They finally closed the hatch. Pressure in the inhabited compartments stabilized. Spektr was lost to the vacuum outside. Foale's bedroom, his personal possessions and a large part of his research equipment were left behind.

If the story ended there, it would be a straightforward rescue. But the station had just traded one critical failure for another.

When the hatch was closed, electrical lines from Spektr's surviving solar panels were disconnected too. Mir now had much less generating capacity. The collision had knocked the whole complex into a spin, and the rotating station could not keep its other solar arrays reliably pointed at the Sun.

The problem was more than darkness. Batteries, control equipment and life-support hardware all depended on energy. Without the usual power and attitude control, recovering that power became harder. The station was still habitable, but it was not working normally.

There is a difference between a ship rolling on the sea and a spacecraft turning in orbit. On Earth, you can point to the horizon. In orbit, the view outside keeps changing even when the station is behaving perfectly. A useful measurement must separate the motion of the station from the apparent motion of the sky.

Foale found a simple reference. Looking through a window, he watched the stars move past a point marked by his thumb and estimated the station's rotation. NASA's account describes him combining that observation with a small model of Mir and a flashlight to think through how sunlight and the station's attitude were related.

The estimate was relayed to the ground. Controllers and the crew used available spacecraft thrusters to begin arresting the spin and recovering a useful orientation. They did not magically restore the original station. But they made it possible to manage the remaining electrical supply and plan the next steps.

The days after the collision were less cinematic and more exhausting. Equipment remained off in some modules. The crew shifted batteries and tried to keep essential systems running. Foale later described spending a remarkable amount of time dealing with condensed water in dark sections of Mir. The collision had changed ordinary laboratory work into a struggle to keep the home functioning.

There was also a scientific loss. Some of Foale's experiments were inside the sealed Spektr module. Not everything he had come to do in orbit could be finished. Yet he was able to continue other work in the accessible parts of Mir, including experiments involving plants and observations of Earth.

The station's recovery would require a kind of repair that sounds backward: people would have to enter a room with no air, while keeping the rest of their home sealed.

In August, two newly arrived Russian cosmonauts, Anatoly Solovyev and Pavel Vinogradov, put on spacesuits and worked inside the depressurized Spektr. A modified hatch allowed electrical cables to pass through while maintaining the pressure boundary. Reconnecting the surviving solar panels restored some of the lost power without making Spektr a habitable room again.

There was a personal moment in the repair as well. A photograph of Foale's children, left behind in his former living quarters, was recovered during the internal spacewalk. It was a small object, but its return made the scale of the loss understandable. They had saved a working station by sealing away the room someone called his bedroom.

In September, Foale and Solovyev went outside Mir to inspect the collision damage. Close observation could help identify the likely area of the breach, but it did not turn the module back into a normal part of the station.

In October, Foale could return to Earth. But the electrical repair had another stage. On October 20, Solovyev and Vinogradov entered the airless Spektr module again. The surviving panels could supply power, but they still needed working controls to track the Sun. During a six-hour spacewalk inside the module, the men connected two of the three control cables to the modified hatch. They could not connect the third. The repair brought back more control, not the missing room.

Mir continued operating and remained in orbit until its planned deorbit in 2001. A photograph of Foale's children had made it back out of Spektr; his sleeping quarters had not. The crew had saved the station's air by sealing that room, and spent months finding ways to recover electricity without opening it again.
