Two minutes after Mount Pelée erupted, a burning cloud struck Saint-Pierre. Almost everyone in the city died. Yet a man with no way to flee survived behind the walls of a prison cell.

It was May eighth, nineteen-oh-two, on the Caribbean island of Martinique. For days, the volcano had thrown ash over nearby towns. Saint-Pierre stood below its slopes, beside the sea.

Three days earlier, a rush of volcanic mud had destroyed a sugar mill. Twenty-three people died. But the next eruption brought a different danger.

Hot gas, ash, and rock fragments raced downhill along the ground. Streets and ships in the harbor burned. The United States Geological Survey estimates about twenty-six thousand deaths within Saint-Pierre.

Louis-Auguste Cyparis was locked in a small stone cell, partly below ground. Hot air and ash entered through its narrow opening and burned him. But the thick walls kept much of the deadly surge from reaching him.

Rescuers did not find him until May eleventh.

Later, Barnum and Bailey circus advertising presented Cyparis as the eruption's only survivor. That wasn't true. Léon Compère-Léandre lived near the edge of town, and others survived at the margins of the cloud.

The cell had not made Cyparis invulnerable. It left him badly burned. It simply put stone between him and a disaster almost nobody in the city could outrun.

And while the mountain grew more dangerous, some families from nearby villages had actually moved toward Saint-Pierre. They believed the larger city offered refuge.
