A prison door closed in Saint-Pierre. By the next morning, the city outside it would be destroyed.

On the Caribbean island of Martinique, Mount Pelée had been throwing ash over nearby towns for days. Some residents moved toward Saint-Pierre, believing the larger city was safer.

Then, on May fifth, a volcanic mudflow destroyed a sugar mill. Twenty-three people died. Three days later, the danger changed completely.

On May eighth, nineteen-oh-two, a scorching cloud of ash and gas raced down the mountain. It reached Saint-Pierre in minutes. Roughly twenty-six thousand people in the city died.

One of the people who lived was a prisoner, known as Louis-Auguste Cyparis or Ludger Sylbaris. He was held in a small stone cell, partly below ground, with very little opening to the outside.

The city above him burned. Even inside, hot ash and air burned his skin. But the thick walls shielded him from most of the blast.

Rescuers found him alive in the ruins several days later.

He was not the only survivor. Another man survived near the edge of town, and others escaped at the margins of the burning cloud. The circus posters that later called Cyparis the sole survivor were selling a simpler story.

He joined Barnum and Bailey and told audiences about the eruption. The cell that had confined him had also sheltered him.

And the strangest part was not that a prisoner had escaped his cell. He had survived because he could not leave it.
