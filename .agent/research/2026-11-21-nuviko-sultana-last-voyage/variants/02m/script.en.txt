A steamboat came back to rescue people in the Mississippi. Then its crew stopped the paddle wheels. They had seen something the swimmers could not escape.

April 1865. The American Civil War was ending. Hundreds of Union soldiers, recently released from prison camps, were finally traveling north. Their boat was called Sultana.

She was licensed for 376 passengers, but more than two thousand people crowded aboard. Some could barely find a place to lie down.

Before dawn on April 27, a boiler exploded. The middle of the boat was ripped apart. Burning fuel set the wreckage alight, and people began jumping into the river.

Sergeant James Kimberlin was among them. He later described a terrible problem: men who jumped immediately landed among others already struggling in the water. The spring river was swollen, cold, and dark.

Kimberlin waited until the fire forced him to leave. He took off his heavy shoes and clothing, tied pieces of wood together, and used them to stay afloat.

A passing steamboat, Bostona, turned back. But charging straight into the swimmers could have made things worse. Its enormous paddle wheels might have struck the people it was trying to save.

So the crew stopped the wheels, let the boat drift, and lowered smaller boats. Those boats went out repeatedly, carrying survivors back.

Kimberlin lived. More than a thousand other people did not. Even the exact death toll is disputed.

The Sultana was supposed to bring people home after a war. Instead, the last part of the journey became a struggle to reach shore.

And there, among swimmers who needed rescuing, was the danger the approaching captain had spotted: a steamboat's turning paddle wheels.
