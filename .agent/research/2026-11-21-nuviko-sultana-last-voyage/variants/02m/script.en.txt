A steamboat turned back toward people struggling in the Mississippi. Then its crew stopped the paddle wheels. Those wheels could have killed the people the boat had come to save.

It was April 1865. The American Civil War was ending. The Sultana was carrying released Union prisoners north, toward home. She was licensed for 376 passengers. More than two thousand people crowded aboard.

Ann Annis was traveling with her husband, Harvey, and their seven-year-old daughter, Belle. Before dawn on April 27, a boiler exploded. Fire spread through the wooden boat.

Harvey opened their cabin door and saw steam. The door leading outside was jammed. He took Belle toward the stern and lowered himself with her to another deck using a rope.

Ann tried to follow. Someone jumping from above struck her and knocked her into the hold. By the time she was helped out, Harvey and Belle had jumped into the river.

Ann followed. Her life preserver was loose. She held on to the rudder until the heat forced her away, then found a small board to keep her afloat.

Elsewhere, Sergeant James Kimberlin took off his heavy shoes, tied pieces of timber together, and waited until the flames forced him into the water. He later described how people jumping from the wreck landed among other swimmers.

The rescue steamer Bostona had passed the Sultana earlier. Now its crew let the larger vessel drift, lowered small boats, and sent them back and forth to collect survivors.

Kimberlin survived. Ann was rescued downstream. Harvey and Belle never returned; their bodies were not recovered. More than a thousand people died, although the exact toll is disputed.

For those still carried along by the current, rescue depended on the next vessel's crew making a choice before coming close.
