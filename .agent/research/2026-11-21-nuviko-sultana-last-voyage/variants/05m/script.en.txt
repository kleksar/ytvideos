How could a woman survive an explosion on a riverboat and still lose the two people traveling with her? And why did the crew of a rescue steamer stop its paddle wheels while people were drowning?

In April 1865, the American Civil War was coming to an end. Men who had survived Confederate prison camps were being sent north, toward their families. The government paid riverboat operators to carry them.

One of those vessels was the Sultana. It was licensed for 376 passengers. More than two thousand people crowded aboard on this voyage, many of them recently released soldiers. There were civilian passengers too.

Ann Annis was traveling with her husband, Harvey, and their seven-year-old daughter, Belle. Harvey was a former army officer, not one of the released prisoners. Their cabin was near the middle of the boat.

By the time Sultana left Memphis and resumed its trip upriver, the Mississippi was in flood. People were sleeping wherever they could find room.

Before dawn on April 27, a boiler exploded.

Ann later described hearing a violent noise. Her husband looked into the cabin passage and found steam. He closed that door. The door leading outside was jammed.

This wasn't a problem anyone could solve by walking down a corridor. The middle of the steamboat had been torn apart, and a fire was spreading from the exposed furnace.

Harvey put life preservers on himself and Ann. They took Belle toward the stern, where they could reach another deck using a rope.

Harvey descended with their daughter. Ann followed. But, as she testified just two weeks later, someone jumping from above knocked her into the hold. She was pulled back out.

When she could move again, Harvey and Belle had already jumped into the river.

Ann followed, but her life preserver was not properly secured. She held on to the rudder until the heat of the burning vessel drove her away. She found a small piece of wood and clung to that instead.

The family had been together in a cabin. Now Ann was drifting downstream alone, with no way to know where the others had gone.

Elsewhere on the boat, Sergeant James Kimberlin faced a different decision. In his later account, he said many people jumped straight into a mass of swimmers. Others waited, removed heavy clothing, and grabbed pieces of wreckage.

Kimberlin waited until the flames forced his hand. He tied small pieces of timber together and entered the water with something to hold.

A steamboat called Bostona had passed Sultana shortly before the explosion. It turned around to help. But there were too many people in the water to approach at speed.

The crew stopped the paddle wheels so they would not strike the swimmers. They lowered small boats, which made repeated trips to collect people and bring them back.

Kimberlin survived. Ann survived too. In a later account, she recalled being found downstream after struggling to attract rescuers' attention.

Harvey and Belle did not return. Their bodies were never recovered.

More than a thousand people died in the disaster. Historical estimates differ, so a precise final number cannot be stated with confidence.

It is tempting to reduce the tragedy to one damaged boiler. A leak had been repaired at Vicksburg before departure, but the chief engineer later said the repaired spot had shown no sign of failing. The precise mechanism of the explosion remains disputed. The overcrowding, the fire, and the flood made escape much harder, whatever ignited the catastrophe.

The surviving soldiers eventually reached land. They had been on their way home after the war, and for many families there was no reunion.

Ann's testimony preserves a much smaller moment inside that enormous loss: a jammed door, a rope, a child going down to the deck with her father, and a mother following too late.

For the people still in the river, rescue also depended on a decision that seemed strange from the water: the approaching steamer had to stop its own paddle wheels before its crew could reach them.
