The last torpedo fired by USS Tang turned sharply left and came back toward the submarine.

October 1944, near Taiwan. Commander Richard O'Kane had been attacking Japanese convoys through two nights. A damaged transport was still afloat, and Tang was closing in again.

The first of her final two torpedoes ran toward the transport. The second broke the surface and began circling. O'Kane ordered emergency speed and a hard turn. Twenty seconds after launch, the torpedo struck Tang.

Water rushed into the rear compartments. Tang sank stern first, coming to rest about fifty-five metres down. Roughly thirty sailors remained alive in the forward part of the hull, with no route through the flooded stern.

Japanese patrol vessels dropped depth charges overhead, delaying their escape. The men had to enter a small chamber, seal the inner hatch, let seawater in until the pressure matched, and then open the outer hatch. Momsen Lungs could help them breathe on the way up. They could not guarantee rescue.

Thirteen men got out through the forward hatch. Only five were picked up alive. Four other survivors had come from the bridge or flooded conning tower.

Japanese sailors recovered all nine. Some had survived ships Tang had attacked. The Americans were beaten and taken prisoner, but all nine survived the war. Seventy-eight members of Tang's crew died.

The survivors could describe the torpedo's turn. The exact internal failure that caused it remains uncertain.

That night, Tang had already fired one of her final two torpedoes at the damaged transport. There was one weapon left.
