How can a submarine be sunk by the last torpedo it fired? And what can its crew do when the boat comes to rest on the seabed?

In October 1944, USS Tang was operating near Taiwan, in waters used by Japanese convoys. Her commander was Richard O'Kane. This was Tang's fifth war patrol, and the crew had already learned how much could change in a few minutes at night.

Tang was not an inexperienced boat. On an earlier patrol she had rescued twenty-two American airmen from the sea. On other patrols she had attacked ships supplying Japan's war effort. Those missions depended on two things: getting close enough to fire, and getting away before an escort could answer.

Now O'Kane was attacking convoys moving through the Formosa Strait. Tang fought on the surface, where she could move quickly and use lookouts to find targets in darkness. In one attack, Japanese vessels were so close that a ship nearly rammed her. Tang escaped that fight and returned the following night.

There was one more damaged transport to attack. Tang had only two torpedoes left aboard.

The crew checked those weapons carefully. Then they brought the submarine into firing position. The first torpedo ran toward the transport. The last one did something the crew could see immediately: it came up toward the surface, turned sharply left, and began to circle.

O'Kane called for emergency speed and a hard turn. For the men on the bridge, the torpedo was no longer travelling toward an enemy ship. It was coming toward them.

Twenty seconds after it was fired, the weapon struck Tang.

The explosion damaged the stern. Water flooded the three rear compartments, and the boat went down stern first. She came to rest on the bottom in about fifty-five metres of water.

But the sinking did not kill everyone at once. About thirty men had survived inside the forward part of the hull. Above them were enemy patrol vessels. Inside, the water had cut off their route through the rear of the submarine.

Their way out was a small escape compartment at the front. They would have to enter it in groups, let it fill with water, equalise pressure, open the outer hatch, and rise through the sea.

They had Momsen Lungs: breathing devices designed to help sailors escape a sunken submarine. Owning one was not the same as reaching the surface safely. The men had to get through the hatch, ascend from fifty-five metres, and stay afloat long enough for someone to find them.

Japanese depth charges delayed the attempt. Eventually, thirteen men got out through the forward escape route.

Only five of those thirteen were later rescued alive.

Four other survivors had escaped from the bridge or flooded conning tower. They had spent hours in the water. Japanese sailors picked up all nine men.

That rescue was another brutal turn. Men from vessels Tang had attacked were on the Japanese ship. The American survivors were beaten and then held as prisoners of war. All nine lived until the end of the war.

Seventy-eight other members of Tang's crew died.

The last torpedo was an electric Mark 18. Accounts agree on its sudden turn and the twenty seconds before impact; that does not establish the precise internal fault that made it circle. The men on the bridge could see the danger, but could not steer the submarine clear in time.

Tang had once brought stranded airmen safely out of the ocean. On her last patrol, a few of her own sailors escaped from the seabed. The rescue did not undo the loss. It meant nine men were able to tell how the final shot had come back.
