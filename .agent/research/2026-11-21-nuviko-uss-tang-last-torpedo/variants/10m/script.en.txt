What happens when a submarine's final torpedo turns back toward the boat that fired it? And if the boat sinks, is there any way to leave it from the bottom of the sea?

Those are two different questions. In October 1944, the men aboard USS Tang would have to face both.

Tang was an American submarine operating in the Pacific during the Second World War. Her commander, Richard O'Kane, had a reputation for taking his boat close to Japanese shipping. The attacks were made possible by machinery, navigation, and torpedoes, but also by the crew's ability to recognise a danger quickly enough to get out of its path.

Before her last patrol, Tang had already shown another side of submarine service. In the spring of 1944, while stationed off Truk, she picked up twenty-two American aviators who had come down in the water. Men who might otherwise have been left at sea came back aboard a submarine.

On other patrols Tang had sunk Japanese merchant ships. By the autumn she was an experienced fighting boat. Success did not mean she was safe. Japanese convoys travelled with armed escorts, and a submarine caught close to one of those ships could be shelled, rammed, or hunted with depth charges.

Tang set out from Pearl Harbor in September for a fifth patrol. Her assigned waters lay near Formosa, now Taiwan, and the Chinese coast. The area mattered because supplies and reinforcements were travelling by sea as the fighting in the Philippines intensified.

There were two ways for a submarine to fight there. It could submerge, approach under water, and rely on its instruments. Or it could use the darkness and its speed on the surface to slip close to a convoy, fire, and disappear. Tang had repeatedly used the second method.

A successful attack could turn dangerous before the submarine was clear. On one night in October, Tang entered a heavily guarded convoy and struck several ships. A transport threatened to ram her. She had to manoeuvre among ships and gunfire while trying to escape.

That was not the end of the patrol. The crew found another convoy the following night, and Tang fired more torpedoes. A number of the ships were hit. When the firing was over, there were only two torpedoes left.

One damaged Japanese transport still offered a target. Tang could turn away and leave it, or use the last weapons. O'Kane chose to return. The details of the final attack come largely from survivors, particularly the commander's report written after the war.

The two remaining torpedoes were checked before firing. A torpedo was not an unguided shell. It had its own propulsion and steering equipment and was supposed to run a course set before launch. The crew needed it to continue toward the target after leaving the tube.

Tang moved into position. The first of the last two weapons ran toward the transport. The second left the submarine, came up toward the surface, and suddenly curved sharply left.

It did not straighten out. Its path was bringing it around toward Tang.

The men watching had seconds to react. Emergency speed was ordered, along with a hard turn. The submarine changed course, but the weapon kept coming.

Twenty seconds after the torpedo left its tube, it hit Tang.

It is easy to retell this as a simple case of a defective weapon. But the precise mechanical defect that caused that final Mark 18 electric torpedo to circle is not established by the accounts of the sinking. The decisive observed facts are its unexpected turn, the attempt to evade, and the impact.

The explosion destroyed the chance of returning to base. Water rushed into the rear compartments. Tang went down stern first. At roughly fifty-five metres, or one hundred and eighty feet, she reached the bottom.

Not all of the men had been below decks. Nine officers and sailors were on the bridge when the torpedo struck. Some were thrown into the water or had to get free of the sinking structure. The crew members trapped inside faced a very different problem.

The rear of the submarine had flooded. Men in the forward compartments remained alive. They could not simply open a hatch and swim out. At that depth, the pressure outside was much greater than the pressure inside, and an ordinary open doorway would allow a violent rush of water.

The men would need the forward escape chamber.

A small group could enter this space and close the inner hatch behind them. Water would be let in until pressure in the chamber matched the sea outside. Then the outer hatch could be opened. Each sailor would have to rise through the water and survive the trip to the surface.

For that ascent the crew had Momsen Lungs, emergency breathing devices carried for submarine escape. The equipment was meant to make an ascent possible. It could not remove the depth, the cold, the strain, or the danger of never being found.

Before the men could begin, Japanese patrol vessels were still moving above the wreck. Depth charges delayed the attempt. Inside Tang, roughly thirty survivors had gathered in the forward section. The number still inside did not guarantee how many could reach the hatch, complete the sequence, or survive outside.

They began leaving in groups through the forward escape route.

Thirteen men made it out. Some reached the surface but were not recovered. Only five from that group were picked up alive.

The men who had been near the bridge had their own struggle. Three survived by swimming for hours after the sinking. A fourth man escaped from the flooded conning tower. Those four, together with the five who had come up from the seabed, made nine survivors in all.

The ship that picked them up was Japanese.

That should not be confused with a friendly rescue. Aboard the Japanese escort were men who had themselves survived attacks by Tang. Some beat the Americans. The nine prisoners were taken to Japan and spent the remainder of the war in captivity.

The outcome has an uncomfortable symmetry. Earlier in the year Tang had rescued stranded American airmen from the sea. Now Japanese sailors had recovered nine survivors from Tang, but those men were prisoners rather than passengers heading home.

Seventy-eight members of the submarine's crew did not survive. Some died when the torpedo struck and the rear compartments flooded. Others were lost inside the boat or after leaving it. The record does not allow the story of the thirteen escapees to be turned into a claim that all thirteen survived.

One of the survivors was William Leibold. In a later oral-history interview, he described life aboard Tang, the attacks on the convoys, the torpedo's sudden movement, and captivity. His account is valuable because it comes from someone who was there. It is still a recollection made long after the war, not an instrument recording.

O'Kane's report, too, was written after he came out of captivity. The surviving records offer agreement on the main sequence while leaving some details and counts disputed across later retellings. The Navy's own historical account distinguishes wartime claims of ships sunk from later checks against Japanese records.

That distinction matters. Tang's last patrol was widely praised, and O'Kane received the Medal of Honor. But decorations and tallies cannot explain the internal failure of the final torpedo, and they do not alter the number of men who failed to return.

There is another reason to avoid making the weapon a mystery with an invented solution. Reports from other American submarines had already described torpedoes taking unexpected circular courses. That establishes the type of danger was known. It does not prove which component failed on Tang's last shot.

After the war, Tang's surviving sailors could describe an attack that had almost finished, a last torpedo turning in the wrong direction, twenty seconds of evasive action, and a desperate exit from a submarine on the seabed.

Five men had escaped through the water from fifty-five metres down. Four others had survived outside the hull. All nine survived imprisonment.

The crew had spent the patrol trying to evade enemy weapons. The final weapon they had to evade was their own. The nine survivors could tell that story, but seventy-eight men were gone before the last patrol was over.
