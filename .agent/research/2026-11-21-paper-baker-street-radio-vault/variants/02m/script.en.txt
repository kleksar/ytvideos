A man walked into a London bank carrying an umbrella. He wasn't checking the weather. He was measuring the room he planned to rob.

It was 1971. A gang wanted the safety-deposit boxes inside a Lloyds Bank vault on Baker Street. They didn't try the front door. They rented a leather-goods shop two doors away and started digging.

The tunnel ran beneath another business and ended under the vault. A heavy jack couldn't break the floor. A cutting torch failed too. Eventually the gang used explosives to get through.

Their lookout spoke to the men underground on a walkie-talkie. But someone else was listening.

Robert Rowlands, a radio enthusiast in a nearby flat, picked up the conversation by accident. He called the police. At first they didn't believe him. So he recorded the voices and kept calling.

Officers started searching London banks. They checked hundreds of them, including the one on Baker Street. Its vault door looked secure. There was a time lock, and they couldn't open it on a Sunday.

So they left. They couldn't see the tunnel beneath the floor.

On Monday, bank staff opened the vault. Two hundred and sixty-eight deposit boxes had been broken into. The robbers had escaped with valuables whose total value was never firmly established.

The police did eventually identify four men who were convicted. One clue was painfully ordinary: a participant had taken the shop lease in his own name.

But the stranger part happened before the arrests. Police had followed the radio warning to the correct bank, stood outside its locked vault and missed the opening underneath.

And before any of that was possible, someone first had to locate the floor of that vault. The trail led back to an ordinary-looking customer and his umbrella.
