How did police reach the right bank during a burglary—and fail to find it? How could officers inspect a vault door while someone had already broken into the room behind it? And who was giving the police directions without ever seeing the crime?

In September 1971, the answer was hidden beneath a busy London street.

At number 185 Baker Street stood a Lloyds Bank. Customers kept valuables in small locked boxes inside its vault. Two doors away was a leather-goods shop called Le Sac.

A man involved in a planned burglary had visited the bank as a customer. He carried an umbrella and used it, along with his arm span, to work out the arrangement of the vault. The gang needed a map, because they weren't planning to enter through the door.

They rented Le Sac. From its basement they dug beneath the business between the shop and the bank, bringing the tunnel up under the vault itself.

The distance was about forty feet. That sounds short until you remember they had to excavate it without attracting the whole street's attention, and haul the earth back into the shop.

Then they reached the concrete.

The men placed a powerful jack beneath the floor. It didn't break through. Beneath the tunnel was an old well, and the pressure went the wrong way. A thermal lance, designed to cut through tough material, was their next idea. That failed too.

They finally used explosives to break into the vault floor.

So far, the plan had been hidden underground. Now a mistake above ground threatened to expose everything.

A lookout on a nearby roof was talking to the men inside by walkie-talkie. Their radios did not keep the conversation private.

Robert Rowlands was at home listening to his radio. He caught voices discussing a bank break-in in progress. He had no address for the crime, but he could hear enough to realise this was not an ordinary conversation.

He telephoned the police. They were sceptical. He kept listening and made recordings. Eventually officers came and heard the transmissions for themselves.

Imagine the problem facing them. A robbery was apparently happening somewhere in London. They could hear the gang. But they did not know which bank to surround.

Police began checking bank after bank. The search eventually covered roughly 750 branches, including the Lloyds Bank on Baker Street.

On Sunday afternoon, officers reached the right building. From the corridor, its vault appeared secure. The door was on a time lock, so they could not open it for a proper inspection.

They went away.

The crucial fact is not whether the thieves were still inside at that exact moment; the surviving accounts do not establish that. The crucial fact is that the vault had been breached through its floor, not its locked door. A door that looked untouched could tell the police almost nothing about the tunnel beneath it.

On Monday, the bank opened the vault and found the result. The gang had forced open 268 safe-deposit boxes. Nobody could produce a reliable total for all the valuables that had been taken.

It was an extraordinary escape. But the same gang that had measured a vault with an umbrella had left investigators a far more ordinary paper trail.

The lease for the leather-goods shop had been signed by Benjamin Wolfe under his own name. Police also followed information about other suspects. Four men were eventually convicted in January 1973; three received twelve-year sentences and Wolfe received eight years.

So the gang did not get away with everything. The police identified the men, but much of the stolen property was not recovered.

And the answer to the opening question is almost painfully simple. The radio warning was real. The officers reached the right bank. But the only thing they could inspect was the wrong entrance.

The burglars had entered through the floor. To work out exactly where that floor was, the story had started with a customer carrying an umbrella.
