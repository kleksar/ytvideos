How did a man listening to a radio discover a bank burglary before the police did? Why did the police check the right bank and leave? And why did the gang's biggest engineering challenge turn out to be less dangerous than an ordinary signature on a shop lease?

The place was Baker Street, London, in September 1971. The bank was Lloyds, at number 185. Behind an ordinary-looking entrance was a vault filled with safe-deposit boxes. People paid to keep things there because they expected that room to be harder to reach than the street outside.

The burglars weren't going to test the front entrance.

Their plan resembled a Sherlock Holmes story: rent a nearby shop, dig a tunnel and enter the bank from below. But there was a problem that a fictional detective could solve in a sentence and real builders could not. Where exactly was the vault, and what part of the floor was safe to tunnel toward?

One of the men, Reg Tucker, visited as a customer. Bank staff gave customers privacy when they entered the vault. Tucker carried an umbrella. Using it, his arm span and the regular pattern of the floor tiles, he mapped the space and the position of the cabinets.

He left with measurements, not money.

Two doors down stood Le Sac, a leather-goods shop. Another member of the gang, Benjamin Wolfe, took the lease in May 1971. From the shop's basement, the men could work underground without standing in front of the bank with tools.

The route was not straight through a wall. A restaurant occupied the space between the shop and the bank. The diggers had to go below it, then come up beneath the vault.

They began the work around the August bank holiday, digging mostly at weekends. A tunnel that eventually stretched about forty feet doesn't sound like a major construction project. But it was narrow. Men had to move through it, carry earth backward and keep the entrance hidden inside a functioning shop.

Accounts of the investigation describe tonnes of earth accumulating behind the premises. The gang also had to work around whatever was already buried under the buildings. There was no architectural drawing they could trust for the exact path they were cutting.

At last they reached the reinforced concrete beneath the bank.

They had obtained a powerful jack, rated at a hundred tons. The idea was to push up and break the vault floor. Instead, the pressure went down. The tunnel ended above an old well, and the ground beneath the jack gave way.

They had built the right tunnel and arrived at the right place, only to discover that their equipment was pushing in the wrong direction.

They switched to a thermal lance. It could cut through stubborn material, but this attempt didn't open the vault either. Finally they used explosives to break through the floor.

Think about what that meant for the bank's security. The heavy door could remain closed and apparently untouched. From the corridor, the vault might look normal. The breach was beneath everyone's feet.

The gang had a lookout above ground on a nearby roof. He spoke by walkie-talkie to the men moving through the tunnel and vault. That solved one problem for them: someone could watch the street while the others worked.

It created a second problem. Their radio conversations travelled beyond the men holding the handsets.

In a flat on Wimpole Street, Robert Rowlands was using his radio receiver. He picked up unfamiliar voices. As he listened, the discussion sounded less like a routine call and more like instructions for a burglary already underway.

Rowlands did not know the bank's address. He knew only what the men said over the air. He called the police.

They were doubtful. If someone had really intercepted a robbery, why couldn't he say which bank was being robbed? The obvious answer was that the criminals hadn't announced their location.

Rowlands kept the receiver on. He recorded the conversations on tape. Officers came to his flat and eventually heard enough to take the warning seriously. By then, the gang had already been communicating for hours.

A contemporary report in TIME describes a succession of officers listening to the recordings and radio traffic while the crime continued elsewhere. The story is frustrating because both sides had useful information, but not the same information.

The police could hear voices without knowing the building. The thieves knew every turn of their tunnel but not that a stranger had tuned in. And the lookout could see the street while his own words were crossing London.

Authorities considered tracing the source of the signal. But by the time a specialist could help, the transmissions had stopped. That is one reason the search turned into a much less elegant operation.

Police started visiting banks.

The sweep ultimately covered roughly 750 branches across a large area of London. Officers and bank employees checked doors and security arrangements. They had to find a building whose occupants, if any were still inside, had no intention of drawing attention to themselves.

One of those branches was Lloyds at 185 Baker Street.

The police arrived on Sunday afternoon. There was no obvious sign on the vault door that someone had broken in. The door was secured by a time lock and could not be opened for an inspection.

They left.

There is an important limit to what anyone can say about this scene. Later accounts disagree or remain uncertain about whether the burglars were physically inside at that precise moment. We don't need to settle that to understand the mistake.

The vault had already been attacked from below. Officers reached the correct building, but the part they could see was not the part the burglars had opened. A secure-looking door was an answer to the wrong question.

On Monday, September 13, bank staff opened the vault. The damage was unmistakable. Two hundred and sixty-eight safe-deposit boxes had been forced. The gang had left with valuables belonging to many different customers.

The headlines couldn't agree on how much had been stolen. These weren't all ordinary piles of banknotes with an inventory. The boxes held private possessions, and reported estimates varied widely. The amount lost is still best described as uncertain.

The escaped burglars had seemed to outwit the police at the exact moment the police were looking for them. Yet their cover was less secure than their tunnel.

Investigators followed the route back to Le Sac. The lease had been signed by Benjamin Wolfe in his own name. That signature gave them a person to find, rather than a voice on a recording.

Police also gathered other leads and information about suspected gang members. By the end of October 1971, four central suspects had been arrested. The case went to court. In January 1973, Anthony Gavin, Reg Tucker and Thomas Stephens each received twelve-year prison sentences. Wolfe received eight years.

Not everyone the police suspected was convicted, and the investigation did not recover all the property. Reports of the amount recovered are only a fraction of even the conservative estimates of the theft.

For the people who rented those boxes, that difference mattered. A spectacular operation had not just embarrassed a bank and the police; it had exposed valuables that were supposed to be secure. The uncertainty over what was in each box also made compensation difficult.

There were later civil claims against Lloyds. They raised a different question from the criminal trial: if customers had paid to keep their possessions in a vault, who bore responsibility when a tunnel defeated its physical protections?

The story has gathered rumours over the years, especially about famous people and compromising material supposedly kept in the boxes. Those claims are not a reliable explanation of why the burglary happened. The documented operation is strange enough without using unproven secret photographs as its payoff.

Here is what we can establish. The gang measured a vault without a blueprint, dug beneath a London shopping street, failed with a jack and a cutter, broke through with explosives, and spoke openly enough on radio for a stranger to report them.

The police got the warning and found the right bank. But they could examine only a locked door, not the hole in the floor. That was the narrow escape in the middle of the story.

The final irony came later. The men had planned a tunnel in remarkable detail. One of them had not thought to keep his own name off the shop lease. Police eventually followed that very ordinary document.

The question that remains after the arrests isn't how anyone could think a bank door was safe. It's how the burglars knew exactly where to dig beneath it. And that takes us back to a customer arriving with an umbrella.
