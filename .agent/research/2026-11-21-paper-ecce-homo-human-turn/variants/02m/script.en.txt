In 2012, eighty-one-year-old Cecilia Giménez left a church in Borja, Spain, with a face still half-painted on its wall.

The original, called Ecce Homo, showed Jesus. Moisture had been eating into it, and Cecilia, an amateur painter, wanted to protect it. She expected to return and finish.

While she was away, someone photographed the unfinished face. Placed beside the older image, it looked so different that the comparison spread worldwide. People nicknamed it Monkey Christ.

Cecilia didn't find it funny. On Spanish television that September, she said the work wasn't finished and she no longer wanted to go back. The ridicule had hurt her.

Then tourists started arriving. They knew that face from the internet and wanted to see the real wall. Borja kept the altered painting. The sanctuary began collecting admission fees and selling souvenirs.

In 2022, the mayor described what the money did. Tickets and merchandise were bringing in roughly fifty to fifty-five thousand euros a year then. About thirty-five thousand paid two workers; the rest helped a foundation cover care for older residents without enough money of their own.

The repainting hadn't become professional restoration. And the charity didn't erase what Cecilia had endured. When she died in December 2025, the mayor remembered her kindness and the pressure she'd survived.

But the face that made strangers laugh had also led them to a town where their visits helped real people.

All of that began with a photograph taken while the painter was away.
