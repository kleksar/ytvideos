Why did people travel to a small Spanish town to see a painting that looked ruined? And why did the town eventually use money from that painting to help people who needed care?

In the church of the Sanctuary of Mercy, near Borja in Spain, there was a picture of Jesus called Ecce Homo. It was painted by Elías García Martínez. For years, dampness had been eating away at the image. The paint was fading and flaking from the wall.

One of the people who worried about it was Cecilia Giménez. She lived nearby, loved painting, and had spent years trying to look after the picture. She wasn't a trained art conservator. In the summer of 2012, when she was eighty-one, she started to repaint part of the damaged face.

She did not finish. Cecilia went away for a short trip, intending to return to the wall.

Before she came back, people saw what she'd done. Someone photographed the partly repainted face alongside the earlier picture. Then those photographs reached the news and spread across the internet.

The old painting had a recognizable face. In the new one, the features were blurred and strangely round. Online, people gave it a nickname: Monkey Christ.

That was the first twist. The world thought it had found a hilarious example of somebody destroying a work of art. Cecilia said they were looking at unfinished work.

She hadn't painted in secret, she insisted. She said the priest knew she had been working there. Whether every part of the intervention had been formally authorized was disputed, and the finished result was plainly not professional conservation. But she had wanted to stop the old image disappearing.

The jokes didn't stop at the picture. They reached the person who had painted it.

That September, Cecilia spoke to Spanish television. She said she was very upset. She didn't want to return and finish what she'd started. Later accounts described how badly the attention affected her health.

Now imagine being a resident of Borja. The only thing many people around the world know about your town is a face on a church wall that makes them laugh.

Then the visitors begin arriving.

People who had never planned a trip to Borja wanted to stand in front of that very wall. They'd seen the before-and-after pictures, and they wanted to check whether the famous new face was real. The painting was no longer just a local embarrassment or an internet image. It had become a destination.

The town did something important: it kept Cecilia's version. That didn't make her repainting a successful restoration of the original. The older image had still been altered. But removing the strange new face would also have removed the reason so many visitors were coming.

The interest lasted long enough for the sanctuary to organize visits and for Borja to open an interpretation center about the painting. There were tickets and souvenirs. This wasn't just a passing joke anymore. People could work at a place that had barely attracted attention before.

In 2022, the mayor described where some of the money went. The sanctuary and its merchandise were bringing in roughly fifty to fifty-five thousand euros a year at that time. About thirty-five thousand went toward the salaries of two people keeping the site open. The remaining money helped a charitable foundation that supported care for vulnerable elderly residents.

The story had changed again. A picture people shared to laugh at a mistake was helping pay for other people's care.

Cecilia lived long enough to see her work treated with more affection. When she died in December 2025, at ninety-four, Borja's mayor spoke about her kindness and the pressure she had survived.

There is no neat way to call the repainting good conservation. And the benefit to the town does not erase the cruelty Cecilia endured.

But if you visit the sanctuary, the picture is still there. You can see why the photographs spread. What you cannot see in the before-and-after image is the path from a damaged wall, to an unfinished face, to the strangers who arrived, and finally to the people those visits helped.

That is why Borja kept the painting everybody had laughed at.
