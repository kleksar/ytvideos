Why does a Spanish town still display a face the internet once called a disaster? What happened to the woman who painted it? And how could an unfinished restoration eventually help pay for care for elderly residents?

The answer starts with a wall. At the Sanctuary of Mercy near Borja, in northeastern Spain, there was a religious painting called Ecce Homo. It showed Jesus and was the work of Elías García Martínez. It wasn't one of the world's most famous paintings. It was a familiar image in a local place of worship.

That wall had a problem. Dampness was damaging the picture. Parts of the paint faded and flaked away. Even before the internet heard about it, there was a real question about how long the image could survive without attention.

Cecilia Giménez was one of the people who noticed. She was a local amateur painter, not a professional conservator. She had been looking after the painting in different ways for years. In 2012, when she was eighty-one, she went further: she began repainting the damaged face.

That distinction matters. Painting a replacement face on top of an old religious image is not the same as conserving the surviving original paint. Someone trained to restore paintings would approach the job differently. But the explanation Cecilia gave later was straightforward. She wanted to save a picture that was deteriorating.

She also said that her work was known locally. In interviews she insisted that she had not been sneaking into the church to paint in secret and that the priest knew. The precise permissions involved became a point of disagreement. What is certain is that she had not yet finished the new face.

Before she returned to it, Cecilia went away for a short holiday. She expected to work on the painting again when she came back.

Instead, the unfinished face was photographed.

That is where the story stops being a small local matter. The photograph was placed beside an image of the painting as it had looked before. The comparison was impossible to miss. Where the older painting had a clear human face, the new one had rounded features, an indistinct expression, and a strange silhouette.

The paired images went into the press. Then they spread around the internet. People didn't need to know the artist's name or the town's location to understand the visual joke. One look seemed enough.

The painting had been called Ecce Homo, or Behold the Man. The internet supplied its own nickname: Monkey Christ. A private attempt at repairing a damaged wall had become a public spectacle.

The joke arrived before Cecilia got to explain what the world was looking at.

She said the work wasn't finished. That was a different story from the one told by a tidy before-and-after photograph. A photograph of an unfinished stage had been treated as if it were the final product. But her explanation couldn't undo the transformation already on the wall, and it couldn't slow the attention.

In early September 2012, Cecilia spoke to Spanish television. She was upset. She said she no longer wanted to go back and finish the image. She thought people who worked professionally with paintings should take over. Other accounts from those close to her described severe anxiety.

This is easy to miss when we look at the famous pictures now. We see two faces. The woman who lived through the reaction experienced something very different. People all over the world were laughing at a picture, and her name was attached to it.

The reactions were not uniform. Some people saw the whole event as a failure to protect an artwork. Others found the new face funny, appealing, or unexpectedly expressive. The wider internet could hold all those opinions at once. Cecilia had no way to choose which version would become famous.

And then a remarkable thing happened, one that the first photographs couldn't predict.

Visitors started traveling to Borja.

These were not all people with a lifelong interest in church murals. Many had come because they recognized that image. The photograph had made them curious; the physical wall was where they could confirm they had seen the real thing.

For the sanctuary, this reversed the usual relationship between art and tourism. Normally the reputation of a place leads a visitor to its picture. Here, a picture famous for being mocked led visitors to a place most of them hadn't known existed.

That created a new problem. What should happen to the altered painting?

There was an obvious artistic objection: the older image had been changed, and a joke wasn't a substitute for conservation. Keeping the new face did not magically make it a skillful restoration. But by now the altered painting was itself the thing people came to see. Removing it would not bring back the original moment of history. It would erase a new and extremely unusual chapter of the wall's story.

The version painted by Cecilia remained. Borja's connection to the image became part of the town's identity.

The interest did not disappear after the first week of headlines. Tourists continued to arrive. The sanctuary organized access for visitors. There were tickets and goods bearing the famous image. In 2016, a center devoted to interpreting the story of the Ecce Homo opened in Borja.

Think about what had changed. At first there was one damaged painting and one person trying to help. The attention produced an enormous cost to that person. But it also gave a little-known town a reason for people to visit.

By 2022, the money was substantial enough for local officials to describe the practical details. The mayor said the sanctuary and merchandise were bringing in roughly fifty to fifty-five thousand euros a year at that time.

About thirty-five thousand euros went to the wages of two caretakers who helped keep the sanctuary open throughout the year. The balance went toward a charitable foundation linked to a residential home for elderly people who lacked the means to pay for care. In the figures reported that year, support reached two vulnerable residents; in some previous years, the organization had helped more.

Those details are more important than a huge lifetime revenue number with no context. A visitor bought a ticket to look at the painting. Part of the money paid people to maintain the site; part helped other people live with care. The connection was tangible.

Cecilia also had an interest in money from merchandise bearing the image. The 2022 reporting described a share of those proceeds going to her and then being directed toward the charitable foundation. It is another unusual layer of the story: the woman once ridiculed for repainting a face became connected to the public use of its image.

But this happy outcome needs a qualification. It doesn't mean the harassment was justified, or that the internet planned to help anyone. The visitors didn't erase the anguish that came before them. And the original painting was still not professionally restored by the mere fact that the new one was popular.

There was a separate question about what the town was now celebrating. Was it the joke, the painting, or the woman who had tried to repair it?

Over time, more of Cecilia's life became part of the public story. She was not only an eighty-one-year-old woman at the wrong end of a viral photograph. She had been an amateur painter long before the story spread, and she had family responsibilities and personal hardships that never appeared in the original meme.

She received offers and attention she hadn't sought. Her own artwork was exhibited. The Ecce Homo story inspired a documentary and even an opera. A church painting whose fame began with ridicule now existed in several very different forms: a local wall, an internet image, a tourist destination, and a subject for other artists.

None of these later versions settles an argument about artistic value. A professional conservator could still object to what happened to the original. A visitor might still laugh at the altered face. Another might see something moving in the story of the woman who made it.

What matters is that the whole outcome cannot be captured by either of the two photographs.

Cecilia lived to see the transformation. She died in December 2025, aged ninety-four. Borja's mayor described her as a deeply kind person and spoke about the pressure she had endured. The town had come a long way from the period when her name seemed to mean only an art disaster.

On the wall, the face is still the one that went around the world. Look at it on a phone and you can see the joke immediately. Visit the site and the story grows more complicated.

The first picture shows an old painting. The second shows an unfinished attempt to save it. Neither shows the tourists at the doorway, the workers who can keep the church open, or the people whose care those visits help support.

The real ending was never visible in a before-and-after photograph.
