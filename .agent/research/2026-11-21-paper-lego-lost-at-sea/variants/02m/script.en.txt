A LEGO octopus.

In April 2024, a thirteen-year-old boy found one on a beach in Cornwall, England. It wasn't a new toy. It had spent twenty-seven years in the sea.

Liutauras had searched for this exact kind of piece for two years. His family had collected hundreds of LEGO pieces from local beaches. But there was a reason the little octopus was harder to find.

On February 13, 1997, a cargo ship called Tokio Express lost sixty-two containers off the English coast. One carried nearly five million LEGO pieces, many from ocean-themed sets: flippers, diving tanks, swords, sharks, and octopuses.

Only about four thousand two hundred of the pieces were octopuses. The shipment held more than fifty thousand sharks.

The sea didn't return everything at once. Some pieces washed ashore. Others sank. For years, beachcombers kept finding toys whose original owners had never even opened the boxes.

A woman named Tracey Williams began documenting the discoveries. The collection became a way to trace a cargo spill long after its headlines disappeared.

Then, in 2024, the boy found his octopus. Just months later, a fisherman pulled up the first reported shark from the lost shipment, caught in a net far offshore.

The finds are charming. The reason they can happen is less charming: plastic remains in the sea for a very long time. Studies of old LEGO bricks found on beaches show how slowly that material wears away, and how it can break into smaller fragments.

An ocean accident had become a treasure hunt, and a record of what doesn't disappear.
