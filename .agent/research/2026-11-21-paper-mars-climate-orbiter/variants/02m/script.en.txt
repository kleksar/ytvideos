The radio signal from NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter vanished 49 seconds earlier than predicted. Mars was supposed to block the signal during arrival. But the spacecraft never called back.

Nine months earlier, the probe left Earth to study Mars's weather. Now it had reached the planet, and its main engine appeared to be working. So why didn't it enter orbit?

One solar panel stuck out to the side. Sunlight slowly twisted the probe, and small thrusters fired to keep it pointed correctly. They fired far more often than navigators expected. Every firing also nudged its path.

Engineers in Colorado logged those tiny pushes. Navigators in California used the numbers to calculate the route. But one ground program wrote them in pound-force seconds, while the next expected newton-seconds. The difference was a factor of about 4.45. The model treated every push as weaker than it really was.

There had been warnings. Tracking observations and predicted positions disagreed during the journey. Near Mars, different navigation calculations pointed to different approach heights. The differences remained unresolved. A final course correction was discussed, but the preparations were not ready.

After contact was lost, investigators corrected the force data. They reconstructed a closest pass of roughly 57 kilometres, against a planned 226. They couldn't see whether the probe burned up or escaped into space. They only knew it was gone.

The wrong unit was the root error. Failed checks and unresolved warnings let it reach Mars. And the final clue arrived during what was supposed to be an ordinary radio blackout.
