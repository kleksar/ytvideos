A NASA spacecraft went behind Mars exactly as planned. Twenty minutes later, it still hadn't come back.

In September 1999, Mars Climate Orbiter was reaching the end of a nine-month trip. Its job was to watch Martian weather and help relay messages from a lander.

To enter orbit, it fired its engine and passed behind the planet. Losing the radio signal was normal. Failing to get it back wasn't.

The spacecraft had been making tiny steering adjustments throughout the trip. One solar panel stuck out to the side, so sunlight slowly disturbed its orientation. Small thruster firings corrected that movement.

Engineers in Colorado sent information about those firings to navigators in California. The navigators expected the figures in newton-seconds, a metric measure of a push. Instead, the ground software supplied pound-force seconds. A pound-force is about 4.45 newtons.

The numbers looked like numbers. But without the right unit, the navigation calculations underestimated those tiny pushes. Over months, the predicted route drifted away from the real one.

There had been differences between the navigation estimates. They were noticed, but not resolved before arrival.

The signal disappeared behind Mars earlier than expected. It never returned. Afterward, investigators reconstructed a closest approach of roughly 57 kilometres, far below the intended safe path. The spacecraft was lost; nobody watched its exact final moments.

NASA's finding wasn't just that someone had mixed up units. The interface specified the correct units. The checks and communication that should have caught the mismatch failed too.

The final radio silence looked ordinary at first. That was the cruel part of the mistake: the last thing operators saw was the spacecraft doing exactly what it had been told to do — disappearing behind Mars.
