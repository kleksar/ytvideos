## Three finalists with equal editorial tests

**A — Larson (selected).** Stop: ice-cream seller wins $110,237 and CBS cannot refuse. Remain: home VHS recordings → five movement paths → first Whammy → repeated money-plus-spin → fairness-room dilemma and hours of taping. Payoff: winner paid because there was no violated rule, machine later redesigned; later attempt to outguess serial numbers reverses his fortune. Opening exact 05m: "How could a man walk into a game show, lose his first turn, and still leave with more than $110,000?" Main objection: already covered in videos and in the 2025 dramatized movie; 2026 comparable Paper demand unverified.

**B — Ingram.** Stop: a competitor wins £1 million, the prize is frozen. Remain: allegation of timed coughs by a fellow contestant, producer reviews footage, criminal trial with disputed evidence. Payoff: defendants convicted of conspiracy to cheat; they maintained innocence. Opening candidate: "What if the winning answer to a million-pound quiz was hidden in a cough?" Strong objection: a declarative reconstruction can hide the key distinction between proven conviction, claims and disputed editing. Sources: https://www.theguardian.com/media/2003/apr/07/broadcasting.uknews1 and https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/crime/millionaire-trio-guilty-of-tv-show-coughing-con-114229.html . Rival could be stronger in scandal curiosity than Larson.

**C — Van Doren.** Stop: a respected professor's spectacular television quiz wins were arranged by its producers. Remain: staged contestants and answers → challenger complaints → investigation → admission to Congress. Payoff: Nov 2, 1959 confession and lost NBC/academic career. Opening candidate: "The biggest quiz winner in America already had the answers before he went on air." Main objection: a straight corruption reveal becomes clear very early; depth depends on well-sourced political, industry and personal costs, with less good-natured visual energy. Source: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/quizshow-charles-van-doren/ and https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/quizshow-dan-enright/ .

**Why A beat closest B:** B has the stronger immediate prize/cheating controversy, but A has a clearer original observable causal chain (video recorder, exact safe squares, extra spin, studio couldn't stop a rules-compliant player) and an honest payoff that revises the apparent meaning of "cheating". B's factual interpretation must continually distinguish prosecutors and convicted defendants; A can be narrated as verified actions. This is an editorial hypothesis, not demand measurement. Strongest losing candidate: **B Ingram**.

## Promise and factual causal beats

Promise: "He won without violating a rule. How?" First 05m questions promise mechanism, control-room choice and aftermath. First visible scene concept: physical paper game-show board with Whammy risk and a rising prize tally, not a fake original TV still. Sequence verified by [S1]–[S3]: machine design → repeated TV study → memorized sequences → first Whammy → repeated money-and-extra spins → production time/fairness dilemma → CBS pays → later different luck-based gamble loses family-reported cash. Payoff: difference between an exploitable repeated machine and unpredictable banknote numbers. Main risk: passing off two sources of evidence (firsthand studio monitor and family afterstory recollections) as equally measured; attribution explicit.
