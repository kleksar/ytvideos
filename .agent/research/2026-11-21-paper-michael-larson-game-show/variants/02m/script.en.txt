An ice-cream-truck driver won $110,237 on a television game show. Not because he got lucky. He had worked out something the producers had failed to hide.

The year was 1984. On Press Your Luck, contestants stopped a flashing light on a board of eighteen squares. Money was good. A cartoon Whammy was bad: it erased your winnings. From the studio, it looked like the light could land anywhere.

Michael Larson had watched the show at home for months. He taped episodes and paused the recording, frame by frame. The light wasn't choosing its next square at random. It followed just five repeating paths. Larson learned them.

His first spin in the studio landed on a Whammy. That looked like the end of his clever plan. But then he started hitting the valuable squares, again and again. Some gave him several thousand dollars and another spin. So a successful stop didn't just win money. It gave him another chance to win.

The people running the show noticed. The problem was that he wasn't changing their machine or breaking a rule. He was pressing the same button as everybody else, only at a better moment.

His winnings reached $110,237. CBS reviewed what happened and paid him. The network later changed the board patterns.

And there was a stranger ending. According to family accounts, Larson later tried another money-making scheme involving the serial numbers on dollar bills. He kept piles of cash at home, and much of it was stolen. He had beaten the television board, but he couldn't make his next gamble work.

The producers' big mistake was simple: they made the light repeat. Which brings us back to the moment someone finally used that mistake against them.
