How could a man walk into a game show, lose his first turn, and still leave with more than $110,000? Why did the people running the show let him keep going when they realized what he was doing? And what happened to that money after the cameras stopped?

In 1984, Press Your Luck was built around a glowing board with eighteen squares. Contestants answered questions to earn spins. Then they pressed a button to stop a moving frame of light. The square where it stopped might award money, a prize, or a cartoon Whammy that wiped out everything the contestant had earned.

The game worked because nobody was meant to know where that light would stop. Winning seemed to depend on luck. Yet on May 19, a contestant called Michael Larson arrived at the CBS studio with a very different idea.

Larson drove an ice-cream truck in Ohio. He had spent months watching Press Your Luck on videotape, often pausing and replaying the board. Instead of following the prizes, he followed the movement of the light. And he discovered something the viewers at home were not supposed to see: the machine didn't choose a fresh, random path each time. It repeated one of only five patterns.

That changed the problem. Larson didn't have to predict every possible prize. He needed to recognize which pattern was running and press the button when the light reached one of two useful squares.

Those squares had an especially valuable feature. In the second round, their cash awards came with an extra spin. One right stop could bring several thousand dollars and give Larson the very turn he needed to try again. A Whammy, on the other hand, could erase the whole total.

But the opening of Larson's televised game looked like a disaster. His first spin hit a Whammy.

For a while, he looked like any other contestant. Then he started stopping the board on those valuable squares. One win produced another spin. Another spin produced more money. The totals kept climbing. The same light and the same button that usually made people gamble now gave Larson something closer to a practiced skill.

The people inside the control room noticed that the same areas of the board kept paying. Darlene Lieblich Tipton, who was there to monitor the fairness of the game, later recalled that staff wanted to know whether they could stop the recording. Her answer was the crucial part of this story: he wasn't violating a rule. The network had created those patterns. Larson had only learned them.

And there was another problem for the producers. This was meant to be a half-hour television show. Larson's run went on so long that the recording no longer fitted the normal format. The host had to spend extra time talking with him. CBS eventually broadcast the game in two parts, on June 8 and June 11.

By the end, Larson had accumulated $110,237 in cash and prizes. CBS examined what had happened, concluded that his method did not breach the rules, and paid him. The game board was later changed to make the pattern trick much harder.

You might expect that to be the end: man sees a flaw, beats the system, goes home rich. But the years after the show make the outcome less tidy.

In a later radio documentary, Larson's brother and former partner described his attempt to win another contest by finding a particular serial number on a one-dollar bill. He brought large quantities of cash into the house so that he could inspect the notes. After some of it had been returned to the bank, roughly fifty thousand dollars remained at home. According to their account, burglars took it while the family was away.

The irony isn't that learning a pattern was somehow wrong. The network paid him precisely because it wasn't. The irony is that his most careful gamble succeeded, while a later attempt to force another win cost him a great deal.

Press Your Luck appeared to ask one question: where will the light land? Larson had found a more useful question: what if the light has already told me where it's going?
