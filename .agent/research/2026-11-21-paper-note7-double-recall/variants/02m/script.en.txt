How can a company recall a dangerous phone, replace it, and then have to recall the replacements? And how does a smartphone end up banned from airplanes?

In August 2016, Samsung launched the Galaxy Note7, a large-screen phone with a pen built into its body. Soon, reports arrived of batteries overheating and catching fire.

On September second, Samsung stopped sales and offered owners a replacement. In the United States, the official recall followed on September fifteenth. Roughly a million phones were covered.

The proposed solution sounded straightforward: give customers another Note7 with a different battery.

But that solution failed. Reports of overheating continued, including in replacement devices. On October tenth, US safety regulators told people to switch off every Note7, whether it was the original phone or the replacement.

Three days later, the recall expanded to all Note7s. On October fifteenth, the United States barred the model from air travel altogether. You could not simply turn it off and carry it onto a plane.

Months later, investigators found that this wasn't one faulty batch repeated twice. The original batteries had a cramped corner that could bend an internal electrode. The replacements had a different manufacturing problem: sharp welding defects could damage the insulation between battery layers.

Either fault could create a short circuit and dangerous heat.

Samsung abandoned the Note7 and introduced additional battery safety checks. The disaster wasn't that every phone caught fire. It was that the company could no longer guarantee which phones were safe — not even the ones it had sent out as the fix.
