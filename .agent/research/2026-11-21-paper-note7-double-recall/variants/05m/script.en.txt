Why would a company ask you to return a smartphone, give you a supposedly safer one, and then tell you to return that one as well? And how could a phone become something an airline refused to carry?

In August 2016, Samsung launched the Galaxy Note7. It had a large curved screen, a pen, and a battery meant to keep all those features running. For a few weeks, this looked like another expensive flagship phone.

Then customers began reporting overheating batteries and fires. Samsung investigated. By September first it had counted thirty-five reported cases worldwide. The next day it stopped selling the Note7 and offered to exchange existing phones.

This was meant to contain the problem. The first phones would be removed, customers would receive replacements with different batteries, and the product could continue.

In the United States, safety regulators announced a formal recall on September fifteenth. It covered about one million phones. Their report described ninety-two battery overheating incidents in the country, including reports of burns and property damage.

Now imagine being the owner. You hand over the phone you've just bought. You receive another Note7 and are told this is the corrected version. Some replacement phones even showed a green battery symbol to help distinguish them from recalled units.

Then something happened that the replacement program was supposed to prevent.

On October fifth, a Samsung phone began smoking aboard a Southwest Airlines plane in Louisville. The aircraft was still on the ground. Passengers and crew evacuated safely. The owner told reporters it was a replacement Note7, not his original phone. At that point, Samsung said it still needed to examine the device before confirming the cause.

That distinction matters. The aircraft incident alone could not prove that every replacement was defective. But further reports of overheating arrived, and the safety authorities could no longer treat the replacement stock as a reliable solution.

On October tenth, the US Consumer Product Safety Commission urged people to switch off every Galaxy Note7, including the replacements. Samsung suspended sales and exchanges of the model. Three days later, the official recall expanded to all Note7 phones: roughly 1.9 million devices in the United States in total, including the original million.

The next restriction was even more unusual. On October fifteenth, the United States banned the Note7 from passenger flights, whether it was switched on, switched off, in someone's pocket, or in a bag. The model had gone from a new product to something you could not carry onto an airplane.

But why had exchanging the battery failed?

The answer only became clear after the product was gone. In January 2017, Samsung announced findings from its own investigation and independent examinations. The batteries used in the first phones had a design problem: inside a cramped corner, an electrode could bend, increasing the chance that the battery's internal layers would short together.

The batteries in replacement phones had a different problem. In their manufacturing process, sharp burrs from a weld could damage the insulating layer. Some also lacked insulating tape in critical areas. Different defects, same possible result: a short circuit and dangerous heat.

The second set wasn't simply a missed pile of the first faulty batteries. Replacing one design problem had exposed another manufacturing problem. That's why the second recall could not be solved by sending out another Note7 of the same model.

Samsung ended the Note7 and introduced additional checks, including X-rays, disassembly, and charge-and-discharge tests. The company had promised that changing the battery would restore confidence. Instead, it discovered that making a replacement is not the same as proving the replacement is safe.
