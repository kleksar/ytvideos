A second Piltdown fossil was meant to settle the argument. Decades later, it helped reveal that the first discovery was a fraud.

In 1912, Charles Dawson told museum expert Arthur Smith Woodward he'd found fragments of an ancient skull in English gravel. The skull looked human. A jaw found with it looked more like an ape's. But its teeth had flat surfaces that looked human.

Dawson and Woodward presented the pieces as one ancestor. Some anatomists objected immediately: why assume that skull and jaw came from the same creature?

Then Dawson reported a tooth and more skull pieces from a different site. A second find made the story harder to dismiss. But Dawson was connected to both.

After he died in 1916, the discoveries stopped. Other fossils raised doubts. In 1949, fluorine tests questioned the claimed age. In 1953, microscopic scratches showed the teeth had been filed down. The supposed ancestor combined human skull fragments with an orangutan jaw.

That explained the first find. But why had a second site produced such similar evidence?

In 2016, DNA linked teeth from both sites to what was likely a single orangutan. Scans showed similar gravel and putty used inside the fakes. The second find wasn't independent confirmation. It bore signs of the same preparation.

The evidence pointed strongly to Dawson, although it couldn't prove whether he worked alone or why he did it. And the sceptics hadn't been silent: some questioned the bones from the start.

What had once seemed to answer their doubts had become the clue that exposed the trick. Back in Dawson's time, without DNA or scans, there was a simpler reply to the sceptics.
