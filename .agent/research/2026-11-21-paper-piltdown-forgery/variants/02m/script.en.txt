Someone filed an ape's teeth until they looked human. Then those teeth helped fool a museum for forty years.

In 1912, an amateur fossil hunter named Charles Dawson brought a remarkable discovery to Arthur Smith Woodward at London's Natural History Museum. The pieces came from gravel near Piltdown, in southern England.

There were fragments of a human-looking skull and a lower jaw shaped more like an ape's. Yet the jaw's teeth looked worn down in a very human way.

Together, Dawson and Woodward presented the bones as one ancient ancestor. It seemed to answer a question scientists were arguing about: what did early humans look like?

There was a problem. Some anatomists said the skull and jaw didn't belong together. But the find became famous, and further supposed fossils turned up nearby.

Decades passed. New discoveries made Piltdown look stranger, not more convincing.

In 1949, chemical tests raised doubts about the bones' age. In 1953, investigators took another look. They found staining that had been applied to make the pieces look old. Under a microscope, the teeth showed marks left by filing.

The supposed ancestor was a mixture: human skull fragments and the jaw of an orangutan. The teeth had been altered to fit the story.

Researchers revisited the fossils in 2016. DNA and scans linked the fakes from two sites to the same orangutan and a consistent method. Dawson became the strongest suspect, though exactly who helped him, if anyone, remains uncertain.

The lesson wasn't that every scientist believed the find. Some objected from the beginning. It was that objections could be overlooked when an answer looked exactly as people hoped.
