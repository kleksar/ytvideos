How could a museum mistake a modern ape's jaw for a human ancestor? Why did the mistake last forty years? And how did scientists finally expose the teeth that gave it away?

In 1912, a man named Charles Dawson contacted Arthur Smith Woodward, a fossil expert at London's Natural History Museum. Dawson said he had found pieces of a very old skull in gravel near the English village of Piltdown.

Finding ancient human relatives was a major scientific prize. Fossils had been discovered outside Britain, and researchers were still arguing over how the human head and jaw had changed. Dawson's pieces appeared to offer a new answer.

Woodward joined the work at Piltdown. Among the material were fragments of a human-looking cranium and a lower jaw that looked much more like an ape's. The strange thing was the teeth. Their biting surfaces were worn flat, in a way that seemed human.

When Dawson and Woodward presented their reconstruction in 1912, they proposed a new kind of ancient human. The pieces were dark brown, like objects that had spent ages in that gravel. The skull suggested a large brain, and the jaw supplied the primitive feature people expected.

But two bones sitting in the same gravel do not necessarily come from the same animal. Some anatomists questioned the combination almost at once. Their objections mattered, yet the famous find survived them.

Then the story appeared to get stronger. Further objects and teeth were reported from the site, and Dawson described a second collection of remains at another Piltdown location. That made it seem less like one odd specimen and more like a repeating pattern.

Dawson died in 1916. No further Piltdown fossils emerged after his death.

As researchers discovered other early human fossils around the world, Piltdown became harder to fit into the picture. But it still had a place in textbooks and museums.

In 1949, Kenneth Oakley used fluorine testing to question the age of the bones. The fragments did not fit the half-million-year claim. The test alone did not yet solve everything.

In 1953, Oakley, Joseph Weiner and Wilfrid Le Gros Clark examined the material more closely. They found that parts had been stained to look alike. The jaw's surface had been treated, but its interior told a different story. And under magnification, the teeth showed scratches that natural chewing could not explain.

Someone had filed them down.

The skull fragments came from humans. The jaw came from an orangutan. They were never one ancient individual. What looked like an astonishing evolutionary discovery was an arrangement of altered remains.

But who had put it together? That question stayed open long after the hoax was exposed.

In 2016, researchers went back to the specimens with DNA analysis and high-resolution imaging. Teeth recovered from the two Piltdown locations appeared to come from one orangutan. Similar fillers, gravel and staining connected the different fakes to one working method.

The evidence pointed strongly toward Dawson, the man present at the discoveries. It did not prove every detail of his role or establish whether he acted alone. The motive is still uncertain.

There is another detail worth keeping. Not every scientist was fooled. Critics had noticed the mismatch early on. What the fraud exposed was not a world without sceptics, but a world in which a tempting answer could drown out sceptical voices.

The museum eventually displayed the objects as evidence of a fraud, not evidence of an ancestor. A jaw that had once seemed to explain human origins now explained something else: how easily people can believe a discovery when its shape matches the story they want to hear.
