Would you book dinner at a restaurant that had never served anyone?

In London, thousands of people trusted a restaurant called The Shed at Dulwich. It climbed to number one on TripAdvisor. Its owner had no kitchen, no chef, and no restaurant. He lived in a garden shed.

In 2017, writer Oobah Butler decided to test how far a made-up restaurant could go. He bought a cheap phone, built a website, and listed only his road as the address. The Shed was supposedly so exclusive you could visit by appointment alone.

Its menu offered moods instead of normal dishes. The appetizing photos were even stranger: shaving foam, painted sponges, and an egg resting on Butler's foot. None of it was edible.

Friends and acquaintances posted false reviews. In May, TripAdvisor approved the listing, placing The Shed last among more than eighteen thousand London restaurants. But the ranking began to climb. Then somebody phoned asking for dinner.

Butler told callers the restaurant was fully booked. People kept calling. By November, the place that had never served a customer had reached number one.

That could have been the ending. Instead, Butler invited genuine diners to his shed for a single evening. Friends posed as delighted customers. A DJ played restaurant noises to cover the microwave. Guests received dressed-up ready meals. They weren't charged.

The trick didn't convince everyone. One visiting couple left looking distinctly unhappy, according to Butler's account. But another couple genuinely asked how to book a table there again.

The original ranking was manufactured, not a fair restaurant review. Still, that last request was real: at least some guests wanted another night in a garden.
