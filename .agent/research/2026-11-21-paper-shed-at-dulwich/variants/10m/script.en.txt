What would it take to make a garden shed London's best restaurant? Could a website full of imaginary meals defeat thousands of real kitchens? And if it did, would anybody still believe the illusion after sitting down to dinner?

These are not hypothetical questions. In 2017, a freelance writer called Oobah Butler set out to make a restaurant that did not exist reach the top of TripAdvisor.

Butler was not completely unfamiliar with the review business. He had previously earned money writing positive online reviews for restaurants he hadn't visited. That work made him curious about the power of a star rating. If strangers were willing to trust somebody's account of a meal that never happened, perhaps they would trust an entire dining room that wasn't there.

His raw material was a shed in a garden in Dulwich, south London. He gave it a suitably serious name: The Shed at Dulwich.

Before he could climb any rankings, he had to get listed. TripAdvisor wanted contact details. Butler bought a cheap phone for bookings. He created a website. And when it came to the address, he deliberately omitted a house number. People would be told that the restaurant was available by appointment only. It sounded exclusive. It also meant curious customers could not simply walk to the door and check.

Then came the menu. A normal restaurant might advertise steak, soup, or fish. The Shed offered dishes organized by moods. And a restaurant needed photos, even if it had no actual dishes to photograph.

Look closely at the results and you would not want to take a bite. One plate was a sponge covered in paint, accompanied by shaving foam shaped like a chef's garnish. Another supposedly appetizing composition included an egg resting on Butler's own foot. Careful framing made these props look like expensive food.

None of this meant he could cook a meal. That was the point: the pictures and the language needed to sell an expectation, not feed anyone.

On May fifth, 2017, TripAdvisor approved the listing. The Shed appeared among London's restaurants at number 18,149. It was last.

That gave Butler a clear target. He contacted friends and acquaintances and asked them to publish favorable accounts of invented visits. The fake reviews began doing their work. In the first few weeks, The Shed broke into the top ten thousand. It kept climbing.

Then the phone rang.

Someone had heard of the supposedly exclusive restaurant and wanted a table that evening. Butler responded that it was booked for the next six weeks. Instead of ending the experiment, the call revealed something new. The restaurant was no longer just an entry in a database. People were planning real nights out around it.

Another caller wanted a table for a seventieth birthday. Others sent appointment requests by email. Butler kept declining them. Every rejection made the imagined restaurant harder to reach, and the absence of an exact address became part of the mystery rather than an obvious warning.

By the end of August, The Shed had reached number 156.

The effects spilled beyond the review website. Suppliers tried to send samples to its approximate location. People asked about employment. Butler said the local council approached him about relocating to a development site. A public-relations agency pitched ideas for promoting the place. Each approach assumed the business existed. None required anyone to have seen its kitchen.

Butler's online performance was changing the behavior of people who weren't part of his original group of reviewers. He had created an awkward practical problem for himself: every serious inquiry needed a new excuse.

At number thirty, the ranking seemed to stop climbing. He continued his campaign. And then a message from TripAdvisor landed in his inbox with a request for information.

It could have been the moment the platform exposed him. But the message, as Butler recounted it, concerned a surge in attention. It reported 89,000 search appearances in a single day.

On November first, his garden shed became the number-one ranked restaurant in London on TripAdvisor.

The victory looked absurd when you placed the two images side by side. On one screen was a ranking ahead of thousands of places with staff, kitchens, and actual paying customers. In the garden stood a modest shed that had not served a single dinner.

Butler kept the listing at the top for roughly two weeks, according to his account. The phone did not stop ringing. When he left it with a friend for a long weekend, it came back with 116 missed calls.

He now had a choice. He could expose the trick and end his story at number one. Or he could find out what happened when the people who believed the ranking finally came for a meal.

He decided to open for one night.

There wasn't a dining room to prepare. He arranged tables in the chilly garden, borrowed equipment, and brought in heaters. Because earlier reviews had described an intimate, rustic place, he tried to create one. He even arranged for chickens in a small house, a piece of theatrical farm-to-table atmosphere.

He asked friends to play diners who loved everything. A friend called Joe, who had worked in fine restaurants, helped prepare the food. The ingredients were not from a fine dining supplier. Butler said the shopping cost thirty-one pounds. The meals came from the supermarket, then received careful finishing touches.

The menu still described moods. The room still needed the sound of a restaurant. So a DJ played restaurant ambience and sound effects that helped cover the actual microwave. A waitress was asked to collect guests' opinions privately at the end.

Notice what Butler had built. The website had first supplied a fictional atmosphere for nonexistent meals. Now he was building a real setting to imitate the online atmosphere. The fake descriptions had become instructions for an actual event.

The first real diners included a couple visiting from California. According to Butler's first-person account, they were met outside, blindfolded, and led to the garden. When the blindfolds came off, they looked around in silence.

They were served the dressed-up ready meals. At one point the woman lifted her phone as if to photograph the food, then put it away. The couple departed after a quiet, uncomfortable visit. Butler described the man as visibly angry. The experiment had not fooled everyone into a happy evening.

Another group of guests encountered the chicken display. One of them believed she had found London's highest-rated vegetarian restaurant. The presence of chickens, offered as an absurd dining gimmick, collided with her expectation. It is difficult to create a flawless illusion when real customers bring their own questions.

The evening carried on. Some diners appeared to enjoy the food. Butler arranged a birthday song for one group. Meanwhile, the actress-diners and atmospheric sound kept up the appearance of a busy, satisfied restaurant.

At the end, Butler apologized to a real couple for the confusion and makeshift arrangements. He expected them to leave.

Instead they asked whether, having been there once, they might be able to get another booking more easily. They wanted to come back.

That was a different surprise from reaching number one. The ranking had been manipulated by false reviews. A request from someone who had actually eaten there was not a forged online testimonial. Yet it still did not prove that the food was wonderful.

Butler hadn't charged the diners for the meal, explaining that the evening was being documented. Other guests were actors, and the whole event had been staged to influence the atmosphere. One dissatisfied couple was no less real than the couple who wanted a second visit. This was a stunt, not a controlled restaurant-quality experiment.

TripAdvisor's own response offered another important distinction. The company said its detection methods were chiefly designed to catch dishonest reviews intended to benefit real businesses. A journalist inventing a whole restaurant was a different kind of attack, and, the company argued, not a normal business incentive. Its explanation did not erase what had happened, but it prevents a simple claim that every restaurant ranking works the same way.

The listing was ultimately removed. Butler published his account in December 2017. The original images became evidence of how the prank was constructed; some screenshots in the first publication were recreated for legal reasons and later corrected so the visible review counts matched. It would be misleading to show those recreations as independent archival captures.

The story also traveled well beyond restaurant websites. In April 2018, VICE reported that Singaporean lawmakers discussed the case during a hearing about online falsehoods. Photographs of the bizarre menu and the non-existent restaurant were useful precisely because the trick was so easy to understand.

There were now two stories competing with each other. One was about a review system that could be pushed to the top with coordinated false testimony. The other was about a night in a garden where human expectations influenced what visitors thought they were experiencing.

The first story ends when the fake listing is discovered. The second ends with a man asking for another reservation.

Neither means that every review is fraudulent or that people cannot recognize bad food. One group left unhappy, and the account comes mainly from the person who staged the whole event. What we can say is narrower, and stranger: a shed became London's top-rated restaurant without feeding anyone, and after it finally served supermarket meals, some real diners still wanted to come back.

The rating was fictional. The evening happened. And the second booking request was the part Butler couldn't simply manufacture with another online review.
