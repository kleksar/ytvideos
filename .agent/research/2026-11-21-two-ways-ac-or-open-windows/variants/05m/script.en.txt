Would I save fuel by opening the windows instead of using my car's air conditioning? And is there really a magic speed where the answer flips?

I'm in a hot car, with two ways to make the trip bearable. For A, I roll the windows down and leave the AC off. For B, I close the windows and use the AC. I want the same car, the same distance and the same speed. I'm judging fuel used per distance, not which ride feels nicer.

Here's the complication: I haven't taken two cars onto a track. This is a comparison of published measurements. An animation can show the choices, but it can't manufacture a measured fuel bill.

Windows seem cheaper because they don't require an engine-driven compressor. That sounds convincing until I picture the car moving. The air has to flow around a body designed, in most cases, to run with its windows closed. Opening them can disturb that flow. At greater speed, the drag penalty becomes more important.

AC has a different cost. Its compressor does work to move heat out of the cabin. If I demand maximum cooling, it can consume substantially more energy than when the cabin is already comfortable. So my contest is really between two changing loads, not between one free option and one expensive option.

This is where an actual comparison is more useful than a rule passed around among drivers.

In research published in 2013, Oak Ridge National Laboratory compared a 2009 Toyota Corolla with a 2009 Ford Explorer. The team gathered data on the road and in a laboratory, using different cooling settings and windows-open conditions. One of its comparisons kept the AC at maximum cooling.

I would expect a neat result: open windows win in the city, AC wins on any highway. But that isn't what the high-load test found.

From forty through seventy miles an hour, both vehicles burned more fuel with maximum AC than with the windows open. At seventy-five, the Corolla's results were approximately level. At eighty, the Corolla finally used more fuel with its windows open. The Explorer did not show the same crossing in the measured range.

That means one often-repeated number cannot be the exact changeover speed for all cars. Even these two vehicles didn't behave alike.

Now I can make the comparison more realistic without secretly changing the original result. A maximum-cooling compressor is the hard case for the AC. Once the cabin reaches a tolerable temperature, it may not need to work flat out. Oak Ridge also investigated varying AC settings; the study was not a claim that every drive runs the compressor at full load.

The US Department of Energy and Environmental Protection Agency's FuelEconomy.gov guidance still recommends windows down at lower speeds and AC at highway speeds. That's practical advice for ordinary driving, not a promise that there is one universal crossover at a precise number on my speedometer.

Another layer matters before I even move the car. If it has been baking in the sun, the cabin may be much hotter than the outside air. Letting that trapped hot air escape briefly can reduce the initial cooling work. FuelEconomy.gov recommends exactly that: air out the cabin before asking the AC to cool it.

But I shouldn't quietly mark that as a third competitor. For my original A/B, either I use windows for the drive, or I use the AC for the drive. A short airing-out step is a useful practical follow-up, not an outcome that was measured in this comparison.

And fuel isn't my only concern. AC can remove moisture from the air, which can help me keep the windows clear and stay alert. If the outside air is scorching, dusty, or humid, an open window may not provide the same comfort at all. I can't call one method superior at cooling if all I measured was fuel.

So what do I choose? For those two older test vehicles, at forty to seventy miles an hour with the AC at maximum, the windows were the fuel-saving option. At eighty, the Corolla changed sides. At normal AC demand in another vehicle, I need its own evidence. My actual trip may not reproduce either result.

The payoff isn't a secret highway speed. It's knowing which two costs are competing and why the supposedly simple answer changes.

Next time I climb into a hot car, before I pretend I know the winner, I have to ask the same thing: windows or AC?
