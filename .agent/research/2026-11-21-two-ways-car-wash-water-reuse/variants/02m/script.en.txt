A garden hose can use a hundred gallons while I wash one car. So why would a car wash with dozens of spraying nozzles sometimes need less fresh water?

I have one dirty car and two choices. A: wash it on my driveway. B: drive through a conveyor wash that reuses water. My score is gallons of new water drawn for one completed wash, not how dramatic the spray looks. I haven't run this experiment; I'm comparing published figures and clearly labeled examples.

A state environment department estimates a typical garden hose at ten gallons per minute. Leave it flowing for ten minutes and A uses a hundred gallons. In one metered study, six conveyor washes with recycling systems used about thirty gallons of fresh water per car on average. In that example, B wins by seventy gallons.

There's the trick: much of the water spraying in a recycling wash has already been collected and used before. The spray is not the same thing as fresh water drawn.

But I can change A without buying a machine. I fit a shutoff nozzle, use a bucket and let the hose run only when rinsing. A state guide gives about thirty gallons for that sort of home wash. Now A and the thirty-gallon commercial example are level.

And an individual fact-checking team's careful home wash used just eleven gallons. That's a separate car, not a controlled head-to-head test. It proves I cannot say a commercial wash always wins.

The water has another destination, too. Runoff on a driveway can reach a storm drain; a properly connected commercial wash sends wastewater for treatment.

So my choice depends on whether the business actually reuses water, and whether I leave my own tap running. If I forget to turn it off for ten minutes, the first number comes straight back: a hundred gallons for one car.
