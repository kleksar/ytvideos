Do I take a new plastic bag, or buy a cotton tote?

I'm at the grocery checkout. I want to carry the same groceries home every week, and I'm trying to choose the option with the lower climate footprint.

Option A: one fresh, lightweight plastic bag for each trip. Option B: I buy one cotton tote today and keep using it. Same shopping load. One trip a week. I'm comparing a published life-cycle model, not pretending I measured emissions in a store.

My first instinct is the tote. But growing cotton, making fabric, and sewing a stronger bag all take resources before I carry a single apple.

In a British environmental study, the cotton tote needed about 131 shopping trips to beat a lightweight plastic bag used once, on greenhouse-gas impact.

After my first year, I've made fifty-two trips. Under those assumptions, A is still ahead. The cotton bag hasn't earned back its larger starting footprint.

After three years, I've made 156 trips. Now B pulls ahead. One tote has replaced 156 new plastic bags.

Except I've been hiding a perfectly ordinary habit: what if I use some of those plastic bags as bin liners instead of buying separate liners? The study's more realistic scenario moves the cotton break-even point to about 173 trips. At year three, the winner flips back to A.

So neither bag wins just because of its material. The answer depends on how often I actually reuse it, and what happens to the other bag afterward. This is about climate impact, not plastic litter, which that study didn't measure.
