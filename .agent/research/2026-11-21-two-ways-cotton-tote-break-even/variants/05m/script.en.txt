How can a new cotton shopping bag lose to a pile of disposable plastic bags on climate impact? How many trips would it take to change that? And what happens if I use those plastic bags again?

I'm standing at the grocery checkout. I've decided to shop once a week, bringing home the same load each time. I can accept a new, thin plastic bag on every visit, or I can buy one cotton tote and bring it back every week.

I'll call the plastic bags A and the cotton tote B. My score is the greenhouse-gas impact of supplying bags for those trips, not their price, appearance, or the damage caused by litter. I can't measure that impact by watching a shopping bag. I'm comparing two choices using a published life-cycle assessment of bags sold in British supermarkets. The report studied the products available in 2006, so the result is a model for those assumptions, not a universal number for every bag today.

Before I make my first trip, B has a problem. A thin plastic carrier uses relatively little material. Making a cotton tote starts with growing cotton, processing fibre, weaving fabric, and manufacturing a sturdier product. The tote can survive many shopping trips, but it begins with a much larger manufacturing footprint.

So the first plastic bag wins my climate comparison. And the second. Buying the tote only makes sense if I really keep using that same bag.

Here's the number that changes the story. In the British study, a cotton tote needed roughly 131 shopping uses before its greenhouse-gas impact became lower than that of conventional thin plastic bags that were used only once.

I reach the end of my first year with fifty-two supermarket visits. If I bought B, it's only been out fifty-two times. Under that study's conditions, the pile of fifty-two plastic bags in A has a smaller climate footprint than the new cotton tote. That's a counterintuitive result, but it isn't a recommendation to litter plastic. Litter wasn't included in that calculation.

The comparison looks different if I actually keep the bag. At one trip a week, two years give me 104 uses. Not quite enough. Three years give me 156. Now B moves ahead, because I bought one cotton tote rather than 156 new plastic bags.

I could stop here, hold up the cotton tote, and declare victory. But there's something missing from my plastic pile.

At home, I sometimes line a small rubbish bin with a grocery bag. If I take B to the supermarket, I may have to buy separate bin liners. If I take A, a portion of those bags can do two jobs. The original study tested exactly this kind of secondary reuse.

In one scenario, about forty percent of the thin plastic bags were reused as bin liners. That pushes the cotton bag's climate break-even point from roughly 131 uses to about 173.

My three-year result changes. I've made 156 trips, which is past the first threshold but short of the second. With that bin-liner assumption, A still has the lower modeled impact. By four years, at 208 trips with the same cotton tote, B passes even that threshold.

It's not a contradiction. I changed what happens to the plastic bags after I carry groceries home. When one of them replaces a separate bin liner, its second job matters to the comparison.

Now think about what happens if I buy cotton totes faster than I use them. Suppose I buy a new one because I forgot the old one, or because a shop printed a nice design on it. I haven't increased the number of shopping trips. I've increased the number of cotton bags that needed making. That isn't a result from an observed experiment here; it's a consequence of adding more production to this model.

And here's the most useful distinction. If the cotton bag is already in my kitchen drawer, its production happened in the past. Buying another new tote doesn't undo it. For my next shopping trip, using the bag I already own avoids another bag purchase. That's a different decision from choosing which new bag to manufacture for a plan of future trips.

There are other limits. The British results describe a particular time, set of bags, and assumptions about rubbish disposal. Cotton's water and land demands are not the same metric as warming. Plastic escaping into rivers is not captured by a climate-only scoreboard. I can't honestly stamp either material with one permanent green label.

My answer is conditional, but it is complete. For a newly bought cotton tote and fresh plastic bags, the cotton option needs repeated use over years to win this particular climate comparison. Secondary reuse of the plastic bags moves the finish line. What I already own is another matter.

I put my existing shopping bag beside the front door so I actually remember it. Next week, when I reach the checkout, I will ask the same question with a much clearer idea of what I'm measuring.
