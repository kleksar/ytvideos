If I replace my plastic grocery bags with a new cotton tote, how many trips does it take before I've actually cut the climate impact? Why can the answer move by more than a hundred trips? And should I buy the tote at all if I already have a bag at home?

I'm at a supermarket checkout with enough groceries to fill a carrier. I have two choices in front of me.

With A, I take a new, lightweight plastic bag on every visit. With B, I buy one cotton tote, and from then on I bring that same tote with me. I'm shopping once a week. I need to carry the same load either way.

A will leave me with a growing pile of plastic bags. B will leave me with a single bag, hopefully for years. The question sounds easy until I decide what I'm measuring.

For this comparison, I want the greenhouse-gas impact of making, using, and disposing of the bags that serve those shopping trips. That's one environmental measure, not a complete score for every type of harm. I'm not recording a real experiment or watching a machine measure emissions. I'm applying scenarios from a life-cycle assessment commissioned by Britain's Environment Agency. It examined supermarket bags available in 2006, and it reported in 2011. I'll stay inside those limits rather than pretend that one number describes every country and every modern bag.

I try to decide before paying. The thin plastic carrier looks flimsy, and the cotton tote feels like something I could keep indefinitely. I expect B to win.

But what happens before I get to this checkout matters. Making a light plastic carrier uses a small amount of material. A cotton tote requires cotton growing, fibre processing, fabric production, and bag manufacture. All of that happens before I put my first shopping item inside it.

A new cotton tote has a larger starting climate footprint than one of the thin plastic carriers in this study. Its advantage is not that it appeared for free. Its advantage is that I can spread that footprint across a long sequence of shopping trips.

So I start the clock at my first weekly visit. For A, another new thin bag enters the count each week. For B, the cotton bag has already been made, and I bring back the same one. My comparison assumes the tote survives and I actually remember to use it.

At the second visit, plastic has accumulated two bags. Cotton is still one. At the tenth, the same thing. That doesn't prove either choice is harmless; it sets up the trade-off. Which grows more important: the plastic pile, or the one-time impact of the cotton tote?

The British study gives me a reference point. Against conventional thin plastic bags used once and not given a second job, the cotton bag needed roughly 131 shopping uses to have the lower global-warming impact.

I'm shopping once a week, so I can put the report onto my calendar. At the end of year one, I've made fifty-two trips. The cotton tote has been reused fifty-two times, far short of the study's threshold. On this particular climate metric, A still wins.

At the end of year two, I have 104 visits. B is catching up, but it still has not reached the threshold.

After 131 weekly visits, about two and a half years, my tote reaches the point described by the study. The score doesn't change because cotton suddenly becomes a different material. It changes because I avoided buying enough new bags to outweigh the larger impact of making this one.

By the end of year three, I have 156 trips. With those original assumptions, B now wins. I've kept one cotton tote in service instead of accepting 156 new plastic carriers. The number 156 is my shopping schedule; it isn't a number measured in that historical study.

I nearly stop there. I'd have a neat winner, a satisfying line crossing another line, and an excuse to feel good about my purchase. Then I notice something on the kitchen counter: a plastic grocery bag folded next to the rubbish bin.

In my house, a thin bag might be used twice. I bring food home in it. Later, I put it inside a small bin. If I didn't have that grocery bag, I might buy a separate bin liner.

That second use matters because A no longer represents a product thrown away as soon as the groceries come out. It has replaced another item. B, meanwhile, hasn't stopped my bin from needing a liner.

The Environment Agency's assessment tested scenarios for exactly this difference. When about forty percent of thin plastic bags are reused as bin liners, the cotton bag's climate break-even moves from about 131 uses to around 173.

Look at my calendar again. End of year three: 156 visits. Under the first assumption, B has already won. Under the assumption that some plastic bags replace separate bin liners, A is still ahead. Same person, same food, same shopping frequency, same cotton tote. The only change is the second life of the plastic bags.

If I keep B for another year, I reach 208 shopping trips. That's above 173, so the cotton tote finally wins this modeled climate comparison even when some of A's plastic bags get a second job. The choice didn't become impossible. It just required more repeated use.

And the report explored more extreme assumptions. If every thin plastic bag replaced a separate bin liner, the cotton break-even point rose to about 327 shopping uses. If the thin plastic carrier itself was used for three shopping trips, the model's comparison with cotton could require roughly 393 uses of the tote. Those are different scenarios, not the rules I secretly switched into A halfway through my original comparison.

At one visit per week, 327 trips are more than six years. That's why I refuse to present 131 as a universal expiration date for the problem. The threshold belongs to a particular bag, a particular disposal system, and a particular alternative.

There's another trap in my closet. I could keep buying cotton totes because they have funny prints, or because a shop gives them away, or because I left the usual bag at home. If I own five totes but shop only once each week, I still have fifty-two shopping trips in a year. The trips don't multiply because I bought more bags. The manufacturing does.

Imagine I alternate equally between the five bags. Each tote gets around ten or eleven outings in that year. I'm not applying a new scientific result to this imaginary cupboard; I'm pointing out that a pile of reusable bags is not the same as repeated use of one reusable bag.

A bag that says 'REUSE ME' cannot guarantee that I will. I need to bring it out of the house. If I keep buying more, their individual starting footprints don't vanish.

I can make the comparison fairer by asking what I am actually about to buy. Do I need a brand-new cotton tote to carry the groceries? Or do I already own a bag that works?

Those are different decisions. The assessment's break-even table asks about producing an alternative bag and using it repeatedly instead of producing new plastic bags. But if there's already a sound tote on my hook, the emissions from making it happened in the past. Buying yet another tote doesn't erase them. Taking the one I own on my next shopping trip avoids another purchase. I don't need to win an imaginary contest for a new cotton bag before I can reuse an existing one.

And the material label isn't the whole environmental argument. My score was global-warming impact. Cotton production also uses water and land. Thin plastic bags can escape into waterways or become persistent litter. That type of damage was not measured in the UK report's shopping-bag climate result. A lower climate footprint is not permission to let plastic loose in the environment.

The report itself was about British supermarket products from 2006, not an experiment at my checkout today. Energy sources, bag weights, whether bags hold the same volume, recycling facilities, and what people do after shopping can change real comparisons. I wouldn't take the 131 figure to a different bag or country and announce it as a law of nature.

So where does that leave A and B?

If I am buying a new cotton tote, and my alternative is a new thin plastic carrier every week that gets no second use, I need a long commitment to the same tote for B to win this particular climate model. At 52 trips, A wins; at 156, B wins. Give some of A's bags a useful second life as bin liners, and the finish line shifts: 156 trips no longer suffice, but 208 exceed the modeled 173-use threshold.

If I already own a sturdy bag, I don't need to manufacture another one for a fresh comparison. I'll take the one I have. And if I end up using a plastic bag, I can keep it in circulation rather than treating it as instantly disposable.

Before leaving the kitchen for next week's shop, I hang my existing bag by the door. That's the part of this problem I can actually control: not a perfect material, but what I buy, what I already own, and how often I truly reuse it.

Then I reach the checkout again, and the same two choices are waiting for me.
