Why can dark mode barely change my battery indoors, yet make a much bigger difference outdoors? If I have to choose between a bright screen and a dark one, which choice actually saves power? And does that answer hold for every phone?

I start with an ordinary moment. I am reading an article on my phone at home. I can keep the page white with dark letters, or switch to a dark background with light letters. Both let me read the same words. My metric is the electrical power used by the whole phone while I do the same task at the same brightness.

Call the white page A and the dark page B. I want B to win. A black background looks as if the display has stopped working quite so hard.

On an OLED phone, that instinct has a real physical basis. Every pixel produces its own light. Darker pixels generally need less power. An older-style LCD is different: a backlight shines through the display even when the picture is dark. So I need to know what kind of screen I am holding before I can make a useful comparison.

But knowing that OLED pixels save electricity still doesn't tell me what happens to the entire phone. I am not turning off the processor when I change themes. The phone may still be searching for a signal, running an app and doing all the other things it did with the white page.

Here is the first surprise. In a Purdue University study, researchers compared dark and light interfaces across six popular Android apps and four OLED phones. They looked at sixty seconds of activity at different brightness settings. At normal indoor levels, roughly thirty to fifty percent brightness, switching to dark mode saved an average of just three to nine percent of total phone power.

That is a win for B. But it is a much smaller win than the almost-black screen led me to expect. I can't translate a six-percent reduction in power during one activity into six percent more battery life for my whole day. The things I do, and how long my screen stays on, matter.

So I change only the setting that matters for the next question: brightness. Imagine I leave the house and need to read my phone in strong sunlight. I don't change my message or my task, but the screen is now much brighter. In Purdue's tests at maximum brightness, dark mode saved an average of thirty-nine to forty-seven percent of phone power compared with light mode.

The winner hasn't changed. B still uses less. What changes is the size of the advantage. At low brightness, there is less display power to save in the first place. At high brightness, the bright pixels cost far more, so making them dark has more room to help.

That sounds like a simple rule: use dark mode outside. But there's a second surprise hidden inside the word 'app'.

Another published comparison from Mobile Enerlytics used a Nexus 6 with an OLED screen at thirty-eight percent brightness. The testers repeated their activities with dark and light interfaces. In that setup, reading news saved much more power than watching a full-screen video. They reported twenty-three point five percent total energy savings for one news-reading task, but just one point eight percent for a YouTube full-screen playback task.

Why the difference? If my app fills most of the screen with video, changing the surrounding menus hardly changes what the pixels show. If I'm reading a page whose background changes from white to black, far more of the picture changes. Dark mode is an interface choice; it doesn't magically darken a video I am watching.

Those figures came from a different device and method, so I don't average them together with Purdue's results. They are useful for a different reason: they show why two people can make the same choice and see very different battery results.

I look at another comparison that helps me understand the brightness question. Purdue reported that, on a Pixel 5 in Google News, light mode at twenty percent brightness used about the same power as dark mode at fifty percent brightness. That isn't a universal exchange rate. It shows that brightness and theme interact, rather than behaving like two separate switches.

So if I want a fair A/B test for myself, I keep the phone, app, activity and brightness identical. Otherwise I might congratulate dark mode for a saving that actually came from dimming the screen. And if I have an LCD phone, I should not expect the OLED result at all.

I started with a yes-or-no question. The useful answer turns out to have three parts: OLED or LCD, indoor or maximum brightness, and how much of the app really changes colour.

For my OLED phone indoors, I would choose whichever theme is comfortable. When I am outside with the brightness high, B becomes a more compelling battery choice. And before I make any sweeping claim about how long the battery will last, I remember these were measured tasks, not a promise for every day of my life.

The practical win is not simply a black screen. It's knowing when that black screen changes the power bill of my phone.
