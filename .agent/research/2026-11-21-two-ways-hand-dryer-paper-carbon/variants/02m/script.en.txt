I have two ways to dry my hands: a warm-air dryer or paper towels. Which one has the smaller carbon footprint?

Before I choose, I need one rule. I'm comparing a deliberately limited carbon model, not a real experiment or a complete environmental assessment.

Both options dry the same number of pairs of hands in one restroom over five years. I count three new paper towels per visit. For the dryer, I count making the machine and the electricity it uses. I don't count paper disposal or delivery, so this is a partial comparison.

The first surprise is the machine itself. In the published model I'm using, manufacturing it accounts for 113 kilograms of carbon-dioxide equivalent before anyone dries their hands.

Now picture a quiet restroom: five uses per day, 250 days a year. Over five years, my paper-towel manufacturing total is about 112 kilograms. The dryer, including electricity, reaches about 130. On this limited scorecard, paper wins.

But I keep the same two methods and change only the number of visits. Fifty uses a day. Now the paper adds up to more than 1,100 kilograms, while the dryer comes to about 286. Air wins by a wide margin.

Nothing magical happened to the dryer. Its manufacturing burden is being shared across many more uses. The paper burden arrives again with every pair of hands.

This does not prove that paper is greener in every quiet bathroom. Waste treatment, paper type and the power grid can change the answer. A newer full life-cycle assessment generally favors efficient electric dryers.

So if I have to choose for a real restroom, I first ask how often it will be used. That's the missing number when I stand between those two choices again.
