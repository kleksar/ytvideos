Which is better for the environment: drying my hands with an electric dryer, or pulling paper towels from a dispenser?

I can see the paper disappearing into the bin. The dryer leaves no crumpled pile. But I cannot see the electricity flowing into the wall, or the emissions from making the machine. So I won't decide by looking at the bin.

I'm going to compare the same task in two ways. One pair of wet hands gets dried per visit. Option A uses three new virgin-paper towels. Option B uses one electric dryer for twenty-one seconds. My score is the carbon dioxide equivalent counted across five years of use in a public restroom.

These are calculations with published factors, not two measurements I've just made. And they are intentionally limited. I count the manufacturing of the towels on one side. On the other, I count the manufacturing of the dryer and its electricity. Paper transport, waste treatment, cleaning, repairs and standby electricity are outside this particular score. That means I can compare the behavior of the model, but not claim a complete environmental winner.

Before I show the numbers, pick a side. Would the option that produces visible waste lose? Or would the machine's hidden cost matter more?

First I need to buy the dryer. In the published analysis, manufacturing a suitable machine contributes 113 kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent. That's a fixed cost in my carbon ledger, even if only one person ever uses it.

Then I switch it on. At 1,600 watts for twenty-one seconds, each dry uses just over nine watt-hours. Using the historical grid factor in the study, that is about 2.76 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per use. The electricity is the part that grows with every visitor.

On the paper side, the study cites about 5.96 grams of manufacturing emissions for each virgin towel. Three towels make 17.88 grams per visit. I do not pretend that this includes the collection of the used towels or the trip to a landfill.

Now I open a very quiet restroom. Only five pairs of hands are dried each working day. It operates 250 days a year for five years. That's 6,250 dries, regardless of which option I install.

With paper, I multiply 6,250 visits by 17.88 grams. About 112 kilograms.

With air, I start at the 113 kilograms it took to make the dryer. Then I add electricity for those 6,250 visits. That brings the total to about 130 kilograms.

The surprising result: within this limited manufacturing-and-electricity ledger, the bin full of paper scores lower than the machine that produces no paper waste.

But why? The dryer didn't break. It simply spent most of those five years waiting for somebody to use it. I had already charged the model for making the machine.

Now I replay the exact same comparison in a busy restroom. Same five years. Same towels. Same dryer. Same electricity factor. I change only one input: fifty dries per day instead of five.

The paper number grows with every visitor. After five years, its manufacturing figure passes 1,100 kilograms.

The dryer still begins with the same 113 kilograms. It has more running time, so electricity rises, but the whole figure is only about 286 kilograms.

The winner flips. One purchase now serves ten times as many people. The fixed manufacturing burden is spread across many more dries, while the paper dispenser consumes new material each time.

Where does the model cross over? With these exact assumptions, at roughly six dries per working day. Below that, the fixed dryer cost dominates this limited ledger; above it, repeat paper production dominates. That is a mathematical threshold, not a universal rule about public restrooms.

To see why, I change one other assumption, but only after finishing the first fair comparison. Suppose I take just two towels instead of three. The paper total per visit falls from 17.88 to 11.92 grams. In this same simplified accounting, the crossover moves toward ten daily uses. The amount of paper I actually pull matters.

Electricity matters too. A cleaner grid makes the dryer side lighter; a more carbon-intensive grid makes it heavier. And if I keep a dryer longer than five years, its original manufacturing emissions are shared across more visits. Those are reasons not to paste one magic number onto every bathroom wall.

This also is not a hygiene contest. A review of the hygiene literature found mixed and inconsistent evidence; the carbon result cannot tell me which method is healthier for a specific restroom.

And I should check my simplified numbers against broader research. A 2015 life-cycle assessment found the conventional dryer performed better in three of four impact categories under its assumptions. A 2026 update from CE Delft generally ranks electric dryers best on environmental impact, especially efficient models with clean electricity. Those studies measure more than my deliberately narrow scorecard. My small-restroom result should not be mistaken for their overall conclusion.

So what would I do? If I'm deciding for a real building, I need visitor counts, the actual dryer power and drying time, the paper type and number of sheets, the local power supply, and waste handling. Then I can run the same rules with real inputs.

I started with one pair of wet hands and an easy choice between paper and air. The number that changed the outcome wasn't printed on either dispenser. It was the number of hands that came after mine.
