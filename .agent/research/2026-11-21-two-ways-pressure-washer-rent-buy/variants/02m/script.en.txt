I have one dirty patio and two ways to clean it: rent an electric pressure washer, or buy one.

A rental listing gives me a one-day price of about thirty-five dollars. For my comparison, I put a similar-capability washer on the buying side at a hypothetical two hundred and ten dollars. I am not pretending I tested either machine. This is a cost model, and I assume both finish the same job.

For one Saturday, renting wins easily. Thirty-five dollars, against two hundred and ten upfront. I also set aside five dollars for wear and upkeep every time I use my own machine. I leave water and power out because the model treats them as equal.

But my patio gets dirty again. At five cleanups, the rental fees total one hundred and seventy-five dollars. Owning costs two hundred and thirty-five. Still cheaper to rent.

At seven cleanups, they meet: two hundred and forty-five dollars each. At eight, renting reaches two hundred and eighty, while owning reaches two hundred and fifty. Buying finally wins.

Then I remember the trip to collect and return a rental. Suppose that round trip costs me eight dollars every time, while buying takes one such trip. Now five rentals cost two hundred and fifteen dollars, compared with two hundred and forty-three to own. At six cleanups, rent costs two hundred and fifty-eight, but own costs two hundred and forty-eight. The winner changes a cleanup earlier.

Those travel dollars are my example, not a measured bill. Storage, repairs, rental terms and actual cleaning performance could move the answer again.

So I cannot choose by the price tag alone. If I only know about this weekend, the choice starts with the same situation I faced at the beginning: one dirty patio, and two ways to clean it.
