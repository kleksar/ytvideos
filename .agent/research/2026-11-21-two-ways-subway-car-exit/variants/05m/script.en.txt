Which subway car gets me out of the station first? The closest door, or the door beside my exit at the other end? And what if the train arrives before I finish walking?

I reach the platform with the entrance right behind me. I'm going to another station where the staircase I need is near the far end of the train. I could stand here and wait. Or I could start moving down the platform now.

I'll compare the two choices using a deliberately simple model. I'm not claiming I've measured any subway system. The platform is one hundred and eighty metres long. The train stops in the same place in both versions. I walk at one and a half metres per second. The train ride lasts exactly five minutes, and the destination stairs are at the far end.

Method A is simple: I board at the entrance. At my destination, I have to walk all one hundred and eighty metres to the stairs. That takes one hundred and twenty seconds.

Method B looks clever. Before the train arrives, I walk one hundred and fifty metres toward the last car. When I get off, I'm only thirty metres from the stairs. Twenty seconds of walking instead of two whole minutes.

But B has a hidden cost. Walking to that car takes one hundred seconds. Am I really saving time, or just moving my walk to another station?

Let's make the first train a little way off. It's due at two minutes and thirty seconds, and leaves fifteen seconds later. I can reach the better door in one minute and forty seconds. So I walk there, then wait. There's no extra train to catch and no sprint for the doors.

I follow the whole journey on two clocks. With A, I stand near the entrance until departure, ride for five minutes, then walk for two minutes. From the moment I enter the platform to the staircase, that's nine minutes and forty-five seconds.

With B, I spend one minute and forty seconds walking before the train comes. But that walk happens while I would otherwise be waiting. I catch the exact same train, ride the same five minutes, and walk twenty seconds at the other end. My clock stops at eight minutes and five seconds.

B saves one minute and forty seconds. There is no magic shortcut through the station. In either case I walk a total of one hundred and eighty metres. I simply used my waiting time for the part of that walk that could be done early.

Now comes the important test. Suppose I step onto that same platform on a different day. The train is already approaching. It arrives in one minute and departs fifteen seconds later. Nothing else has changed: not the station, not the two doors, not my speed.

A boards immediately. Its clock ends at eight minutes and fifteen seconds: seventy-five seconds before departure, five minutes on the train and two minutes after it.

If I stubbornly follow B, I haven't covered the full one hundred and fifty metres before the doors close. I don't board. In this model the next train departs four minutes after the first one. B now arrives at the stairs in ten minutes and thirty-five seconds. Its final twenty-second walk cannot recover four minutes lost on the platform.

A wins that second round by two minutes and twenty seconds.

This isn't proof that the first car is usually best, or that the last car is usually best. The decisive question is whether the walking can fit into time I already have before departure. In my simplified numbers, I need one hundred seconds merely to reach that target position, plus a sensible boarding margin. If the countdown is shorter, the plan is wrong before I take a step.

And I need to know which end actually contains my exit. It might be the front, the middle or the back. A different destination, a changed platform entrance, luggage, crowds, a lift route or an unreliable countdown changes the decision. I'm not modelling any of those here.

So my choice is conditional. When I have plenty of safe time and know my exit, I can move toward the useful car while waiting. When the train is close, I board where I safely can. In this four-minute-service case, missing the train to save the short walk isn't a win. A shorter service interval would need its own calculation.

Before I walk down the platform, I look at the departure time. That's the part of this journey the car number can't tell me.
