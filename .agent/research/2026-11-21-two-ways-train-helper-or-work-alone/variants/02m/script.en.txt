I've got twelve flat-pack party boxes to assemble before the guests arrive. Should I work alone, or spend time teaching a friend to help?

I built a time model, not a real experiment. I take eight minutes per box. After an eighteen-minute lesson, my friend takes ten minutes per box. During the lesson, neither of us assembles anything.

Choose A: I work alone. Or B: I teach first, then we work at the same time.

With just five boxes, A takes forty minutes. With B, I teach for eighteen minutes, then make three boxes while my friend makes two. My share takes twenty-four minutes; theirs takes twenty. So B finishes in forty-two. Training loses.

With six boxes, both methods take forty-eight minutes. With seven, training finally wins: fifty minutes with B versus fifty-six alone.

Now reset to all twelve. Alone I need ninety-six minutes. But the party starts in ninety. I'd miss it.

Under B, I teach for eighteen minutes, then assemble seven boxes while my friend assembles five. My seven take fifty-six minutes; their five take fifty. Because we work in parallel, the slower pile sets the finish: seventy-four minutes total.

B saves twenty-two minutes. The same lesson that wasted time with five boxes now gets us ready sixteen minutes before the guests arrive.

These times are assumptions, not measurements. A longer lesson, mistakes or a shared tool could change the result.

So before I refuse help because I'm faster, I need to know how much repeated work is waiting. That's why the first thing I count is how many boxes I've got.
