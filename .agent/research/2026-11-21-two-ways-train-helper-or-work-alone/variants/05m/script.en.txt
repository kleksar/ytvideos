The guests arrive in ninety minutes, and I still have twelve flat-pack party favour boxes to build. I can do them myself, or ask a friend to help. But my friend has never assembled this design before. By the time I explain the tabs, the inserts and the order of assembly, couldn't I have finished several boxes already?

That's the choice I want to test: A, work alone from the first minute; or B, teach my friend, then split the work. I'm using a small transparent model, not pretending that we actually ran two parties. Every kit is identical, there are no mistakes, and all the supplies are already on the table.

Here's the starting point. I can assemble one finished box in eight minutes. My friend will take ten minutes for each finished box once trained. Training uses eighteen minutes of both our time. While I'm explaining, neither of us assembles anything. Afterward we have separate workspaces, so we can make boxes in parallel without getting in each other's way.

The clock starts now and stops only when every box is finished. The deadline is ninety minutes. Choose A or B. I'll try a small stack first, and then the actual twelve.

With five boxes, I stay on A. Eight minutes each, five times. Forty minutes and I'm done.

For B, the clock runs for eighteen minutes before the first box can even start. After training, I take three boxes and my friend takes two. I need twenty-four more minutes. My friend needs twenty. Since those jobs overlap, we finish when I finish, after forty-two minutes total. A wins by two minutes.

It's a strange outcome. Two people are working, but adding the second person makes the job slower. Nothing went wrong. We simply paid for teaching when there weren't enough boxes left to earn that time back.

Let's add just one more box. At six boxes I take three, and my friend takes three. I finish my three in twenty-four minutes. My friend finishes theirs in thirty. Add the eighteen-minute lesson, and B now takes forty-eight minutes. Working alone on six boxes also takes forty-eight. Neither method wins.

So six boxes is the tie point in this particular model. Now imagine a seventh arrives. Alone, I need fifty-six minutes. With B, I take four boxes, my friend takes three, and after training we finish in fifty minutes. For the first time, teaching wins.

But there were twelve boxes on the table at the start, not seven. Let's reset to the original deadline. Method A is brutally simple: twelve times eight minutes is ninety-six. I miss the party start by six minutes.

Now method B. I lose the first eighteen minutes to instruction. The pile is still untouched. After that, I assign seven boxes to myself and five to my friend. My seven take fifty-six minutes. My friend's five take fifty. The two piles are built at the same time, so the later pile sets the finish. Eighteen plus fifty-six: seventy-four minutes.

We beat A by twenty-two minutes. More importantly, this model finishes the boxes sixteen minutes before the party, rather than six minutes afterward. The deadline changes the human consequence, not the math.

Why not give my friend six and keep six myself? That sounds fairer, but equal numbers aren't equal workloads. My six would take forty-eight minutes. My friend's six would take sixty. With the lesson, we'd finish after seventy-eight minutes, four minutes slower than the seven-five split. Dividing work by count can leave the faster worker waiting.

There is an important limit here. The eighteen-minute lesson is an assumption, not an observed fact. What if a friend already knows the job? We might not need a lesson. What if the assembly is fiddly and they make mistakes? Then training and quality checks could take longer. The model doesn't prove what will happen at a real party.

I can, however, ask a useful question before deciding: is this a one-off pile, or enough repeat work to recover the training cost? At five, doing it alone is faster. At six, we're tied. At twelve, teaching buys me twenty-two minutes and meets the deadline.

So the next time I look at a job and think, "It's faster if I just do it myself," I should count how many times I'll have to do that same job. That's the number that decides whether my first instinct is right.
