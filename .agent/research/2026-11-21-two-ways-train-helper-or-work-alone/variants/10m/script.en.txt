There's a table covered in flat pieces of cardboard, and I have ninety minutes before the guests arrive. Twelve party favour boxes need to become twelve finished boxes. A friend offers to help. My immediate reaction is, "It's quicker if I just build them myself." But is it?

I could start now, finish one box after another, and never stop to explain anything. Or I could spend part of my limited time teaching someone else to assemble this design and hope two sets of hands make up for the delay. This is a familiar decision in the kitchen, at work, or when helping somebody move. Teaching takes time. Repeated work takes time too. Which one is the bigger cost?

I'll compare two fixed methods in a deliberately simplified model. These are invented, clearly stated timings, not a secretly recorded real experiment. The same twelve kits start on the same table. Every box needs the same work and must be completed correctly. All materials are ready. There are no broken pieces, no distractions and no unexplained second worker already trained.

Method A: I assemble everything alone. Method B: my friend starts untrained, I teach them first, and only then do we split the remaining boxes and work at the same time. I'll choose the split that gives us the earliest finish, not the most visually equal piles. The clock stops when the last box is complete, not when I personally finish my share. If you're watching, choose A or B now.

Here are the model's inputs. I can finish a box in eight minutes. After being trained, my friend takes ten. We spend eighteen minutes on the lesson. While I teach, neither of us completes any boxes. After training we have separate workspaces and enough tools, so there are no collisions. The party begins ninety minutes after the clock starts.

One detail is easy to overlook: in B I don't get eighteen free minutes to keep assembling while my friend somehow learns. Those minutes are spent by both of us. Otherwise I would be giving B an unfair advantage. Both methods also have to produce twelve good boxes, not twelve partially taped shells.

First I want to know whether asking for help always wins. Let's start with fewer boxes than the party needs. Five boxes lie on the table. Method A: five times eight minutes. Forty minutes and everything is finished.

With B, I spend eighteen minutes teaching. Then I assemble three boxes while my friend assembles two. I need twenty-four minutes after the lesson; my friend needs twenty. Those tasks overlap. We don't add twenty-four and twenty; we wait for the later finisher. Total time: eighteen plus twenty-four, or forty-two minutes. Two people have finished later than one.

That's the uncomfortable part of the result. The helper was slower at first not because they failed, but because I invested more time in instruction than the remaining work could repay. For five boxes, my first instinct was correct.

I reset the same little model and place six boxes on the table. Alone, I need forty-eight minutes. With B, the eighteen-minute lesson is followed by three boxes for me and three for my friend. I need twenty-four more minutes; my friend needs thirty. The slower worker sets the finish. Eighteen plus thirty: forty-eight minutes. We're tied.

The number six matters here, but it isn't a universal law about teamwork. It's the break-even in this exact set of assumed times. Change the lesson or the work rate and the threshold changes. This is a model for making the trade-off visible, not a life hack promising six is always the magic number.

At seven boxes, A takes fifty-six minutes. Under B, I make four boxes in thirty-two minutes while my friend makes three in thirty. Add the lesson and our finish is fifty minutes. Teaching finally wins by six. One extra repeated task tipped the comparison.

Now bring back the original twelve boxes. With A, I work through them all at eight minutes each. Ninety-six minutes. The first guest has arrived six minutes before I finish. Remember, I set the ninety-minute deadline before running either method. I didn't invent a new deadline because one result looked better.

B starts with the same twelve kits. I spend eighteen minutes showing the assembly process to my friend: this flap goes under that one, the insert must fit before the final fold, and we check the shape before stacking the finished box. These are illustrative model actions, not a recording of an actual lesson. At minute eighteen we haven't completed a single box.

Then I take seven boxes. They take me fifty-six minutes. My friend takes five; those take fifty. We work at the same time, so the last box finishes fifty-six minutes later. Eighteen plus fifty-six is seventy-four minutes. We have sixteen minutes to spare before the party.

A takes ninety-six. B takes seventy-four. That's a twenty-two-minute difference and a completely different ending: one method misses the deadline and the other doesn't. The solution wasn't simply "hire more hands." It was "pay the cost of teaching once, then repeat the task enough times."

You might ask why I didn't divide the twelve boxes equally. Six each feels fair. But equal counts produce unequal finish times when the workers have different speeds. I would do my six in forty-eight minutes; my friend would need sixty. With training, that totals seventy-eight minutes. It's still better than doing everything alone, but it's four minutes slower than giving me seven and my friend five.

What about a different split: eight for me, four for my friend? My eight need sixty-four minutes. Their four need forty. With eighteen minutes of teaching we'd finish after eighty-two minutes. My friend is ready much earlier but waits while I finish. A split can look busy without being faster.

Seven and five works because the two piles end at nearly the same time. I finish after fifty-six minutes of assembly; my friend finishes after fifty. There's a six-minute difference, but shifting one whole box the other way makes the slower side take sixty. We can't split a box in half without changing the task rules.

That's useful even without the party. When two people work at different speeds, sharing an equal number of tasks isn't always the same as sharing an equal amount of work. But this is only one model, and the person who takes more boxes might reasonably care about fairness as well as the deadline.

The next question is how fragile that twenty-two-minute advantage really is. My friend needed an eighteen-minute lesson because I made that the model input. Suppose the lesson actually lasts thirty minutes. The optimal seven-five split still takes fifty-six minutes after training, so B now finishes at eighty-six. It's still ten minutes faster than A, and just four minutes before the party. A small overrun or packing mistake could erase that deadline margin.

Suppose the lesson stays eighteen minutes but the helper needs fifteen minutes per box instead of ten. With twelve boxes, a balanced split is eight boxes for me, four for my friend. My share needs sixty-four minutes; their share needs sixty. Add the lesson and B finishes after eighty-two minutes. Still faster than ninety-six, but less dramatic. This isn't evidence about how fast a real newcomer learns. It shows which assumption controls the outcome.

If both things go wrong together — thirty minutes teaching and fifteen minutes per box for my friend — the same eight-four split finishes after ninety-four minutes. That's two minutes faster than A but four minutes after the guests arrive. On pure completion time B still wins, yet it no longer solves the practical deadline problem. A headline that said "Teaching guarantees the boxes are ready" would be false even in this model.

And what if there's only one pair of scissors, one glue gun or just enough table space for one person? Then my assumption that both can assemble at the same time breaks. I'd need to measure or model how long they wait for the shared tool. Parallel work isn't magic when the bottleneck is a single object in the middle of the table.

Quality matters as well. If a trained helper produces more rejects, the target isn't simply twelve attempted boxes. I would need twelve usable ones, plus the cost of remaking failures. If I need to stop every five minutes to answer a question, then the "separate workplaces" condition doesn't hold. Real measurements could make this outcome more or less favorable. I haven't collected them.

This is why I chose a simple, reproducible problem rather than pretending there is one perfect rule for every job. The model makes a clear prediction only under its declared conditions. It won't tell me whether the helper wants to spend their afternoon assembling boxes, whether the results look good, or how quickly a child, friend or colleague might learn.

There's another practical point. After the party, the same friend might help again. If they keep the skill, I might not pay the full training cost next time. But that's an extra assumption, and today's two methods compare only this one batch. Carrying learning into a second event would be a new model with a different time horizon, not a hidden bonus for B in the original comparison.

With a single box, teaching is plainly expensive. With five, A wins by two minutes. At six, the methods tie. At seven, B first wins. At twelve, teaching saves twenty-two minutes and beats the deadline. The number of repetitions changes the result even though the people, the box design and the lesson stay the same.

So when I see a pile of work and someone offers to help, the decision isn't just "Am I quicker than they are?" I am quicker per box. That's true throughout the model. The real question is whether the remaining repetitions are enough to cover the time we both spend teaching and learning. Before I pick up the first box, that's the comparison I want to make.
