Were the bow doors closed?

On the evening of March sixth, 1987, a car ferry left Zeebrugge, Belgium, carrying hundreds of people across the Channel. From the bridge, nothing seemed to demand an emergency stop. The sea was calm. The ferry was on its way.

But the two doors at its bow, where vehicles drove aboard, were wide open.

The crew member normally assigned to close them had fallen asleep in his cabin. Another officer had finished supervising loading and gone to the bridge without confirming that the doors were shut. The captain had no indicator to show their position. Under the company's usual procedure, no warning meant everything was ready.

As the ferry accelerated, seawater poured through the opening and spread across the broad vehicle deck. That water could move from side to side. The more the ship leaned, the harder it became to recover.

Four minutes after passing the harbour entrance, the Herald of Free Enterprise rolled onto its side.

It came to rest in shallow water. Rescuers reached the wreck, and roughly three hundred and fifty people survived. One hundred and ninety-three ultimately lost their lives.

The investigation found more than a missed task. Other ferries in the same fleet had already sailed with doors open. Captains had asked for warning lights on the bridge. The company had not acted on those requests.

A single sleeping crew member was part of the story. A system in which nobody verified the doors was the larger failure.

The inquiry recommended an indicator visible from the bridge, so the answer would no longer depend on silence. It was a question that should have been settled before departure.

Were the bow doors closed?
