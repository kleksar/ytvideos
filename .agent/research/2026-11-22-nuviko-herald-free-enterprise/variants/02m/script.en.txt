Both loading doors at the front of the ferry were wide open.

On March sixth, 1987, the Herald of Free Enterprise left Zeebrugge, Belgium, with hundreds of people aboard. Cars had driven through those doors. Before the crossing, someone was supposed to close them.

The crew member assigned to that job was asleep in his cabin. The chief officer had left the vehicle deck without seeing the doors shut. On the bridge, the captain had no light showing their position. Company procedure assumed the ship was ready unless someone reported a problem.

No report came.

At six twenty-four, the ferry passed the outer harbour wall and accelerated. Water rushed through the open bow and across the broad vehicle deck. As the ship leaned, the water shifted to the lower side, making the lean worse.

About four minutes later, the Herald lay on its side. The shallow seabed kept it from disappearing underwater. Rescuers reached the wreck. Around three hundred and fifty people survived. One hundred and ninety-three died.

The inquiry uncovered something that changed the story. Another ferry in the same fleet had already sailed with its doors open. Captains had asked for indicator lights on the bridge, but management had not installed them.

The investigators faulted the officers and company management. The disaster was not explained by one man sleeping through his task. It was also a system that allowed departure without proof that the doors were closed.

At the moment the Herald sailed, the bridge lacked the one fact that mattered most.
