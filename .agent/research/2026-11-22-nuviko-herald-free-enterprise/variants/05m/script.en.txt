How could a passenger ferry leave port with its loading doors wide open? Why did nobody on the bridge know? And what had the company been told about this danger years before the disaster?

On March sixth, 1987, the Herald of Free Enterprise was preparing to carry passengers, cars and freight trucks from Zeebrugge, Belgium, to Dover. The weather was calm. About four hundred and fifty-nine passengers and eighty crew members were aboard. For the people making that crossing, there was nothing unusual about a ferry with a large opening in its bow. Vehicles drove through it during loading. Before departure, the opening was supposed to become a sealed wall again.

At five minutes past six that evening, the ship left its berth.

What the people on the bridge did not know was that both bow doors were still fully open.

The Herald was a roll-on, roll-off ferry. Its broad vehicle deck made loading efficient. But it also meant that water entering the deck could spread across a large, open space. Water moving sideways inside a ship changes its balance. A vessel that might survive a small, contained leak can become dangerously unstable when a large sheet of water is free to move.

The immediate question is obvious: why had the doors not been closed?

The assistant bosun normally responsible had worked on the car deck and then returned to his cabin, where he fell asleep. He did not wake for the call to departure stations. The chief officer had taken over supervision of loading. He left the vehicle deck to take his place on the bridge. He had not actually seen the doors close.

And on the bridge there was no simple light telling the captain whether that opening was secure.

Instead, the company operated on an assumption: if no one reported a problem, the ship could proceed. The captain received no warning. He did not ask for a positive confirmation.

The ferry passed the outer harbour wall at six twenty-four. As it gathered speed, seawater rushed through the open bow onto the vehicle deck. It spread and shifted. The deck had been designed to carry vehicles, not to hold a moving sheet of seawater. Its stability collapsed with startling speed.

About four minutes after passing the harbour entrance, the Herald lay on its side in the water.

That location mattered. The ferry had come to rest on the shallow seabed, so it did not disappear entirely beneath the surface. People still had to escape from a dark, tilted structure with flooded spaces. The formal investigation recorded that none of the lifeboats were launched. Belgian responders, divers, helicopters and nearby craft worked to reach those trapped and bring survivors ashore.

Roughly three hundred and fifty people were saved. The final death toll was one hundred and ninety-three. The initial inquiry had recorded fewer confirmed deaths at the time, a difference worth remembering when older accounts give a different figure.

If this story ended with a crew member asleep and an officer who failed to check, it would explain the open doors. But it would not explain why nobody noticed the same vulnerability before.

The official inquiry went back through the company's records. In October 1983, another ferry in the fleet, Pride of Free Enterprise, had already left with both its bow and stern doors open after the person responsible apparently fell asleep. A later circular reported further sailings with a bow or stern door open. This was no longer an imaginable accident. The fleet had experienced warnings.

In June 1985, a captain proposed putting indicator lights on the bridge, so officers could tell whether important watertight doors were closed. The proposal was not acted on. The official investigation also found unclear responsibilities and poor management attention to safety reports.

The court did not say a single employee caused the entire disaster. It attributed serious negligence to several officers, and fault to the ferry company at every level of management.

That changed the central question. The problem was not just who should have shut two doors. It was why a ship carrying hundreds of people could sail without anyone responsible for departure receiving proof that the doors were shut.

The inquiry called for indicators and stronger checks. After the tragedy, the United Kingdom created a specialist Marine Accident Investigation Branch, established in 1989.

The Herald's last crossing took only minutes to unravel. The record of warnings leading up to it stretched back years. That is the part of this disaster a single sleeping man cannot explain.
