A wave broke a small window on the Ocean Ranger. Hours later, the entire offshore drilling rig sank with eighty-four people aboard.

The rig stood above the North Atlantic on floating columns. It needed seawater inside ballast tanks to stay level. Operators used a control panel to move that water and adjust the rig's balance.

On February fourteenth, 1982, a winter storm struck the oil field off Newfoundland. The crew had stopped drilling to ride it out.

That evening, a wave shattered the window in the ballast control room. The window had a heavy steel cover, but it was normally left open so workers could read markings outside.

Water reached the electrical controls. Other vessels overheard radio calls about shocks from the panel and ballast valves opening and closing by themselves.

The crew tried to restore control. Later investigators found that manual control rods could open valves when the operators believed they were closing them. More water entered the wrong places. The rig began to tilt forward, letting waves flood spaces near its bow.

Soon the ordinary pumps could no longer clear those forward tanks. The growing tilt made the problem harder to reverse.

Just before half past one in the morning, the crew sent a Mayday and tried to leave.

A supply vessel reached a lifeboat, but the storm snapped the rescue line and the boat capsized. Nobody was rescued. By shortly after three, the Ocean Ranger was gone. All eighty-four workers died.

The Canadian inquiry found a chain of failures: an exposed window, confusing controls, inadequate training, and lifesaving equipment that could not protect the crew in that sea.

The inquiry did not say the platform was simply too weak for the storm. It found that people and equipment had been left without enough protection against a failure that began much earlier.

Even the first opening had its own protection: a steel cover that had become routine to leave open.
