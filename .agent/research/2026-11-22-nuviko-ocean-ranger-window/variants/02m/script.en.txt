At nine that evening, the Ocean Ranger reported a broken window in its ballast-control room. The glass had been cleaned up. It did not seem serious.

Before dawn, the offshore drilling rig would sink. None of the eighty-four people aboard would survive.

In February 1982, the crew had stopped drilling off Newfoundland to ride out a North Atlantic storm. The rig floated on huge supports, balanced by seawater moving through ballast tanks.

Workers looked through a small window in the control room to check how deeply the rig sat in the water. A steel cover could protect that window, but leaving it open for those readings had become routine.

That night, a wave shattered the glass. Seawater reached the electrical panel controlling the ballast valves. Nearby crews overheard radio reports of shocks from the panel and valves moving without commands.

The crew tried the manual controls. Investigators later found that operators apparently believed inserting the rods would close the valves. That movement could actually open them.

The rig began to tilt forward. As its bow sank lower, waves flooded forward spaces. The usual pumps could not remove the water fast enough. Now the flooding was making its own escape harder.

Just before one thirty in the morning, the Ocean Ranger sent a Mayday. Investigators found evidence of only one launched lifeboat. It held at least thirty people, but was damaged and overturned in the storm.

A standby vessel reached the scene, yet lacked suitable equipment to lift people from the water. Some tried to board a supply ship on improvised lines. Nobody survived.

The inquiry traced the disaster through an unprotected window, confusing controls, inadequate training and rescue equipment that failed when it was needed.

The first warning, though, had sounded almost ordinary.
