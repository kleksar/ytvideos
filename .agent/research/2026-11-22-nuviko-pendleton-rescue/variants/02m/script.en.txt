A lifeboat just thirty-six feet long returned with thirty-six people aboard. Four were rescuers. The other thirty-two had been stranded on half of a broken oil tanker.

Off Cape Cod in February 1952, a winter storm split the tanker Pendleton in two. Its radio room was destroyed. The crew on the surviving stern couldn't send a distress call.

The Coast Guard was already fighting to save another tanker. Four men, led by Bernard Webber, took a wooden rescue boat toward the Pendleton in darkness and snow.

The first enormous waves hit at Chatham Bar. The windshield broke. The compass was torn loose. They kept moving, although finding the wreck was now difficult and finding their way home would be harder.

A searchlight finally caught the towering stern. Thirty-three sailors were there. They lowered a rope ladder, but the waves lifted the little boat toward the steel hull and then dropped it away. Webber made pass after pass. His crew pulled sailors from the ladder and the freezing water.

One sailor died during the transfer. Thirty-two reached the boat. Moments after the rescue, the stranded stern rolled over.

Now the rescuers had a second problem. Their boat carried thirty-six people, and they still had no compass. They searched the darkness for a familiar sign. At last they found the little red light of a buoy marking the harbor entrance.

All thirty-six reached shore. The rescue had begun with four men looking for a ship that couldn't call for help. It ended with a packed lifeboat looking for one small light to bring them home.
