A Coast Guard aircraft spotted half an oil tanker in a snowstorm off Cape Cod. Thirty-three sailors were trapped on its stern. The other half, with the radio room, had broken away.

It was February 1952. Another tanker had split in the same storm, and larger rescue ships were already working there. Bernard Webber and three other men set out from Chatham in a wooden lifeboat only thirty-six feet long.

At the harbor bar, a wave smashed their windshield and tore the compass loose. Webber kept going. Beyond the bar, the engine stalled as the boat rolled. Andrew Fitzgerald restarted it, but the problem kept coming back. They still had to find the wreck, then find their way back without a compass.

A searchlight picked out the tanker's towering stern. The sailors lowered a rope ladder. Each wave lifted the little boat toward the steel hull, then pulled it away. Webber brought the boat in again and again while his crew hauled men aboard.

There had been thirty-three sailors on the stern. One died during the transfer. Thirty-two made it into the lifeboat. Shortly after it moved clear, the stern capsized.

Now thirty-six people were packed into a thirty-six-foot boat. Their compass was gone, and the harbor was hidden in darkness.

Webber looked for a small red flashing buoy the crew had passed on the way out. They found its light, followed it through Chatham Bar, and brought all thirty-six people ashore.

They were safe in Chatham. Yet the stranded sailors had never sent the distress call that normally starts a rescue.
