How could four rescuers bring thirty-two sailors off a broken tanker in a wooden boat? Why couldn't the sailors call for help? And after the rescuers lost their compass, how could everyone find the shore?

In February 1952 a fierce winter storm struck the waters off Cape Cod, Massachusetts. Two enormous oil tankers were caught in it. Both had been built during the Second World War. Both broke in half.

The Coast Guard first concentrated on the Fort Mercer, whose distress was known. Larger cutters and a Navy vessel went to its aid. But another tanker, the Pendleton, was drifting near Chatham in two pieces. The break had wrecked its radio room. The men trapped aboard its stern could not send a distress signal. A Coast Guard aircraft searching during the other rescue spotted the second wreck.

This was no longer one rescue. It was two disasters in the same storm, and the available crews were already stretched.

At Chatham station, Bernard Webber was assigned a thirty-six-foot motor lifeboat, CG-36500. Three men went with him: Andrew Fitzgerald, Richard Livesey and Ervin Maske. They had to cross Chatham Bar, a stretch of shallow water where incoming waves can break violently. Night had fallen, snow reduced visibility and the Pendleton was out beyond the bar.

Their first challenge nearly ended the attempt. A huge wave struck the lifeboat, smashed the windshield and tore away its compass. The boat kept going, but even if the men found the wreck, they no longer had the instrument that would guide them home.

The searchlight eventually revealed an enormous mass of steel in the waves: the stern of the Pendleton. The Coast Guard boat was so small beside it that getting near the ship was dangerous in itself.

Thirty-three sailors waited aboard. They lowered a rope ladder over the side. A man climbing down could be lifted high when the tanker rolled, then thrown toward its hull as the rescue boat rose beneath him. Webber had to time his approaches with the waves. Too far away, and a sailor would fall into the sea. Too close, and the boat could hit him or be smashed against the tanker.

The first transfer did not clear the problem. There were still many men above, another wave coming, and almost no room in which to work. Webber brought CG-36500 alongside repeatedly. Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske hauled men aboard from the ladder and the water. The number of survivors in the wooden boat grew until thirty-two of the thirty-three had been rescued.

One sailor died during the operation. He was not an anonymous figure in the count; the loss belonged to the same rescue in which so many people survived. Shortly after the lifeboat pulled clear, the Pendleton's stern capsized.

The obvious ending would be the moment the last rescued sailor reached the boat. But the crew was still out at sea, in the storm, with thirty-six people aboard and no compass. The rescue boat had become the only way home for everyone it carried.

Webber turned toward the coast. There was no clear landmark to follow. The crew watched for any light that could tell them where they were. Eventually they saw a small red flashing buoy. It marked the inside of Chatham Bar, near the entrance into Old Harbor. That tiny familiar point gave them a way back through the darkness.

The loaded lifeboat reached the Chatham Fish Pier with all thirty-six people aboard. On the same day, rescuers from other Coast Guard vessels saved thirty-eight sailors from Fort Mercer. In May, Webber and his three crewmates received Gold Lifesaving Medals.

The story is often told as an impossible crossing out to a wreck. But there were two crossings, and they demanded different kinds of judgment. First Webber had to bring a tiny moving boat close enough to a rolling wall of steel. Then, carrying thirty-two survivors, he had to find a harbor he could no longer navigate toward by compass. One red light finished what the rescue ladder had begun.
