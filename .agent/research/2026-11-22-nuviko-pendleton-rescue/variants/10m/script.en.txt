Why did a Coast Guard crew sail into a deadly storm to find a ship that had sent no distress call? How could a thirty-six-foot wooden lifeboat take thirty-two people off a towering tanker? And once the rescuers lost their compass, how did any of them reach land again?

The answer begins with two ships, not one.

In February 1952, a violent winter storm struck the Atlantic coast of Massachusetts. Snow covered Cape Cod. Offshore, large waves and high winds battered two oil tankers built during the Second World War. Each was more than five hundred feet long. Their names were Fort Mercer and Pendleton.

They were T2 tankers, a type constructed in great numbers during the war. Now, in the same storm, both had broken in two. The Coast Guard already knew about the Fort Mercer. Larger rescue vessels and a Navy ship were sent toward it. But a separate disaster was developing much closer to the small town of Chatham.

The Pendleton had also split. Its forward section carried the bridge and radio room. When that part was wrecked, the crew left on the stern had no way to send an SOS. Thirty-three men were stranded on a section of ship that was still floating, but could not be trusted to stay upright. The Coast Guard aircraft looking for trouble during the Fort Mercer rescue spotted the second wreck.

That discovery changed the operation. Rescuers were now dealing with two tankers broken into four pieces, without knowing at first how many people were alive in each one.

The first experienced crew sent from the Chatham station searched in another motor lifeboat but found no survivors. Meanwhile, many of the larger Coast Guard vessels were occupied farther offshore with the Fort Mercer.

Back at the station, Bernard Webber had spent much of the day helping fishing vessels grounded by the storm. He was ordered to take out a different lifeboat, CG-36500. It was only thirty-six feet long. His crew consisted of engineer Andrew Fitzgerald and seamen Richard Livesey and Ervin Maske.

The contrast was extreme: four men in one small boat, trying to reach sailors trapped inside half of a ship over five hundred feet long before it broke.

The first obstacle wasn't the wreck itself. Chatham Bar lies between the sheltered harbor and the open Atlantic. In shallow water, incoming waves can rise and break with particular violence. Crossing it in daylight would already demand care. Webber's crew approached in darkness and blowing snow.

They had passed a small red harbor buoy on the way out. They did not yet know how important that light would become.

As the lifeboat climbed the bar, a tremendous wave struck it and sent it back toward the harbor. Water came through the windshield. The glass shattered, and the compass was ripped from its mount. The four men had not even reached the stranded sailors, but one of the instruments they needed to return was gone.

Webber brought the bow back toward the seas and continued. After the breaking waves of the bar, the boat climbed and fell through heavy water beyond it. A small craft can accelerate dangerously down the back of a large wave. Webber used the engine and throttle to control that motion instead of simply racing ahead.

The engine presented another problem. As the boat rolled, it sometimes stopped. Fitzgerald had to get into the machinery compartment and start it again. Steering, keeping the engine running and watching for the wreck became separate problems, all competing for attention.

In the darkness, the men did not first identify the Pendleton by a clear silhouette. Webber later recalled hearing a heavy noise of metal and water. Only then did the searchlight pick out a great broken mass rising and dropping in the seas.

They approached its damaged side. At first there were no obvious lights or people. Then Webber came around the other side and saw the stern's deck lights and the survivors above him.

The rescue had reached its most difficult stage, and nobody had been taken off the ship yet.

From the deck of the Pendleton, the thirty-three men could lower a rope-and-wood pilot ladder. But that did not put them safely aboard the CG-36500. A ladder hangs from the larger vessel. The lifeboat was moving independently below it, carried up and down by waves that might strike the tanker at a different moment.

If Webber came in too early, the rising lifeboat could hit a sailor partway down the ladder. If he came too late, the man would fall into the water, beyond anyone's easy reach. And if he let the boat stay pressed against the steel hull, a roll of the tanker might crush it.

Each transfer was a fresh maneuver. Webber steered in close, judged the water and pulled away when the ship came down. The other three men grabbed sailors who jumped, slipped from the ladder or fell into the cold sea.

The boat did not simply park beside the wreck. It needed repeated approaches, sometimes more than one for a single survivor. The men aboard the Pendleton had to wait while the small boat moved away and returned. Each time the rescuers took another person aboard, there was one more person to protect and less room to work.

Nor was their plan a comfortable division into two trips. There were too many people on the stern, the weather was too severe and the wreck was unstable. Webber continued taking sailors aboard as the lifeboat became heavily crowded.

Thirty-two of the thirty-three stranded crewmen made it into the boat. One man died during the attempted transfer. That loss cannot be erased by the scale of the rescue. It is also important not to turn his last movements into an invented speech or a neat movie moment: accounts describe a violent, dangerous separation between ladder, tanker and lifeboat.

Then the stern itself rolled over. The men who had just left it were alive on a smaller vessel, while the section of tanker they had occupied could no longer keep them above the sea.

At this point, there were thirty-six people inside the thirty-six-foot rescue boat: thirty-two sailors and the four Coast Guardsmen. It was a remarkable result, but still far from a completed rescue.

They were outside Chatham Bar. Snow and darkness hid the coast. Their compass had been torn away during the outward crossing. The crowded boat did not handle like the lighter craft that had left the pier. And beyond the waves lay the same shallow-water bar that had smashed their windshield in the first place.

What could Webber actually steer toward? Not a modern electronic navigation display. Not a precise set of coordinates. He and the crew searched for any fixed marker that would tell them where the entrance lay.

They kept moving toward land and looking. Eventually, a small red flashing light appeared. It was the buoy on the inside of Chatham Bar, marking the turn into Old Harbor. The same kind of ordinary navigation marker that a boat might pass without a second thought had become their way home.

Webber guided the overloaded lifeboat into the harbor. All thirty-six aboard reached the Chatham Fish Pier safely.

The second disaster was ending elsewhere. Other rescue teams had saved thirty-eight men from the Fort Mercer. In total, seventy sailors survived through the Coast Guard's operations around the two wrecked tankers. That figure should not conceal the men who died or imply that the entire job was done by CG-36500. Its crew saved thirty-two of those seventy.

In May 1952, Webber, Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske received the Gold Lifesaving Medal. Decades later, the original boat was restored and recognized as a historic vessel. It still exists, not as an enlarged monument, but as a real thirty-six-foot craft whose size makes the numbers difficult to imagine.

A rescue at sea is usually pictured as a trip out, a desperate transfer and a triumphant return. On this night, those were three different challenges. The outward trip cost the crew its compass. The transfer required precise timing beside an unstable wall of steel. The return depended on finding a single harbor light with thirty-six people packed aboard.

The same storm broke two large tankers apart. One small boat couldn't repair them, calm the sea or bring back everyone who was lost. But four men kept their boat close enough to help, and then kept it moving until the last thirty-two survivors were ashore.
