Why did a Coast Guard crew sail into a deadly storm to find a ship that had sent no distress call? How could a thirty-six-foot wooden lifeboat take thirty-two people off a towering tanker? And once the rescuers lost their compass, how did any of them reach land again?

The answer begins with two ships, not one.

In February 1952, a violent winter storm struck the Atlantic coast of Massachusetts. Snow covered Cape Cod. Offshore, large waves and high winds battered two oil tankers built during the Second World War. Each was more than five hundred feet long. Their names were Fort Mercer and Pendleton.

Both vessels were wartime T2 tankers. Fort Mercer managed to call for help. Pendleton could not: the break had carried away its bridge and radio room with the forward section. The captain and seven men were on the bow. None of them would survive. On the other half, the lights and machinery still worked, and chief engineer Raymond Sybert took charge of the thirty-three men, including himself. They had power but no means of sending a distress call.

At Chatham, shore radar showed two contacts. An aircraft diverted from the Fort Mercer rescue identified both halves of Pendleton. There were now four pieces of ship to account for, and rescuers didn't yet know how many people were on each one.

At first, the station sent Webber and others to a beach in case part of Pendleton washed ashore. When it became clear that neither section was coming in there, they went back to prepare a boat. The first Chatham lifeboat crew found no survivors. The larger ships were already dealing with Fort Mercer. To reach the men on Pendleton's stern, Chatham would have to send out another small boat.

Back at the station, Bernard Webber had spent much of the day helping fishing vessels grounded by the storm. He was ordered to take out a different lifeboat, CG-36500. It was only thirty-six feet long. His usual engineer was ill. Andrew Fitzgerald, a junior mechanic at the station, went in his place. Richard Livesey joined them, as did Ervin Maske, a sailor from the nearby Stonehorse lightship who was waiting for transport back to his ship.

The contrast was extreme: four men in one small boat, trying to reach sailors trapped inside half of a ship over five hundred feet long before it broke.

The first obstacle wasn't the wreck itself. Chatham Bar lies between the sheltered harbor and the open Atlantic. In shallow water, incoming waves can rise and break with particular violence. Crossing it in daylight would already demand care. Webber's crew approached in darkness and blowing snow.

They had passed a small red harbor buoy on the way out. They did not yet know how important that light would become.

As the lifeboat climbed the bar, a tremendous wave struck it and sent it back toward the harbor. Water came through the windshield. The glass shattered, and the compass was ripped from its mount. The four men had not even reached the stranded sailors, but one of the instruments they needed to return was gone.

Webber turned the damaged boat into the seas again. Beyond the bar, he controlled its speed down the back of each large wave rather than simply driving faster. They were still searching for a ship whose crew could not call out to them.

The engine sometimes stopped when the boat rolled. Fitzgerald kept going below to bring it back to life. He suffered burns and bruises working in the cramped engine space. The lifeboat depended on its only motor, and the junior engineer had to keep that motor running in the same sea that had already taken their compass.

In the darkness, the men did not first identify the Pendleton by a clear silhouette. Webber later recalled hearing a heavy noise of metal and water. Only then did the searchlight pick out a great broken mass rising and dropping in the seas.

They approached its damaged side. At first there were no obvious lights or people. Then Webber came around the other side and saw the stern's deck lights and the survivors above him.

The rescue had reached its most difficult stage, and nobody had been taken off the ship yet.

The survivors lowered a rope-and-wood ladder from the stern. It hung from the moving tanker, while the lifeboat rose and fell on a different rhythm. One moment the ladder was just above CG-36500; the next, dark water opened between them.

Webber could not hold the rescue boat against the steel hull. A roll of the tanker might crush it; a badly timed surge could strike a man halfway down the ladder. He brought the boat close and steered clear again, while Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske pulled sailors aboard from the ladder and the sea.

Each successful transfer changed the next. Another man was safe, but there was less room for the rescuers to work. Men were still waiting high above on the stern. The boat had no steady place to wait for them; every new wave changed its distance from the ladder.

The boat was designed to carry six crew members and up to ten survivors. The rescue boat was already carrying more people than that design allowed for, and Webber continued bringing sailors aboard. Making another trip would mean leaving people on a piece of ship that might not last until he returned.

Thirty-two of the thirty-three stranded crewmen made it into the boat. One man died during the transfer. The number saved was extraordinary, but not everyone who had waited on the stern made it aboard.

Then the stern itself rolled over. The men who had just left it were alive on a smaller vessel, while the section of tanker they had occupied could no longer keep them above the sea.

At this point, there were thirty-six people inside the thirty-six-foot rescue boat: thirty-two sailors and the four Coast Guardsmen. It was a remarkable result, but still far from a completed rescue.

Webber managed to contact the Coast Guard by radio and report how many men he had picked up. The station and the cutter McCulloch then discussed a rendezvous at sea. A larger ship could take the survivors, but that would require the little lifeboat to spend longer in the storm and put the rescued men through another transfer.

Webber switched off his radio. His alternative was to head for shore and, if he could not find the harbor entrance, run the boat onto a beach so the men could climb out. The group on board welcomed the plan. It was not a safe landing yet. Snow hid the coast, the compass was missing, and the boat still had to get through the surf.

On the outward journey they had passed a small red buoy. Now they searched for its flashing light somewhere inside the bar. At last it appeared through the darkness. That fixed marker showed Webber where to turn into Old Harbor, instead of guessing where the entrance began.

Once he recognized the buoy, Webber called the station and asked for help at the pier. People were already gathering there. They caught the boat's lines and helped the men out when CG-36500 reached the dock. All thirty-six had made it to shore. No second transfer at sea had been necessary.

Elsewhere, other Coast Guard crews saved thirty-eight men from Fort Mercer. Seventy sailors survived across the two tanker rescues; Webber's CG-36500 carried thirty-two of them, plus four rescuers. That combined number does not erase the losses.

In May 1952, Webber, Fitzgerald, Livesey and Maske received Gold Lifesaving Medals. The original CG-36500 was later restored and recognized as historic. Its thirty-six-foot hull still exists, making the number of people who came ashore in it hard to picture.

Stand beside that boat today and the scale of the rescue becomes clear. Thirty-two sailors had come down a ladder, some falling into the sea before being pulled into its narrow hull. The crew still had to bring them home. The rescue ended at the pier, where people on land could finally reach out and take their hands.
