Two cosmonauts flew to a space station that could no longer answer them.

In February 1985, the Soviet station Salyut 7 stopped communicating with Earth. No crew was aboard. Without power, its automatic docking equipment could not help a visiting spacecraft.

Four months later, Vladimir Dzhanibekov and Viktor Savinykh approached in Soyuz T-13. The station was slowly turning. Its solar panels pointed in different directions.

Dzhanibekov had to match that movement himself. The crew checked their distance with a handheld laser instrument, circled the station to inspect it, and docked by hand on June eighth.

They had reached Salyut. They had not rescued it.

The electrical system was dead. Before opening the hatch, they tested the air. Inside, frost covered the walls and equipment. Eight batteries were drained; two were damaged beyond use.

The problem was not simply a shortage of sunlight. A failed sensor had stopped the station from turning its solar panels toward the Sun. The batteries had run down, taking the radio and other systems with them.

The crew used their Soyuz to point Salyut toward sunlight. They worked to recharge the batteries without relying on the broken system.

On June tenth, they switched on the air heaters. By June thirteenth, Salyut could control its orientation again.

Even then, the frost had to thaw without sending water into electrical equipment. Later, a cargo spacecraft brought replacement parts.

A station that could neither talk nor help another spacecraft dock was working again.

And it all began with the problem Dzhanibekov faced outside: how do you approach a station that cannot help you park?
