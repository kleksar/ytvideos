Salyut 7 was slowly turning when Soyuz T-13 approached. Its radio was silent, and the station's automatic docking equipment could not guide the visiting crew.

In February 1985, controllers had lost contact with the empty Soviet station. Four months later, Vladimir Dzhanibekov and Viktor Savinykh came to bring it back to life.

They could see solar panels pointing in different directions. They used a handheld laser instrument to judge the distance and circled the station to check its exterior. Dzhanibekov then matched its movement and docked by hand on June eighth.

But a successful docking only put them at the door.

The crew tested the air before opening the hatch. They could breathe inside, but frost covered the walls and equipment. All eight batteries had run down. Two were destroyed.

A failed sensor had stopped the solar panels from following the Sun. The station needed sunlight to charge its batteries, but it needed power to turn the panels. The crew could not solve that problem by simply switching the station on.

Their Soyuz supplied the missing movement. With the two craft connected, they turned Salyut toward the Sun and began restoring power.

On June tenth, the air heaters came on. By June thirteenth, the station could control its own orientation again. The crew still had to keep melting frost away from the electrical equipment.

On June twenty-third, Progress 24 docked with replacement batteries and a water heater. Salyut could receive another spacecraft again.

The first spacecraft had approached a very different station.
