How do you dock with a station that cannot answer your radio? How do you tell whether it is safe to enter? And once you're inside, what if the first thing you need to repair is the power that makes repairs possible?

In February 1985, Salyut 7 stopped communicating with Soviet mission control. The station was unoccupied. From Earth, controllers could follow its orbit but could not ask it what had failed.

The obvious solution was to send a crew. The problem was getting that crew aboard.

Normally, a visiting Soyuz relied on equipment at the station to help with the approach. That equipment was now silent. Salyut was also slowly rolling, its solar panels no longer tracking the Sun.

On June sixth, Vladimir Dzhanibekov and Viktor Savinykh launched in Soyuz T-13. They spent two days catching up with the station. They carried a handheld laser rangefinder because they could not count on its usual docking signals.

As Salyut filled their window, the astronauts could see the panels pointing in different directions. They flew around the outside to check whether the station was visibly damaged.

Then Dzhanibekov matched its movement and guided Soyuz to the docking port himself.

On June eighth, the two spacecraft connected. It was the first Soyuz docking with an inactive space station.

But making contact with the hull was only the beginning.

A check through the docking connection showed that Salyut's electrical system was dead. The cosmonauts tested the air before opening the hatch. The air was breathable, although the station was very cold.

Frost covered the walls and equipment. The men entered wearing winter clothing. Salyut had become a frozen workshop where almost every tool needed electricity, and the electricity was missing.

They examined eight onboard batteries. All eight had run down. Two were destroyed. Reconnecting the remaining batteries would not solve the problem unless the crew understood why they had stopped charging.

The investigation led to a sensor failure in the system that pointed the solar panels. The panels were no longer following the Sun. The batteries lost their charge, and without electricity the station could not correct its own position.

They needed the solar panels working to obtain power. They needed power to turn the panels.

The way out was the Soyuz already docked to Salyut. The crew used their visiting spacecraft to turn the combined craft toward the Sun, then arranged to charge the surviving batteries.

It was painstaking work. They could not simply throw every switch at once on a station whose electrical condition had been unknown for months.

By June tenth, they had enough power to switch on the air heaters. By June thirteenth, Salyut's own orientation control was running again.

That sounds like the finish. Now the walls began to thaw.

Water is useful inside a spacecraft only when it stays where it belongs. Frost melting over electrical equipment was another problem. The crew waited to operate the wall heaters until the frost had evaporated, and the station's humidity took weeks to return to normal.

There was also a damaged water heater. The crew used a powerful television light to warm fluids until replacements arrived.

On June twenty-third, Progress 24 docked with supplies: new batteries, a new water heater, solar-array extensions and other spare parts. A cargo ship could now reach a station that had been unable to receive anything.

Salyut 7 did not simply get switched back on. It had been approached without guidance, entered without knowing the air was safe, repaired without ordinary power, and warmed without letting the thaw ruin the equipment.

The surprise isn't that two men reached a silent station. It's how much work began after the docking was over.
