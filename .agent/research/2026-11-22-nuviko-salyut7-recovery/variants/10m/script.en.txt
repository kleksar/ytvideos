How do you dock with a station that no longer answers Earth? How can you tell if the air inside is safe? And why might turning its heaters back on threaten the very equipment you're trying to save?

In 1985, the Soviet Union faced that combination of questions with Salyut 7.

This was not an experimental satellite with nothing left to lose. Salyut 7 had hosted working crews and scientific equipment since 1982. It could receive visiting Soyuz spacecraft and Progress cargo ships. Keeping it operational mattered to the program preparing for larger stations.

The station had already taught its operators how awkward orbital repairs could be. In 1983, a propulsion-system leak developed. During the following year, cosmonauts went outside repeatedly to fit bypass plumbing and complete repairs. Even then, the main propulsion system was not used again to raise Salyut's orbit.

The last resident crew left in October 1984. The station remained in space, unoccupied and under remote supervision.

Then in February 1985, communications stopped.

Ground controllers could determine where Salyut was, but not what condition its equipment was in. Sending a routine command was no help if the radio receivers were dead. Nor could they ask the station to prepare for the next ship.

A public announcement in March described Salyut as deactivated after completing its work. Behind the scenes, planners decided by the end of that month to try to recover it.

The mission was different from an ordinary crew exchange. The visitors would have to reach a station that might not be able to accept visitors.

Vladimir Dzhanibekov was an experienced cosmonaut and docking pilot. Viktor Savinykh knew the station's equipment. They would travel aboard Soyuz T-13, prepared for an approach without the normal active guidance from Salyut.

The two men launched on June sixth. For two days, Soyuz adjusted its orbit to meet the silent station.

As they closed in, they finally saw what controllers on the ground could not: Salyut was slowly rolling. Its solar arrays pointed in apparently arbitrary directions. Even before going inside, the picture suggested that something was very wrong with its electricity and orientation.

The crew used a handheld laser instrument to measure distance. They flew around Salyut to inspect its exterior. That mattered because they could not safely assume that the docking port or the hull was undamaged.

There was no powered station ready to straighten itself for them. Dzhanibekov had to match its movement from the Soyuz controls and guide the approaching craft into the port.

On June eighth, he succeeded. Soyuz T-13 had docked with an inactive station.

For a moment, that might sound like the rescue was complete. But docking did not tell them whether they could safely open the hatch—or whether the station could be made to work again.

The electrical contacts in the docking collars gave them their first answer: Salyut was without power. But there were still two closed hatches between the crew and the station. An attempt to equalize pressure electrically failed. The surviving radio exchanges describe the crew checking the space between the hatches from the Soyuz side, then working a valve by hand. They heard air passing through. They reported that the pressure had stabilized, and continued the checks before opening the station itself. Only then could they establish that its atmosphere was breathable.

They opened the hatch and encountered the next obstacle: the station was cold enough for frost to cover the walls and equipment. The cosmonauts put on warm clothing as they entered.

The next day, the crew reported how difficult the work became during orbital night. In another exchange with the ground they were taking voltage readings, looking for a connector, warming it and asking for a screwdriver and a flashlight. The controllers could help the men work through the problem when they were in radio contact. They could not send a command to the dead station and do the job themselves.

Even breathing on Salyut still depended on the visiting ship: until the station's own air-regeneration system was working, the crew relied on the Soyuz system. They had brought a functioning spacecraft to a laboratory where several essential systems had stopped at once.

They started with the batteries. There were eight. All had been drained, and two were destroyed.

That was an important discovery, but it was not yet a repair. New batteries—or even charging the surviving ones—would be of little use if the same fault kept the station from collecting solar energy.

The investigation identified a failed sensor in the solar-array pointing system. The arrays had stopped tracking the Sun properly. The batteries drained. A separate problem with the telemetry radio had prevented controllers from discovering the charging failure in time. The crew now had to restore the station from inside rather than simply correct its pointing by a command from Earth.

Consider the repair problem. To collect sunlight efficiently, Salyut needed to turn. To turn under its own control, it needed electrical power. And its batteries needed sunlight to regain that power.

The two systems were trapped in a circle.

The crew had another way to turn the station. Their Soyuz was still attached and its own orientation system worked.

With Soyuz still attached, the cosmonauts used the visiting spacecraft to turn Salyut toward the Sun. That gave the solar panels a useful direction even while the station's own orientation equipment was dead.

They worked through the charging arrangements for the surviving batteries, restoring electricity in stages rather than demanding that every piece of equipment start at once.

Each successful step changed the next problem. With some power returned, the station could begin warming up. On June tenth, the crew turned on the air heaters.

But a frozen station does not become an ordinary room as soon as you raise the temperature.

The frost coating its walls and apparatus began to disappear. Liquid water drifting into electrical equipment could turn a successful repair into another failure. The crew held off on the wall heaters until the frost had evaporated.

On June twelfth, radio monitor Geoff Perry detected Salyut's own voice transmitter again. The station that had been silent for months was becoming audible. This did not mean all its equipment was working, but it was a change that people following the mission from Earth could detect.

The next test was whether the station could hold its own attitude. The crew had been relying on Soyuz to point the docked pair.

On June thirteenth, its attitude-control system was successfully reactivated. The crew no longer had to depend on Soyuz for the station's orientation.

That recovery also mattered to the next spacecraft: a Progress cargo ship could now approach the station normally. On June eighteenth, radio monitor Sven Grahn received both voice and telemetry signals from Salyut.

Yet the crew's work was not over. The station's water tanks took until the end of June to thaw, and its humidity did not return to normal until the end of July. Freezing had also destroyed the water heater.

For a temporary way to heat fluids, the cosmonauts used a powerful television light. It was an improvised use of equipment already aboard, not evidence that every system had returned to normal.

On June twenty-third, a Progress cargo spacecraft docked with Salyut. It delivered a new water heater, three new batteries, solar-array extensions, propellant and replacement parts.

The cargo included new semi-rigid spacesuits. The crew would need them for a job that could not be done from inside the station. Another cargo spacecraft, an upgraded Progress called Cosmos 1669, docked on July twenty-first. Supplies were no longer limited to what the rescue crew had brought with them in Soyuz.

On August second, Dzhanibekov and Savinykh put on the new suits and went outside for about five hours. They installed the third and final pair of solar-array extensions. In June, the two men had needed their Soyuz just to point the existing arrays at the Sun. Now they were increasing the station's ability to collect power.

On September eighteenth, another Soyuz docked with Salyut. Dzhanibekov eventually returned to Earth, but Savinykh stayed with the new crew. He could continue his work aboard the station instead of abandoning it after the repair.

Think back to that first June docking, with the two hatches still closed and no answer from Salyut. By autumn, spacecraft were arriving with people and equipment again. The station that the two men had entered in warm clothing was once more someone's workplace.
