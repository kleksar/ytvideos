### Three equally real proposals

**TACA 110 — selected.** Audience: English general-history viewers, not airline specialists. Exact possible first line: “A passenger jet landed on a strip of grass beside a canal. Both engines had stopped.” Early proof: FAA records severe hail and simultaneous engine flameout. After hook: failed restart, no reachable airport, a grassy levee, all 45 safe, the logistical question of recovering the airliner. Second payoff: June 6 departure from a repurposed WWII-era runway-road, 17-minute return flight. Final insight: hail behaves differently from rain in a turbofan, changing certification assumptions and hardware. Objection: well-covered aviation incident; narrow subject and a technical second half can depress interest. The fresh search demand and retention are **UNVERIFIED**, not assumed.

**Qantas 32 — strongest rival.** Exact possible line: “One engine exploded; the danger spread to systems across an entire A380.” Early proof: 4 Nov 2010, four minutes after takeoff, engine #2 disc fragments damage multiple systems and fuel tank. After hook: crew works through failures and lands at Singapore; ATSB traces the origin to an improperly manufactured oil feed pipe. Payoff: disaster avoided for 469 on board plus concrete quality-control lessons. Strong objection against selected TACA: Qantas offers much larger scale and an escalating chain of failures. Reason TACA wins this one: two crisp linked outcomes — the levee *and* the later road takeoff — without compressing 50+ minutes of systems management into short narration. Sources: [ATSB final investigation](https://www.atsb.gov.au/investigations/ao-2010-089), [ATSB findings](https://www.atsb.gov.au/statements/2022/atsb-releases-report-qantas-a380-engine-failure), [FAA summary](https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/VH-OQA).

**Kegworth — third finalist.** Exact possible line: “One engine was damaged. The crew shut down the other.” Early proof: FAA account 8 January 1989, left-engine fan blade failed; the crew disabled the operating right engine. After hook: temporary improvement reinforces mistaken diagnosis; approach ends short of runway. Payoff: 47 deaths, difficult lesson on cockpit evidence, communication and design. Objection: this tragedy deserves precise treatment of uncertainty, survivors and decisions, not a clever aviation twist. TACA provides a hopeful resolution and avoids manufacturing blame. Source: [FAA account](https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/G-OBME). Neither rival was deliberately chosen as a weak strawman.

## Verified core — source location, what it establishes and boundary

All cited webpages read/retrieved **2026-11-22**; web search snippets alone are not treated as viewing footage.

1. **FAA, Boeing 737-300 / TACA 110**: https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/N75356 , *History of Flight*, *Engine Response to Heavy Precipitation*, *Flight Crew Actions*, *Engine Ingestion Testing*, *Subsequent Design Changes*. Establishes date 1988-05-24, route via Belize, 38 passengers + seven crew = **45**, about 30 seconds of hail near 16,000 ft, both engines losing power, attempted windmill restart and APU-supported ignition without stable idle, planned ditching, successful dead-stick landing on levee near NASA Michoud, **no reported injuries**. Test findings: hail can enter core more readily at some fan/flight speeds than rain; former test parameters masked susceptibility; modified spinner, splitter, bleed valves, FAA airworthiness directives. **Does not establish** unrecorded crew emotions, passenger dialogue or exact touchdown speed.
2. **UPI contemporary wire, June 6, 1988**: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1988/06/06/Emergency-shortened-flight-is-completed/6569581572800/ , *Emergency-shortened flight is completed*. Establishes later engine replacement, subsequent takeoff June 6 from little-used WWII-era runway at NASA Michoud, only pilot/copilot on transfer, light fuel load, 17-minute flight to New Orleans International. **Important conflict:** UPI calls original occupancy 41; the FAA retrospective specifies 38 passengers + seven crew = 45. Scripts explicitly follow FAA for manifest and UPI for later recovery. UPI describes it as an old runway, which had a road function; do not call it an ordinary active public highway. Claim that *both* engines were replaced is **not** supported by this dispatch: it reports replacement of the right engine.
3. **FAA Qantas 32**: https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/VH-OQA and ATSB above, used only for competitor comparison.
4. **FAA Kegworth**: https://www.faa.gov/lessons_learned/transport_airplane/accidents/G-OBME , used only for competitor comparison.

Evidence ladder: government accident reconstructions are retrospective official syntheses; UPI June 1988 is contemporaneous journalism; no original audio/transcript or exact manufacturer maintenance logs reviewed. Do not invent technical callouts, passenger reactions, pilot quotations or motives. A previous technical claim of “engine completely dead after storm” needs qualification: assisted relight ignited but could not reach stable idle; scripts distinguish ignition from usable thrust.

## Causal chain and promise

A routine descent → severe hail despite weather avoidance → both engines lose usable thrust → attempts to relight cannot sustain idle → runway beyond glide → levee visible below clouds → no-injury landing → aircraft stranded near NASA → existing former military runway-road + on-site repair → prepared ferry flight to airport → technical investigation reveals inadequate hail assumptions → design and procedure changes.

Promise/title: **“The Jet Landed on Grass — Then Took Off From a Road”**, not “pilots landed a jet on the highway.” First supported image: a B737 on a narrow grass levee; second: WWII-era strip at NASA (both depicted as *reconstructions* until actual visual provenance confirmed). Payoff for 2m is dual rescue/recovery. 5m adds failed relight and the contrast between emergency landing and engineered ferry flight. 10m adds certification failures and lasting hardware changes, with real risk of an explanatory tail after emotional resolution.

**Why stop:** engines fail simultaneously and a commercial jet lands on a dam. **Why remain:** can it land, then can it ever leave? **What viewer gets:** two concrete answers and the reason certification changed. **Strongest objection:** many similar aviation videos and no verified matched audience baseline; 10-minute mechanism may dilute the payoff.
