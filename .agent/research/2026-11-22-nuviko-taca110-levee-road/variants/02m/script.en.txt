A passenger jet landed on a strip of grass beside a canal. Both engines had stopped.

On May 24, 1988, TACA Flight 110 was descending toward New Orleans. Thirty-eight passengers and seven crew members were aboard a Boeing 737.

The pilots tried to avoid the worst of a thunderstorm. Then the aircraft flew through intense rain and hail. For about thirty seconds, pieces of ice poured into its engines.

Both engines lost power.

The crew tried to restart them. Airflow alone couldn't do it. An auxiliary power unit helped ignite the engines, but in the continuing storm they couldn't reach a stable idle.

Now the pilots had to land without thrust. The airport was beyond reach. They prepared for a possible landing on water.

Then the aircraft came below the clouds. Ahead was a narrow, grassy flood levee near a NASA facility.

The pilots put the jet down on that levee. All forty-five people survived without reported injury.

But they had saved an airliner in a place with no normal airport runway. How could anyone get it out?

Technicians moved it to the nearby facility and replaced a damaged engine. Thirteen days later, with only a small flight crew aboard, the Boeing rolled along an old wartime runway that had become a road.

It took off, flew to New Orleans airport, and finished the journey it had missed.

Investigators found that hail could enter an engine's core differently from ordinary rain. The tests used to certify these engines hadn't reproduced the most dangerous combination of conditions. Engine designs and procedures changed.

The plane flew away because a road had once been a runway. It survived the storm because a grassy bank became one first.
