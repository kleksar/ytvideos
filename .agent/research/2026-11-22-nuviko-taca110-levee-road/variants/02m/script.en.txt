A Boeing 737 was gliding toward a strip of grass beside a canal. Neither engine was producing usable thrust.

On May 24, 1988, TACA Flight 110 was descending toward New Orleans with thirty-eight passengers and seven crew aboard. The pilots tried to avoid the worst of a thunderstorm. They still flew through violent hail for about thirty seconds.

Both engines lost power. The crew tried restarting them using airflow. Then they turned to the auxiliary power unit. Fuel ignited, but the engines could not settle into a stable idle. They still had no thrust.

The airport was too far away. The crew prepared for a water landing. As the jet came below the clouds, they spotted a narrow grassy flood levee. It was meant to hold back water, not land aircraft.

The pilots lined up with the levee and landed. All forty-five people survived without reported injury.

That solved the passengers' problem. It left another one: a Boeing stranded beside a NASA facility, far from an airport runway.

Technicians moved it to the facility and replaced a damaged engine. Thirteen days later, with no passengers aboard and little fuel, a small flight crew took off along an old military runway that had become a road. Seventeen minutes later, they reached New Orleans airport.

The investigation found that hail could enter the engine cores under conditions earlier certification tests had missed. Engine hardware and procedures changed afterward.

The departure on the road was a planned flight in a repaired jet. On the day of the storm, there was no time for repairs. With the airport out of reach, the pilots had only the ground ahead.
