What do pilots do when both engines of a passenger jet go silent? How can they land on a place that was never built for airplanes? And even if everybody survives, how do they get the aircraft home?

On May 24, 1988, a Boeing 737 operated by TACA was approaching New Orleans. It had begun its journey in El Salvador, stopped in Belize, and was carrying thirty-eight passengers and seven crew members on the final leg.

The flight had been ordinary until the descent. Ahead lay powerful thunderstorms. The pilots tried to avoid the worst cells, but their route briefly crossed a region of extreme precipitation.

Around sixteen thousand feet, hail struck the airplane for roughly thirty seconds.

The engines began losing thrust. Then both stopped producing usable power.

There was still a fuel supply. There was still an aircraft the pilots could control. But a jet without engine thrust cannot simply continue toward a distant airport forever. Every mile of descent consumes altitude they cannot get back.

The crew declared an emergency. They attempted to restart both engines using the airflow passing through them. Neither returned to useful power.

So they started the auxiliary power unit, a small engine that could help spin the larger ones for another attempt. This time the main engines ignited. But ice and water were still entering. They would not accelerate to a stable idle, and the restart attempts caused damaging heat.

That was the central problem: the engines had not just switched off like lights. They were being fed conditions in which combustion could not remain stable.

The crew shut down the engines and prepared for an emergency landing. The airport was out of reach. For a time, landing on water was the possibility they had to consider.

Then the 737 descended below the clouds.

There, near the NASA Michoud facility outside New Orleans, was a narrow grassy levee beside a waterway. A levee is an embankment built to hold back water, not to stop an airliner.

The pilots lined up the powerless Boeing with that strip of grass. They landed successfully. There were no reported injuries among all forty-five people aboard.

They had solved the urgent question: where could a jet with two dead engines land? But a second, stranger question remained. How could the aircraft leave a flood bank?

A normal airport could have received it for repairs. Instead, the intact jet was sitting on grass next to a facility that built hardware for the American space programme.

Engineers considered how to remove it. A dismantling operation would have been difficult. But the nearby NASA site had something useful: an old runway from the Second World War, later used as a road.

The plane was moved to the facility. Technicians replaced a damaged engine. On June 6, thirteen days after the emergency landing, a Boeing flight crew prepared a short flight with no passengers and only the fuel necessary for the transfer.

The Boeing accelerated along that former runway. It lifted off and flew about seventeen minutes to New Orleans International Airport.

The airplane had landed on grass, then departed from a road. Neither surface had been the airport runway it was originally trying to reach.

The investigation also answered why a modern airliner had lost both engines at once. Large hail does not behave like rain inside a turbofan. At certain aircraft speeds and low engine speeds, ice can pass between fan blades and enter the engine core, where too much water and hail disrupt stable combustion.

Earlier certification tests had used combinations of speed and engine power that did not reveal how vulnerable the engine could be in these conditions. The manufacturer subsequently changed parts that guided water and hail through the engine, and aviation authorities mandated changes.

So the landing was not proof that airplanes can safely use any field. It was an emergency solution made possible by one strip of land and a crew that reached it. The flight out was different: a repaired aircraft, a prepared crew, a light load, and a road that had once been a runway.

Everyone came home. The aircraft did too — thirteen days later, by a completely different route.
