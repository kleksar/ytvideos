Why would a Boeing 737 need a flood levee instead of an airport runway? Why did its two modern engines fail in the same storm? And how did that same airplane take off again, thirteen days later, from what had become a road?

On May 24, 1988, TACA Flight 110 was descending toward New Orleans after a journey from San Salvador, with a stop in Belize. There were thirty-eight passengers and seven crew members aboard.

The airplane was a Boeing 737-300 with two CFM56 engines. As it descended from cruising altitude, the pilots reduced engine power in preparation for landing. That ordinary change in power would matter later, when engineers tried to understand what happened.

But the approach path crossed a line of intense thunderstorms. The pilots were trying to avoid the worst cells. Even so, the airplane briefly entered precipitation far more violent than ordinary rain.

At around sixteen thousand feet, it flew through roughly thirty seconds of heavy hail.

The thrust began to drop. Then both engines stopped producing usable power.

A Boeing 737 is still an aircraft when its engines stop. Its wings continue to make lift while it moves through the air. But without power, it becomes a glider, losing altitude as it travels. The pilots could steer it and choose where to go. What they couldn't do was add energy to extend the glide as far as they wanted.

The crew declared an emergency as the aircraft descended. The first hope was to get an engine running again.

One method uses the wind rushing into an engine to spin its internal machinery fast enough for ignition. The crew tried that with both engines. Neither returned to useful power.

Next they started the auxiliary power unit. This small onboard engine can provide power and help drive the starter system. The main engines did ignite during those assisted attempts. But ignition is not the same as a running engine. Water and ice were still entering. The engines would not build up to a stable idle, and temperatures rose beyond safe limits.

The hotter the engines became without accelerating, the less reason there was to keep trying. The right engine's turbine was badly damaged by the overheating; that damage would only become clear on the ground.

The crew had reached a harsh conclusion. There was no useful response when they asked the engines for thrust. They shut them down and turned to the problem directly ahead: where could they land?

New Orleans airport was beyond reach. The crew prepared for a possible water landing.

Then, descending through the cloud layer, they could see the ground.

Near the NASA Michoud complex they identified a long strip of grass on the landward side of a flood-control levee. The embankment protected the land from the waterway; a drainage canal ran beside the grass.

The pilots steered the Boeing onto that strip. It cleared the top of the embankment and touched down with the landing gear extended. The grass was wet and sloped slightly downhill, but the airplane kept its direction. Its wheels stopped about eight hundred metres from where they had touched down.

The exits and slides that were used worked normally. The passengers and crew evacuated, and all forty-five people survived without reported injury.

On the ground, the Boeing's condition was surprising. There were traces of hail on the nose and tail, but the airframe was intact. The people had been evacuated; the machine itself could still be saved. Before anyone could fly it away, though, they had to understand which parts were damaged.

The investigators took fuel and oil samples. They looked inside both engines with a small inspection instrument. The findings on the left and right were quite different. On the right, there was loose metal inside the engine and heat damage to the turbine. On the left, the internal inspection found no apparent damage.

That was an important clue. The same storm had silenced both engines, yet it had not physically destroyed both of them. The left engine could help explain how an apparently sound engine had lost all useful power. The right one needed to be replaced.

Meanwhile, the repair team had a practical problem. They were next to a drainage canal, with a passenger jet sitting on grass. A regular airport would have a paved route to a maintenance hangar. There was no such route from this landing site. But there was a road inside the NASA property.

They towed the Boeing about two miles from the levee to that road. It was secured near the building where large space hardware was assembled. Boeing and General Electric technicians worked on the airplane. They replaced its damaged right engine, checked the systems and ran both engines at takeoff power on the ground.

Getting the Boeing to the airport still meant finding enough room to fly. The road at Michoud had an unusual advantage: it followed a former military runway from the Second World War. It was not a regular commercial airport, but it provided a long, straight surface that had once been designed for aircraft.

The repaired jet would carry no passengers. Boeing supplied a pilot and copilot, and the fuel load was kept light. The crew also had to account for obstacles near the end of the strip, including a bridge and power lines. This time, they could plan the departure around those obstacles in advance. The landing thirteen days earlier had allowed them no such preparation.

By then, the engine failure was already affecting other airplanes. On May 30, just six days after the incident, the American aviation regulator issued an interim directive changing procedures for the Boeing 737 in severe rain and hail. It was a temporary response while the permanent fix was still being worked out.

On June 6, employees at Michoud came outside to watch. The 737 accelerated along the road. It was airborne after a much shorter run than the full length available. Seventeen minutes later, the airplane was at New Orleans International Airport, where it could receive further maintenance. The employees watching at Michoud had applauded as the jet lifted away.

The aircraft had finally reached the destination of its interrupted passenger flight. But the storm still presented a puzzle for the people trying to make sure that another crew would not need a flood levee.

There had been a warning the year before. In August 1987, another Boeing 737 with the same engine family lost power in both engines while approaching Thessaloniki, Greece, in rain and hail. Its pilots managed to restart the engines and land normally. The investigation had focused mainly on rain, and the weather was considered unusually severe.

The engines had passed the existing tests. In fact, they had demonstrated a substantial margin over the water-ingestion requirement. How could the margin be real in a test and disappear in the sky?

The left engine from Flight 110 was sent for extensive testing. At low power, with enough water entering, the internal rotation slowed below the speed required to keep the engine running. Some fuel was still burning. Increasing the power demand in that condition did not make the engine accelerate; instead, it heated the turbine, recreating the type of damage found in the right engine.

The testing exposed a problem with the way the engines had been certified. Rain droplets tend to turn with the airflow. Hailstones can travel more directly through the fan or bounce inward off its central cone. With the airplane moving quickly and the fan turning slowly during descent, too much ice can reach the part of the engine where fuel burns. The old test arrangement had sent relatively little water into that critical area.

The manufacturer reshaped the cone and the division between the fan's two air paths, and added more outlets through which moisture could escape. The aim was to keep the engine running in severe precipitation, not simply protect its metal parts from impact. The FAA required hardware and procedure changes. The broader investigation of rain and hail failures eventually produced a revised certification standard in 1998.

The lasting consequence of that day was not a new kind of landing technique. Forty-five people had already left the aircraft safely. The Boeing went back to an airport; its damaged engine went for examination. The next generation of tests would have to account for a storm that the earlier tests had failed to reproduce.
