Why would a Boeing 737 need a flood levee instead of an airport runway? Why did its two modern engines fail in the same storm? And how did that same airplane take off again, thirteen days later, from what had become a road?

The date was May 24, 1988. TACA Flight 110 was making its final approach toward New Orleans after a journey from San Salvador, with a stop in Belize. There were thirty-eight passengers and seven crew members aboard.

The airplane was a Boeing 737-300, fitted with CFM56 turbofan engines. On an ordinary descent, the pilots would reduce power, lose altitude, then bring the engines up again as they approached the runway. Nothing about that routine should require finding a place to land beside a canal.

But the approach path crossed a line of intense thunderstorms. The pilots were trying to avoid the worst cells. Even so, the airplane briefly entered precipitation far more violent than ordinary rain.

At around sixteen thousand feet, it flew through roughly thirty seconds of heavy hail.

Both engines lost thrust. Not one engine with the other ready to carry the aircraft. Both.

A Boeing 737 is still an aircraft when its engines stop. Its wings continue to make lift while it moves through the air. But without power, it becomes a glider, losing altitude as it travels. The pilots could steer it and choose where to go. What they couldn't do was add energy to extend the glide as far as they wanted.

The crew declared an emergency as the aircraft descended. The first hope was to get an engine running again.

One method uses the wind rushing into an engine to spin its internal machinery fast enough for ignition. The crew tried that with both engines. Neither returned to useful power.

Next they started the auxiliary power unit. This small onboard engine can provide power and help drive the starter system. The main engines did ignite during those assisted attempts. But ignition is not the same as a running engine. Water and ice were still entering. The engines would not build up to a stable idle, and temperatures rose beyond safe limits.

The restart attempts damaged turbine components. Investigation later found that operating in so much precipitation made restarting more difficult than keeping an engine running under the same conditions.

The crew had reached a harsh conclusion. There was no useful response when they asked the engines for thrust. They shut them down and turned to the problem directly ahead: where could they land?

New Orleans airport was beyond reach. The crew prepared for a possible water landing.

Then, descending through the cloud layer, they could see the ground.

They identified a narrow levee near the NASA Michoud complex. This was an embankment built beside a waterway to control flooding. It was not designed as a runway, and it did not have the broad safety margins of an airport.

Still, it was a firm strip of land ahead of them. The pilots lined up the 737 and made a powerless landing on the grass.

The Boeing stopped. All forty-five people survived without reported injury.

The story could end there: an extraordinary landing, a modern jet left beside a canal, and a passenger cabin that had escaped a fatal outcome. But the aircraft was not a wreck. It was intact enough that recovering it presented a different sort of problem.

How do you move a full-size jet from a grassy embankment? It cannot simply drive along an ordinary road with its wings extended. Dismantling and transporting it would require substantial work. And its engines had sustained damage.

The nearby NASA Michoud site offered an alternative. There was an old runway dating from the Second World War, no longer serving as a regular airport runway and used as a road inside the facility.

The plane was moved from the levee toward the facility. Technicians came to work on it and replaced the damaged right engine. A short transfer flight began to look possible. The aircraft would not carry its passengers again that day. Only a small Boeing flight crew would be aboard, and the fuel load would be kept low.

On June 6, thirteen days after the forced landing, the Boeing lined up on that former wartime runway.

It accelerated, lifted off after a comparatively short run, cleared the surrounding obstacles, and headed for New Orleans International Airport. Seventeen minutes later, it landed where Flight 110 had originally been going.

The airplane had used two improvised airfields in different ways. The grass saved the people when there was no power. The old runway, by then a road, allowed a repaired aircraft to fly away.

But the more important technical question had not disappeared with the successful rescue. If commercial jet engines had been certified to survive heavy precipitation, what had happened inside these two?

The investigators distinguished rain from hail. Rain tends to follow airflow more readily. Hailstones carry more momentum. Under some conditions they pass between the large fan blades, or strike the central spinner and are directed into the engine core.

That core is where fuel and compressed air burn to produce hot gas and power the turbine. Too much water and ice there can disrupt combustion. The flames go out, and the engine stops delivering thrust.

The amount of hail reaching the core depends on a combination of factors, not just how dramatic the storm looks from the cockpit. The FAA later described low fan speed, high aircraft speed, and high altitude as conditions that can make hail ingestion worse.

That combination mattered on Flight 110. The 737 was descending at relatively low engine power when it met the storm. Its speed through the air carried hail toward the engine, while the slower-turning fan was less effective at sending ice away from the core.

Earlier certification tests had not reproduced this dangerous combination. The tests placed emphasis on heavy rain and the physical damage that foreign objects might cause. They also used engine and airflow conditions that could push much of the water away from the core.

In that setup, the engine looked more resistant than it turned out to be on the real approach to New Orleans. Even a generous apparent margin in testing did not cover the specific behaviour of hail in flight.

The designers had learned something different from the one dramatic fact that both engines stopped. The geometry of the entrance to a turbofan determines where ice and water can travel.

After the accident, CFM International investigated and tested the engines again. The company modified the central spinner shape to direct hail outward. It revised the splitter so that more water and hail could move away from the hot core, and increased the number of bleed-valve doors that could extract moisture at low engine speeds.

The FAA mandated relevant changes through airworthiness directives and changes to flight procedures. These are the less visible consequences of the incident: not a dramatic photograph of a Boeing on grass, but alterations to machinery and instructions intended to prevent another aircraft from facing the same combination of failures.

There are limits to what this story proves. The crew did not demonstrate that every levee is a safe runway. Their landing depended on the particular piece of land, the airplane's remaining control, and the decisions made as it descended. Nor was the flight from the NASA facility another powerless glide. It was carefully prepared, with an engine replacement, a lighter load, and a former runway available.

The first landing answered an immediate question: how do forty-five people survive when a passenger jet loses both engines? The second flight answered an almost absurd one: what happens to the jet after everyone steps off in the middle of nowhere?

And the investigation answered a third: what must change so that the next crew is less likely to face that choice?

A grass levee was never supposed to receive Flight 110. A road was no longer supposed to launch an airliner. For this Boeing, each became a runway once, and the lessons from the storm outlasted both flights.
