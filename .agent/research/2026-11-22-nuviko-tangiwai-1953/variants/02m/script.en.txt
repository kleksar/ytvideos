A night express was already braking when a railway bridge gave way beneath it.

Christmas Eve, 1953. The train was carrying people north through New Zealand, many on their way home for the holiday.

Two hours earlier, high on Mount Ruapehu, the rim of a crater lake had broken. A surge of water, mud, ice and rock raced down the Whangaehu River. There had been no heavy rain nearby to warn anyone.

At Tangiwai, the flood struck the railway bridge and swept away one of its supports. Minutes later, the express approached.

Cyril Ellis had stopped his car at a flooded road bridge. He saw the train's lights and ran toward the tracks, waving a torch. Evidence later showed the engine driver applied the emergency brakes. But the train still reached the broken crossing.

The engine and the five second-class carriages went into the river.

The first first-class carriage, known as Car Z, hung at the edge. Ellis and train guard William Inglis climbed aboard to move the passengers back. The coupling broke. The carriage fell too.

When it came to rest, Ellis, Inglis and passenger John Holman helped people out through broken windows. Twenty-one of the carriage's twenty-two passengers survived. Others formed a human chain to reach the bank.

But across the train, 151 of the 285 people aboard died.

The investigation found that the flood came from the volcanic crater lake, not from local rainfall. It recommended an upstream warning system. In 2007, another flow was detected and caused little damage.

The brakes may have saved the last three carriages. They could not save the first ones, because the river had arrived before the train.

And only minutes after the river struck the bridge, another sound came down the tracks.
