In 1953, a volcanic flood reached a railway bridge before the Christmas Eve train did.

The express was carrying 285 people across New Zealand. At Tangiwai, the weather was fine. A goods train had crossed the same bridge earlier that evening.

But high on Mount Ruapehu, at eight oh two, the rim of a crater lake broke. Water, mud, ice and rocks raced down the Whangaehu River. Just after ten ten, the surge swept away a support beneath the railway bridge.

A motorist named Cyril Ellis stopped at a flooded road crossing. He saw the approaching locomotive and ran toward the tracks, waving a torch. Train driver Charles Parker applied the emergency brakes. Investigators found evidence of braking, but could not establish that Ellis's torch made him do it.

At ten twenty-one, the locomotive reached the damaged crossing. The bridge collapsed. The engine and five second-class carriages fell into the river. Three rear carriages stayed on the rails.

One more carriage, called Car Z, hung at the broken edge. Ellis and train guard William Inglis went inside to guide its passengers back. Then the coupling broke, and Car Z fell too.

The carriage came to rest near a bank. Ellis, Inglis and passenger John Holman helped people escape through broken windows. Twenty-one of its twenty-two passengers survived. Across the whole train, 151 people died.

The inquiry recommended an upstream warning system. In 2007, another volcanic flood came down that river. Monitoring and alarms were in place. The flow caused little damage and no injuries.

That time, there was a warning while the flood was still on its way.
