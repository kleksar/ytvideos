Why did a passenger train fall into a river on a clear Christmas Eve? Why did some carriages stay on the rails while others disappeared? And what did one man with a torch manage to do when he saw the train coming?

On December 24, 1953, the Wellington-to-Auckland night express was carrying 285 passengers and crew through New Zealand. People were traveling to spend Christmas with family. Ahead lay the railway bridge at Tangiwai, crossing the Whangaehu River near Mount Ruapehu.

Earlier that evening, a goods train had crossed safely. The local weather was fine. There had been little rain. Nothing at the railway station suggested that a flood was racing toward the bridge.

But high on the volcano, the rim of a crater lake had given way at eight oh two. An immense flow of water, mud, ice and rocks rushed down the valley. This is called a lahar: a flood of volcanic debris, able to move far beyond the mountain itself.

Shortly after ten ten, the lahar hit the bridge. It tore away one of the concrete supports.

The passenger express was minutes away.

At a nearby road bridge, Cyril Ellis found the crossing under water and stopped his car. Then he saw the approaching train lights. He ran toward the railway line, waving his torch. The driver, Charles Parker, applied the emergency brakes. The subsequent investigation found braking evidence about two hundred metres before the bridge. It did not change the fact that the train was already too close.

At ten twenty-one, the engine reached the damaged crossing. It went into the river, followed by all five second-class carriages. Four of those carriages were smashed by the torrent.

But the carriages behind them did not all follow.

The first first-class carriage, Car Z, remained hanging at the broken end of the bridge. It was still attached to the three carriages farther back.

Ellis and guard William Inglis climbed aboard, trying to move the people from Car Z into the safer carriage behind it.

Then the coupling broke.

Car Z fell and was carried downstream. When it came to rest on a bank, water was still passing through the damaged carriage. Ellis, Inglis and passenger John Holman helped occupants out through broken windows and onto the side. As the current fell, survivors formed a human chain to reach land.

Twenty-one of Car Z's twenty-two passengers survived.

Others were trying to help along the river. Arthur Bell, who had also stopped at the flooded road bridge with his wife, worked to reach people from another carriage. A young police constable, Leo Smidt, came after hearing an unexplained roar and began directing rescue work. Military personnel, farmers and local volunteers arrived through the night.

Within less than an hour, much of the volcanic flood had passed. It left broken carriages, mud and wreckage scattered across the river valley. The rescue became a search for people who had been carried downstream.

Of the 285 aboard, 151 died. Almost everyone who died had been in second class, which was at the front of the train. Many passengers in the last three first-class carriages did not initially realize what had happened ahead of them.

Those last carriages were still on the rails. Investigators concluded that the driver's emergency braking had helped prevent them from falling.

There is an uncomfortable distinction here. Ellis's warning and the driver's braking were not enough to stop the crash. But the change in speed may have stopped a still worse one. We should not invent how many individual lives either action saved.

On Christmas morning, Prime Minister Sidney Holland announced the tragedy by radio. The search continued along the river. Twenty of those who died were never found.

The investigation went back up the mountain, to the lake that had released the flood. It also found that a smaller lahar had already damaged the bridge in 1925 and that concerns about the crater lake's rim had been raised before 1953.

The board of inquiry concluded that no person was legally to blame. It recommended an early warning system upstream, so a flood could be detected before it reached the tracks.

In March 2007, a new lahar flowed down the same river. This time, monitoring and alarms were in place. The flow caused little damage and no injuries.

No warning system could change the night in 1953. But the difference between those two dates explains why the train reached a broken bridge without enough warning, and how later trains could have a chance to stop.

At Tangiwai, the water reached the bridge first. A man ran with a torch. The driver braked. The final carriages stayed on the rails. And rescuers brought twenty-one of twenty-two people out of the carriage that fell last.

That was not a rescue of everyone. It was the difference between the people who could still be reached and those the flood had already taken.
