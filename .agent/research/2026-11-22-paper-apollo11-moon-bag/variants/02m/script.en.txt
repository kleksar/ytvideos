Nancy Carlson paid nine hundred and ninety-five dollars for a white sample bag at a federal auction in 2015. The listing said it had flown in space. It didn't say the bag was from Apollo 11.

She saw dark marks inside the fabric and sent it to NASA. The lab found particles of lunar dust. When staff checked the numbers on the bag against mission records, they identified the outer bag that protected the first Moon samples on their journey home.

The mystery was solved. But NASA wouldn't return her purchase.

The agency said the bag belonged to the government and had never been cleared for sale. It offered to refund her. She refused. She had bought it from the government itself.

How had it reached that auction? The bag was among items seized in a case involving a former space museum curator. Its exact path into those possessions remains unclear. Inventory records confused it with a similar bag from Apollo 17. Officials auctioned it without recognizing the Apollo 11 connection.

In Kansas, a judge acknowledged that NASA apparently owned the bag before the sale and hadn't received proper notice. But the federal auction was complete, and Carlson had bought in good faith. The judge found no basis to reverse it. A separate case in Texas led to an order for NASA to hand over the bag.

In July 2017, Sotheby's sold the same bag for one million, eight hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred dollars, including fees.

NASA's test proved what it was. The government's own paperwork explained why NASA couldn't keep it. Two years earlier, that bag had been listed at a federal auction where no one recognized which Moon mission it came from.
