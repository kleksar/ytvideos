A bag carried on Apollo 11 sold at a government auction for just nine hundred and ninety-five dollars.

It was white, with a metal zipper and the words LUNAR SAMPLE RETURN printed across it. The listing said it had flown in space. It did not say which mission.

Nancy Carlson bought it in 2015. Curious about the dark marks inside, she sent it to NASA for testing.

The result changed everything. Those marks included Moon dust. NASA matched the bag's identification number to Apollo 11, the mission that first brought people to the Moon.

Neil Armstrong had used this outer bag to protect the first lunar samples on their journey home. But decades later, its records had been confused with those of another Moon bag.

The pouch had turned up among objects connected to a former museum curator convicted of stealing and selling space artifacts. When officials sold seized property to recover money, they failed to recognize what this particular bag was.

Now NASA wanted it back. The agency said the bag was government property and had never been approved for sale. It offered to refund Carlson's purchase.

She refused. She had bought it from the government, not from a stranger in a parking lot.

In court, the mix-up was acknowledged. But the government could not simply cancel a completed sale to a buyer who had acted in good faith.

A Texas judge later ordered NASA to hand the bag over.

On July twentieth, 2017, Sotheby's sold it for one million, eight hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred dollars, including fees.

The argument was not about whether the bag had reached the Moon. NASA's own tests established that. It was about whether the government could take back something it had sold by mistake.
