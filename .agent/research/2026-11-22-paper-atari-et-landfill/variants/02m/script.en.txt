In 1983, truckloads of Atari video games went to a landfill in New Mexico. Local newspapers reported the dumping. So how did it become a thirty-year mystery?

A year earlier, Atari had rushed its E.T. game into stores for Christmas. Programmer Howard Scott Warshaw had just over five weeks to make it. The game frustrated players, although the Smithsonian says more than a million copies sold.

Atari had a bigger problem. Stores were crowded with games, and warehouses held stock the company couldn't sell. James Heller, who arranged the disposal from Atari's El Paso operation, later described loads of mixed unwanted inventory.

Over the years, the landfill turned into a legend: millions of copies of one terrible game, buried in secret to hide a company's embarrassment.

In April 2014, a crew began digging. Someone found what looked like an Atari joystick grip. That alone proved nothing. Old electronics could have come from anywhere.

Then a boxed copy of E.T. came out of the dirt. The burial was real. The cameras had their answer.

But the crew kept digging.

Out came Centipede. Then Missile Command. Games that had once been hits were lying beside E.T. Why would Atari bury successful titles if one failed Christmas game explained everything?

The finds pointed to a warehouse problem during a much wider video-game market crash. Atari had discarded a mixture of products. The hole wasn't a special grave for E.T.

One recovered E.T. cartridge entered the Smithsonian's collection. It became evidence of what Atari threw away, not proof that one game had destroyed an industry.

The story of that museum cartridge started with a shipment that was never really secret.
