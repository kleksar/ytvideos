A dirt-covered E.T. game cartridge came out of a New Mexico landfill in 2014. Right beside it were games that had once been hits.

For thirty years, people had told a much simpler story. Atari made a terrible video game based on the movie E.T. Nobody wanted it. So the company secretly buried millions of copies in the desert.

There was a real game. And there was a real burial. But the explanation was wrong.

In 1982, Atari gave programmer Howard Scott Warshaw just over five weeks to turn E.T. into a Christmas release. The game sold more than a million copies, according to the Smithsonian. It also disappointed buyers, and Atari was left with too much stock.

But E.T. wasn't the only problem. The market had been flooded with games and competing machines.

In 1983, truckloads of Atari inventory went from a warehouse in Texas to a landfill in Alamogordo. Local newspapers reported the dumping at the time. It wasn't exactly a buried secret.

The secret was what was still down there.

In April 2014, a crew dug into the old landfill. They found E.T. cartridges. Then they found Centipede, Missile Command, and other Atari titles. Even successful games had been thrown away.

That changed the story. The hole wasn't a private grave for one disastrous game. It was a snapshot of a company stuck with inventory in a collapsing market.

One recovered E.T. cartridge went to the Smithsonian's collection. It had traveled from a Christmas product to garbage to a museum artifact.

The legend was real enough to dig up. It just wasn't the story people thought it was.
