Why did Atari send truckloads of video games to a desert landfill? And if its notorious E.T. game was really buried there, why did excavators find successful games beside it?

In 2014, a crowd gathered around a hole in Alamogordo, New Mexico. They were waiting for a machine to bring up something that had been underground for more than thirty years.

To understand why anyone cared, go back to Christmas 1982.

E.T. was one of the biggest movies in the world. Atari wanted a game bearing the same name on store shelves before the holiday. Programmer Howard Scott Warshaw had just over five weeks to make it. A normal project could take many months.

He finished. Players got an unusual adventure in which E.T. searched for pieces needed to contact his home planet. But navigating the game could be confusing, and falling into pits became one of the things people remembered most.

The game wasn't invisible. The Smithsonian records that more than a million copies sold. That still fell far short of what Atari needed to justify its expensive movie license.

And there was another problem. Stores were full of games. Competing companies were selling machines and cartridges, and Atari had more stock than it could move. The whole American console market was heading into a crash. No single cartridge could explain that.

In September 1983, trucks carried unwanted Atari inventory from a warehouse in El Paso, Texas, to the Alamogordo landfill. The load was destroyed and buried. Local newspapers actually reported the disposal. Yet over the years it became a story about millions of E.T. copies secretly hidden to erase the company's shame.

That story had a convenient villain: one disappointing Christmas release. It also had a physical location. If someone could find the dump, they could test the legend.

In April 2014, a documentary team, local organizers, and archaeologists opened the site. They had old reports and people who remembered the disposal, but knowing the general location wasn't the same as knowing where the games lay under decades of rubbish.

The excavator removed layers of debris. Spectators waited behind the barrier. Then the crew recovered a boxed copy of E.T.

The game existed. The burial was real. The cameras had their answer.

Except the dig wasn't finished.

More cartridges emerged. There was Centipede. There was Missile Command. There were other Atari games, some still in their packaging. These weren't all copies of one infamous title. They were different products, including games that had sold well.

That single detail changed the meaning of the hole. Atari hadn't created a special burial ground just to hide E.T. It had disposed of a mixed pile of inventory at a moment when the business could no longer sell what it had made.

One of the people present was Howard Scott Warshaw, the programmer who had built E.T. against that impossible-looking deadline. The archaeological find was now also a record of how an entire market had been simplified into blame for one game and one person.

And the story had one more destination.

A recovered E.T. cartridge, with documentation tying it to the excavation, entered the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. The museum did not treat it as proof that one bad game destroyed an industry. It treated it as an object that could tell a more complicated story.

The most interesting thing about Atari's buried game wasn't that somebody finally found E.T.

It was everything buried alongside it.
