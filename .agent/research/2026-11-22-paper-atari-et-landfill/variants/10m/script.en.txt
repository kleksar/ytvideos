How did a Christmas video game end up under a landfill, and then inside a national museum? Who moved the unwanted games there? And what did excavators find beside Atari's infamous E.T.?

It began with a deadline.

In 1982, Steven Spielberg's E.T. was a huge cinema success. Atari, then a giant in home video games, wanted the name on a game in time for Christmas. But the holiday deadline left almost no room for development.

The programmer was Howard Scott Warshaw. He had already made successful games for Atari, including Yars' Revenge and a game based on Raiders of the Lost Ark. For E.T., he had just over five weeks. Games ordinarily took months.

This was not simply a case of changing the pictures on an existing game. Warshaw tried to build an adventure around the movie. The player guided E.T. around an unfamiliar landscape, collected pieces to make contact with home, and tried to avoid obstacles. The design had ambitions that were hard to explain on a screen with very few pixels.

And those obstacles became its reputation. Players found the adventure confusing. E.T. repeatedly fell into pits. Getting out of one could lead straight into another. The most memorable part of the game was often the struggle to move around it.

That made E.T. easy to ridicule years later. But the sales history is less tidy than the joke. According to the Smithsonian, more than a million copies sold. That is not a game nobody bought. Yet Atari had paid heavily for the movie connection, and the sales were nowhere near what it needed to recover the cost.

While customers argued with the game, a larger problem was unfolding around the company.

Atari had been a leader in a rapidly growing business. Retailers stocked cartridges because the market looked unstoppable. Other companies brought their own consoles and games into the same stores. There were more products competing for the same buyers, and enthusiasm did not guarantee that every shipment would be sold.

When demand faltered, unsold inventory did not disappear from the books or the warehouses. It occupied space. It cost money to store. Older products became harder to move as new ones arrived. This was part of the wider North American video-game collapse of the early eighties, not a catastrophe that a single E.T. cartridge could cause.

By 1983, Atari had unwanted stock at a warehouse in El Paso, Texas. The company needed to dispose of it. James Heller, who managed the warehouse, later described arranging the removal. His recollection was of truckloads of games and other material, not an orderly collection of nothing but E.T.

The convoy went north to Alamogordo in New Mexico. Trucks brought the inventory to the city landfill. Much of it was crushed and covered. Local scavengers had an obvious reason to be interested in boxed electronics being discarded, and Heller later recalled efforts to stop people retrieving them.

The exact number of buried cartridges remains disputed. Different witnesses and later accounts describe different totals. We can say truckloads were dumped. We cannot honestly turn every truck into a measured pile of millions of E.T. games.

There is an even stranger part of the supposed secret.

Local newspapers reported the disposal in September 1983. The burial was not hidden so perfectly that nobody knew about it. The details were available long before the excavation.

Yet over time the event became a clean, irresistible story. Atari had made the worst game ever, failed spectacularly, and buried millions of copies out of embarrassment. The North American games business crashed; the legend gave that complicated event a single villain and a single physical grave.

The trouble was that a good legend is not the same thing as an accurate inventory list.

Thirty years later, somebody had to find the actual hole. That was harder than looking up the city on a map.

Joe Lewandowski, a local organizer, had seen Atari goods being dumped in 1983. He helped narrow the search using old records and clues from the layers of rubbish. The excavation needed official permission, and it could not simply open holes all over a closed landfill. The team had one area to test. A mistake meant another pile of ordinary trash.

In April 2014, crews began digging with archaeologists, filmmakers, and spectators watching. The machine removed loads of earth and rubbish. During the search, a visitor picked up what looked like the rubber grip of an Atari joystick. But old electronics could have been thrown away by anybody. A joystick handle alone could not prove that this was Atari's shipment.

The team kept going.

Then, from a bucket carried to the display table, came a boxed copy of E.T. Howard Scott Warshaw, who had made the game under that punishing Christmas deadline, was present at the dig. The cameras finally had the object they had come to find. The grave was real.

But finding E.T. answered only the easiest question. Why would Atari discard so much?

As more material came up, the inventory began to speak. There was Centipede, still in its wrapping, and copies of Missile Command. Other recovered titles included Pac-Man and Yars' Revenge. These weren't all games that buyers had rejected. Some had been hits.

If a failed E.T. release were the whole reason for the burial, the other titles were difficult to explain. If a company needed to empty its stores of excess stock, they made sense together.

One person at the site could explain how the loads got there. James Heller had worked in Atari's El Paso operation and arranged the disposal. He later told an interviewer that the material was excess inventory and that he had been instructed to save money. He did not know which executive had originally ordered the disposal. That matters: the account supports a warehouse decision, not a secret boardroom scene about hiding E.T. out of shame.

Heller said a disposal attempt in El Paso attracted people looking to recover the games. The loads were moved to Alamogordo, where keeping scavengers away seemed easier. He also recalled that the buried material included different game titles and other equipment. The excavators could confirm a mixed deposit, but they couldn't count everything still underground.

Even the famous concrete has a more complicated story. Heller said concrete was poured over part of the burial after people tried to retrieve goods. The excavation did not find a concrete cap in its particular hole. Those two observations need not contradict each other: one sampled hole is not the whole landfill.

By then the dig had produced a better answer than the legend. Warshaw's notorious Christmas game was present. So were successful products, which pointed to Atari's broader inventory problem. The person who arranged the dumping could describe the work, yet not name the executive who first made the decision. The evidence narrowed the story instead of solving every rumour.

There was still one last problem. How could a game pulled from a rubbish dump become a legitimate museum object?

Before the excavation, Smithsonian museum specialist Drew Robarge wanted a cartridge that could be tied to the original burial. A loose copy offered by a collector would not be enough: there would be no reliable chain linking it to the site, and material removed from the landfill without permission could not simply be collected. Robarge contacted the documentary team and arranged for a recovered artifact to be passed to the museum if the dig succeeded.

Now it had succeeded. The museum received an E.T. cartridge with documented excavation provenance. Its value was not that the game had become enjoyable or that anyone had proved E.T. alone caused the industry's collapse. The object connected a famous story to a real place, a real disposal operation, and an era when too many games were chasing too few buyers.

Think about the three people connected by that one object. Warshaw had to finish a Christmas game in weeks. Heller had to clear unwanted stock and move it out of El Paso. Years later, Robarge had to establish exactly where one discarded cartridge had come from.

The same piece of plastic passed from urgent product to unwanted goods to historical evidence. The dig didn't uncover a private grave containing nothing but one terrible game. It uncovered part of Atari's discarded stock, with successes and failures lying together.

That is why the games found beside E.T. mattered as much as E.T. itself.
