How does a game go from a Christmas blockbuster to a landfill, and then into a national museum? Who decided to bury it? And what did archaeologists actually find when they went looking for Atari's infamous E.T. cartridge?

A movie tie-in made those questions possible.

In 1982, Steven Spielberg's E.T. was a huge cinema success. Atari, then a giant in home video games, wanted the name on a game in time for Christmas. But the holiday deadline left almost no room for development.

The programmer was Howard Scott Warshaw. He had already made successful games for Atari, including Yars' Revenge and a game based on Raiders of the Lost Ark. For E.T., he had just over five weeks. Games ordinarily took months.

This was not simply a case of changing the pictures on an existing game. Warshaw tried to build an adventure around the movie. The player guided E.T. around an unfamiliar landscape, collected pieces to make contact with home, and tried to avoid obstacles. The design had ambitions that were hard to explain on a screen with very few pixels.

And those obstacles became its reputation. Players found the adventure confusing. E.T. repeatedly fell into pits. Getting out of one could lead straight into another. The most memorable part of the game was often the struggle to move around it.

That made E.T. easy to ridicule years later. But the sales history is less tidy than the joke. According to the Smithsonian, more than a million copies sold. That is not a game nobody bought. Yet Atari had paid heavily for the movie connection, and the sales were nowhere near what it needed to recover the cost.

While customers argued with the game, a larger problem was unfolding around the company.

Atari had been a leader in a rapidly growing business. Retailers stocked cartridges because the market looked unstoppable. Other companies brought their own consoles and games into the same stores. There were more products competing for the same buyers, and enthusiasm did not guarantee that every shipment would be sold.

When demand faltered, unsold inventory did not disappear from the books or the warehouses. It occupied space. It cost money to store. Older products became harder to move as new ones arrived. This was part of the wider North American video-game collapse of the early eighties, not a catastrophe that a single E.T. cartridge could cause.

By 1983, Atari had unwanted stock at a warehouse in El Paso, Texas. The company needed to dispose of it. James Heller, who managed the warehouse, later described arranging the removal. His recollection was of truckloads of games and other material, not an orderly collection of nothing but E.T.

The convoy went north to Alamogordo in New Mexico. Trucks brought the inventory to the city landfill. Much of it was crushed and covered. Local scavengers had an obvious reason to be interested in boxed electronics being discarded, and Heller later recalled efforts to stop people retrieving them.

The exact number of buried cartridges remains disputed. Different witnesses and later accounts describe different totals. We can say truckloads were dumped. We cannot honestly turn every truck into a measured pile of millions of E.T. games.

There is an even stranger part of the supposed secret.

Local newspapers reported the disposal in September 1983. The burial was not hidden so perfectly that nobody knew about it. The details were available long before the excavation.

Yet over time the event became a clean, irresistible story. Atari had made the worst game ever, failed spectacularly, and buried millions of copies out of embarrassment. The North American games business crashed; the legend gave that complicated event a single villain and a single physical grave.

The trouble was that a good legend is not the same thing as an accurate inventory list.

Thirty years later, people wanted to dig. A documentary project gathered the people and permissions needed to excavate the Alamogordo landfill. Local organizer Joe Lewandowski had worked to identify the likely location. Old reports, memories, and clues about the layers of the dump narrowed down the search.

In April 2014, the dig went ahead in front of spectators, journalists, and filmmakers. Archaeologists joined to record the objects and the site. A modern rubbish dump is not a neat collection of labelled boxes. Excavators had to work through other discarded material from different years. At first, the promised video games were still just a possibility under the next load.

Then came the recognizable material. Controllers and Atari packaging were found. Eventually a boxed E.T. emerged from the earth. The crowd had come to see the myth tested, and a real E.T. game was now on the table.

Howard Scott Warshaw was there. The person who had made the game under that tiny deadline could look at a physical copy that had spent decades underground. The spectacle proved that buried E.T. games existed, but it did not yet explain why Atari had discarded them.

That answer was in the rest of the excavation.

More titles came out. Centipede was there. Missile Command was there. Archaeologist Andrew Reinhard also reported games such as Pac-Man, Ms. Pac-Man, and Yars' Revenge. Some products appeared unused or still packaged. Others were damaged.

If this had been a secret operation to erase one terrible game, the successful games made very little sense. Their presence pointed back to the ordinary, less cinematic explanation: the disposal of mixed, unwanted inventory after a business downturn.

There were still uncertainties. The excavation exposed part of a large site, not a complete catalogue of every item buried in 1983. A worker's memory could help explain the operation without establishing the precise quantity in every truck. Photographs of a recovered object proved that it was found, not that all the later stories about it were true.

And the ground held a second question: what happens to ordinary objects when they become historical evidence?

Once a cartridge was dug out, it no longer had to be treated only as a failed product. A game box could tell us what Atari sold, what it could not sell, and how people later chose to remember an entire industry. Archaeologists documented finds. The city took possession of recovered objects. Some entered collections rather than returning to the landfill.

One E.T. cartridge was accepted by the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. The museum could identify it as an object from the 2014 excavation, with documentation tied to the site. It was not kept because it had suddenly become a great game. It was kept because people had built a history around it.

That last journey makes the story more interesting than the original legend.

The cartridge began as a product meant for a Christmas shopping rush. It was sold in enormous numbers, but not enough to solve the company's problems. It became unwanted stock in a business whose market was shifting. In a landfill, it was waste. Once excavated, it became evidence.

And it was never alone.

The burial happened. The supposedly secret grave was described by newspapers. The game was not literally unsold by everyone. The excavation uncovered many titles, not a mountain consisting only of E.T. The industry suffered a larger collapse than one badly received game could explain.

The museum object does not tell us who deserved blame for every decision inside Atari. It tells us something better supported: the story of one cursed cartridge had buried the much bigger story alongside it.

This time, the excavation brought both back into the light.
