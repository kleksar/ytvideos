How do you steal a cannon from one of America's leading science colleges? Why would anyone drive it three thousand miles? And what do you leave behind when its owners come to take it back?

The cannon stood at Caltech, in Pasadena, California. It was an antique on a wheeled carriage, traditionally fired on special college occasions. Students from Fleming House treated it as their own landmark.

Its history was less tidy than its place on the lawn. Caltech students had acquired the cannon from another local school decades earlier. Even its original military service was uncertain. But by 2006, whether it had ever fired in a war mattered less to the residents than the fact that it was theirs and was supposed to stay where they could see it.

In March 2006, it had been moved temporarily while part of the campus was being renovated. At five-thirty one morning, people dressed as movers arrived to take it away. Campus security challenged them. The movers produced work orders.

Those orders were fake.

For a while, Caltech thought it knew the culprits. Exactly twenty years before, in 1986, students from nearby Harvey Mudd College had stolen the same cannon as a prank and later brought it back. Another anniversary theft seemed entirely possible.

But nobody from Harvey Mudd claimed this one. Caltech's security chief began to consider whether it might be an actual theft. Then an anonymous caller said the cannon was safe and that an explanation would come soon.

Meanwhile, the cannon was travelling toward the opposite coast.

Why would students from MIT, in Massachusetts, want an antique gun in California? The answer started a year earlier.

In 2005, Caltech students travelled to MIT's event for prospective freshmen. They distributed hundreds of shirts packaged to display the letters MIT. Only after opening them could the recipients see the message on the back: a joke about not everyone getting into Caltech.

They also modified a campus sign. MIT students promptly modified it again. The rivalry was already becoming a contest over who could turn the other school's campus into part of the punchline.

The answer arrived on April sixth, 2006.

Visitors to MIT found Caltech's cannon on display near the Green Building. It had been transported across America and pointed back toward Pasadena. Someone had fastened an enormous imitation MIT class ring around its barrel.

This was not a plastic party prop. Students had machined the ring from aluminum, plated it with gold, and fixed it to the barrel with screws. A plaque named a moving company, Howe and Ser. The company was fictitious. Its name was another joke: it sounded like howitzer, a kind of artillery piece.

And the timing mattered. MIT was holding the same prospective-student weekend that Caltech had targeted the year before. A cannon in the middle of campus was a reply those visitors could hardly miss.

Caltech could now see where its landmark had gone. Getting it home was a different problem.

Students from Fleming House began planning as soon as they saw the pictures. Twenty-three students joined seven alumni. They flew to Massachusetts, and on April tenth they arrived with a truck to recover the cannon.

Even that return met a complication. Contemporary reporting says MIT campus police wanted proof that the visitors owned the cannon. Once that was resolved, the heavy gun was loaded for the trip west.

MIT students turned up for the departure. The giant ring came off first. Instead of taking it back, Caltech left that part of the joke with MIT.

Then the visitors placed a miniature cannon under glass where the real one had stood.

The old cannon went back toward Pasadena. The oversized ring eventually entered the MIT Museum collection. Both schools got to keep a souvenir of the same operation.

The first joke required convincing a guard to accept a work order. The last one needed almost nothing: a toy cannon small enough to fit under glass.
