What would it take to make an antique cannon disappear from a university without breaking through a gate? Why did it turn up three thousand miles away wearing a gold ring? And when its owners came for it, why did a second college have to let go of its prize?

Before the cannon went missing, it sat on a wheeled carriage at Caltech in Pasadena, California. Students of Fleming House considered it part of their identity. It was fired for special occasions, and a campus renovation had temporarily moved it from its usual position. There was nothing remotely like it in the ordinary furnishings of a student residence.

Its history already sounded like a student prank. The cannon had come to Caltech from another local school decades earlier, in circumstances that Caltech itself described with a wink. Whether the old gun had really seen military service was unclear. What was certain was that generations of students had come to recognise it.

And it had disappeared once before.

In 1986, eleven students from Harvey Mudd College, a nearby rival, had removed the same cannon. They eventually returned it. By March 2006 that story had become campus folklore, and the twentieth anniversary was approaching.

Keep that in mind, because it shaped the first reaction to what happened next.

On March twenty-eighth, 2006, at about five-thirty in the morning, a group arrived to move the cannon. They looked like workers doing an ordinary job. Campus security challenged them while they were taking it away. The crew produced work orders. They appeared to have a reason to be there.

The orders were not real. The moving company was not real either.

And once the cannon had gone, nobody immediately claimed responsibility.

Caltech initially suspected another anniversary prank by Harvey Mudd. That was a sensible guess based on the previous theft. But days went by without a familiar college admitting it had the cannon. Caltech's head of security began to fear something less amusing: the heavy old gun might simply have been stolen.

Then an anonymous caller gave him a peculiar reassurance. The cannon was safe, and its whereabouts would soon become clear.

To understand why someone had planned such a complicated disappearance, we need to go back one spring.

In April 2005, students from Caltech had flown to MIT in Cambridge, Massachusetts, for an event where future students were getting their first look at the campus. They brought hundreds of T-shirts. The packages showed the letters MIT on the front. Only when the shirts came out did the message on the back become visible: a joke that not everyone could get into Caltech.

A T-shirt is small. These students did not stop at shirts.

They also changed a prominent campus sign so that MIT appeared to be another institute of technology. MIT students changed the sign again, turning the joke back on the visitors. Caltech placed inflatable palm trees and sent a small blimp and balloons into the buildings. They even projected the name of their own institution onto an MIT building.

None of that damaged MIT's laboratories or changed anyone's grades. The whole point was to take an important college event and make MIT's own campus advertise its rival.

When MIT got its turn, it chose a very different kind of prop.

The fake moving crew in Pasadena was one end of a plan spanning both American coasts. What happened between the cannon's removal and its installation in Massachusetts is not fully established by the public reports. It would be easy to invent secret routes, whispered orders or a dramatic overnight drive, but the documents do not supply them. We know the cannon left on March twenty-eighth. The next public scene came nine days later.

On April sixth, visitors to MIT encountered an antique cannon near the Green Building. It was the Fleming House cannon from Caltech. The gun was pointed back toward Pasadena, as though MIT had parked a trophy that could still see its old home.

And it had been dressed for the occasion.

Around the barrel was an enormous copy of the MIT class ring, known as the Brass Rat. The ordinary ring is worn by graduates; this one had been made for a cannon. MIT's account describes three precision-machined aluminum sections weighing twenty-one pounds together. They were gold-plated, fitted around the barrel and held in place by four screws.

The ring was not the only label. A plaque identified the people responsible as the Howe and Ser Moving Company. Say that name aloud and it sounds like howitzer, a type of artillery weapon. The bogus movers had supplied a bogus business name that doubled as the punchline.

MIT did not select an ordinary day to display the cannon. This was Campus Preview Weekend, the same event Caltech's students had disrupted with shirts and banners in 2005. A year later, the visitors now had a real cannon standing in front of them, wearing MIT's ring.

The prank had answered the previous year's insult. But it had also created a practical problem: Caltech wanted its landmark back.

Caltech's official account says its students began planning the recovery once they saw where the cannon had appeared. Twenty-three students joined seven alumni. They flew east over the weekend, not to carry a banner or hand out a shirt, but to bring a real piece of their campus home.

They had considered a spectacular extraction. Contemporary newspaper reporting describes an idea to lift the cannon away by helicopter. Aviation rules and the difficulty of the operation made that plan impractical. The recovery would be done on the ground instead.

By the morning of April tenth, the Caltech party had reached the MIT campus with a truck. The goal was straightforward in words: take the cannon and load it for the journey west. The actual object, with its huge metal barrel and old wheeled carriage, was not something anyone could simply lift under an arm.

There was another twist before they could leave. According to an Associated Press report, MIT campus police wanted evidence that the visitors were entitled to take the cannon. The university whose students had moved it thousands of miles was now asking the people reclaiming it to establish ownership.

The party ultimately succeeded. Using ropes and a handmade dolly, the visitors hauled the cannon to a flatbed truck. MIT students were on hand. Rather than staging a physical fight for the gun, they gave it a send-off. An archived MIT post even preserves a message from the fictitious moving company inviting people to see the departure.

The giant ring was removed before the cannon left. Caltech reclaimed the antique gun, but the distinctive MIT-made accessory stayed behind.

Then Caltech answered with one final piece of engineering.

Where the enormous cannon had stood, they left a miniature version under glass, accompanied by a note. The fake movers had taken a historic object across a continent; the returning visitors had reduced their hosts' prize to something that fit on a small display.

Both campuses kept something tangible. The cannon returned to Pasadena. The golden-looking aluminum ring became an object in the MIT Museum's collection.

You might expect a stunt involving a cannon, a fabricated business and an intercontinental journey to end with one university publicly declaring victory. Instead, its most enduring objects tell two halves of the same joke.

At Caltech there was the cannon, back where students could see it. At MIT there was a giant ring made for a barrel no graduate would ever wear. And between those two objects sat the little replacement left behind by the people who came to collect their property.

What made the original move possible was neither a secret passage nor a new invention. It was an old, familiar sight: people arriving early, dressed for a job, holding paper that seemed to authorize it.
