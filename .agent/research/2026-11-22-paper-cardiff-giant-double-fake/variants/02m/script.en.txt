In 1869, two men dug a well in New York and found a ten-foot stone giant.

The farmer, William Newell, put a tent over the discovery and charged people to look. Soon the giant had investors, a tour, and crowds willing to pay to see what might be a petrified human body.

But this giant had been buried less than a year earlier.

A tobacco dealer named George Hull had ordered a huge block of gypsum carved into a man. He arranged for it to be brought to his relative's farm and secretly buried. The unsuspecting workers were digging exactly where the farmer wanted them to dig.

Even before the trick was fully exposed, some experts were suspicious. Gypsum could not survive in wet soil for centuries looking like that.

Then the famous showman P. T. Barnum offered to buy a share of the giant. The owners refused.

So Barnum found another sculptor, made his own copy, and advertised that one to paying visitors. His imitation competed with the first one—and attracted even more attention.

The people selling the original giant tried to stop Barnum through the courts. Imagine the problem: they wanted to defend an authentic attraction that was itself a counterfeit.

The stone workers who had carved Hull's giant were eventually named in newspaper reports. The secret was out. Both giants were fakes.

Yet the original survived its tour, its owners, and the public argument. It eventually reached a museum in Cooperstown, New York, where the carved stone itself became a historical object.

There was no giant man beneath the farm. There was a carved giant and a surprisingly successful business built around it.

And that business began when a farmer chose a very particular spot and hired two men to dig a well.
