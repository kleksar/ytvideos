How can one fake giant end up taking another fake giant to court?

The answer begins with a well, a carefully chosen patch of dirt, and a farmer who already knew what was underneath it.

In October 1869, two workers were digging a well near a barn in Cardiff, New York. Their tools struck stone. They cleared the earth and uncovered the foot of what appeared to be a human body. Except this body was enormous. The figure was roughly ten feet long.

The farm belonged to William Newell. He put a tent over the find and began charging visitors fifty cents to see it. People came to examine the stone man. Was it an ancient statue? Had a real giant somehow turned to stone? Some visitors were convinced. Others simply wanted a look.

The discovery was so successful that a group of businessmen bought shares in it and sent the giant on tour. They were selling tickets to a mystery.

But the mystery had been manufactured before the first shovel hit the ground.

The man behind it was George Hull, Newell's relative, a tobacco dealer who had argued with a preacher about the Bible's references to giants. Hull arranged for a large block of gypsum to be carved into a human shape. To make it look old, the surface was worked and stained. Then the massive sculpture traveled by rail and wagon to Newell's farm.

In 1868, they buried it. Nearly a year later, Newell hired those two unsuspecting men to dig a well in precisely that spot.

They found exactly what he wanted them to find.

There were reasons to doubt the stone body. A geologist could tell that gypsum would not hold up for ages in the damp soil there. Paleontologist Othniel Charles Marsh examined the object and publicly called it a recent hoax. And the discovery had an awkward beginning: the farmer himself had selected the place to dig.

None of that immediately made the crowds disappear.

Then P. T. Barnum entered the story. He ran popular exhibitions, and he knew an attraction when he saw one. Barnum tried to buy a stake in the Cardiff Giant. The investors refused to sell.

That should have been the end of his involvement. Instead, Barnum commissioned a second giant.

He displayed his copy in Manhattan, presenting it as the genuine attraction. The first giant's owners had invested in transporting their exhibit and collecting admissions. Now another man was selling tickets to an almost identical object.

And Barnum was better at drawing a crowd. His duplicate was paraded through the streets, and it drew thousands of visitors. When the original finally appeared in New York, it could not count on being the most popular giant in town.

The investors behind the first giant went to court in an attempt to stop Barnum's exhibition. But their claim came with an embarrassing problem. The thing they wanted protected as the authentic Cardiff Giant was itself a carving made to deceive paying customers.

The request to halt Barnum's show failed. The precise courtroom dialogue repeated in later accounts is not securely established, but the practical result was clear: Barnum kept exhibiting his giant.

Meanwhile, the other part of the illusion was falling apart. The people who had carved Hull's figure were identified in newspaper investigations. Hull acknowledged his role. By early 1870, the giant was no longer a credible petrified human being.

It still existed, though. Its owners could keep displaying a very large stone sculpture, even when its story was exposed.

The Cardiff Giant traveled through exhibitions, changed hands, and eventually ended up at the Farmers' Museum in Cooperstown. The same object that people once paid to see as proof of an ancient giant became a real artifact of nineteenth-century showmanship.

So which giant was real? Neither was a petrified person. But one really had been planted in a field, and another really had been built to steal its audience.

And before the competing attractions, the lawyers, and the newspaper confessions, everything depended on a farmer pointing to the right patch of ground.
