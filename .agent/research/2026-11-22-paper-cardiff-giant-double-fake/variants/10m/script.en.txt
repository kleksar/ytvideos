What happens when two exhibitors accuse each other of selling a fake—and both are right?

In New York in 1869, the answer involved a ten-foot stone man, a traveling exhibition, a much better showman, and a legal dispute that could not turn a carving into a human corpse.

To see how it happened, begin in a field near a barn.

Two men named Gideon Emmons and Henry Nichols had been hired to dig a well on the farm of William Newell in Cardiff, New York. On October 16, their tools struck stone. They cleared the soil and found something that looked like a human foot. They kept digging.

The object stretched roughly ten feet from head to toe. It had the shape of a man lying beneath the ground, but it was made of stone. There was no convincing explanation for how a person that size could have ended up there.

Newell did not send everyone away while the matter was examined. He erected a tent over the discovery. Admission cost fifty cents. The stone man had become a business almost as soon as the dirt was removed.

That detail matters because the exhibition was not an accidental response to an unexpected archaeological find. It was the last step in a plan started more than a year earlier.

George Hull, a tobacco dealer and relative of Newell, had argued with a preacher about the passage in the Bible that refers to giants. Later accounts describe Hull's scheme as both a way to challenge literal belief and a way to make money. We do not have to invent a private speech to understand what Hull actually did next.

He arranged for a massive slab of gypsum to be obtained in Iowa. The material was large enough to carve into an entire human figure. It was also a curious choice for something supposed to have lain in the earth for ages: gypsum can be damaged by water.

Hull had stonecutters work the slab into a giant. This was not a skeleton assembled from ancient bones. It was one uninterrupted, person-shaped block of rock.

The maker needed more than a recognizable face. To persuade viewers that the object had been buried for a very long time, the surface had to look weathered. It was scraped, worked, and stained. The giant was made to appear as though the soil had changed it.

Then came the part of the operation that a drawing of a giant tends to hide: transportation. Hull's heavy stone man traveled by rail and wagon for a journey of roughly a thousand miles before reaching the farm in New York. A colossal carving does not simply appear in a barnyard without a route or people to move it.

In the night, Hull and his associates put the giant into a pit and covered it with earth. They left it there for close to a year.

That wait created the apparent history of the object. When the workers finally found it in October 1869, it was not newly carved stone sitting in a workshop. It was a newly carved stone that had actually spent months underground.

Now put yourself among the first spectators. The workers had dug down into the soil. They had brought up something large enough to be seen from across a room. The marks on it seemed old. And there was no obvious sign on the surface that said who had made it.

One explanation was that someone had buried an ancient statue. Another was that this was an enormous human being petrified into stone. People disagreed, but both possibilities helped sell tickets. The attraction did not need every visitor to believe the same story.

Word spread. Visitors came from other towns. Some were convinced; others came precisely because the object was controversial. Newell continued charging for admission.

Before long, a group of investors purchased shares in the exhibit. The Cardiff Giant was taken beyond the farm and shown in Syracuse and Albany. Instead of waiting for the public to visit one patch of New York countryside, the owners carried their attraction to larger audiences.

The story was moving faster than its verification.

There were cracks in the claimed antiquity. Specialists questioned how the gypsum could have survived in wet ground for centuries without deteriorating. A stone that cannot endure the local conditions is poor evidence of an age-old burial.

Then paleontologist Othniel Charles Marsh saw the giant. He did not present the carving as a new species or as proof of a biblical account. He called it of very recent origin—a humbug.

So the public had two competing claims about the same object. The exhibition said, in effect, here is something that might rewrite what we know about the past. The scientific objection was much more ordinary: here is something that looks recently made.

Neither argument immediately ended the show.

That is where the story changes from a fake discovery to a contest between two fakes.

P. T. Barnum was already known for staging attractions. When he learned how many people wanted to see the Cardiff Giant, he tried to buy an interest in it. One detailed account says he offered fifty thousand dollars for a quarter stake. The owners rejected the offer.

Barnum did not need permission to make stone look like stone. He hired another sculptor to produce a replica of the famous giant.

He could have displayed it honestly as a copy and invited people to compare the two. Instead, advertisements presented Barnum's display as the genuine Cardiff Giant.

The first owners had created a market for an object they knew was not what it seemed. Barnum used that same uncertainty against them.

He also knew how to make a public entrance. An account by a museum curator describes Barnum's giant being paraded down Broadway with a team of horses and a large procession. People did not need to read a technical dispute about gypsum to notice a giant moving through New York.

His copy drew thousands of paying visitors. When the original Cardiff Giant was displayed only a short distance away, Barnum's rival attraction could still draw the bigger crowd.

There were now two objects with the same claim. Each could be presented as the stone man found on a farm. One was the original deliberately buried sculpture. The other was a copy made to imitate that attraction. Neither was a petrified human being.

This created a problem that could not be solved simply by pointing to the first object's age. Being the first counterfeit did not make it the thing it claimed to be.

The investors who owned Hull's carving sought legal help to stop Barnum's exhibition. They wanted to protect their attraction from a competing imitation. Their request for an injunction was denied.

Later retellings give a judge a very sharp joke about making the original giant swear that it was genuine. That line is famous, but the exact words are not securely supported by the sources checked here. The result we can use without inventing courtroom dialogue is that the attempt to shut Barnum down failed.

Meanwhile, the original secret was coming apart in public.

Reporters followed the trail back toward the stone's creation. Men who had worked on the sculpture were identified. Accounts of the transportation and burial undermined the idea of a body that had waited underground for centuries. Hull acknowledged his role in the hoax.

By early 1870, the question had changed. It was no longer sensible to ask which of the two stone men was a real ancient giant. The useful question was why the giant remained an attraction even after the mechanism of the fraud was known.

The answer is visible in the way the exhibition itself changed. A crowd could stop believing in a petrified man and still pay to see the actual carved object. It had already been part of a national argument, a tour, and a fight between rival showmen.

But a discovered secret changes the market. More replicas appeared. What had once seemed like a unique marvel became an exhibit people could encounter in several places, with competing advertisements and competing claims.

Interest eventually declined. The original was sold and moved through exhibitions, fairs, and private ownership. It even broke at the knees at some point in its later travels, according to a curator who handled it for a museum show.

One more consequence belongs to the man who started it. Hull did not simply vanish into the success of the Cardiff Giant. Accounts of his later life record another attempt to fool spectators with a different fake petrified man, this time in Colorado. That scheme did not recreate the first one's triumph. The trick had become familiar.

The Cardiff Giant itself outlasted its makers and its competing copy's moment of fame. In the twentieth century it was acquired by the Farmers' Museum in Cooperstown, near the region where it had been buried. In 2023 it traveled on loan to the Bruce Museum for an exhibition about historic natural-history hoaxes.

For that journey, museum staff had to deal with the real practical problem that Hull had once faced: a stone giant weighing nearly three thousand pounds is difficult to move. They used a hoist and a reinforced platform. The object was still stone, even though everything claimed about its human origin had been false.

That is the ending the first ticket buyers could not have predicted. The statue never proved that ancient giants had lived in America. It became evidence of how a discovery could be staged, advertised, disputed, copied, and sold.

Barnum's replica was an imitation of a carefully planted imitation. The court case could not transform either into a genuine body. And the original eventually found a home where people could look at it without being told it was a prehistoric person.

Its first audience saw a figure emerging from the earth. Its later audience sees the tool marks of a fabricated attraction.

And none of those audiences would have been there without a farmer who knew exactly where to tell two workers to dig.
