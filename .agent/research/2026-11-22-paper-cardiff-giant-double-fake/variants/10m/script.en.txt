How could people charge admission to see a giant they knew was fake? And when another showman made a copy, why did the owners of the first fake try to stop him in court?

The answer begins with two men hired to dig a well. They had no reason to expect that their employer had already chosen what they would find.

On October 16, 1869, Gideon Emmons and Henry Nichols were working beside a barn on William Newell's farm in Cardiff, New York. Their tools hit stone. They moved the soil aside, uncovered what looked like a human foot, and kept digging.

The rest of the figure was enormous: about ten feet from head to toe. It lay underground like a body, but it was stone. People could see that something had really been buried there. They could not see who had made it.

Newell put up a tent and charged fifty cents to look. Some visitors wondered whether the giant was a statue. Others considered the possibility of a person turned to stone. Many came simply because everybody was talking about it.

Soon the spectators were no longer the only people paying. Investors bought shares in the attraction and took it on tour. The discovery had become a business with tickets, transport, and competitors waiting to see if the crowds would last.

But the two well-diggers had found precisely what Newell intended them to find.

More than a year earlier, Newell's relative George Hull had arranged to make the giant. Hull was a tobacco dealer. According to later accounts, an argument about biblical giants helped inspire the scheme. Whatever his private reasons, the work he commissioned is much easier to trace.

A large block of gypsum came from Iowa. Stonecutters carved it into a reclining human figure and worked the surface to make it look older. The stone was stained and marked. It had to resemble something that had spent far longer underground than it actually would.

Then the giant had to travel. The heavy carving was carried by rail and wagon for roughly a thousand miles to Newell's farm. It was not a mystery object that suddenly appeared in a field. People had cut it, moved it, and paid to bring it there.

In 1868 it was buried in a pit. The men behind the scheme left it under the soil for months. When Newell later hired Emmons and Nichols, he directed them to the chosen spot.

That was the trick's strongest piece of theater. The workers did not need to pretend to be surprised. They were surprised. The audience could believe the account of a discovery without knowing that someone else had arranged the discovery beforehand.

And the giant was large enough to make doubt feel inconvenient. Visitors stood beside an object that had been dug out of real earth. They saw a human outline and a weathered surface, not a receipt from a stonecutter.

But a surprising excavation is not the same thing as proof of an ancient body.

The material itself was a problem. Gypsum is vulnerable to water. Specialists questioned how such a figure could have remained intact for centuries in damp ground. Paleontologist Othniel Charles Marsh examined the giant and identified it as a recent hoax.

Those objections did not immediately empty the tent. People who doubted the story could still want to inspect the object for themselves. And a curious visitor paid the same admission as a convinced one.

The investors had a successful attraction and an increasingly difficult claim to defend. Then a far more experienced exhibitor wanted a piece of it.

P. T. Barnum tried to buy an interest in the Cardiff Giant. The owners refused. For a moment, keeping control of the only giant must have seemed like keeping control of the audience.

Barnum found another way in. He commissioned a sculptor to make a second giant.

The copy was not advertised merely as a replica for study. Barnum presented it as the real Cardiff Giant. The people who had paid to see a famous stone man were now being offered a famous stone man by someone with an even bigger talent for publicity.

Barnum made sure New York noticed. A museum curator's account describes the copy being carried down Broadway in a procession with horses. Long before anyone reached the ticket booth, the giant itself was an advertisement.

His attraction drew thousands of visitors. When the first Cardiff Giant was displayed nearby, being the first one did not guarantee the bigger crowd. The original owners now faced the kind of competition they had never planned for: somebody else had copied their deception and was selling it more effectively.

It was easy to tell the two carvings apart if you knew their history. One had been carved for Hull and hidden on a farm. Barnum's had been made later to imitate the sensation. But if either show claimed to display a prehistoric human body, neither claim was true.

The original investors tried to stop Barnum's exhibition in court. Their attempted injunction failed.

Later accounts give the judge a memorable joke about asking the giant itself to swear that it was genuine. The exact courtroom words are not securely established, so the important fact is not the joke. The owners sought a legal way to halt the rival show, and they did not get one.

That failure did not prove Barnum's carving was genuine. It left the rival attraction open while the first giant's deeper secret was being exposed.

Reports began to identify the stonecutters and explain how Hull had arranged the figure's journey and burial. Hull acknowledged his part in the deception. By the end of 1869 and into early 1870, the claim of a petrified giant had lost the ground beneath it.

Consider what the original investors were left with. They still owned a large object. They could point to the real hole where it had been discovered. They could describe the genuine excitement of the first visitors. None of those things could turn the object into an ancient person.

And the public story had become almost as unusual as the object. A fabricated find had attracted crowds, investors, scientific criticism, an imitation and a failed effort to stop that imitation in court. People no longer had to believe in a petrified body to find that sequence worth seeing.

More copies appeared, and the initial sensation faded. The original traveled through exhibitions and changed owners. It was no longer the unique mystery it had been when the soil first came off its face.

Yet one version of the giant outlasted the ticket war. The original carving eventually entered the Farmers' Museum in Cooperstown, New York. In 2023 it was lent to the Bruce Museum for an exhibition about natural-history hoaxes.

Moving it still presented a very real problem. A curator described a stone object weighing nearly three thousand pounds, requiring lifting equipment and a reinforced platform. More than a century after Hull arranged its transport, people once again had to work out how to move the same heavy figure safely.

There is no ancient giant inside that stone. There never was. But the object in the museum really is the one hidden on the farm. It really was dug up by workers who did not know the plan. And its rival really was made because Barnum could not buy into the first exhibition.

The first fake had a genuine history. The second fake copied the first one's claim. The court could not settle that history by making either one a real body.

The strangest part is where the story began: not with an ancient burial, but with a farmer sending two unsuspecting workers to a spot he had already chosen.
