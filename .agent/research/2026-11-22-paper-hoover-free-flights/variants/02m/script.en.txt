A vacuum cleaner in Britain cost £119. The two “free” return flights offered with it could cost Hoover more than £500.

In 1992, Hoover wanted to sell more appliances. Its British business offered two airline tickets to customers who spent over £100. The first destinations were in Europe. Then the offer expanded to the United States.

Think about that price tag. People could spend £119 on a cleaner and apply for tickets worth several times as much. Many bought the cheapest qualifying machine. The free trip, not the vacuum, was what they wanted.

There was paperwork. Customers had to send forms, choose dates and airports, and wait for a flight offer. Hoover had expected many people to give up along the way.

But the applications kept coming. Customers said they couldn't get the flights they had been promised. A consumer group formed. A television investigation brought the complaints to a wider audience. In Parliament, an MP reported that hundreds of people had complained and asked for an inquiry.

The company couldn't simply treat the whole thing as a successful sales campaign. In March 1993, its American parent, Maytag, announced a rescue effort. Three senior executives lost their jobs. Hoover bought seats from airlines and chartered planes to try to meet its obligations.

The cost kept growing. A year later, Hoover reported £48 million spent on the promotion. It said 220,000 customers had flown or had seats booked.

So this wasn't a story where nobody got a ticket. Many did. And it wasn't a harmless mistake either: others fought over the conditions and the delays long after the ads stopped.
