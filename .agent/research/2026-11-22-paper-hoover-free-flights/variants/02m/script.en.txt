In 1992, Pauline Keen already had an almost-new vacuum cleaner. She bought another one anyway. Her family wanted to fly to New York.

Hoover was offering two return flights with a qualifying appliance purchase. The cheapest eligible cleaner cost £119. Two tickets to America could cost Hoover more than £500. For a family planning a holiday, that looked like a bargain.

But a receipt was not a plane ticket. Buyers had to send paperwork, meet a tight deadline, and wait for travel dates to be confirmed. Pauline and her son Timothy exchanged letters and calls with Hoover's agents. Twice, they were asked for copies of documents. In the end, the disputed deadline could not be proved, and the family was told it did not qualify. They planned their trip without Hoover and sold the extra cleaner at a loss.

Other customers kept asking for their flights. Complaints reached a BBC consumer programme and Parliament. Hoover's American owner, Maytag, set aside £20 million. Hoover announced plans to buy seats on more than 2,200 flights and charter entire aircraft.

And some customers really did fly. Steve and Mandy Holbrook took their children to Florida on Hoover tickets. Their requested dates were unavailable, and their connections were long. But the holiday happened.

In April 1994, Hoover reported £48 million in promotion costs. It said 220,000 people had flown or had flights booked. A booking wasn't proof that every passenger had already travelled, or that every complaint had been settled.

The Keens were arranging their own journey. The Holbrooks had returned from theirs. Hoover was paying to organise flights for the people it had persuaded to buy appliances.

All of this began with a strange calculation: the promise of a holiday made another vacuum cleaner seem worth buying, even when the first one worked.
