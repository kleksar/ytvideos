Why would a company offer two airline tickets with a vacuum cleaner? Why would customers buy appliances they barely wanted? And how could a sales promotion cost its owner tens of millions of pounds?

In Britain in 1992, Hoover was selling vacuum cleaners and other household appliances. It needed customers to walk into shops. Its answer was an offer that made the product almost secondary: spend more than £100 on Hoover goods and become eligible for two return flights.

At first, the flights were to European destinations. Later that year the offer expanded to the United States. For a shopper looking at a cleaner priced at £119, that was a remarkable comparison. A pair of return tickets to New York could cost Hoover more than £500.

The first question is why anyone would buy the expensive machine. They didn't have to. Customers picked the least costly products that qualified. Reports from the time described people who were more interested in obtaining the travel offer than in taking the appliance home.

The giveaway had a catch, although not a hidden one. Buying a product did not put two tickets straight into a customer's hand. There were forms and deadlines. Applicants had to propose travel dates and departure points, and the company could offer alternatives. It was an administrative journey before the actual journey.

Hoover's plan depended on how many people would complete that process. But the size of the reward made people unusually determined to finish it. The company had created a situation where the incentive to claim the gift was stronger than the incentive to buy the product.

Then the problem moved out of the shops and into the mail.

Customers waited for confirmations and complained that they were not receiving usable flights. In March 1993, members of the British Parliament signed a motion referring to a newspaper investigation and the BBC's Watchdog programme. The motion said the programme had received more than 600 complaints and inquiries since March 8. It also reported that only a small proportion of a sample of previous complainants had seen their cases resolved. Those were complaints and allegations, not a court finding that every application was valid.

Meanwhile, the offer was still being described as a bargain. That contrast became the central problem. For Hoover, an application was an obligation to manage. For the customer, it was the reason for making the purchase.

By the end of March 1993, the company's American owner, Maytag, said the campaign had brought significant unexpected costs. Three senior Hoover managers lost their jobs. The business set aside £20 million to deal with the trouble.

That did not end the story. In April, Hoover announced that it would buy seats on thousands of scheduled flights and charter aircraft of its own. It was no longer merely selling vacuum cleaners. It was trying to arrange holidays for the people who had bought them.

And the money kept moving in the wrong direction. In April 1994, Hoover disclosed that the promotion had cost £48 million, more than twice the sum reserved a year earlier. The company said 220,000 customers had either flown or been booked onto flights.

This matters because a simpler telling gets the ending wrong. Hoover did not promise flights and give absolutely none. Many people received or were booked for travel. Others continued to contest what they had been offered, when, and under which conditions. The lawsuits and reputational damage did not vanish when the booking figures improved.

The fiasco also did not stay neatly inside a marketing department. It cost senior managers their jobs, forced the American parent to intervene, brought complaints before Parliament, and became a lasting example of what happens when the value of a promotion dwarfs the value of the thing being sold.

The tempting question is whether the British shoppers outsmarted Hoover. But they largely did what the advertisement invited them to do. They bought a qualifying product and tried to collect the promised benefit.

The company had bet that enough of them would not.

The most revealing object in this story is not an aeroplane, or even a lawsuit. It is a mundane shop label: £119 for a vacuum cleaner, with two transatlantic tickets riding on the purchase. That gap between the price and the promise explains both the rush to buy and the cost of keeping the promise.
