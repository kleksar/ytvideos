How can a company sell an appliance for around a hundred pounds and end up arranging air travel for hundreds of thousands of people? What happens when customers take a promotion more seriously than the company planned? And who pays when the paperwork turns a promised holiday into a long dispute?

The appliance was a Hoover vacuum cleaner. The country was Britain. The year was 1992.

Hoover's British business sold vacuum cleaners, washing machines and other household appliances. In a difficult market, it wanted more people to buy. A familiar idea would have been to reduce prices. Hoover chose a more striking one. Spend more than £100 on its goods, and you could apply for two return flights.

The first version, launched in the summer, offered European destinations. That was already a substantial incentive. Then, later in 1992, the promotion expanded across the Atlantic, with trips to places such as New York and Orlando. The change made the advertised benefit much more valuable.

Consider the shopper's side of the bargain. A qualifying vacuum cleaner could be bought for £119. Two return tickets to the United States could cost Hoover more than £500. The purchaser was paying for a household appliance and potentially receiving travel worth several times the price of that appliance.

It was a product offer with a strange imbalance. The journey might be worth more to a customer than the machine that supposedly justified it.

For Hoover, more appliances sold meant the promotion was attracting attention. But that wasn't the full calculation. Every customer who completed the flight process could create a cost far beyond the income from the purchase. Extra sales and a flood of successful claims were not the same kind of success.

Contemporary reporting described buyers choosing the cheapest qualifying machines. Some were drawn mainly by the flights. Hoover was a brand associated with keeping houses clean. For these customers it became a route to the airport.

There was a practical obstacle between buying and flying. A qualifying receipt did not instantly become a pair of boarding passes. Customers had to make applications, send documentation, propose dates and airports, and wait for a travel arrangement. The terms allowed alternatives to the dates customers preferred.

It is possible to imagine why a company might expect many people to abandon a process like that. But we don't need to invent a meeting where executives said so. The material point is visible in the result: an unusually valuable reward gave buyers a strong reason to keep applying.

The business had to handle two things at once. It had attracted shoppers with a simple offer, and it had created a complex obligation that would be resolved much later, through other companies, schedules and paperwork.

That difference between the simple advertisement and the difficult claim process became the story.

As 1992 turned into 1993, complaints accumulated. People described repeated efforts to get dates confirmed, travel arrangements that did not suit them, and documents that did not arrive. These reports weren't proof that every application complied with every rule. But they showed that the sale had not finished at the checkout.

A British parliamentary motion in March 1993 recorded complaints reported by the Daily Record newspaper and the BBC's Watchdog programme. It stated that Watchdog had received more than 600 complaints and enquiries since March 8. It also referred to a sample of 300 earlier complainants, of whom only seven percent reportedly had their issues resolved by the middle of March.

Those figures describe the people who complained or were surveyed, not every Hoover customer. The motion accused the promotion's operators of placing obstacles in customers' way and urged the government to examine free-flight offers. A parliamentary motion is evidence of the controversy and the allegations made in public, not a final legal finding of fraud.

But the argument was now visible far beyond the shops. The question was no longer whether the deal sounded attractive. It was whether a purchaser who had followed its conditions would receive usable flights.

For customers, the ticket was the point of the purchase. For Hoover, each fulfilled trip added to the bill.

In late March 1993, the American parent company, Maytag, intervened. It announced a provision of £20 million for the promotion and removed three senior Hoover managers. The company's public explanation referred to demand far beyond expectations and difficulties with travel operators. It said commitments would be honored subject to the terms and conditions.

A promotional campaign had reached the point where the parent company was paying to repair it. But setting aside the money did not put passengers in airline seats.

That took another step.

In April 1993, Hoover announced a plan to buy space on more than 2,200 flights operated by eleven airlines and to charter entire aircraft as well. It was planning European journeys and transatlantic travel, and it intended to strengthen the staff and systems handling applications.

Think about the change in what this business was now doing. It had begun by trying to sell more vacuum cleaners. It had ended up negotiating blocks of airline seats and deciding how to get customers from one country to another. The promise had become a second operation, one for which the company had not originally been known.

The plan also showed that there was real travel to arrange, not only angry letters to answer. A claim that absolutely nobody received flights would be wrong.

Still, some customers remained dissatisfied. In early 1994, a pressure group led by Harry Cichy was pursuing complaints, and newspapers reported the prospect of court action. The dispute was no longer just about a general shortage of seats. It was about whether specific people had received the journeys they thought they had qualified for, and whether the alternatives offered under the small print were acceptable.

One parliamentary motion from 1994 described a concrete example involving a customer who had supplied three different dates and airports, then complained about a narrow set of substitute dates. That was a politician's allegation in support of a proposed change to the law; we should not turn it into a court verdict. But it reveals exactly why the conflict lasted: a technically offered flight was not necessarily the holiday a purchaser believed had been promised.

Then a financial figure changed the scale of the story.

In April 1994, Hoover reported that the promotion had cost £48 million. The original £20 million provision was no longer enough. The company said about 220,000 customers had either flown or been booked on flights. It had assembled a staff team of 250 to cope with the applications.

That combination is the real twist. Hundreds of thousands of people had received or were due to receive a travel booking, while the campaign was still remembered as a disaster. Those two things do not contradict each other. The company could carry out a huge number of journeys and still pay an enormous price, in money and in trust, for the way it had sold them.

Consider what a successful sale normally means. The shop receives payment, hands over an appliance, and eventually deals with any warranty problem. Here the sale was also the start of a holiday application. One transaction produced another promise whose cost depended on how many people followed through. As more people did exactly that, the total cost grew.

In 1995, Hoover's European business was sold to the Italian group Candy. That corporate change had several causes and should not be presented as proof that the flight promotion alone forced the sale. It is fairer to say that the giveaway became one conspicuous part of the damage suffered by the business and its reputation.

And the story did not disappear from public memory. A BBC television documentary in 2004 revisited the free-flight affair. By then, the promotion had become a familiar British example of a spectacularly misjudged sales incentive. Customers had wanted their flights; the company had found itself dealing with travel arrangements, complaints, regulators and a bill measured in tens of millions of pounds.

There is a neat way to tell this as a joke: a vacuum cleaner company accidentally became a travel agency. That is funny, but it leaves out the people on the other side of the bargain. Some did fly. Others spent months pursuing what they believed they were owed. Public reporting and parliamentary motions captured both the size of the response and the disagreement over the conditions. Neither the frustration of customers nor the company's effort to deliver bookings should be erased to make the joke cleaner.

The idea behind the promotion was to make a product irresistible. The mistake was that the offer itself became the product.

A shopper did not need to be unusually clever to notice the difference between £119 for a cleaner and more than £500 for two flights. The company put that difference directly in front of them. Each completed claim turned the dramatic promise into an expense.

So when the cost finally appeared in Hoover's accounts, it was not one freak accident on a single day. It was the accumulated result of a simple advertisement, many ordinary purchasing decisions, a difficult claim process and a large number of real travel bookings.

The deal that was supposed to make people want a vacuum cleaner made them want something else. And once customers had bought the appliance, the promise followed the company all the way to the airport.
