On a 2004 invoice, a New York gallery listed an $8.3 million painting as a Mark Rothko. It also listed the painting's past: a private Swiss collection.

The buyers, Domenico and Eleanore De Sole, wanted more than a label. Domenico asked the gallery director for an assurance in writing. He got one.

But who could confirm the Swiss owner's story?

For years, dealer Glafira Rosales had been bringing supposedly unknown works by famous American painters to New York. Between 1994 and 2009, she supplied more than sixty such works to two galleries. Their customers paid over eighty million dollars.

There were warnings. In 2003, an art research foundation could not authenticate a supposed Pollock and questioned its ownership history. Tests in 2008 found materials in two purported Motherwells that did not fit their claimed dates.

The investigation eventually moved from paint to payments. Rosales had claimed much of the money went to her anonymous clients. That gave prosecutors another way to test the story.

In 2013, Rosales pleaded guilty to taking part in the fraud. Prosecutors said the Swiss client never existed. The Spanish collector supposedly behind other paintings had never owned them. A painter working in a home in Queens had made the pictures; false signatures and artificial aging helped sell them as masterpieces.

Knoedler had closed in 2011. Its former director denied knowingly selling fakes. Later civil cases ended in settlements, not a verdict proving she knew.

An invoice could give a painting a price, an artist, and a convincing past. But it couldn't give that Swiss collector an existence.

And before anyone knew that, the whole thing looked like ordinary paperwork.
