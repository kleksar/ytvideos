How can a painting appear decades after its supposed creator died? Why would a respected New York gallery trust a seller who could not identify the owner? And what happens when that owner turns out not to exist?

In 2004, a couple buying art through the Knoedler gallery received an invoice for a painting attributed to Mark Rothko. It was dated 1956. The price on the gallery's invoice was 8.3 million dollars.

The page did more than name an artist. Under the painting's ownership history it placed a private collection in Switzerland. It said the work had passed from the artist to that collection, then by inheritance to the current owner.

For an expensive painting, that chain of custody matters. A picture can resemble a Rothko. A story about where it has been is supposed to help explain why it really is one.

Knoedler was an established Manhattan art gallery. From 1994 onward, dealer Glafira Rosales had been bringing it paintings attributed to major American artists. They were previously unknown works, apparently emerging from private ownership after decades out of public view.

There was a catch. The supposed owner was not available to confirm the story.

Rosales said that some paintings belonged to an anonymous client associated with Switzerland. Others, she said, came from a Spanish collector. The gallery had works to show buyers, but the personal history behind them depended heavily on what the dealer said.

And it was not just one painting.

Between 1994 and 2009, Rosales sold more than sixty purported masterworks to two Manhattan galleries for about 33.2 million dollars. The galleries then sold them to buyers for more than eighty million.

How could so many long-lost works appear at once?

Behind the names on the canvases was an artist working in his home in Queens, New York. He made the paintings. In some cases, he applied a famous signature; in others, a co-conspirator did. The paintings were also treated to give them the appearance of age.

That explanation came from the federal case, not from an art-world rumor.

But doubts had already surfaced years before the guilty plea.

In 2003, the International Foundation for Art Research examined the claimed history of a painting attributed to Jackson Pollock. Its report could not authenticate it and questioned the proposed ownership trail. More scrutiny followed. In 2008, a forensic conservator reported that materials in two paintings attributed to Robert Motherwell were inconsistent with when those paintings were supposed to have been made.

A painting can look convincingly old while containing a material that does not belong to the claimed period. The surface and the paperwork can tell the same false story; the chemistry need not cooperate.

The scheme also depended on money going somewhere after each sale.

In May 2013, Rosales was arrested on accusations involving false tax returns and an undisclosed foreign bank account. Prosecutors said she had claimed to pass most sale proceeds to her supposed clients while retaining substantial sums herself.

The financial trail made the ownership story testable in a different way.

In September, Rosales pleaded guilty. Federal prosecutors said the Swiss client had never existed and the Spanish collector had never owned the works. The anonymous collection was not a hidden treasure waiting to be discovered. It was part of the deception.

Knoedler had closed in 2011. In later civil proceedings, buyers argued that the gallery and its former director should have spotted the warning signs. They denied knowingly selling forgeries. A prominent lawsuit ended in settlements in 2016, not a trial verdict establishing their intent.

That distinction matters. Rosales admitted her criminal role. It does not follow that every person who handled a fake knew it was fake.

The buyers paid for paintings they believed came from great artists. But the most important thing sold with those paintings was invisible: a convincing history.

And in one of those histories, the owner did not exist.
