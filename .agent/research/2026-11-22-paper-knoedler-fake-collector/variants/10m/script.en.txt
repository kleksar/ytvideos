How could one dealer bring so many unknown masterpieces into New York? What could make an established gallery confident enough to sell them for millions? And why did the mystery of an anonymous collector end in a federal criminal case?

Begin with a document that the buyers could actually hold.

In November 2004, the Knoedler gallery billed a buyer's representative 8.3 million dollars for a painting attributed to Mark Rothko. The document described a work dated 1956. It did not simply list oil paint and canvas. It supplied a history: the artist, then a private collection in Switzerland, then an heir.

Domenico and Eleanore De Sole were buying through a gallery whose name meant something in the art world. Domenico later testified that he asked former director Ann Freedman to put her assurance about the painting in writing. She did. The invoice also anticipated the work appearing in a future supplement to a scholarly Rothko catalogue. The couple had reason to take those papers seriously, even though the papers could not make the work authentic.

The question was not whether the canvas existed. It was whether the claimed path from Rothko's studio to the gallery had ever happened.

Yet an unusual supply of paintings had been entering its rooms.

In 1994, dealer Glafira Rosales began bringing works that she said came from private owners. The names were among the most sought-after in twentieth-century American painting: Jackson Pollock, Mark Rothko, Robert Motherwell, and others.

These were not familiar works passed between known collectors. They had apparently remained unseen for decades. In an art market where a genuinely rediscovered work can be worth a fortune, that might sound like an exciting opportunity.

Rosales said some works belonged to an anonymous client linked to Switzerland, who had inherited a collection and wanted privacy. Others supposedly belonged to a Spanish collector. Those accounts explained the paintings' long absence from public view. But anonymity also put the alleged owners beyond a buyer's direct questioning.

A gallery could show someone a painting. It could issue a receipt, and experts could examine the surface. None of those steps supplied a missing document proving that the anonymous owner had ever possessed it.

The scale grew over fifteen years.

From 1994 through 2009, Rosales sold more than sixty works to two prominent Manhattan galleries for approximately 33.2 million dollars. Those galleries in turn sold the paintings to buyers for more than eighty million dollars.

The two amounts describe different transactions: what Rosales received from galleries and what their customers paid. They are not separate totals to add together. The galleries' reputations helped turn unfamiliar paintings into major sales.

Warnings existed alongside those sales. At a 2016 civil trial, testimony described doubts raised in 1994 about works attributed to Richard Diebenkorn. Those doubts did not prove every Rosales painting was forged. They did mean the asserted source of the works deserved scrutiny.

In 2003, the International Foundation for Art Research examined a painting presented as a Jackson Pollock. It could not authenticate the painting and called the proposed ownership history into question. Trial reporting later described that history being changed after the criticism. A revised explanation was not the same as a document showing where the painting had really been.

This was already before the De Soles' 2004 invoice. So the issue was not simply that experts discovered the problem years after every buyer had paid. Questions about parts of the collection had been raised while other works were still being offered for sale.

Then material evidence made the disagreement harder to dismiss as a matter of taste. In 2008, conservator James Martin examined two paintings attributed to Robert Motherwell and found materials inconsistent with the periods in which they were said to have been created.

That finding did not tell an investigator which individual had painted them. It did challenge the dates in their stories. A convincing-looking signature or an artificially aged surface could not reconcile the material with the claimed time.

The gallery closed in 2011. It said business reasons, rather than the legal disputes, explained the closure. The competing accounts must be kept distinct; the closing of a business is not a criminal judgment about what its employees knew.

The next decisive path was not through a museum. It was through bank records.

In May 2013, Rosales was arrested over alleged false tax returns and an undisclosed foreign bank account. Prosecutors said she had portrayed herself as an intermediary passing most of the proceeds to her clients, while retaining substantial sums and hiding funds abroad.

That financial claim made the anonymous-owner story vulnerable in a new way. If a client inherited the pictures and received most of each sale price, who was receiving the money? A laboratory could inspect paint; investigators could follow transfers.

In September 2013, Rosales pleaded guilty to participating in the fraud and to related money-laundering and tax offenses. Federal prosecutors said the purported Swiss client had never existed. The supposed Spanish collector had never owned the works.

They also described where the paintings had really come from. A painter in Queens, New York, made them at his home. Sometimes he put the famous signatures on the canvases; sometimes Rosales's associate did. The associate also treated them to suggest age. The paintings came from an actual workshop, but the histories that placed them in inherited private collections were invented.

The person holding a brush was real. The source claimed on the sales documents was not.

For the De Soles, the criminal admission did not automatically settle their separate dispute with the gallery. They sued over the alleged Rothko and argued that warning signs had been overlooked. The gallery and its former director denied knowingly selling fakes.

The 2016 civil trial brought specific numbers into the open. A forensic accountant testified that the gallery would have been unprofitable without its Rosales sales. Trial reporting also described a very large markup on the De Soles' painting. Those were pieces of testimony and evidence in a civil case, not a criminal verdict establishing the director's knowledge.

The parties settled before the jury decided the question. Rosales's guilty plea established her own admitted role; the settlements did not establish that everyone who handled a painting had knowingly joined the scheme.

Return to that 2004 invoice. The buyer had asked for a written assurance and received one. The paper carried an artist's name, a price, and a path through Switzerland. The painting was real enough to hang on a wall. The promised ownership trail was not.

That was how a painter working at home could end up behind sales of more than eighty million dollars: the canvases entered the market attached to a history that no real collector could confirm.
