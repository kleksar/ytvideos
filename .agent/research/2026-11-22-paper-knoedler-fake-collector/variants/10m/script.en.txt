How could one dealer bring so many unknown masterpieces into New York? What could make an established gallery confident enough to sell them for millions? And why did the mystery of an anonymous collector end in a federal criminal case?

Start with a real invoice.

In November 2004, the Knoedler gallery billed a buyer's representative 8.3 million dollars for a painting attributed to Mark Rothko. The document described a work dated 1956. It did not simply list oil paint and canvas. It supplied a history: the artist, then a private collection in Switzerland, then an heir.

The couple interested in the painting, Domenico and Eleanore De Sole, were buying through a gallery whose name carried considerable weight. The invoice also said the painting was expected to appear in a future supplement to a catalogue of Rothko's work.

That was an important part of the offer. Buyers were not just being shown a surface. They were being given a name, a date, a chain of ownership, and an expectation of scholarly recognition.

For most of us, buying an old painting means choosing something we like. At this price, liking the picture is not enough. A buyer needs an answer to a question that cannot be settled by looking: who actually made it?

Knoedler had been dealing art in New York for generations. The gallery was not supposed to be a place where extraordinary claims arrived without any scrutiny.

Yet an unusual supply of paintings had been entering its rooms.

In 1994, dealer Glafira Rosales began bringing works that she said came from private owners. The names were among the most sought-after in twentieth-century American painting: Jackson Pollock, Mark Rothko, Robert Motherwell, and others.

These were not familiar works passed between known collectors. They had apparently remained unseen for decades. In an art market where a genuinely rediscovered work can be worth a fortune, that might sound like an exciting opportunity.

It also creates the central problem of this story. If nobody has seen the painting in public for decades, what connects the canvas now on the table to the famous artist who supposedly made it?

Rosales offered an answer.

Some paintings, she said, came from an anonymous client associated with Switzerland. That owner had inherited the collection and wanted privacy. Other works, she said, belonged to a Spanish collector.

Such a story is difficult to disprove just by staring at a painting. The anonymity that sounds like a reason for missing information also prevents buyers from checking the account directly.

And the gallery had tangible works to show them.

The De Soles' Rothko was one of those works. Court records later described the invoice and the discussions around it. The gallery's former director, Ann Freedman, gave written assurances about the painting. A buyer could point to an actual document from a respected business, not just a dealer's verbal claim.

The price was also real. The 2004 transaction led to a court dispute over a work sold for about 8.3 million dollars.

But a real invoice is not proof of a real artist.

Behind the paintings was a very different chain of production.

According to the federal case, a painter living in Queens made the works at his home. Rosales and an associate commissioned or obtained the paintings. Sometimes the painter added a famous signature. Sometimes the associate did. The associate also treated paintings to create an appearance of age.

The images and their signatures were false. So was the history supplied for their ownership.

That second part made the scheme more than a matter of brushwork. A signature can be compared with other signatures. A canvas can be examined. But a supposed private owner who cannot be named offers very few leads.

The scale grew over fifteen years.

From 1994 through 2009, Rosales sold more than sixty works to two prominent Manhattan galleries for approximately 33.2 million dollars. Those galleries in turn sold the paintings to buyers for more than eighty million dollars.

These are two different figures: what Rosales received from the galleries, and what the galleries' customers spent. They should not be added together, as if they were separate piles of losses.

The financial difference shows the importance of the gallery in the process. Its name, exhibitions, paperwork, and sales contacts connected an unknown dealer's work to buyers willing to spend millions.

But questions had been accumulating.

In 1994, according to testimony later discussed in court, an expert raised doubts about works attributed to Richard Diebenkorn. The warning did not by itself prove a specific work was fake, but it should have made the gallery look harder at the story.

Then came a much more formal test. In 2003, the International Foundation for Art Research examined a painting attributed to Jackson Pollock. The organization could not authenticate it and questioned the proposed provenance, the record of where it had supposedly been.

Think about what that means. A painting's surface might look like a Pollock. The problem is the path between the painter's studio and the sale. If that path changes when experts ask questions, the uncertainty has not been solved by a better story.

Later, specialists looked beyond appearances altogether.

In 2008, a conservator analyzing two paintings attributed to Robert Motherwell found materials inconsistent with the dates when they were supposed to have been made. This is the sort of problem a forgery cannot fix by adding more scratches or yellowing to a canvas.

A painting that claims to be from a particular period should be physically possible in that period.

Science does not automatically prove who made a picture. But a material that does not fit the claimed date can undermine the whole attribution. The gap matters when the object is being sold for an exceptional price.

By now, buyers were not merely disagreeing about artistic taste. They were asking whether the paintings were what they had been sold as.

Knoedler closed in 2011. The gallery said its closure was for business reasons unrelated to the lawsuits. Later accounts of the forgery dispute placed the closure amid mounting challenges to works it had sold. Those statements must not be collapsed into a claim that a criminal court found the gallery's staff guilty of knowing fraud.

The decisive admission came from the dealer.

In May 2013, Rosales was arrested in a case involving false tax returns and a foreign bank account she had not disclosed. The investigators were following money as well as paintings.

The ownership story had a financial consequence. Rosales had said much of the sale price would go to the clients for whom she acted. Prosecutors said she actually retained substantial proceeds and hid money abroad.

That raised a question no catalogue or art expert could answer by studying paint: where was the owner's share of the sale?

In September 2013, Rosales pleaded guilty to her role in the scheme and related financial offenses.

Federal prosecutors said the purported Swiss client had never existed. The supposed Spanish collector had never owned the paintings. The works were not a forgotten family inheritance coming back into view. They had been produced in Queens and sent into the market under manufactured identities.

The person behind the canvas was real. The people behind its supposed ownership were not.

That distinction also explains why the case was so damaging to the art market. It questioned not merely an expert's eye, but the system of trust that allows dealers, galleries, scholars, and collectors to transact when nobody can see the artist making the work.

The aftermath was more complicated than a single guilty plea.

Collectors sued the gallery and its former director. One prominent dispute concerned the De Soles and the painting sold as a Rothko. They argued that signs of trouble had been ignored. The gallery and its former director denied knowingly selling counterfeit art.

During a 2016 civil trial, testimony and records described the gallery's dependence on sales from Rosales and the warning signs around some works. The parties reached settlements. The trial ended without a jury deciding that the gallery's director had knowingly participated in the fraud.

It matters to tell these outcomes apart. A dealer's guilty plea is a criminal admission. A buyer's allegation is not proof against everyone named in a lawsuit. A settlement is not a guilty verdict.

In 2017, Rosales was sentenced to house arrest and probation after having spent time in custody. That sentence did not make the paintings authentic or restore the missing history to them.

So what did the buyers actually purchase?

They received objects that had paint, canvas, and signatures. They had invoices from businesses with real addresses. Some had been examined by knowledgeable people. All of that was visible, or at least documentable.

But the crucial link to the great artists was a story of ownership that could not withstand investigation.

An anonymous Swiss collector sounded like a doorway into a lost collection. Instead, investigators found a doorway drawn onto a wall.

The fraud began with paintings. It succeeded by selling certainty about where those paintings came from.

And the most expensive thing attached to them was not the paint. It was the history that did not exist.
