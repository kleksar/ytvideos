I open my backpack at the bus stop. My yellow umbrella is sitting on top of the ticket I need. The bag looks tidy. I still have to move something out of the way.

So I compare two ways to pack the same eight things. This is a simple illustrated model, not a timed experiment with a real bag.

A groups things by kind: umbrella and ticket near the top, then food, electronics, a notebook and a sweater. B follows my planned order of use: ticket, snack, charger, umbrella, and then the remaining things.

Pick A or B before I count.

The rule is strict. One compartment, one stack. Every time I take something, I put it back in its original position. I count objects lifted from above the thing I want, not seconds.

First I need the ticket. A takes one lift; B takes none. Then the snack: three versus one. The charger: five versus two. Finally the umbrella: zero versus three.

For those four stops, B wins. Six lifts against A's nine.

But the weather in my model changes. I close the umbrella, put it back, and need it again. Now both methods have nine lifts. Another shower means another reach for the umbrella. A stays at nine; B rises to twelve.

The tidy bag wins only because I reached for the same thing again. If that doesn't happen, B wins. This says nothing about real travel time or bags with extra pockets.

What matters isn't how neat the stack looks. It's what I will reach for, and how often. Tomorrow's route could change that again. The next simulated morning starts at the same bus stop.
