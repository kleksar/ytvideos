I'm at a bus stop with a backpack that looks beautifully organized. I open it for my ticket, and the first thing I meet is an umbrella.

That raises a question. Should I group things by category or pack them in the order I'll need them? I'll use a reproducible stack model, not pretend I raced real backpacks.

I have eight things: umbrella, ticket, bottle, snack, headphones, charger, notebook and sweater. Both versions use the same top-opening compartment. No side pockets. Only the order changes.

A groups things by category. From top down: umbrella, ticket, bottle, snack, headphones, charger, notebook and sweater. Weather and travel gear are easy to reach; food and electronics sit together.

B follows my planned journey. Top down: ticket, snack, charger, umbrella; then bottle, headphones, notebook and sweater. It isn't necessarily prettier, but it follows the day I expect.

Before I show either result, choose a side.

Each time I take an item, I lift every object above it and then restore the original stack. Each lifted obstacle counts once. I'm not measuring seconds, weight or comfort.

First I need the ticket. A requires one lift, B zero. Then I want my snack: A requires three lifts, B one. I restore the stack after each request.

At the next stop I want my snack. In A, I have to move the umbrella, ticket and bottle: three lifts. In B, just the ticket: one. I put everything back, so the positions remain unchanged for the next request.

For the charger, five objects block A and only two block B. I've buried the third item on my planned route beneath the neatly grouped food and travel gear.

Finally, I need the umbrella. In A it is on top: zero lifts. In B it is fourth: three. After four requests the totals are nine lifts for A and six for B.

If the journey ends there, B wins. It needs three fewer lifts, one-third fewer than A in this model. That is a result, not a claim that it saves a third of someone's time.

But the reason I packed an umbrella was the weather. Imagine I fold it after the rain, return it to the same place, then need it again at another stop. A pays nothing for that extra retrieval. B pays three. Now the total is nine against nine.

One more shower changes the winner. A is still at nine. B is at twelve. The first packing order, the one that lost at four stops, now wins without changing a single object.

The bags did not change. Only how often I needed the umbrella changed. Every extra umbrella request adds nothing to A and three lifts to B.

Now suppose the rain stops but I need the charger again. That item is deep in A and near the top in B. From the rainy result, one extra charger request makes the total fourteen against fourteen. Another extra charger request would give A nineteen and B sixteen. The winner flips back.

I'm not adding these as real journeys I took. They are alternative request lists, run through the same fixed positions. That is also the limitation: I chose the lists. Different lists can produce different winners.

The model assumes each item returns to the same depth. A pocketed ticket, an umbrella kept in my hand or a side bottle pocket would change the results. Real testing would also require measured timings.

So which method should I use? When I know the next few things I'll need and won't keep digging for the same item, order of use can be better. When one item keeps coming out, keeping it accessible can matter more than the route order.

The useful answer is not A forever or B forever. It is to check the next repeated reach before I close the zipper. Tomorrow I could plan a completely different trip. In the model, I come back to the same bus stop and open the bag again.
