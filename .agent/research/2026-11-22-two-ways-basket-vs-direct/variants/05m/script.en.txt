I pick up a sock from my living-room floor. Its drawer is in the bedroom. Should I carry it there now, or fetch a basket and collect the rest of the mess first?

I keep hearing two different pieces of advice. One says, “Don't put it down; put it away.” The other says to gather misplaced things in a basket so I don't keep leaving and re-entering the same room. Both sound reasonable. They cannot both be the best choice every time.

Here is the test I can actually make without pretending I cleaned a real apartment. I draw one straight hallway. My living room is at zero. The kitchen is five corridor steps away, the bedroom eight. My empty basket is in a closet four steps in the opposite direction. I begin and end in the living room. If I use the basket, I must return it to that closet. Each object starts in the living room and ends in its proper room. Neither method is allowed to leave things hidden in a full basket.

My score is corridor steps, not seconds. I am ignoring the small movements inside rooms, the weight of the basket, and how long it takes to handle an object. Those limits matter, but they don't stop us from comparing routes.

Method A is direct delivery. I pick up an object, walk it home, then come back for the next one. Method B fetches the basket, collects the objects, carries them along the hallway, deposits things where they belong, returns to the living room, and puts the basket away. I can carry eight small, safe objects in it.

Which side wins?

The room looks almost clean. Only one sock is on the floor. With A, I walk eight steps to the bedroom and eight back: sixteen. With B, the closet adds an eight-step round trip to fetch the basket, delivery still takes sixteen, and returning the basket adds another eight. That's thirty-two. For one sock, the basket is twice the walking.

I put the drawing back to its starting state. This time there are eight things beside the sofa: four for the kitchen and four for the bedroom. The sock is one of the bedroom items. The kitchen is on the way to the bedroom, so the basket can stop there without a separate outward trip.

A does four kitchen returns at ten steps each, and four bedroom returns at sixteen each. Forty plus sixty-four: 104.

B has the same eight objects to deliver. Fetching and returning the basket costs sixteen. The delivery route costs sixteen more: five steps to the kitchen, three to the bedroom, and eight home. Total: thirty-two.

That is the reversal. The basket loses by sixteen steps with one sock, then beats direct delivery by seventy-two with eight items. I did not make it faster by hiding the clutter. Every object reaches its home and the basket goes back too.

But eight is not a magic number. Suppose I find only two bedroom items. Direct delivery needs thirty-two steps. The basket needs thirty-two as well. In this floor plan, that is a walking tie. With three bedroom objects, direct takes forty-eight, while the basket still takes thirty-two. The number of repeated trips, not a special property of baskets, creates the advantage.

What if the basket is already next to me? I remove the sixteen-step closet detour. With one sock, the corridor distance becomes a tie. With eight items, the basket route becomes sixteen steps, not thirty-two. That does not mean I finish in half the time: I have not counted the extra handling.

One more complication changes what “clean” means. If I stop after throwing everything in a basket and leave it beside the door, my living room looks clear, but the objects are not back in the kitchen or bedroom. That is a different finishing rule. I cannot compare that shortcut against a method that actually puts everything away.

So I don't crown a universal winner. If I spot one stray sock, I take it straight to the bedroom. If I have a pile bound for several rooms, I consider grouping the deliveries. And if I'm already walking into the kitchen for another reason, taking one kitchen item along may cost no extra hallway trip at all.

My model gives a useful test, not a promise about real cleaning speed: count the trips you're repeating, and count the extra trip needed to fetch the container. Tomorrow the pile might be smaller, and the choice starts over.
