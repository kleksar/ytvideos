Should I check every stage of this shelf, or wait until it's finished? One method feels slower. The other can make me take the whole thing apart.

I'm not measuring a real build. This is a six-stage model with invented, clearly stated times. Every stage takes four model minutes. Checking a stage takes one.

Method A checks after every stage. Six stages plus six checks: thirty minutes. Method B builds all six stages and checks once at the end: twenty-five. If nothing is wrong, B wins by five.

Now I put the second panel in backwards in both versions.

With A, I see it before adding the next piece. I undo that stage, redo it and check again. The total is thirty-seven.

With B, I keep building over the mistake. The final check finds it, but five stages now have to come off and go back on. I spend fifty-six model minutes. My five-minute saving has turned into nineteen extra minutes.

There's one more twist. If the mistake happens at stage six, B finishes in thirty-two minutes. A still needs thirty-seven. The late mistake is cheap to fix; the early one isn't.

These are model numbers, not a stopwatch result or a reason to ignore real assembly and safety instructions. What matters is where the mistake gets buried.

When I open another box, that first decision is waiting for me again.
