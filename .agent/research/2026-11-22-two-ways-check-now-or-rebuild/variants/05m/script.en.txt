Why can checking my work more often actually make a build quicker? And why does checking only at the end sometimes win anyway?

I have the same six-stage flat-pack shelf in two versions of a simple model. I haven't built or timed a real shelf for this comparison. Each stage takes four imaginary minutes. A check takes one. If a stage has to come off, removing it takes two, putting it back takes four, and I check the repair once more. Every later stage depends on the stages beneath it.

My choice is simple. A means check each stage before moving on. B means assemble everything and inspect the finished unit. I count total model minutes, including inspection and any repair. Both versions start with identical parts and identical mistakes.

First, nothing goes wrong. A takes twenty-four minutes of building and six minutes of checking: thirty. B takes twenty-four minutes of building and just one final check: twenty-five. The shortcut wins. I can see the temptation. I'm saving five checks without changing a single part.

But the first real question is what happens when a mistake is hidden by later work.

I turn the second panel the wrong way. I'm not claiming that any particular commercial shelf behaves like this; in my model, stages three through six cover the joint. With A, I inspect stage two, find the error, remove that stage for two minutes, rebuild it for four and inspect the repair for one. Everything else proceeds normally. The final total is thirty-seven.

With B, I don't look until all six stages are together. The mistake is still at stage two. To reach it, I must remove stages six, five, four, three and two. That is five removals at two minutes each. Then I rebuild those five stages at four minutes apiece and check the repair. Add those steps to the original twenty-five and I get fifty-six.

B was five minutes ahead when everything went perfectly. Now B is nineteen minutes behind A. The mistake itself hasn't changed. Only the time I waited before detecting it has changed.

So is A always better? I move the same single mistake later, keeping the two methods and all the times fixed.

At stage five, B needs to take off just stages six and five. Its total is thirty-eight, still one minute slower than A's thirty-seven. At stage six, only the last stage comes off. B finishes in thirty-two, five minutes faster than A. If there is no mistake at all, B is also faster, twenty-five against thirty.

That means the winner flips at a very specific point in this little model. A protects me from work that will bury an early mistake. B avoids extra checking when the work is clean or when an error is easy to reach.

Watch what the numbers don't say. A check doesn't magically prevent an error; it only catches one that my model says is visible. A perfect end check is also an assumption. And a model minute isn't a real minute of assembling furniture. Actual hardware, tools and safety steps can change every cost.

For a real project, I still follow the manufacturer's instructions, including any required safety checks. My model answers a narrower question: when does the cost of checking pay for itself through avoided rebuilding?

Before the next flat-pack box lands on my floor, I have to choose where to look for mistakes.
