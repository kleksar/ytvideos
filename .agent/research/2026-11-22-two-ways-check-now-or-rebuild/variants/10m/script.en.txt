When I build a shelf, should I stop to check every stage or finish the whole thing first? And what if the answer changes when each check takes a little longer?

I set two identical six-stage shelves on the floor. They are drawings in a model, not furniture I actually built. I want to compare the time it takes to finish a correct shelf, including the time wasted when I have to undo a mistake. I can choose two methods. With A, I inspect each stage before covering it with the next one. With B, I put everything together and inspect the finished shelf only once.

Before choosing, I give both shelves the same rules. Each stage takes four invented model minutes to build. An inspection takes one. Removing a stage takes two, rebuilding it takes four, and checking a repair takes another minute. Any mistake is visible when I inspect, even at the end. The later pieces cover the earlier connections, so to reach a mistake I have to remove every stage above it. These are deliberately simple assumptions, not stopwatch measurements or instructions for a real product.

First I build the shelf without making any mistakes. Both methods spend twenty-four minutes fitting the six stages. A adds six one-minute inspections. I reach thirty. B adds one final inspection. I reach twenty-five. The shelf looks the same on both sides, but B is five minutes faster. I understand why I'd be tempted to skip the checks. On a clean build, stopping after every step has given me no visible benefit.

Now I turn the second panel the wrong way in both models. I haven't changed the parts, the tools, or the amount of work required. Only the panel's orientation is wrong.

With A, I finish stage two and look before covering it. The panel is reversed. I remove the stage for two minutes, rebuild it for four, and spend one more minute checking that correction. Stage three can now go on without hiding the mistake. I finish with thirty-seven model minutes. Seven minutes more than my clean A build, and the shelf is correct.

I try B with that identical reversed panel. I finish stage two and keep going. Stage three sits over the joint. Then stage four, stage five, stage six. When I finally inspect the shelf, I discover the wrong panel. It has been there the entire time. The final inspection doesn't make the mistake more serious; it makes finding and reaching it more expensive.

I have to take off stages six, five, four, three, and two. That's five stages at two minutes each: ten minutes spent taking things apart. Then I install those same five stages again, costing twenty more. I make the extra repair check for one minute. Those thirty-one minutes come on top of the twenty-five already spent. B finishes at fifty-six. A finished at thirty-seven. My original five-minute saving has become a nineteen-minute loss.

That isn't proof that checking more often always wins. It is one result for an early error that later work has buried. If I change where the mistake happens, the story changes again.

I return both shelves to the same starting point and put the wrong panel at stage one. A still finds it immediately and finishes at thirty-seven. But B must remove and rebuild all six stages. Its total climbs to sixty-two. Each new layer above the mistake adds another removal and another rebuild. That is six model minutes of repair work for every layer I need to uncover.

I move the mistake closer to the top. At stage three, B takes fifty minutes. At stage four, forty-four. At stage five, thirty-eight. A is still thirty-seven in each case because the immediate inspection catches the error before the next stage goes on. The results are moving toward each other, not because I work faster, but because fewer completed parts need to be undone.

Then I put the only mistake at stage six. B needs to remove and rebuild that last stage, followed by one repair check. It finishes at thirty-two. A still finishes at thirty-seven. B is five minutes ahead again. It was also ahead when there was no mistake. The difference between the methods is therefore not simply whether an error exists. It's how deeply the finished work hides it.

For a moment, stage five is the most interesting position. With the original one-minute checks, A takes thirty-seven and B takes thirty-eight. A wins by a single model minute. That's a fragile victory. What happens if inspections are harder than I first assumed?

I change exactly one kind of cost. Every inspection now takes two model minutes instead of one, including the final inspection and the extra inspection after a repair. All six building stages still take four minutes. Removing a stage still takes two and rebuilding it still takes four. The shelf, the two methods, and the location of each mistake stay the same. I am not claiming that real inspections take two minutes. I'm testing how sensitive my earlier choice was to that invented assumption.

On a clean build, A now spends twenty-four minutes assembling and twelve inspecting. Its total is thirty-six. B spends the same twenty-four assembling and just two inspecting. B reaches twenty-six. When checks cost twice as much, B's advantage on a perfect build grows from five minutes to ten.

I repeat the early stage-two mistake. A has six longer checks in its normal thirty-six-minute routine. It must also remove the wrong stage for two, rebuild for four, and check the repair for two. It finishes at forty-four. B starts from twenty-six, removes and rebuilds five stages for thirty, then checks the repair for two. B takes fifty-eight. Even with slower inspections, catching this early buried mistake still saves me fourteen minutes.

Now the stage-five case. With A, I again finish at forty-four. With B, the original correct assembly and final check cost twenty-six, removing and rebuilding stages six and five costs twelve, and the repair check costs two. B reaches forty. Last time A won this case by one minute. Now B wins by four. I did not move the error or change how deeply it was buried. Changing the cost of inspection alone moved the point where my choice flips.

This is the new result I would have missed if I had announced a universal winner after the first reversed panel. Frequent inspection protects me from an expensive hidden repair, but I pay for those checks on every stage. Waiting until the end saves that repeated cost, but I might have to dismantle work I thought was finished. At stage two the hidden repair dominates either inspection assumption. At stage five the balance is close enough that the price of inspecting matters.

There are important things this calculation cannot tell me. In my model, an inspection always notices the error. Real eyes miss things. Sometimes the mistake isn't visible even if I stop. Sometimes a fast check takes seconds and a final check takes much longer. Sometimes taking apart a joint damages it. I can't transfer my made-up four-minute stages or two-minute inspections to an actual shelf, and I have not tested either method with real people.

So I won't use this to replace assembly instructions, required checks, wall anchoring, or safety rules. The model answers a narrower but useful question. Before I cover a connection, I can ask how much later work would have to come off if that connection were wrong. I can also ask how expensive it is to check now, while I can still reach it.

I began with a shelf that made the final-only method look obviously faster. An early reversed panel made the step-by-step method win. A late error made the final-only method win again. And when I doubled the inspection cost, even the near-tie at stage five changed sides.

I haven't found one method that wins every build. I have found the two costs that decide this particular model: checks I pay for now, and rework I may pay for later. The next time I open a flat-pack box, that's the choice I'll have to make before I cover the first joint.
