I have forty clean socks on my bed and two ways to get rid of the pile. Do I grab one sock and hunt for its twin, or spend time sorting everything first?

The second method looks organized. That doesn't mean it wins. I want a number I can check, not a feeling about a tidy bedroom.

So I make a small model. Twenty pairs, each marked with a different tiny symbol. Five pairs are red, five blue, five yellow, and five green. I write down the exact mixed order of all forty socks. Both methods start with that order. I count one look whenever I inspect a sock to identify it. I do not call these seconds. I haven't filmed or timed real hands doing the job.

For method A, I pick up the first sock. It's green, with the mark for pair twenty. I scan the remaining socks from left to right, stopping when I see the matching mark. It is far down the pile: thirty-three comparisons. Including the sock I picked up, that first pair costs thirty-four looks.

Next comes a red sock. Its partner is near the other end too. Thirty-one more comparisons. I keep removing matching pairs and preserving the order of everything left. The pile gets smaller, but the searches still add up. Twenty socks picked as the first half of a pair, plus two hundred and twenty-one comparisons. Method A finishes at two hundred and forty-one looks.

Method B pays a fee before it gets a single pair. I look at all forty socks and place each in its color group. That is forty looks already. Then I pick the first sock in each group, find its matching mark, remove the pair, and repeat. Across the four groups, that takes twenty first-sock looks and sixty matching comparisons.

Forty plus twenty plus sixty: one hundred and twenty looks. On this exact pile, B saves one hundred and twenty-one looks. I was expecting sorting to be extra work. Instead, it cuts out a lot of searching through the wrong colors.

But I have been quietly assuming something about my goal: that I want every pair finished.

Suppose I am late and just need one clean pair. With A, the first match costs thirty-four looks. With B, sorting all forty takes forty looks before I even start matching. The first green pair brings B's count to forty-eight. For getting one pair, the supposedly losing method A wins.

Now I keep the same order and the same marks, but I change the size of the job. If I only have one pair, sorting is silly: two looks without sorting, four with it. With eight selected pairs from this same inventory, A and B both need forty-six looks. With ten pairs, A needs sixty and B fifty-six. By twenty pairs, the difference has grown to one hundred and twenty-one looks.

That crossover is not a magic rule about eight pairs. It belongs to this arrangement and these marks. What matters is that sorting has a fixed setup cost, and that cost only pays back if it saves enough later searches.

There is one more test. I recolor every sock black. The twenty tiny pair marks stay different and the mixed order stays identical. A still needs two hundred and forty-one looks. B still sorts forty socks, but now they all land in one group. It does exactly the same pair searches as A, on top of its sorting step. B needs two hundred and eighty-one looks. A wins.

If I instead use two useful color groups, B needs one hundred and seventy-one looks. Four groups bring it back to one hundred and twenty. Those numbers are not advice about which socks to buy; they show what a good grouping actually does: it removes candidates I would otherwise have to examine.

Real laundry includes reaching, folding, different labels, missing partners, and the time spent moving socks around. This model counts looks, not those costs. If all my socks are truly interchangeable, I may not need to search for exact marked pairs at all.

So my answer is conditional. For this big, colorful pile, I sort first and finish with far fewer checks. For a single pair, or a pile where color tells me nothing, I start matching. The best method depends on what I need to finish, and how much the grouping helps.
