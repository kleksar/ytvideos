## Three serious finalists at comparable readiness

**1. Dictation vs typing (selected).** Hook: "Can saying a message out loud actually take longer than typing it?" After the apparently decisive 24-second voice draft, an extra correction step reverses the result. Payoff: the **same sixty-word message** flips at **48 seconds** of dictation cleanup. Viewer relevance: nearly every phone user sends messages; no specialized background required. Strong objection: comparing hypothetical speeds feels academic, and directly comparable entertainment video evidence is weak. This is an experimental audience hypothesis, **not established demand**.

**2. Shared bathroom scheduling (strongest competing story).** Hook: "I'm already late, but there is one bathroom for two people. Is taking turns really slower?" After an attractive alternation, a blocked task can delay both people; payoff would be the actual earliest joint departure on a disclosed activity/dependency table. Viewer relevance: morning conflict is instantly recognizable; two protagonists create readable comedy without invented danger. Objection: to avoid a contrived winner, real constraints on sinks/shower/occupancy need a careful verified or explicitly modelled scenario. The YouTube results found for "couple morning routine" are giant lifestyle Shorts, **not** comparable A/B timing tests. It may beat dictation visually, but no honest winner or good ten-minute expansion was established within this run.

**3. Family group photo ordering.** Hook: "Why do twelve people keep climbing into and out of the same photo?" After one group takes its picture, the next group forces a reshuffle; the competing method keeps the most frequently needed people in place. Payoff would be fewer person-position changes for a **fixed exact shot list**. Strong objection: the winner depends on a fixed agenda and rules; without that, this can become an opaque optimization puzzle. Comparable YouTube performance, real questions and a grounded 10-minute development were not verified.

**Decision:** voice/typing has an external empirical foundation and a simple honest, replayable change of winner; bathroom has stronger human stakes, but no complete defensible scenario was found. The choice is conditional and may be rejected by an independent first listener.

## Verified external evidence and strict boundaries

1. **Stanford HCI research project (2016)**, https://hci.stanford.edu/research/speech/ (read 2026-11-22). It describes a controlled comparison of speech recognition and touchscreen keyboard input, with correction allowed, 32 participant logs and 3,200 trials. It reports English speech entry 3.0× faster than typing and notes experiment-specific technology. This is **not** evidence that today's dictation on every phone is three times faster or that message completion includes no extra edits.
2. **Stanford Engineering explainer**, https://engineering.stanford.edu/news/smartphone-speech-recognition-faster-and-more-accurate-typing (read 2026-11-22). Describes the device/software, short-phrase conditions and 32 participants. The speech/keyboard experiment motivated the *question*, not the chosen numerical scenario; model uses rounded rates **150/50 words per minute**, not measured results.
3. **CHI 2020 paper, composition vs transcription**, https://programs.sigchi.org/chi/2020/program/content/32204 (search metadata / abstract inspected 2026-11-22). Speech is reported faster than typing for both tasks, but the advantage is smaller for composition and error patterns differ. Full article was not accessible in this run; no detailed conclusions about the study are used in narration.

## Reproducible two-method model — the only source of illustrative outcome numbers

Start: **one prewritten 60-word message**, same hypothetical touchscreen, same language, same accuracy requirement, same recipient. Metric declared first: elapsed seconds to **send-ready accurate text**, with no manual Send latency included for either method. No new messages invented mid-comparison. Method A typing a finished output at 50 words/min (includes typing corrections); B speech draft at 150 words/min, then a declared extra check/edit time E. These **rates and E are model parameters**, not measurements, and not precise estimates of any specific current recognizer. The selected message is described, not printed or independently timed; its exact content is an unmaterialized model input, not a real test asset.

Formula A(N) = 60N/50 seconds; B(N,E) = 60N/150 + E seconds. For N=60: A=72 s, B draft=24 s, B with E=20: 44 s (B wins 28); B with E=55: 79 s (A wins 7). Equal when E=48 s.

Sensitivity: N=12 => A=14.4 s, B(E=20)=4.8+20=24.8 (A wins 10.4). N=120 => A=144 s, B(E=20)=48+20=68 (B wins 76); B(E=55)=48+55=103 (B wins 41). Difference before review =0.8 second per word; E=20 breaks even at N=25 words, E=55 at N=68.75 words. Keyboard final review +10 (separately declared) => A=82 and B(E=55)=79, flipping winner back to B. Voice rate 100 instead of 150 => 60 words take 36 s; with E=20 B=56 (beats original A by 16).

**Limitations:** no actual typing or dictation, no claim about app performance; correction times assumed, not measured. A includes corrections in its rate and B uses explicit correction stage. Voice entry may fail privacy/noise feasibility, and review time may grow with length. Research about transcription is not a validated estimate of composing a new 60-word message. Fair real-world validation would require a fixed exact text, same correctness rule, paired alternating attempts, several replicates, and reported spread. All external facts live here, not in narration.

## Narrative/payoff trace

Opening promises a counterintuitive reversal. Same text and device lead to A=72 and B=44; when E changes, B=79. At the 48-second cutoff the seemingly fast draft stops being the fastest *sent* text. Short variant completes its answer and bridges its last question into the first line. Five-minute version adds a short and long message, practical limits. Ten-minute version adds threshold-per-word reasoning and experiment-design limits at pacing cost; not recommended as master.

## 2026-11-23 revision-specific sensitivity case (10m only)

Using the existing openly hypothetical model `A(N)=1.2N` and `B(N,E)=0.4N+E`, the revised 10m examines a **new assumed**, not observed, correction burden `E=110` for `N=120`: voice `48+110=158` seconds versus keyboard `144`, so keyboard wins by `14`. This specifically challenges the previously fixed E55 assumption (which gave voice `103` and a `41`-second win). The 110-second case is not sourced to a real device or study and is clearly declared hypothetical in EN and RU; it requires no factual claim beyond the existing model. A separate conditional keyboard final check of `+10` seconds for the 60-word E55 case is retained from the original draft (keyboard `82` vs voice `79`). The Stanford studies remain inspiration for the question only, **not** measurements of these parameters.
