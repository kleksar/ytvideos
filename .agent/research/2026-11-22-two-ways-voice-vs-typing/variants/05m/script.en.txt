Which gets a message sent sooner: my thumbs or my voice? And what happens if the microphone gets just one important detail wrong?

I'm leaving home, and I need to send a sixty-word update about a meeting. The other person needs the correct time and place, not a pile of words that merely looks complete. I have one phone, one message, and two ways to enter it.

A is typing. B is dictation. Before seeing either result, I pick a score: seconds until the same sixty words are accurate enough to press Send. I don't count a voice transcript as finished while I am still fixing it.

This is an illustrated calculation, not footage of a real timed experiment. I specify the speeds and the correction time, so anyone can change them.

First, I choose A. In my model I type finished text at fifty words per minute. That's a rate that already includes any little corrections made while typing. At that rate, sixty words take seventy-two seconds.

Now I reset the message and choose B. I say those same sixty words at a hundred and fifty words per minute. A draft appears after twenty-four seconds.

That looks decisive. The microphone has finished the first part while my thumbs are still working.

But I'm not sending it yet. I still need to read the draft, check the meeting place, and make sure the person's name is right. For this first case I allow twenty seconds of review and correction.

Twenty-four plus twenty is forty-four. B is ready twenty-eight seconds before A. If I stopped the video there, the title could simply say that talking beats typing.

Then I give the microphone a more difficult version of exactly the same job.

Nothing changes about the sixty-word message, the phone, or the typing speed. I change only one declared input: the time required to turn the voice draft into a correct message. Instead of twenty seconds, that takes fifty-five. Maybe an unusual name demands several edits. That is a possible explanation for the assumption, not an event I measured.

The microphone still creates its first draft in twenty-four seconds. But twenty-four plus fifty-five is seventy-nine. Typing stays at seventy-two.

Now A wins by seven seconds.

So the switch happens after the microphone has apparently finished. The slow part can be the work between the draft and the Send button.

I can find the exact boundary without guessing another example. Typing takes seventy-two seconds. Speaking the words takes twenty-four. That leaves forty-eight seconds for corrections. At exactly forty-eight, it's a tie. At forty-seven, voice wins by one. At forty-nine, typing wins by one.

But a sixty-word update is only one kind of message. What about a tiny reply?

For twelve words, the same typing rate gives about fourteen seconds. Dictation takes just under five. Add the same twenty seconds of checking and the voice method reaches almost twenty-five. A is now roughly ten seconds ahead, even though I haven't made the microphone any worse.

This isn't a contradiction. I am paying a fixed checking cost to save only about ten seconds of input.

Now imagine a hundred and twenty words. Typing takes a hundred and forty-four seconds. Dictation takes forty-eight. Even with fifty-five seconds of correction, voice finishes in a hundred and three. B wins by forty-one.

Here the longer message gives the faster input method room to recover its checking cost. But that last calculation assumes correction time stays fixed at fifty-five seconds. Real correction effort might grow with message length. I haven't measured that, and the model cannot promise the same outcome for longer real messages.

One more boundary matters before I even start a timer. If I am on a crowded train and cannot reasonably say a private message aloud, B isn't a usable method. Declaring the microphone twenty-eight seconds faster would not make it a sensible choice there. The result answers a time question under stated conditions, not every question about privacy or convenience.

If I wanted real evidence from my own phone, I'd prepare the same text, alternate which method goes first, repeat several times, and stop only when the words meet the same accuracy rule. I'd report the range, not my luckiest round. I haven't done that test here.

For this model, the answer is clear. With twenty seconds of checking, dictation wins the sixty-word job. With fifty-five, typing wins. For short replies, typing may win even against quick speech. And for longer messages, dictation gets more opportunity to make up for checking.
