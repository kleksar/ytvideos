Which gets my message ready to send first: my thumbs or my voice? And what if the method that fills the screen fastest needs the most fixing?

I'm leaving home and need to send someone a meeting update. It includes a name, a place and a time. Those details matter more than the speed at which letters appear. I have one imaginary sixty-word message and two ways to enter exactly the same words.

A is my phone keyboard. B is its microphone. Before either one starts, I set the finish line: the message must be correct and ready for me to tap Send. A draft with the wrong location has not finished.

This is a calculation with assumed speeds, not footage or results from a phone I tested. I will change one assumption at a time. The numbers tell us what would happen under those conditions, not which method is faster for everyone.

I try A first. In my model I type fifty finished words each minute, including the little corrections I make as I go. For sixty words, that is seventy-two seconds. I reach the end of the message and can send it.

Now I reset the imagined message and choose B. I set the voice-input rate to one hundred and fifty words a minute. The whole draft appears in twenty-four seconds.

It looks like the microphone has won. It has put the same words on screen in a third of the time.

But I still need to check what it wrote. In this first case, I allow twenty seconds to read the name and meeting details and fix the draft. The microphone finishes in forty-four seconds. Against the keyboard's seventy-two, that is a twenty-eight-second win for B.

If I stopped here, I could make a very confident claim about dictation. It would also be a claim about only one set of assumptions.

I run the same comparison again. The message stays sixty words. The phone and the two input speeds stay the same. I change only the microphone's checking time: instead of twenty seconds, it now takes fifty-five.

An unusual name could require several edits, but I have not observed that happening. Fifty-five seconds is a condition I chose for this model, not a recording of someone's mistakes.

The voice draft still appears in twenty-four seconds. I add fifty-five for the corrections. That makes seventy-nine seconds. The keyboard still needs seventy-two.

A wins this time, by seven seconds.

The surprising part is where the winner changed. Nothing happened during the speaking stage. All the extra time came after the screen already looked full. The race ends when the message is ready, not when the microphone stops listening.

I can locate the turning point. A needs seventy-two seconds. B spends twenty-four before checking anything. That leaves forty-eight seconds. If I can check the voice draft in less than forty-eight, B wins. At forty-eight they tie. If it takes longer, A wins.

But this only answers the sixty-word question. My next message might be a quick reply rather than a meeting update. Does the same winner survive?

I switch to twelve words and keep the original speeds. Typing now needs about fourteen seconds. Speaking takes just under five. If B still needs twenty seconds of checking, its total is almost twenty-five.

A wins, although the microphone still enters words three times faster. I've paid twenty seconds to check a draft that saved me less than ten seconds of typing. The short reply had too little room for the faster method to make up that cost.

Now imagine a longer, one-hundred-and-twenty-word message. A needs one hundred and forty-four seconds. B drafts it in forty-eight. Even if I give B the difficult fifty-five-second checking condition, it finishes in one hundred and three seconds. Voice wins by forty-one.

So length creates another reversal. The short reply favors A, while this longer text favors B. But before I celebrate, I need to ask whether the checking time should really stay fixed as the text gets longer.

What if the longer draft is harder to review? I change that one assumption again. For the same one-hundred-and-twenty words, suppose checking takes one hundred and ten seconds, rather than fifty-five. B now needs forty-eight plus one hundred and ten: one hundred and fifty-eight seconds.

A still takes one hundred and forty-four. Typing wins by fourteen.

This is a third hypothetical condition, not an observation about any speech recognizer. It shows why making the message longer does not automatically guarantee a microphone victory. The added words may save more input time, but they can also create more work after input.

There is a useful rule hiding behind these comparisons. At the modeled speeds, each word takes one point two seconds to type and point four seconds to speak. Before corrections, voice saves eight tenths of a second per word.

For sixty words, that saving is forty-eight seconds. That's exactly the correction budget I found earlier. For twelve words, it is only about ten seconds. For a hundred and twenty, it is ninety-six. A checking bill larger than that saving wipes out the voice advantage.

The figures aren't a performance claim. They are the reason the model changes its answer when I change the length or the effort needed to fix the text.

And I have made one more choice that needs scrutiny. A's fifty-words-per-minute rate already includes corrections; I treated the keyboard output as finished. What if I need a final ten-second check of the typed message too?

For the sixty-word case, A would rise from seventy-two to eighty-two seconds. Under the hard fifty-five-second voice-check condition, B would still take seventy-nine. That three-second gap switches the winner back to the microphone.

I haven't discovered a hidden truth about keyboards. I changed another disclosed input. The exercise is useful precisely because a tiny change in the finish-line rules can move the result.

There are limits that no stopwatch formula can settle. If I'm in a quiet room, I might be willing to speak a private meeting place aloud. In a crowded train, I might not. If I am composing a message rather than copying already chosen words, thinking time becomes part of the task too.

I shouldn't mix those cases and call them one experiment.

To test my own phone honestly, I would first write the exact message, decide what counts as correct, and use the same device for both methods. I'd alternate which one goes first, repeat the attempts, and keep the slow results as well as the fast ones. I would stop the clock only when I could safely press Send. I have not performed that test here.

Back at my door, the imagined sixty-word comparison has a precise answer. With twenty seconds of voice corrections, B finishes in forty-four and beats A's seventy-two. With fifty-five seconds, B takes seventy-nine and loses. The dividing line is forty-eight.

The longer-message comparison adds a warning: fifty-five seconds of correction still lets voice win for a hundred and twenty words, but a hypothetical one hundred and ten seconds makes it lose. Faster drafting is only useful when its savings survive the final check.

I can choose a method after I know how much text I have, where I am, and how much correction I expect. What I can't do is call a draft the winner before the message is ready to send.
