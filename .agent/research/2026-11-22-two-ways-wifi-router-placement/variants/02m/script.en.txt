On my drawn apartment plan, the router in the middle misses my work laptop. So is the middle really the best place for it?

I put the same router in two positions. A is in the middle of the apartment. B is on an open shelf near my desk. Both shelves are the same height. Pick A or B before I show the result.

I mark six places where I use a device: my desk, a bedroom, the television, the kitchen, another bedroom and my phone's usual spot.

This is a simple model, not a Wi-Fi speed test. I draw a circle with a radius of five grid units around either router. A device inside the circle counts as one successful use. Outside counts as zero. Real walls and interference are not in this drawing.

First, it's a quiet day: I check each device once.

A reaches five of the six spots. B reaches four. If I'm counting rooms, the middle wins.

But look at my desk. It's the one spot A misses. B reaches it easily.

Now I change the schedule, not the router or the apartment. I use the laptop three times instead of once, while the other five spots still get one use each. That's eight attempted uses.

A still succeeds five times. It misses all three laptop sessions. B succeeds six times. It loses two distant spots but gets all three laptop sessions.

So A wins the room count, and B wins this modeled workday. Neither result tells me actual Wi-Fi speed. I'd test both shelves with my real devices before moving any cables.
