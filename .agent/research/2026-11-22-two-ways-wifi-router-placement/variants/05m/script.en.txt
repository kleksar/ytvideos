Which shelf is better for my Wi-Fi router: the middle of the apartment, or the shelf right beside my desk? And why could the answer change between Sunday afternoon and Monday morning?

I start with one drawn apartment. The TV works in the living room, a phone sits near the hallway, and the laptop at my desk is the device I cannot afford to lose during a work call. I want to compare two locations before buying any new equipment.

A is the open shelf in the middle. B is another open shelf near the office. I use the same router at the same height, with the same six device locations. I'm not pretending I went around a real home with a meter. This is an illustrated distance model; the numbers describe the drawing, not actual Wi-Fi performance.

My scoreboard counts successful device-use sessions. Every time I try a device, it scores one point if that location falls within five grid units of the router, and zero if it does not. Walls, interference, hardware differences and speed are deliberately outside this simple rule. That means I can check the arithmetic, but I cannot claim that a real router will behave like the circles on this page.

Before the result, choose a side. A has the advantage of being central. B is closer to the laptop. Which advantage matters more?

On a quiet day I try each of six locations exactly once: the office desk, one bedroom, the television, the kitchen, another bedroom and my phone's usual spot. The floor plan is twelve units wide and eight high. A sits at six across and four up. B sits at two across and six up. The other dots do not move.

I draw the first circle around A. The television, both bedrooms, the kitchen and the phone are inside. That's five successes. My office desk, near the far edge, is just outside.

I draw the same-sized circle around B. It covers the desk, a bedroom, the television and the phone. It misses the kitchen and the other bedroom. Four successes.

The center wins five to four. That's what I'd expect from the standard advice to put the router somewhere central.

But I did not set out to collect rooms. I set out to get through a workday.

So I keep the router positions and the six device locations exactly as they were. I change only my schedule. On this next day I use the laptop at my desk three times, instead of once. Every other location still gets one use. That means eight attempted sessions.

The central router doesn't suddenly stretch to the desk because I need it more. A still gets its five successful uses, and loses all three office sessions.

B loses its two distant spots, exactly as before. But it passes all three office sessions, plus its other three covered spots. Six successes. B wins the workday, six to five, even while it still covers fewer separate locations.

One extra laptop use makes the two arrangements tie at five. Two extra uses make B the leader. The result isn't hidden in a complicated Wi-Fi formula. It's hiding in how often I need the one place that A misses.

Now I ask a different practical question. Do I have to move the router at all? In a second version of this model, I move my desk three grid units toward the center, while the shelves stay fixed. Both routers now reach the laptop.

On that adjusted plan, A reaches all six places. During the same eight-use workday, A scores eight and B scores six. Moving a work spot instead of the router flips the choice back.

None of those scores is a measured speed, a signal-strength reading or a recommendation to drag a live router across the room. Real walls, floors, cable routes and interference could change everything. I'd start with the spot where the connection actually fails, check its signal and throughput, and compare real placements if the cables allow it.

The drawing answers a smaller but useful question: what am I optimizing? If I care about every location equally, the center wins this test. If my office gets repeated use while the other rooms don't, the side shelf wins. If the desk moves closer, the center wins again.

That is why I won't pick a router position just by putting a pin in the middle of a floor plan. I'll draw the places where I actually need the connection first.
