How could a town be buried by volcanic mud when scientists had already mapped the danger? And why didn't that map save its people?

In November 1985, Armero sat in a Colombian valley, far below the snow-covered volcano Nevado del Ruiz. The mountain had shown signs of waking for months. Scientists had identified valleys that could carry destructive flows toward towns below.

The strange thing was that Armero wasn't directly beside the crater. The threat could travel through rivers.

On the night of November thirteenth, an eruption threw hot debris across ice near the summit. Water rushed downhill, picking up rock, soil and trees. It became a lahar: a moving mass of mud and debris powerful enough to destroy buildings.

Rain hid the eruption from people down in the valley.

The flow took roughly two hours to reach Armero. By the time it spread beyond the river channel, much of the town had not evacuated. In minutes, streets and homes were swept away or buried. Tens of thousands died across the affected communities.

But the route wasn't a surprise discovered afterward. Scientists had prepared a hazard map before the eruption. A monitoring station had recorded activity. Information reached emergency coordinators.

What failed was the chain between knowing the danger, explaining it, making a decision, and getting people to safer ground in time. Later studies found that the maps were remarkably accurate, while plans and warnings had not become an effective evacuation.

Today, Colombia monitors its volcanoes with far stronger scientific systems. Armero's lesson wasn't simply to draw a better map. It was that a warning is only useful when somebody can act on it.
