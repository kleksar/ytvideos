What happens when a warning is scientifically right, but the people in its path still don't escape? How can a volcano destroy a town far from its summit, without a river of lava anywhere near the houses?

Those questions lead to Armero, Colombia, in November 1985.

The town lay on low ground beside the Lagunillas River. Nevado del Ruiz stood above the surrounding valleys, its summit covered in ice. If the volcano erupted, the nearest slopes wouldn't be the only places in danger. Water and debris could travel through the river system for many kilometres.

Months before the disaster, the mountain had already been showing signs of activity. Earthquakes and gases near the summit prompted calls for monitoring and planning. Scientists worked to identify the paths a destructive volcanic mudflow might follow. A hazard map was prepared before the fatal eruption.

Yet a map couldn't decide who should leave their homes. It couldn't call a family at night, or explain which hillside was safe. Those jobs belonged to a chain of scientists, officials, emergency services and the people at risk.

And that chain was not ready.

In September, a smaller explosion had pushed the danger back onto officials' agendas. Meetings followed. There was more scientific information, but not a working evacuation plan that people throughout Armero understood and could use. Later studies found that some emergency plans had been prepared but not shared effectively. Responsibility for turning an assessment into an order was unclear.

Then came November thirteenth.

A storm gathered over Nevado del Ruiz. The weather hid the summit from towns below. Late that evening, the volcano erupted. Hot rock and ash moved over the snow and ice.

The dangerous result was not a wall of lava rolling down the mountain.

Heat melted ice. Water began running through the valleys, carrying loose volcanic material. As the flood tore into the riverbeds, it collected more rocks, soil and debris. The mixture grew into lahars: moving rivers of mud that could carry boulders and destroy buildings.

The valleys acted like channels, guiding the flows away from the crater. The Lagunillas carried one toward Armero.

There was a terrible interval between the eruption and the town's destruction. The mudflow took roughly two hours to arrive. This wasn't an instantaneous blast that left no possible time to move. But time on a clock is not the same as a warning reaching a household.

Information about volcanic activity had reached emergency coordinators. Risk areas had been mapped. Some communities had been told they were vulnerable. Still, warnings and decisions did not produce a broad evacuation of Armero before the mud arrived. The rain and poor communications made a confused situation worse.

When the lahar broke out of the river channel onto the flat ground, it spread through homes and streets. Buildings were torn apart or buried. The town suffered catastrophic loss of life, and nearby communities were also struck by other flows. Roughly twenty-five thousand people died across the disaster, according to Colombia's geological service.

Rescue was extraordinarily difficult. Fresh volcanic mud can remain unstable and deep. Streets, landmarks and buildings vanish beneath debris. Reaching survivors does not mean rescuers can immediately lift them free.

Afterward, investigators compared what the scientists had predicted with where the mud actually travelled. The remarkable finding was not that nobody had understood the volcano. The hazard maps had outlined the danger with considerable accuracy.

That made the other failure harder to ignore. A warning must travel through several hands: someone interprets the science, somebody decides what action is necessary, a message reaches the public, and people know where to go. Lose one link and even a correct map may do little.

Armero changed volcanic risk work in Colombia. Over the following decades, the country built stronger monitoring and a much larger effort to explain risks to communities. At the disaster's fortieth anniversary, Colombia's geological service was still revisiting the event with scientists and survivors.

The final answer is uncomfortable. The volcano provided the force. But it was the failure to turn a mapped threat into an effective evacuation that left so many people in its path.
