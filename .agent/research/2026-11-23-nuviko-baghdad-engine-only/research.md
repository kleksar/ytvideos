## Three competitive bids
1. **DHL A300:** exact Long opening: “How do you land a burning cargo jet when the steering wheel no longer works?” First visible action: A300's damaged left wing in flight, not a generic explosion. Development: loss of control → throttle discovery → gear/fuel constraints → right-to-left runway change → final thrust correction → landing. Payoff: three survivors and an Airbus safety analysis explaining why two throttles sufficed. Objection: TV episode *Attack over Baghdad* (2005) and several established online retellings; factual source availability is not proof of format demand.
2. **Qantas QF32:** opening proposal: “The engine failed. Then the cockpit's warnings kept multiplying.” Development: uncontained turbine failure → interlinked damage → crew assesses landing performance → touchdown → delayed shutdown/evacuation. Payoff: complex teamwork prevents casualties. Primary factual research still needed for exact technical steps. Objection: heavily covered by Air Crash Investigation; a ten-minute retelling may lose the non-specialist audience among systems details.
3. **USS Samuel B. Roberts:** opening proposal: “The mine ripped open the frigate, and the crew chose not to leave.” Development: minefield detected → mine strike → cracked structure, fire and flooding → five hours' work → towing and repair. Payoff: crew saves ship. Source: Navy History and Heritage Command. Objection: same naval rescue/fire energy as recent USS Franklin Draft PR; strongest alternative for non-aviation variety but less distinctive in today's rotation.

Why DHL wins narrowly: learning a new control method **during the event**, a forced runway switch, and last-moment power increase each change the next action rather than repeat a big explosion. QF32 is a genuine rival, not a straw man. Distinctive angle: the crucial act was not “pilot heroism” in general, but discovering a controllable relationship among pitch, speed and engine thrust while the airplane remained damaged. The 10m version risks converting that lesson into a lecture.

## Primary factual source and claim map
**Airbus flight safety presentation:** Yannick Malinge, “A300B4 Loss of All Hydraulics, Baghdad: A Remarkable Example of Airmanship,” ISASI 2004, https://asasi.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Malinge_Airbus-Bagdhad_ISASI04.pdf (read pages 2–7; document is a post-incident technical presentation, not a public investigation transcript). On 2026-11-23 the document text and representative page images were inspected.

- pp. 2–3: Nov 22 2003 flight Baghdad → Bahrain, three crew and their flight hours; takeoff weight ~100 tonnes, slats-only climb at 215 knots; missile at ~8000 ft; green/yellow hydraulic loss and third system fading ~20 seconds later; no normal control movement, fixed flight-control configuration.
- pp. 3–4: climb and large left turn, thrust affects pitch, paradoxical increase of speed with lower power, gravity gear lowering, wing damage, ~3500 kg fuel loss, differential-thrust bank with lag; route back, long final, wind and slow pitch cycle; intended 33R changed to 33L, ~220 knots target.
- pp. 4–5: at ~250 feet nose down and engines increased to recover; thrust kept up on landing, mild bank/heading deviation, touched down 25 minutes after strike, ran onto sand through fence and stopped after ~1 km; crew survives.
- pp. 6–7: distinguish knowledge of general principles from simulator training for an identical incident. No universal safety checklist is claimed.
**Contemporaneous reporting:** *Washington Post*, December 12, 2003, https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/2003/12/13/missile-strike-in-iraq-leads-to-phenomenal-landing/fef6e26e-0988-4df3-ab0d-3ac18945d867/ ; source behind a possible access restriction; available indexed full article corroborates basic chronology and off-pavement stop, but reported 16 minutes differs from Airbus 25 minutes. ## Source-grounded 10m chronology correction — 2026-11-23
The revised Long variant corrects the order of events against Malinge (Airbus/ISASI 2004): the first learning period was symmetrical thrust/pitch and an unsteady initial 360°; emergency gravity-gear extension occurred while speed was already slightly above 270 knots, slowing toward 210; **only then** did the captain learn effective lateral control through unequal thrust with delayed roll response and several excursions just beyond 30° (PDF pp. 3–4). The later return included a separate 360°, planned long final, course change from 33R to 33L, power increase at about 250 ft, and right-engine reduction at about 100 ft while left thrust remained high (p. 4). These are distinct decisions, not cockpit dialogue. No new precise time for each turn or fuel crossfeed activation is asserted. The report states the engineer was *prepared* to open crossfeed if necessary; the narration does not claim he did. All other variants preserve their previous evidence. Primary: https://asasi.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Malinge_Airbus-Bagdhad_ISASI04.pdf; report pp. 3–5 (web PDF viewed and selected page images inspected on 2026-11-23).
