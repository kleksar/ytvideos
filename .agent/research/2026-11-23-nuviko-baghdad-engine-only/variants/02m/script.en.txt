The pilot moved the controls. The cargo jet barely responded.

A missile had struck its left wing just after takeoff from Baghdad. Now the wing was burning, and all three hydraulic systems were failing. The pilots could not move the normal flight controls.

This was November 2003. Three men were aboard an Airbus A300 carrying mail to Bahrain. The aircraft began climbing and turning almost by itself.

Captain Eric Gennotte discovered something strange. Reducing engine power lowered the nose. Increasing it raised the nose. He could also turn by pushing one engine harder than the other, but the response came late.

That was all the steering he had.

First officer Steeve Michielsen helped work out their position. Flight engineer Mario Rofail monitored the remaining fuel. Fuel was escaping through the damaged wing, and both engines had to keep running.

They lowered the landing gear using an emergency procedure. The added drag helped bring the speed down. Then they aimed for Baghdad airport.

A crosswind pushed them off line with the longer runway. They adjusted for the shorter one. The aircraft was still moving far faster than a normal landing.

Near the ground, its nose began to fall. Gennotte added power to lift it again. He couldn't simply close the throttles to land; that could have sent the damaged plane into the ground.

The wheels touched. The jet ran off the runway, through sand and a fence, and finally stopped.

All three crew members survived. The flight controls never recovered. Twenty-five minutes after the missile hit, they had landed by moving two engine throttles.

And those controls had been useless from the instant the pilot first tried to steer.
