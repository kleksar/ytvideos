How do you land a burning cargo jet when the steering wheel no longer works? Why would lowering the power make its speed increase? And what if the runway you're aiming for stops being the right one?

In November 2003, an Airbus A300 took off from Baghdad. It was carrying mail toward Bahrain. There were no passengers, only three crew members: Captain Eric Gennotte, First Officer Steeve Michielsen, and Flight Engineer Mario Rofail.

Their departure was designed to get them away from the ground quickly. Baghdad was dangerous airspace. The jet climbed with its engines working normally, and the crew had every reason to expect the short flight to continue.

At about eight thousand feet, a missile struck the left wing.

An explosion shook the aircraft. Warnings sounded in the cockpit. The left wing was on fire, and hydraulic pressure disappeared from one system, then another, then the third. The controls the pilots normally used could no longer move the wing surfaces or the tail.

The airplane still had two running engines. But when Gennotte moved the controls, the aircraft did not obey. It began an uneven climb and a wide turn to the left. A damaged wing and leaking fuel made that turn even harder to stop.

For a moment, the most basic question was not where to land. It was how to make the aircraft fly in any chosen direction.

Gennotte began moving the throttles. Lowering power brought the nose down, but it also made the aircraft accelerate. Raising power lifted the nose again. The tail was stuck in the position it had been set to during climb, so changing speed and changing attitude were tangled together.

The crew then needed to solve a second problem: direction. More thrust from one engine could make the aircraft bank. But unlike a steering wheel, the response was delayed. The turn might continue after the pilot made a correction.

While Gennotte learned this awkward new way of flying, Michielsen helped with position, height, and the route back. Rofail watched the fuel. Thousands of kilograms had escaped through the damaged wing. If one engine stopped, the crew would lose part of its only remaining control system.

They could not use the normal landing configuration. The flaps were fixed in place. Even the landing gear had to be lowered by an emergency gravity procedure. That helped: the gear created drag, slowing the jet and making it more manageable.

Eventually the crew could head back toward Baghdad. But steering with engine power did not mean they could point at a runway and hold a straight line. The aircraft's nose continued to rise and fall. A crosswind disturbed its course. Michielsen called out heights and distances while Gennotte made corrections whose effect arrived a moment later.

Their first target was the longer runway. The aircraft drifted away from that approach, so Gennotte chose the nearer, shorter runway instead.

This was not an elegant landing plan. It was the strip of pavement the aircraft could still reach.

On final approach the jet was moving at roughly two hundred and twenty knots. Close to the ground, the nose started to drop. Gennotte increased engine power to lift it. Normally a pilot reduces power to touch down. Here that familiar movement might have caused the damaged wing to fall and the airplane to strike the ground.

The A300 touched the runway while still slightly banked. The wheels held, but once on the ground the pilots still had no ordinary steering. The jet veered off the pavement, threw up a cloud of sand, and dragged a fence along its path.

The sand, unexpectedly, helped slow it. The aircraft stopped after a long run. The three crew members got out alive.

Twenty-five minutes had passed since the missile struck.

The surprising part is not that an engine can make an aircraft go forward. It is that the crew learned to use two engines to raise the nose, correct a turn, control a descent, and reach an actual runway while a wing was damaged and burning.

The aircraft's normal flight controls never returned. Its safe landing came from the crew discovering what still worked, dividing the tasks, and refusing to treat a familiar cockpit gesture as the only way to fly.
