How could three men land a large jet after every hydraulic system had failed? Why did the aircraft become harder to handle when they tried to slow down? And why did the last few hundred feet of the flight demand a move that would be wrong on an ordinary landing?

The answers begin with a flight that was supposed to last only a short time.

On November 22, 2003, a DHL-operated Airbus A300 prepared to leave Baghdad for Bahrain. It was a cargo flight carrying mainly mail. The three people aboard were Captain Eric Gennotte, First Officer Steeve Michielsen, and Flight Engineer Mario Rofail.

Baghdad was not a routine departure airport that year. Aircraft were exposed to threats from the ground, so this crew used a steep climb intended to leave the vulnerable area quickly. The A300 was relatively light for its size. It departed with its wing slats set for takeoff, retracted them early, and climbed at about 215 knots.

At first, the plan worked. The engines were producing thrust, the aircraft was climbing, and the pilots were flying a machine they knew.

At roughly eight thousand feet, a missile hit the left wing.

The blast tore through part of the wing structure. Fuel escaped, and a fire spread along the damaged area. In the cockpit, warning sounds followed one another. The flight engineer reported that two hydraulic systems had failed. About twenty seconds later, the remaining system was losing pressure too.

The flight controls had lost their power. Moving the control wheel or pedals no longer commanded the ailerons, elevator, or rudder. The slats and flaps were frozen. The spoilers could not be used, and the horizontal stabilizer was stuck where it had been trimmed for the climb.

The engines still ran. But with the tail fixed in place, engine power now affected more than speed.

The aircraft climbed and began a wide, uneven turn to the left. Its damaged left wing no longer produced the same forces as the right one. Fuel loss changed the balance again. Meanwhile the crew was trying to keep a burning aircraft in the sky without the controls pilots normally rely on.

They declared an emergency, but that did not solve the first practical problem: how to stop the jet doing whatever its damaged shape and fixed controls made it do.

Gennotte experimented with the throttles. Moving both of them back made the nose fall. That sounded useful—until the aircraft picked up speed. Adding power raised the nose and could restore a climb. Instead of simply commanding a descent with the control column, the crew had to work out the relationship between thrust, pitch, and speed.

There was a reason the response seemed so strange. The stabilizer had been set for the aircraft's climb. With no hydraulic power, it could no longer move to compensate when the aircraft's speed changed. Each adjustment of engine thrust produced a new balance, and the nose moved with it.

They were learning the characteristics of this particular damaged jet in real time. A procedure that worked at one moment could not simply be copied without watching what the aircraft did next.

The first recovery was not a turn. It was a decision to slow down without losing the fragile pitch control Gennotte had just found.

As the aircraft kept climbing and circling left, its speed rose above 270 knots. The crew had no working flaps or spoilers to add drag. Gennotte ordered an emergency gravity extension of the landing gear, even though the jet was already slightly above the gear's normal extension limit. The wheels descended. Their resistance finally helped bring the airspeed back toward 210 knots.

Only then could Gennotte seriously work on steering. One throttle pushed farther forward than the other could make the aircraft yaw, then bank. But the bank arrived late. During the experiments, the roll went a little beyond thirty degrees more than once. A correction that looked ineffective at first could become too strong seconds later.

The damaged left wing complicated everything. Its shape and weight no longer matched the right wing, so the aircraft kept trying to turn left. Gennotte had to find enough difference in engine power to resist that tendency, while using both levers together to hold an average descent. A level attitude one moment offered no guarantee of a level attitude the next.

There were now three separate jobs. Gennotte worked the thrust levers and watched how the aircraft responded. Michielsen declared the emergency and took responsibility for navigation and the captain's distance and altitude calls. Rofail watched the engines and the fuel flowing from the damaged wing.

The Airbus technical account records about three and a half tonnes of fuel escaping. If the left tank emptied, Rofail could open crossfeed to supply its engine from the other side. Doing that too soon, however, could send fuel from the intact wing straight out through the breach. He had to preserve both engines without sacrificing the remaining supply.

This was the second trap. A normal airplane can lose one engine and still have working flight controls. Here, losing either engine would also take away a large part of the crew's improvised steering.

The first circle had been an uncontrolled consequence of learning pitch by thrust. When the crew finally learned to influence bank as well, the airport had vanished from sight. Getting back would now require a planned route, not another instinctive turn. Once they could alter both pitch and direction, Michielsen worked out a route back. They needed a long, straight approach—about twenty nautical miles—because quick corrections were not available.

They circled and began to line up for runway 33 Right, the longer of Baghdad's two parallel runways.

On an ordinary approach, the crew can correct a wind drift with the normal controls and can reduce speed as needed. Here, every adjustment had a cost. The airplane continued to rise and dip in a slow oscillation, taking roughly a minute to complete a cycle. A crosswind stirred that motion and pushed the aircraft sideways.

Warnings sounded because the landing configuration was abnormal. The pilots could hear them, but they had no way to restore the missing flaps or the hydraulic systems.

Michielsen watched the numbers and called out distance and altitude. Gennotte judged the trend of the aircraft rather than a momentary pointer: was the average path taking them toward the runway, or would the next pitch change carry them too high or too low?

The longer runway gradually ceased to be the reachable one. Their actual track lay closer to runway 33 Left.

So Gennotte changed the target. It was shorter, and the aircraft was fast. But a landing strip you can still reach is more useful than a longer strip your controls will not let you line up with.

The approach speed was around 220 knots, far higher than a comfortable normal landing. The crew accepted that number because their improvised control depended on keeping enough thrust and airspeed to manage the aircraft.

And now came the most counterintuitive part.

Normally, a pilot reduces power shortly before the wheels touch the runway. Here, that could be disastrous. Lowering both throttles would change the airplane's pitch. Reducing the unequal thrust could also allow the damaged left wing to pull the aircraft into another turn.

Near 250 feet, the nose began to fall. Gennotte increased power on both engines to lift it again. The action bought the height he needed but could not simply be repeated without consequence: more thrust also changed the aircraft's speed and path.

At about a hundred feet, the aircraft was reaching the runway threshold but its nose still pointed roughly ten degrees away from the runway's direction. Gennotte reduced power on the right engine while keeping the left engine working hard. The jet started banking right and its path came closer to the runway axis. That remaining left-engine thrust also kept the nose from dropping in the final moments.

The A300 was still not perfectly aligned. It approached the runway slightly nose-up, with a noticeable bank and an awkward sideways heading.

Twenty-five minutes after the missile impact, the wheels touched runway 33 Left.

Landing did not mean the pilots had regained control. On the ground, the jet still could not steer normally. It veered off the side of the pavement, while the engines were selected to reverse thrust. Sand billowed around the fuselage as the aircraft crossed the unprepared ground and dragged through a fence.

The sand provided extra braking. After a ground run of about a kilometre, the A300 stopped.

The crew evacuated. All three survived. No passenger had been aboard, and no one on the aircraft was killed.

The rear section of the left wing had been torn open. The ordinary controls never returned. Yet the engines had remained available all the way from the first erratic climb to the last correction above the runway.

The result was not an effortless emergency landing. The captain had first learned what adding and removing power did to the nose. The landing gear had then slowed the aircraft enough for him to learn how unequal power affected the turn. Michielsen's call-outs made a long approach possible, and Rofail kept watching the fuel that fed the improvised controls.

Afterward, an Airbus safety presentation drew a careful distinction. No crew could train for every possible pattern of complete hydraulic failure. What pilots could learn were general principles: thrust may affect pitch; unequal thrust may affect bank only after a delay; the safest manageable speed may be much higher than normal; and pulling both levers back just before landing can remove the only control still available.

Over Baghdad, the crew had to discover which of those ideas worked in this particular damaged airplane. Their decisive act was not a single heroic gesture. It was a chain of adjustments, each one possible because the last one had kept the engines running and the aircraft in the air.
