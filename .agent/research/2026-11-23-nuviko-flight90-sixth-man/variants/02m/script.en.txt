Why did a rescue helicopter leave one passenger in the freezing Potomac? And why did it take more than a year to give that man a name?

Washington, January 13, 1982. Snow has delayed Air Florida Flight 90. The Boeing 737 has been deiced, but it spends more time on the ground while snow keeps falling.

During takeoff, the engines are not producing the thrust the crew expects. Ice has also damaged the wings' ability to lift the aircraft. Seconds after leaving the runway, the jet strikes the Fourteenth Street Bridge and drops into the river.

Six people make it to the surface. Ice surrounds them. A United States Park Police helicopter arrives and lowers a rescue line.

The crew reports seeing one man pass that line to other survivors instead of taking it himself. The helicopter carries people toward shore, returns, and tries again. A bystander, Lenny Skutnik, jumps into the water to help pull a struggling woman out.

Five people from the aircraft survive. When the helicopter comes back for the man who passed the line, he is gone.

Investigators soon know that most victims died from crash injuries. One passenger, however, died from drowning and exposure. His name is Arland Williams Junior.

But the identification isn't simple. Eyewitness descriptions of the man in the water don't fully match Williams's photograph. The Coast Guard reviews the evidence, including medical findings and the positions where bodies were recovered.

In June 1983, it officially identifies Williams as the man who repeatedly passed the lifeline to others. He receives a posthumous lifesaving medal.

The crash investigation also establishes preventable failures in the aircraft's preparation and takeoff. But the rescue leaves a different record: five survived the flight, and the sixth man who reached the surface was remembered first for what he did, then for his name.
