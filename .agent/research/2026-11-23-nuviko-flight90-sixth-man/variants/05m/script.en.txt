Why did a helicopter keep returning to a man in the Potomac River without bringing him ashore? How did a short flight in Washington end beside a bridge? And why was the identity of the man in the water still disputed the following year?

On January 13, 1982, a snowstorm closes Washington National Airport for part of the afternoon. Passengers on Air Florida Flight 90 are waiting for a flight to Florida. Their Boeing 737 is deiced, but the work does not create a permanent shield. While the aircraft waits for clearance, snow continues to fall.

The pilots finally taxi out. During takeoff, the first officer notices unusual engine indications. The captain continues. The airplane lifts off, but it cannot climb normally. Its nose rises while its speed drops.

At 4:01 p.m., seconds after takeoff, the jet strikes the Fourteenth Street Bridge, hitting occupied vehicles, and falls into the frozen Potomac. Four people on the bridge are killed.

There are seventy-nine people aboard. Six of them manage to reach the water's surface. The rest of the story is no longer happening in a cockpit. It is happening among broken ice, in sight of the riverbank.

A United States Park Police helicopter comes in low. Pilot Donald Usher and crewman Gene Windsor have a rescue line, but bringing someone across that water takes repeated approaches. The helicopter cannot simply pick up all six at once.

Its crew later describes a man who receives the line and passes it to someone else. The helicopter returns with the line, and the man passes it again. That is what the crew says it saw. There is no need to invent what the man was thinking.

One survivor, Priscilla Tirado, is still struggling in the water. Lenny Skutnik, watching from shore, jumps in and helps bring her to safety. Another bystander, Roger Olian, also enters the river during the rescue. By the time the operation ends, five people who had been on the plane are alive.

The helicopter crew has not forgotten the remaining man. When it returns for him, he has vanished beneath the water.

Now the story divides into two investigations. One concerns the airplane. The National Transportation Safety Board examines the delay after deicing, snow and ice on the wings, and the crew's failure to use engine anti-ice. The investigators find that the engine instruments gave misleading indications of thrust and that the takeoff was continued despite warnings. They conclude that several failures combined: impaired lift, insufficient thrust and a decision not to stop when the indications were abnormal.

The second investigation begins with an unnamed passenger. Witnesses remember a balding man with a moustache. A possible match is Arland Williams Junior, a forty-six-year-old bank examiner. But photographs of Williams show a beard. One survivor, Joseph Stiley, also disputes the simple account that the man repeatedly handed the line to others.

There is another kind of evidence. Medical examiners find that Williams, unlike most of those who died, survived the initial impact and died from drowning and exposure. Recovery divers know where the bodies were found. The Coast Guard does not treat a single photograph or recollection as sufficient. It puts those records together.

Williams's mother asks that the unidentified rescuer, if he was her son, be acknowledged by his name. The Coast Guard reopens its inquiry. In June 1983, it announces its conclusion: the man who gave others the lifeline was Arland D. Williams Junior. The president presents his family with a posthumous Gold Lifesaving Medal.

That does not undo the disagreements between eyewitness accounts. It tells us what the official investigation concluded after weighing them.

The crash has a public lesson about ice and decisions in an aircraft. The rescue has a narrower, human ending. Six people reached the river's surface. Five survived. For months, the sixth was known only as the man who kept passing the line. His name was the last part of the rescue to reach shore.
