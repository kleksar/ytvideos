Who was the man who passed a helicopter's rescue line to other people while he remained in a freezing river? Why did the aircraft behind him crash within moments of takeoff? And why could investigators not simply put a name to his actions?

Washington, January 13, 1982. Heavy snow has interrupted normal operations at National Airport. Air Florida Flight 90 is scheduled to fly south, away from the storm. It has seventy-nine people aboard, including the crew.

The airport closes while snow-clearing work goes on. When flights resume, the Boeing 737 must be deiced. Snow and ice are removed from the aircraft before it heads toward the runway. But the protection has a practical limit: the aircraft is about to spend more time on the ground in falling snow.

That delay matters. Deicing is an event, not a guarantee that a wing stays clean while the weather continues. The airplane waits for clearance. During the long ground operation, the crew also makes decisions that investigators will later examine one by one.

The wait has created two problems the pilots cannot see from their seats. Snow can settle on the wings again after treatment. Ice can also affect the pressure measurements used to judge engine thrust. Deicing the airplane earlier does not solve either problem indefinitely.

The crew has not switched on engine anti-ice, although the conditions require it. In that state, ice can distort the pressure signals and make the engines appear to be producing more thrust than they really are. The instruments can look reassuring at the very moment the airplane needs every bit of power.

As the Boeing begins its takeoff roll, the first officer notices readings that do not look right. The captain continues. After liftoff, the nose rises, but the airplane fails to gain enough speed or height. Snow and ice on the leading edges of the wings have also weakened their lift. Two problems that were difficult to recognize separately now meet in the same few seconds.

At 4:01 p.m., Flight 90 hits the Fourteenth Street Bridge. It strikes occupied vehicles, killing four people on the bridge, before falling into the ice-covered Potomac. The jet had been meant to fly to Florida. Instead, the broken aircraft is in the river beside one of Washington's busiest routes.

What people on the bank see next will leave a second question that no flight recorder can answer.

Six people from the aircraft have reached the water's surface. They are surrounded by floating ice and pieces of wreckage. The other seventy-three people aboard will not survive. Rescuers cannot treat this as a normal water landing: they have to reach people in water cold enough to make every attempt difficult.

A United States Park Police helicopter arrives. Pilot Donald Usher and crewman Gene Windsor fly low enough to offer a lifeline. Their method is to pull or carry people toward the riverbank and then return. The helicopter can approach individual survivors, but it cannot load all six at once.

The crew remembers a particular man in the water. When the line is offered to him, he passes it to someone else. On a later approach, he does so again. The helicopter's account becomes the foundation of a story told around the country: an unknown passenger helping others leave while his own chance recedes.

It is important to keep the limits of that account in view. We have the crew's observations and the rescue record. We do not have the man's private thoughts or a recording that tells us why he acted as he did.

Other people are also trying to help. Roger Olian goes into the river. On the shore, bystander Lenny Skutnik sees one of the survivors struggling. Her name is Priscilla Tirado. He enters the freezing water and helps bring her out. The rescues are not the work of one person or one device. The helicopter crew, people on the bank and survivors themselves are all part of what happens.

Five people from the plane are saved. Eventually the helicopter returns for the man it remembers passing the line. There is no one left to lift. The sixth person who reached the surface has disappeared.

The helicopter has made repeated journeys to shore, and the result is stark: of the six people seen alive at the surface, five have been brought out of the water. The sixth is missing. Witnesses can describe the choice they believe they saw him make, but nobody on the helicopter can identify him by name.

The investigators examining the Boeing are asking a different question. How could a jet that had received deicing treatment still fail to climb? The answer begins with the time spent waiting in continuing snow. The treatment had removed accumulation earlier; it had not kept the wings clean until departure. Investigators also find that the crew did not use engine anti-ice.

That second omission mattered in an unexpected way. On this aircraft, engine pressure readings helped the crew set takeoff power. Icing affected those readings, leaving less real thrust than the instruments implied. The first officer noticed abnormal indications during the roll, but the takeoff continued. At the same time, contamination of the wings reduced lift and increased the airplane's tendency to pitch up.

The safety board's conclusion is about the combination. Either degraded thrust or contaminated wings alone might have left the plane able to continue flying. Together, they took away the margin it needed. The board also cited the extended wait after deicing and the crew's limited winter jet experience as contributing factors. Its recommendations address how crews prepare for and respond to the conditions, not the identity of the passenger in the river.

That other investigation has a more fragile starting point: what two people in a moving helicopter remember seeing while they were trying to save lives.

Meanwhile, the unidentified man has become a public figure without an identity. Reports call him the sixth man. The helicopter crew describes him as balding, with gray at the sides and a moustache. Their description includes no beard.

A possible match emerges: Arland D. Williams Junior, a forty-six-year-old bank examiner from Atlanta. His body has been recovered, and the medical findings are striking. Unlike the other victims, whose fatal injuries were attributed to the impact, Williams died of drowning and exposure. He had survived the crash itself. That makes him a plausible candidate for the person seen alive near the wreckage, but it does not by itself establish what he did with the rope.

Then the eyewitness accounts complicate the picture. The helicopter crew remembers a balding man with graying hair at the sides and a moustache, without a beard. Photographs of Williams show a beard. Survivor Joseph Stiley also challenges the uncomplicated account of a stranger repeatedly passing the line away. He recalls events in the water differently.

Those contradictions are not disposable details. The crew is looking down from a helicopter; Stiley is struggling among wreckage and ice. The two views are not interchangeable. Nor can an old photograph prove exactly how someone looked at the river that afternoon. None of those observations, taken alone, settles the question.

The Coast Guard has been considering awards for the rescuers, but identifying the unnamed man requires another examination of the evidence. Williams's mother, Virginia, writes to President Reagan asking for the unnamed rescuer to be acknowledged by name if he really was her son. The request puts an unresolved human question back before officials months after the crash.

Commander D. C. Addison and other investigators compare more than faces. They return to medical reports, recovery locations recorded by divers, photographs and interviews. They ask who could have been alive in the water after the impact, and whether those accounts are consistent with the places where the bodies were recovered.

The medical findings establish that Williams lived through the collision; the recovery and witness information helps narrow the candidates. The Coast Guard has to weigh all of it. The descriptions do not suddenly match perfectly, and a single clue cannot transform the entire account into certainty.

The Coast Guard reaches a conclusion. In June 1983, the White House announces that the sixth man was Arland D. Williams Junior. At a ceremony, President Ronald Reagan presents his family with the Coast Guard's Gold Lifesaving Medal awarded to Williams after his death.

The name comes from an official finding, not a unanimous change of memory. The helicopter crew's account of the lifeline remains part of the record, as does the disagreement of a survivor who was there. It is possible to state the Coast Guard's conclusion without pretending that every witness saw the same thing.

By then the aviation investigation has also published its conclusions. One establishes how the plane was lost: contaminated wings, misleading thrust indications and decisions made during a winter takeoff. The other identifies the man who, according to the helicopter crew, gave up his turn at the rescue line.

The span of the Fourteenth Street Bridge struck by Flight 90 would later carry Arland Williams's name. But the recognition came long after the rescue helicopter had flown away. Six people had reached the surface of the Potomac. Five survived. A family waited seventeen months for the official answer about the sixth.

The flight ended at the river. The work of determining what happened, and remembering who was there, took much longer.
