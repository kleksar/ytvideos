Who was the man who passed a helicopter's rescue line to other people while he remained in a freezing river? Why did the aircraft behind him crash within moments of takeoff? And why could investigators not simply put a name to his actions?

Washington, January 13, 1982. Heavy snow has interrupted normal operations at National Airport. Air Florida Flight 90 is scheduled to fly south, away from the storm. It has seventy-nine people aboard, including the crew.

The airport closes while snow-clearing work goes on. When flights resume, the Boeing 737 must be deiced. Snow and ice are removed from the aircraft before it heads toward the runway. But the protection has a practical limit: the aircraft is about to spend more time on the ground in falling snow.

That delay matters. Deicing is an event, not a guarantee that a wing stays clean while the weather continues. The airplane waits for clearance. During the long ground operation, the crew also makes decisions that investigators will later examine one by one.

To understand what happens in the next few seconds, it helps to separate two problems. Ice changes how air flows over a wing. A small amount near the front can reduce the wing's ability to lift the plane. Separately, an engine instrument tells pilots how much thrust the engines appear to be producing. That reading depends on the instrument sensing conditions correctly.

The crew does not use engine anti-ice as the situation requires. Ice can interfere with the engine pressure measurement, making a reading look reassuring when actual thrust is lower. This is not a case where the pilots simply forgot to push the engines forward. They were flying with misleading information about the force available.

As the plane starts its takeoff roll, the first officer calls attention to unusual indications. The captain chooses to continue. The runway is behind them when the airplane begins to climb. But the nose rises sharply and the aircraft does not gain the speed and height it needs.

At 4:01 in the afternoon, the jet strikes the Fourteenth Street Bridge. It hits occupied vehicles, killing four people on the bridge, then drops into the ice-covered Potomac River. An ordinary route out of a city has become a rescue scene within sight of its streets.

The National Transportation Safety Board will eventually identify a chain of failures, not one magic switch that explains everything: delay after deicing, snow and ice still on the wings, failure to use engine anti-ice, misleading engine indications, and a takeoff continued after the crew noticed trouble. The two physical effects reinforce each other. The aircraft has less lift than it should and less thrust than its instruments suggest.

That explains the crash, but it does not explain the scene that people on the riverbank are now watching.

Six people from the aircraft have reached the water's surface. They are surrounded by floating ice and pieces of wreckage. The other seventy-three people aboard will not survive. Rescuers cannot treat this as a normal water landing: they have to reach people in water cold enough to make every attempt difficult.

A United States Park Police helicopter arrives. Pilot Donald Usher and crewman Gene Windsor fly low enough to offer a lifeline. Their method is to pull or carry people toward the riverbank and then return. The helicopter can approach individual survivors, but it cannot load all six at once.

The crew remembers a particular man in the water. When the line is offered to him, he passes it to someone else. On a later approach, he does so again. The helicopter's account becomes the foundation of a story told around the country: an unknown passenger helping others leave while his own chance recedes.

It is important to keep the limits of that account in view. We have the crew's observations and the rescue record. We do not have the man's private thoughts or a recording that tells us why he acted as he did.

Other people are also trying to help. Roger Olian goes into the river. On the shore, bystander Lenny Skutnik sees one of the survivors struggling. Her name is Priscilla Tirado. He enters the freezing water and helps bring her out. The rescues are not the work of one person or one device. The helicopter crew, people on the bank and survivors themselves are all part of what happens.

Five people from the plane are saved. Eventually the helicopter returns for the man it remembers passing the line. There is no one left to lift. The sixth person who reached the surface has disappeared.

For rescuers, the event is already over. For investigators, two different questions remain. One is technical: what prevented the 737 from climbing? The other is human: who was the man who had stayed in the water?

The aviation investigation follows records, crew actions, aircraft characteristics and weather. The airplane had been deiced; that fact matters. It means the chain of events cannot be reduced to a claim that nothing at all was done about the snow. But the time between treatment and takeoff in continuing precipitation matters too. A surface that was clear earlier may not be clear when the engines accelerate.

Investigators also examine what the pilots could see. The first officer's attention to abnormal engine indications did not lead to an abandoned takeoff. The Board treats the decision to continue as part of the probable cause. In its findings, limited winter operating experience contributes to the accident, but the report is specific about the crew's actions and the aircraft's behavior. It does not need imaginary dialogue or a simplified villain.

The Board's analysis finds that neither reduced thrust nor contaminated wings, taken alone, should have prevented sustained flight. Together they leave too little margin. That is a more revealing explanation than saying only that the plane was icy: two degraded systems met at the instant when the airplane most needed both.

The findings lead to safety recommendations. They cannot rescue the people who were on Flight 90, but they explain how an accident in one snowstorm can change the precautions considered on later flights.

Meanwhile, the unidentified man has become a public figure without an identity. Reports call him the sixth man. The helicopter crew describes him as balding, with gray at the sides and a moustache. Their description includes no beard.

One possible name is Arland D. Williams Junior. He was forty-six and worked as a bank examiner in Atlanta. His remains have been recovered from the river. Medical evidence indicates that he was the only one among the recovered fatalities who died from drowning and exposure rather than lethal injuries during the impact.

That sounds decisive, until the next detail arrives. Photographs of Williams show a beard. They do not neatly match the description given by the men in the helicopter. And survivor Joseph Stiley, who was close to another person in the water, does not confirm the straightforward version of the repeated handoffs. His recollection differs in significant ways.

What should an investigator do with that? A witness who saw the rescue from the air may have perceived something differently from a survivor fighting to stay afloat. A photograph may not represent every moment of a person's appearance. Yet those possibilities are explanations for uncertainty, not proof that one account must be right.

The Coast Guard already reviews the actions of the rescuers for lifesaving awards. The unresolved identity becomes another part of its work. Williams's mother, Virginia, writes to the president. She does not ask for a medal as a substitute for knowing. She asks for the man's name to be established, if that man was her son.

The inquiry is reopened. Commander D. C. Addison and other investigators return to evidence not fully available in the first days: medical examiner reports, photographs, interviews and the accounts of divers who recovered bodies and knew their positions in the wreckage.

Those positions are important because the unidentified rescuer was seen alive after the crash, in a location reachable from the wreckage. Most victims had sustained fatal impact injuries. Williams's recorded cause of death establishes that he lived beyond the initial collision. The evidence does not magically erase the eyewitness discrepancies, but together the records narrow the possibilities.

The Coast Guard reaches a conclusion. In June 1983, the White House announces that the sixth man was Arland D. Williams Junior. At a ceremony, President Ronald Reagan presents his family with the Coast Guard's Gold Lifesaving Medal awarded to Williams after his death.

Official identification is the payoff to the second investigation, not a claim that every witness suddenly remembered the same face. The distinction matters because an act can be well documented even while the record of exactly who did what remains contested for a time.

The first investigation also has a final answer. The NTSB finds that the aircraft took off with contaminated wing surfaces, without the required engine anti-ice, and without stopping after an abnormal indication was noticed. No single heroic action in the river could reverse what happened on the runway. The safety lesson lies in understanding that chain before another aircraft reaches the same point.

The second investigation ends with a name, but there is no comparable repair. Six people had reached the surface after the crash. Five got out of the river alive. The man the helicopter crew remembered passing the rescue line to others never did.

For seventeen months, his story was told without his name. What remained after the reports and awards was more precise than the early legend: a record of the rescue, the disagreements that complicated identification, and a Coast Guard finding that the sixth man was Arland Williams.

The flight ended at the river. The work of determining what happened, and remembering who was there, took much longer.
