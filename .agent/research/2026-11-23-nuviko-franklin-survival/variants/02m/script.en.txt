Two bombs struck an aircraft carrier packed with fully fueled, armed planes.

It was the morning of March 19, 1945. USS Franklin was launching an air strike off Japan when a Japanese aircraft hit her.

The first blasts ignited fuel. Then the American bombs and rockets stored aboard began exploding. Fires spread through the hangar and flight deck.

The crew lost much of its firefighting equipment. Smoke forced men out of the engine rooms. Franklin stopped moving, not far from the Japanese coast.

Below deck, engineer Donald Gary found hundreds of sailors trapped in a smoke-filled compartment. He searched for an escape route, then went back three times to lead groups out.

On deck, the ship's chaplain, Joseph O'Callahan, helped organize firefighting and directed sailors handling ammunition that could still explode.

A cruiser called Santa Fe came alongside and took off wounded and other survivors. Destroyers pulled men from the sea.

But the ship itself still had no power. Another cruiser, Pittsburgh, attached a towline to Franklin's anchor chain and began hauling the burning carrier away.

Even under tow, a Japanese plane attacked. Other ships and aircraft kept it away.

The next morning, Franklin regained steering and enough engine power to cast off the towline. She could move on her own.

She reached the American repair port at New York in April. Hundreds of her crew had died. Two men who helped save their shipmates, Gary and O'Callahan, received the Medal of Honor.

The Navy's later damage study found an important distinction: the fires were disastrous, but the ship's watertight structure below the hangar had remained intact. The crew still had to survive the fire, escape danger and restore power.
