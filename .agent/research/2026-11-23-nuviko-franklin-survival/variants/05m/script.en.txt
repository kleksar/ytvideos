How can an aircraft carrier lose its engines, most of its firefighting system and hundreds of men, yet still reach home? Who got the sailors out when the corridors filled with smoke? And what actually kept the ship from sinking?

On March 19, 1945, USS Franklin was off the coast of Japan. Her crew was preparing another air strike. Planes on the flight deck carried fuel, bombs and rockets. More aircraft were waiting in the hangar beneath them.

A Japanese aircraft got through the defenses and struck with two bombs. The explosions landed at the worst possible time: when the ship was full of weapons ready to be taken into the air.

Fire raced through the aircraft spaces. Then Franklin's own bombs and rockets began detonating. Some explosions opened gaps in the decks. Others damaged equipment the sailors needed to fight the fires. Smoke spread into spaces far below the flight deck.

The crew was no longer dealing with one fire. They were trying to reach trapped men, control armed explosives and keep the ship functioning, while the tools for those jobs were failing.

Engineer Donald Gary was below deck when he found several hundred sailors trapped in a smoke-filled mess compartment. The obvious exits were blocked or unusable. He searched through dark passages until he found a way out.

But finding it did not free everyone. Gary returned to the compartment three times and guided groups of men along the route. Only after that did he join the firefighting and help restore power.

Above him, a different kind of work was under way. The ship's chaplain, Joseph O'Callahan, was not simply tending wounded men. He organized firefighting parties, helped move ammunition and worked a hose to cool armed bombs on the deck. A bomb that had not exploded yet could still change the whole situation.

One cruiser, Santa Fe, moved directly alongside the carrier. Wounded sailors were transferred first, followed by many other survivors. Destroyers rescued people who had jumped or been thrown into the sea.

The men remaining aboard were fighting to keep the ship. But smoke had driven the engineering crew from the machinery spaces. Without engines, Franklin was motionless close to enemy territory.

Then another cruiser approached. Pittsburgh secured a towing wire to Franklin's starboard anchor chain. Moving a ship that size, damaged and without its own propulsion, was slow work. The tow began while fires and scattered explosions had not completely stopped.

This was not a safe escape route. During the tow, another Japanese aircraft attacked. The escort and air cover drove it off. Franklin still had to keep moving, and the people inside still had to get her engines back.

Gary entered a fireroom to help raise steam in a surviving boiler. Meanwhile, damage-control teams continued to contain the fires and restore systems. On the morning of March 20, steering came back. By midday the carrier could make enough speed on her own to release the towline.

So why had she stayed afloat through all of this? The Navy's later damage report offers an answer less cinematic than a miracle. Fire destroyed large parts of the upper ship, but the armored hangar deck shielded the vital spaces below. The main watertight structure remained intact, and there had been no torpedo strike below the waterline.

That did not make the disaster harmless. Hundreds had died, and many more were wounded. It explains why a ship unable to move for hours could still be recovered rather than lost at sea.

Franklin reached Ulithi on March 24 and eventually crossed the Pacific and Atlantic under her own power. On April 28, she arrived in New York, her flight deck visibly wrecked.

Gary and O'Callahan both received the Medal of Honor. Franklin would be repaired, but she never returned to combat. The men who had brought her home had saved a ship while losing many of the people who served on it.
