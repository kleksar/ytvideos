What does it take to move an aircraft carrier after its engines stop? How do you evacuate hundreds of sailors while bombs are still exploding on the deck? And if the ship survives, does that mean the men who saved it have won?

In March 1945, USS Franklin was operating close to the Japanese home islands. American carriers were launching strikes ahead of the battle for Okinawa. The distance to the enemy coast mattered: a damaged ship had very little room to retreat.

Franklin had been through another damaging attack the previous October. Repairs had put her back with the fleet. Now she was preparing a fresh wave of planes.

The flight deck was not an empty runway. Thirty-one aircraft were on it, fueled and armed for the strike. In the hangar below stood another twenty-two. Some were armed as well. The exact mix of weapons varied, but there were bombs and rockets in places where a fire could reach them.

On the morning of March 19, a Japanese aircraft appeared and dropped two bombs. They struck the carrier while the crews were working with those planes. That timing changed everything.

The initial bomb damage was serious. What followed was far larger. Fuel caught fire around the aircraft. Then American bombs and rockets began going off, one after another. The Navy's damage report describes five hours when the damage-control situation was effectively beyond control.

The explosions did not stay on the open deck. Some bombs fell through damaged surfaces into the hangar below. Fire spread into the galleries and the island that rose above the flight deck. More ammunition caught. Smoke traveled into compartments that had never been directly hit.

That is the first important distinction. Franklin was not struck by a great number of enemy bombs. Two hits triggered a chain of disasters because of the materials already aboard.

The second distinction was less visible. A large warship is built around separate systems. Engines move it. Pumps feed firefighting hoses. Compartments and armored decks protect vital areas. When those systems fail at different times, people face different emergencies in the same ship.

On Franklin, firefighting water mains were damaged early. Much of the equipment could no longer deliver water where crews needed it. Smoke and heat drove personnel from engineering spaces, taking the engines out of action. By morning the carrier lay motionless off Japan.

Below decks, engineering officer Donald Gary found several hundred sailors trapped in a compartment used for meals. Smoke had filled the space, and the usual routes out were blocked. There was no way to send everyone through an exit he had not yet found.

Gary searched dark passages and discovered a usable route. He returned to the trapped sailors, led a group out, then went back again. His Medal of Honor citation records three return trips until the men in that compartment had been brought to safety.

Saving the men did not restore the ship. Gary next joined firefighting parties and later entered a fireroom. The first two firerooms could not be used. In the third he helped direct the work of raising steam in a boiler that might give Franklin power again.

Up on the damaged decks, the chaplain, Joseph O'Callahan, was doing work far beyond leading prayers. His citation describes him organizing firefighting crews, tending the wounded, helping dispose of ammunition and directing the flooding of a magazine to protect it from a larger explosion.

One task was particularly dangerous. Live bombs had become hot on the deck. O'Callahan handled a hose to cool them while the ship was still shaking from detonations. He had to work near the very weapons that could destroy the remaining route to safety.

Neither man's actions happened alone. Other crew members fought fires, moved the wounded and kept whatever equipment remained usable. Both men would later receive the Medal of Honor, but there was no single person or single hose that saved the carrier.

Outside, other American ships had to make choices too. The light cruiser Santa Fe came alongside Franklin despite the fire and continuing explosions. Her crew took off wounded men and large numbers of other survivors. Nearby destroyers recovered sailors from the water.

Getting people away and saving the ship were different tasks. The rescue could continue even if the carrier itself could not be saved. But hundreds remained aboard with work still to do.

The carrier's commander, Captain Leslie Gehres, had been advised to abandon ship. He did not act on that recommendation. That decision kept a damage-control effort aboard Franklin, but it also meant remaining crews had to work in the damaged ship as explosions continued.

By late morning, Franklin no longer had propulsion. She was still close enough to Japan to face another air attack. Other ships could remove her crew, but moving the carrier demanded something more difficult.

The heavy cruiser Pittsburgh approached. A towing wire was attached not to a convenient powered winch, because power to the forward equipment was gone, but to the carrier's starboard anchor chain. The Navy's technical account describes men working the gear without normal machinery until a towing arrangement could be completed.

Pittsburgh began towing the carrier away at only a few knots. It was not an immediate escape. Franklin's damage and her tendency to turn across the tow made keeping course difficult. Fires were still being extinguished, and some ammunition continued to detonate.

During the tow, another Japanese aircraft came in. Anti-aircraft fire and covering fighters drove it away. If that attack had hit, the story might have ended differently. It did not, and the tow continued.

Here the priorities on board changed. Getting more people out remained essential, but restoring control of the ship could also increase everyone's chance of reaching a safe port. The fire parties continued their work while engineering crews tried to make a damaged system run.

On March 20, the bridge regained steering control. Before long Franklin could produce enough speed to release the towline. By early afternoon she was making roughly fifteen knots under her own power.

The image is startling: the same carrier that had burned from one end of its aircraft spaces to the other was now moving through the sea without another ship pulling it.

But there is an important correction to the familiar description of a ship that should not have survived. The official damage report concluded the fire damage alone would not have sunk Franklin. Her main structural strength and watertight integrity below the armored hangar deck had held, as had vital machinery.

She had no torpedo holes below the waterline. The floodwater was largely connected to firefighting. The armor and internal divisions gave the crew an opportunity. The crews, the assisting warships and the absence of another successful attack made it possible to use that opportunity.

The distinction matters because it explains rather than diminishes the rescue. A ship can be structurally afloat and still be impossible for its crew to keep alive. The pumps, passages, power and safe working space must be recovered by people who can reach them.

On March 24, Franklin reached Ulithi, an American fleet anchorage. A memorial service was held among the ruined hangar spaces the following day. The ship could travel again, but many men were missing from its crew.

Franklin later crossed the Pacific, passed through the Panama Canal and reached New York on April 28. Her shattered flight deck was still visible. She was repaired, yet she did not return to combat before the war ended and was eventually placed in reserve.

How many men died is not as straightforward as one fixed number. Navy histories use different totals, in part because they count members of the air group, Marines and men who died later in different ways. The scale is not disputed: the losses were enormous.

Gary and O'Callahan received the Medal of Honor in 1946. Their awards recognized specific actions: guiding trapped men out, restoring machinery, organizing firefighting and working among unexploded weapons.

So the answer to the opening questions has two parts. Franklin stayed afloat because her protected lower structure survived. She got home because people fought through a series of separate problems, and nearby ships stayed with her long enough for propulsion to return.

The carrier entered New York alive in the mechanical sense. Many of the sailors who had sailed out with her never came back. Both truths belong in the ending of the story.
