What did it take to move an aircraft carrier after its engines stopped? How could sailors get hundreds of trapped men out while bombs were still exploding? And why did fire wreck the upper decks without sinking the hull beneath them?

In March 1945, USS Franklin was operating close to the Japanese home islands. American carriers were launching strikes ahead of the battle for Okinawa. The distance to the enemy coast mattered: a damaged ship had very little room to retreat.

Franklin had been through another damaging attack the previous October. Repairs had put her back with the fleet. Now she was preparing a fresh wave of planes.

The flight deck was not an empty runway. Thirty-one aircraft were on it, fueled and armed for the strike. In the hangar below stood another twenty-two. Some were armed as well. The exact mix of weapons varied, but there were bombs and rockets in places where a fire could reach them.

On the morning of March 19, a Japanese aircraft appeared and dropped two bombs. They struck the carrier while the crews were working with those planes. That timing changed everything.

The initial bomb damage was serious. What followed was far larger. Fuel caught fire around the aircraft. Then American bombs and rockets began going off, one after another. The Navy's damage report describes five hours when the damage-control situation was effectively beyond control.

The explosions did not stay on the open deck. Some bombs fell through damaged surfaces into the hangar below. Fire spread into the galleries and the island that rose above the flight deck. More ammunition caught. Smoke traveled into compartments that had never been directly hit.

Two bombs had hit Franklin. But the ship's own fuel and ammunition were now making the disaster grow. Fire reached spaces that neither enemy bomb had struck.

The sailors needed water to stop the fire. Damaged water mains cut off most of the usual supply. Later that day, the Navy's technical report records just one forward diesel fire pump supplying pressure aboard. Destroyers helped with their own hoses. And water used to fight the fires collected inside the ship, making Franklin list to starboard.

Below the armored hangar deck, smoke and heat drove men from machinery spaces. The engines stopped. Franklin was still afloat, but she could not move away from the Japanese coast. Finding exits, cooling ammunition, fighting fires and restoring propulsion had become four different emergencies aboard the same ship.

Below decks, engineering officer Donald Gary found several hundred sailors trapped in a compartment used for meals. Smoke had filled the space, and the usual routes out were blocked. There was no way to send everyone through an exit he had not yet found.

Gary searched dark passages and discovered a usable route. He returned to the trapped sailors, led a group out, then went back again. His Medal of Honor citation records three return trips until the men in that compartment had been brought to safety.

Getting the trapped men out solved only one emergency. Gary joined the firefighting crews. The machinery spaces were still too dangerous for normal work, and Franklin had no propulsion. Gary's next assignment would matter once the tow began.

Up on the damaged decks, the chaplain, Joseph O'Callahan, was doing work far beyond leading prayers. His citation describes him organizing firefighting crews, tending the wounded, helping dispose of ammunition and directing the flooding of a magazine to protect it from a larger explosion.

One task was particularly dangerous. Live bombs had become hot on the deck. O'Callahan handled a hose to cool them while the ship was still shaking from detonations. He had to work near the very weapons that could destroy the remaining route to safety.

Neither man's actions happened alone. Other crew members fought fires, moved the wounded and kept whatever equipment remained usable. Both men would later receive the Medal of Honor, but there was no single person or single hose that saved the carrier.

Outside, other American ships had to make choices too. The light cruiser Santa Fe came alongside Franklin despite the fire and continuing explosions. Her crew took off wounded men and large numbers of other survivors. Nearby destroyers recovered sailors from the water.

Getting people away and saving the ship were different tasks. The rescue could continue even if the carrier itself could not be saved. But hundreds remained aboard with work still to do.

The carrier's commander, Captain Leslie Gehres, had been advised to abandon ship. He did not act on that recommendation. That decision kept a damage-control effort aboard Franklin, but it also meant remaining crews had to work in the damaged ship as explosions continued.

The damage report offers an important distinction. Despite the destruction above, Franklin's principal structure and watertight integrity below the armored hangar deck had survived. There was no torpedo hole below the waterline. The report concluded that the fire damage alone would not have sunk the ship. That did not make her safe: she was still burning, motionless, and within range of another air attack. A sound hull could float, but it could not carry itself to port.

The heavy cruiser Pittsburgh approached to take Franklin in tow. But the forward winches and anchor machinery had no power. Sailors hauled a messenger line by hand, then used a winch on Pittsburgh to help bring the towing wire aboard. They connected it to Franklin's starboard anchor chain. Even getting the two ships ready to move was an operation of its own.

The work consumed hours. According to the technical report, the actual tow began at about two in the afternoon, while fires and smaller ammunition explosions still continued. Pittsburgh pulled at only a few knots. Franklin kept sheering off course, forcing the cruiser to correct her own heading. The tow bought distance from Japan slowly, while men aboard the damaged carrier kept trying to restore its machinery.

During the tow, another Japanese aircraft attacked. Anti-aircraft fire and covering fighters drove it away. Pittsburgh kept pulling, but the carrier could not rely on a towing wire forever.

Gary, who had led men through the smoke earlier, later worked in a surviving fireroom to help raise steam. Other engineers brought boilers back into operation as damage-control parties contained the fires. The Navy's technical account describes the boilers coming back overnight and on the next morning. This was the task the tow had bought them time to attempt.

On March 20, steering control returned to the bridge. Engines came back gradually. Around midday Franklin could make enough speed on her own to cast off the towing wire. By early afternoon she was moving at roughly fifteen knots. Santa Fe had taken survivors off; Pittsburgh had moved the carrier away; the men still aboard had finally returned propulsion. Each part of the rescue had answered a different problem.

On March 24, Franklin reached Ulithi, an American fleet anchorage. A memorial service was held among the ruined hangar spaces the following day. The ship could travel again, but many men were missing from its crew.

Franklin later crossed the Pacific, passed through the Panama Canal and reached New York on April 28. Her shattered flight deck was still visible. She was repaired, yet she did not return to combat before the war ended and was eventually placed in reserve.

Navy accounts differ on the exact death toll, but all agree that hundreds were killed. The carrier's survival did not undo those losses.

Gary and O'Callahan received the Medal of Honor in 1946 for their actions amid the fire and explosions. They were two of many sailors whose separate tasks had kept the rescue going.

Franklin reached New York under her own power, with damaged upper decks still visible. Her protected lower structure had given the crew a chance to save the ship. Many of those who sailed out aboard her never came back.
