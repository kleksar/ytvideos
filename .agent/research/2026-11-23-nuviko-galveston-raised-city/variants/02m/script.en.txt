Why did ships sail through a Texas city? And why were people living in houses standing on wooden supports?

Galveston was a prosperous port on a low island off the Texas coast. In September 1900 a hurricane swept across the island. Thousands of people died, houses were destroyed and the harbor was wrecked.

The city began building a concrete seawall on the Gulf side. But a wall alone would leave much of the town sitting close to sea level. So the engineers proposed changing the ground beneath the buildings.

That meant lifting a city that people still lived in.

Workers raised houses and even churches. Some residents reached their front doors on temporary wooden walkways above the old streets. Water pipes and tram tracks had to come up as well.

And where would all the new ground come from? The builders cut a temporary canal through the town, moving buildings out of its path. Special dredges could travel through the canal and pump a mixture of sand and water into enclosed sections of the city.

As the sand settled, the new ground gradually reached the raised buildings. The city filled in the canal and put streets back where it had been.

Then another major hurricane struck in 1915. Water still flooded the streets and damaged the connection to the mainland. The local archive records twelve deaths in the city itself. The 1900 death toll across the island was much higher. These aren't identical areas, but the new defenses had helped avert another disaster on the earlier scale.

Galveston had not become safe from every storm. What it had gained was a better chance of surviving one. The people who had walked above their own front yards were now living on the ground they had built.
