How do you raise a city without emptying it of people? Why did Galveston need ships that could sail down a temporary canal through town? And what happened when another hurricane arrived?

At the end of the nineteenth century, Galveston was one of the great ports of Texas. Ships came to its wharves, people lived close to the waterfront, and the streets ran across a narrow island that stood only a few feet above the sea.

On September eighth, 1900, a hurricane overwhelmed the island. Houses near the beach disappeared. At the harbor, the storm damaged wharves and ruined goods waiting to be shipped. Thousands of people lost their lives.

Rebuilding the houses in the same places would also rebuild the city's vulnerability. So Galveston commissioned engineers to work out a defense.

The first part was a concrete seawall along the Gulf. It was wide at the bottom, narrow at the top, and curved on the seaward face to direct waves upward. Huge pieces of granite protected its base. The first section, completed in 1904, extended more than three miles.

It was a formidable structure. But behind it stood a town on very low ground. The engineers had another part to their plan. They wanted the new ground to rise toward the seawall, rather than simply placing a barrier in front of the existing streets.

Imagine owning a house in one of the blocks that had to be filled. The builders could not simply pump sand into the neighborhood with your house still sitting at its original height. The front door would end up below the street.

So the house had to go up before the ground did. Some larger houses were raised onto new foundations. Smaller buildings could stand on temporary supports while the fill accumulated underneath. One of the buildings lifted was Saint Patrick's Church, which went up about five feet.

And people didn't all leave while this happened. Residents in some neighborhoods walked along raised wooden platforms to reach their homes. Some walkways ran at the height of the old fence tops. The streets and the doors no longer met at the same level.

The city still needed a way to bring in vast amounts of material. The source of much of that material was in the water near the island. The builders dug a temporary canal through part of Galveston. Buildings in its path were moved aside. A drawbridge carried traffic across it.

Specially built dredges collected sand from the water near the island. They carried it into the canal and discharged a mixture of sand and water through pipes. The town was divided into sections so the fill could be controlled. The water drained away and the sand remained.

This was not a simple matter of lifting houses and letting the sand do the rest. Pipes had to be adjusted. Streets had to be rebuilt. Tram tracks had to be brought up to the new level. Salt water killed much of the existing vegetation. And property owners had expenses of their own. Some could not afford to raise their buildings and lost the use of lower rooms.

Once the temporary canal was no longer needed, it was filled in. Buildings that had been moved could return. What had been a waterway for construction ships became dry land again.

The work left Galveston with a new shape. On the Gulf side, the land behind the wall was much higher than before; toward the bay it was lower. And in 1912 a new causeway connected the island to the mainland.

Three years later, another powerful hurricane arrived.

The city was flooded again. Buildings were damaged, electricity and fresh water were disrupted, and parts of the causeway were swept away. The seawall and raised ground did not make Galveston immune.

But this was not a repeat of 1900. The local archive records twelve deaths in the city in the 1915 storm. The much larger island-wide toll in 1900 cannot be used as an exact like-for-like comparison, but the scale of the human disaster was far smaller this time.

The seawall is the most visible part of what Galveston built. The other part is under the feet of the people walking behind it. A city had lifted its houses, brought in the ground underneath them and lived through the test of another hurricane.
