Why did ships sail down a canal through Galveston, Texas? How did people reach their homes while the ground beneath them was being raised? And when another hurricane hit, did all that work actually protect the city?

At the end of the nineteenth century, Galveston was a busy port on a narrow island. The Gulf of Mexico lay to the south, and across the bay was the Texas mainland. Cargo moved from ships to railways. Families lived a few streets away from the water. Much of the developed land stood only a few feet above the sea.

On September eighth, 1900, a hurricane drove water over the island. Houses near the beach were swept away. Ships could return to the port, but many of the wharves they used had been wrecked. Thousands of people died. The city could rebuild its homes, but there was nothing to stop another storm from reaching them at the same height.

The engineers Galveston brought in proposed two changes. They would build a seawall facing the Gulf and raise the land behind it. And the moment you picture a familiar house on one of those streets, you can see a problem with the second part of their plan.

Leave the house where it is, and start adding ground around it. The new sidewalk will eventually reach the old front steps, then the door. Put enough sand there and the entrance could end up below the street. For many buildings, the house had to rise before the ground did.

The first section of the seawall was completed in 1904. It was about seventeen feet high and more than three miles long. Its curved face directed waves upward, and heavy granite blocks helped protect the base. The engineers wanted the ground behind that wall to slope toward the bay. That meant the amount of fill would vary from one neighborhood to another.

The ground had formerly averaged perhaps five or six feet above mean low tide in the area being raised. The finished elevations varied from roughly eight feet near the bay to twenty-two feet on the Gulf side. Those are elevations of the land, not the height of the wall itself. People living close to the seawall faced a very different construction job from those farther inland.

Workers raised thousands of buildings. Some large ones received new permanent foundations before the sand came in. Smaller houses could spend part of the construction period resting on temporary timber supports. Saint Patrick's Church, the largest building lifted in the project, was raised about five feet. The job extended to schools and homes as well as places of worship.

But the men operating the lifting equipment weren't the only people with a decision to make. Taxpayers helped pay for the public works, while individual owners had to cover expenses on their own property. And there was still a way to keep a building without paying for an entire lift: accept that part of it would end up below the new ground level.

At the corner of Broadway and Twenty-fourth Street stood a brick mansion called Ashton Villa. It had belonged to James Moreau Brown, and passed to his wife when he died in 1895. The house survived the 1900 hurricane. During the raising, however, its basement was filled with sediment and about two feet of earth were added around the grounds. The building stayed where it was; the city changed around it. We don't have to guess at the owner's private reasoning to see that the house lost its basement.

Meanwhile, other residents stayed in their elevated houses. A wooden walkway could carry them from one property to the next at the height of the old fences. At the West End Grocery, a woman climbed steps to enter the raised shop, while a child and a dog remained below at street level. For a while, even going to the store could mean changing floors.

And the ground still hadn't caught up. To deliver enough fill, the builders needed ships that could carry sand into the city rather than unload everything at the waterfront.

In July 1904, digging began on a temporary canal. Houses in the route were moved sideways. Some of the first material removed from the canal was sent toward the seawall, where the biggest increase in height was needed. The excavation itself was therefore already supplying part of the land that the new ships would help build.

The builders now had to keep that route open while raising everything around it. They lined the canal with timber pilings so the new fill wouldn't slide back into the water. They also made room for the ships to turn, and at Twenty-third Street a drawbridge let pedestrians and wagons get across. An ordinary trip down a city street might now involve waiting for a construction vessel to pass.

Special dredges collected sand in Galveston Bay. The first two arrived in 1904, including the largest, Leviathan, which arrived in December. Two more followed in 1906. The vessels brought the material into the canal and stopped at discharge stations. From there, a mixture of sand and water traveled through pipes to the blocks that needed it.

Look at what had to happen at a street railway crossing. Pipes carrying the fill needed a route across the tracks. Sometimes workers lifted the tracks to get the pipes underneath. The tracks would ultimately have to be relaid at street level, but during construction the delivery system and the railway had to occupy the same space.

The mixture flowed into separate enclosed areas, the sand settled and the water drained away. Houses on supports gradually found themselves closer to ground level. Old sidewalks disappeared beneath the fill, and replacement sidewalks were another expense residents faced. The salty water also killed much of the vegetation, and residents had to replant trees and bushes.

Then the builders had to get rid of the canal itself. They blocked it off in sections, filled it and restored the streets. Houses that had been moved aside could return to their original sites. The main phase of raising took place from 1904 to 1910, but the final filled area was not completed until 1928.

In 1912 a new causeway connected Galveston with the mainland. Three years later, the city faced a major hurricane again. The seawall was there, and so was much of the new ground. Yet water entered the city, reaching depths of six to nine feet in places. Electricity went out. The supply of fresh water was cut.

The middle of the causeway survived, as did its movable bridge, but the storm destroyed the approaches from both shores. The island had a bridge it could no longer use normally. A temporary railway trestle was needed while the connection was rebuilt.

The problem with drinking water was worse than the visible damage to the road suggested. Workers shut off the thirty-inch mains along the causeway and found that the submerged water lines had broken as well. To bring fresh water back from the mainland, crews ran a temporary eight-inch pipe along the damaged crossing. Raising the town had changed one kind of vulnerability. Now workers had to restore a service that still depended on the land across the bay.

Twelve people died in the city during the 1915 storm. The thousands of deaths recorded for 1900 cover the island rather than the same city boundary, so we can't turn those numbers into an exact fraction of lives saved by the seawall or the raised ground. Storm conditions also differed. But the human disaster this time was much smaller, even though Galveston had been flooded again.

The two hurricanes left their marks in different ways. One had torn down streets of houses. In the other, the city had to repair damaged buildings and reconnect its utilities. The sea was still beside Galveston. What had changed was the city itself.

For years, people had climbed wooden steps and walked along elevated paths to reach the homes where they had stayed. In the finished neighborhoods, a front door could once again open onto an ordinary street. The street had risen to meet it.
