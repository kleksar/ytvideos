Why were ships sailing through Galveston instead of around it? How did people reach their front doors while engineers were raising the very ground beneath their houses? And could that strange construction project protect the city from another hurricane like the one that had destroyed it?

To understand the problem, start with the island rather than the wall. Galveston sits off the Texas mainland, with the Gulf on one side and a bay on the other. In the late nineteenth century it was a thriving port. Goods passed through the wharves, railways connected the city with inland markets, and residential streets covered the low ground near the sea.

The island's modest elevation was part of everyday life. A city could function perfectly well there until an extreme storm brought a great volume of seawater across it.

On September eighth, 1900, that happened. The hurricane destroyed homes across the island. Near the beach, buildings vanished from their sites. Piers were wrecked and cargo was damaged. Thousands of people died. The survivors faced a question that went beyond the reconstruction of individual buildings: what would happen to the rebuilt city during the next great storm?

The solution could not be a promise that hurricanes would never come again. Galveston needed to change the place where the people lived.

In 1901 the state authorized action to protect the city, and Galveston brought in a board of engineers. Their report proposed a seawall and a different elevation for the ground behind it. Those two parts addressed different problems. The wall would intercept waves coming from the Gulf. The raised land would reduce the vulnerability of the streets and buildings behind the wall.

The wall was the easier part to understand. From the sea it would look like a long concrete barrier. Its cross-section was wide at the base and narrower at the top. The curved face on the Gulf side would turn waves upward; a bed of large granite blocks in front protected the foot of the structure. The first section was about seventeen feet high and stretched for more than three miles. It was finished in 1904.

But now look on the other side of that structure. The existing buildings had doors, windows, foundations and connections to buried pipes, all designed around the old elevation of the island. Putting more ground around them would change the entire relationship between a house and its street.

A new sidewalk could end up halfway up the wall of a building. To get the new streets to meet the front doors, the buildings themselves often had to be raised first.

This became a citywide operation involving thousands of structures. The job varied from one property to another. A large building might be lifted and given a permanent foundation before the surrounding ground was filled. A smaller house could stand on temporary supports until the fill had settled around it.

Saint Patrick's Church was raised roughly five feet. For a building that large, the task was very different from moving the little wooden houses of the neighborhood. Yet the objective was the same: the building had to end up at a level that made sense when the rest of the city came up around it.

Not every owner could simply pay to have an entire building lifted. The public project and the work required on private property were different expenses. Some residents sacrificed lower rooms rather than pay for a full lift. One notable residence, Ashton Villa, was left in place while the ground around it rose. The result was not a city of uniformly elevated houses.

And Galveston did not become an empty construction site. People continued to live in some of the houses on their temporary supports. Wooden walkways crossed ground that was not yet at its final elevation. Some were built along the tops of old fences. The familiar route from the street to the front door had been replaced by a bridge.

The engineers still needed enough material to fill entire neighborhoods. Sand was available near the island, but the problem was getting it to the streets where it was needed.

The solution temporarily gave Galveston a new piece of geography. A canal was cut through part of the city. Buildings that stood in the route were moved to the sides. The construction ships could then come into the area where the fill was needed rather than stopping at the shore.

There was even a drawbridge at Twenty-third Street. It let people and vehicles cross the temporary waterway. A canal created for a project that would ultimately make the land higher now had to coexist with the city it was helping to rebuild.

The first two of four specially built hopper dredges arrived in 1904. Two more followed in 1906. They could collect sand near the island, carry it in their hoppers and discharge it at stations along the canal. Pipes took a mixture of sand and water onward to the building sites.

The builders divided the area to be filled into sections with temporary barriers. The mixture could flow into a section, the solid material could settle, and the water could escape. As the new ground accumulated, it approached the level of the buildings that had been lifted in advance.

The work affected much more than the buildings. Galveston had a street railway. Its tracks could not remain buried at the old street level, and sometimes they had to be raised temporarily to make room for the pipes carrying fill. Water and sewer lines needed to function at the new elevations. New streets and sidewalks had to be built. Salt water in the fill destroyed much of the existing vegetation, so gardens and other green areas had to be established again.

The temporary canal also had to disappear. Once it had served its purpose, it was closed and filled. Houses moved out of its route could be returned, and streets could be put back into service.

By the end of the main grade-raising work, the ground sloped down toward the bay from a much higher level on the Gulf side. This was a different city in the most literal sense. If you stood on a finished street, you were standing above the ground that had been there before the hurricane.

A new causeway to the mainland opened in 1912. It gave the island an improved connection for people, goods and services, a connection that would soon be tested along with the seawall.

In 1915 another major hurricane approached Galveston. This time the city had the wall and the elevated land. It still experienced serious flooding. Water entered the city, structures were damaged, and electrical service and the supply of fresh water failed.

The storm demonstrated that protection of the city and protection of its links to the outside world were not the same thing. The approaches to the causeway were wrecked, even though the central structure survived. Workers shut off the damaged thirty-inch water main, but they also discovered that pipes beneath the water had broken. To restore a connection, they installed a temporary eight-inch water pipe along the causeway.

It was an expensive disaster. There were still deaths, twelve in the city itself according to the local historical archive. The thousands who had died in 1900 were counted across the island, so those numbers have different boundaries. We cannot calculate a precise percentage saved by either the wall or the fill. But the difference in the scale of human loss helps explain why the city's rebuilding mattered.

Areas outside the protected part of the island could still be inundated, as later storms demonstrated. It was never possible to guarantee that every house and every connection to the mainland would survive.

What Galveston had done was to change what happened when a hurricane arrived. The city had endured a second great storm with a much smaller human catastrophe than before.

The most obvious monument to that effort is the seawall. But an equally important monument has no separate entrance or plaque. Every time somebody walks along the raised streets behind the wall, they are walking on the other part of that defense. For years, people crossed wooden bridges to their own homes. Then the ground rose to meet them.
