A rescue chamber began climbing from a sunken submarine with eight survivors inside. Then its cable jammed.

May 1939. The new American submarine Squalus had gone down during a test dive off New Hampshire. Water flooded the rear compartments. Twenty-six people died. Thirty-three others remained alive behind closed watertight doors, 240 feet below the surface.

A rescue ship came above them with a steel chamber. Divers connected it to a hatch. The chamber could seal against the submarine, take people aboard, and carry them up without forcing anyone to swim through the sea.

The first three trips worked. Twenty-five survivors reached the surface.

On the fourth trip, the remaining eight climbed aboard. The chamber undocked and started up. Then its machinery jammed on a cable, leaving it stuck underwater.

A diver went down and cut the jammed line. But the separate cable meant to retrieve the chamber began to fray under the load. Almost all its strands broke.

Pull too hard, and the last strand could snap. Add too much air, and the chamber could shoot upward out of control.

The rescuers tried a third approach. They released small amounts of ballast water, then tested the weakened cable by hand. They adjusted the chamber until it was almost buoyant enough to rise on its own.

Now the cable only had to guide it.

Just after midnight, the chamber reached the rescue ship. Every one of the thirty-three survivors was safe. The twenty-six who had died in the flooding could not be saved.

Three trips had already reached the ship. The fourth was supposed to bring up the last eight.
