How do you lift people from a submarine on the seabed without making them swim through the water? And what happens when the lifting equipment breaks with the last eight survivors inside?

In May 1939, the American submarine Squalus was still new. She had been commissioned only weeks earlier and was making test dives off New Hampshire. On May 23, an air-intake valve failed to close during a descent. Seawater rushed into the rear of the boat.

The crew shut watertight doors, separating the flooded section from the front. Those doors kept thirty-three people alive. They could not save the twenty-six who died in the rear compartments.

Squalus settled on the seabed, roughly 240 feet down. The surviving men and one civilian were trapped forward. Their problem was not finding a way to sail home. The submarine could no longer rise. They needed the surface to come to them.

A sister submarine, Sculpin, helped locate the wreck after the crew released a smoke signal. Rescue ships gathered, but finding a position on the surface did not mean reaching the people below. A line had to be attached to the right hatch, and a heavy rescue chamber had to be guided into place.

That chamber had been built for just such a disaster. It looked like a small steel room suspended beneath a ship. Divers prepared the submarine's forward hatch, then the chamber was lowered from the rescue ship Falcon. Its bottom could settle against the hatch and form a seal. The operators could move the water out of the space between them, open the doors, and let people walk into a dry compartment.

On May 24, the first trip brought seven survivors up. A second brought nine. A third brought another nine.

Twenty-five people were aboard the rescue ship. Eight were still waiting in the submarine.

The chamber went down once more. Its crew connected to the hatch, opened the passage, and took the last eight aboard. This ought to have been the familiar part: shut the hatches, release the chamber, and winch it back to daylight.

Instead, during the ascent, its downhaul equipment jammed. The chamber stopped far below the ship.

It was no longer simply a rescue chamber coming up from a wreck. It had become another trapped compartment, with eight rescued submariners and two operators inside.

The surface team could not just haul harder. A diver went down and cut the jammed downhaul line. The chamber started moving, but the separate wire carrying its weight began to unravel. The crew had to lower the chamber back down. Only one small strand of the retrieval cable remained intact.

There were two obvious ways to make this worse. Pull on that strand, and it might break. Blow too much water out of the ballast tanks, and the chamber might leap upward without control.

They needed the chamber to be neither heavy nor wildly buoyant.

From above, the rescue team instructed the operators to release ballast water in short, measured bursts. Men on Falcon tested the damaged line with their hands. Each adjustment took a little more of the chamber's weight off the cable.

Just after midnight on May 25, the balance was finally right. The chamber rose without demanding a full lift from the damaged wire. At 12:23 it reached the surface. Two minutes later the last survivors came out.

All thirty-three who had survived the original flooding were now safe.

The Navy did not assume everyone else was gone without checking. A further chamber descent reached the rear part of the submarine and confirmed that it was flooded. Twenty-six people had died, and the rescue could not change that loss.

Months later the damaged submarine itself was raised and returned to the shipyard. It would eventually sail again under a new name: Sailfish.

The rescue had proved that a steel chamber could make repeated trips to a submarine deep underwater. But the decisive trip succeeded only when the crew stopped treating the chamber like a weight to be winched up. They made it nearly buoyant, and used the last sound strand of cable to keep it under control.
