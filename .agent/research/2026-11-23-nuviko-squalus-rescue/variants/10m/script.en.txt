How do you reach thirty-three people in a submarine 240 feet beneath the sea without sending them into open water? And what happens if the machine bringing them out becomes trapped on its last trip?

On May 23, 1939, the new American submarine Squalus descended on a test dive off New Hampshire. Eighteen earlier test dives had succeeded. This time the main engine air-intake valve failed to close. Seawater rushed through the opening into the rear of the boat.

The rear compartments began flooding. The crew closed watertight doors. Behind some doors, twenty-six people died. On the other side, thirty-two naval personnel and one civilian remained alive. The hull settled on the bottom, about 240 feet below the surface.

The division mattered. A submarine has no single open interior where every crew member can walk from bow to stern after an accident. Closed doors can isolate flooding, but they can also leave people separated permanently. The survivors had air and space for the moment. They had no means of bringing the submarine to the surface.

The trapped crew released a smoke marker. The searching submarine Sculpin spotted it several miles from where Squalus was expected to dive. Through a telephone attached to the marker buoy, the officers briefly spoke. Then that buoy cable parted. The searchers had a location, but the first communications link was gone. Further messages could be tapped through the hull.

The rescue team now had to reach people who could not simply be hauled up on a rope. Swimming from this depth was dangerous, and the submarine's escape hatch was still shut beneath the sea. The crew needed a way out that kept the water outside.

The Navy had been working on another answer for years. In 1927, the submarine S-4 sank, and attempted rescue efforts failed. After that loss, Charles Momsen and other Navy specialists developed equipment intended to connect a rescue vessel to a submarine that could no longer surface. They did not have proof it would rescue a crew from a real wreck at this depth. In May 1939, they were about to find out.

The device was known as the McCann rescue chamber. Imagine a short steel room lowered from a ship on a cable. Its lower section could be drawn against a submarine's hatch and sealed. Water in the connection could be displaced, allowing an operator to open a dry passage into the boat. People could walk through that passage, climb inside, and be carried back up together.

Falcon arrived early on May 24 carrying that chamber. Before it could lower anything, wind and waves kept pushing the rescue ship off position. The crew rearranged anchor lines until Falcon could hold above the wreck. A chamber lowered in the wrong place would never find the hatch.

The first diver, Martin Sibitzky, found a marker-buoy line lying across the forward escape hatch. He cleared it, then fixed the chamber's downhaul wire to the hatch fitting. The old line might otherwise have tangled the new one or prevented the chamber from sealing. A piece of rope left from the search had become an obstacle to the rescue.

The first chamber went over Falcon's side at 11:30 in the morning. It reached the submarine and sealed to the forward hatch. Operators cleared the connection, opened the passage, delivered supplies and helped ventilate the forward compartment. Seven people crossed into the dry chamber and reached the surface at 1:42 p.m.

Twenty-six survivors were still below. This was no permanent passage. Every group needed a fresh descent, a new seal, the careful closing of hatches and another lift. One successful trip did not guarantee the next.

The second trip brought up nine, one more than the rescue coordinator had planned. He then directed that the third trip take nine as well. So twenty-five people were now safe aboard Falcon, with eight still waiting. The change in the loads mattered: one final passenger ascent could finish the work, if the chamber held together.

The remaining eight included Squalus's commanding officer. They entered the chamber on the fourth trip, along with the operators already riding in it. The hatches closed. The chamber left the submarine's hull and began rising toward the ship.

This was supposed to be the last familiar climb.

At 8:22 that evening, the air motor working the downhaul reel stalled. The operators tried controlling the reel with the brake. At roughly 155 feet beneath the surface it jammed again. The downhaul line, which had guided the chamber toward the hatch, now held it under the ship. The separate cable used to retrieve the chamber could not simply wrench it free.

The men on Falcon now had a second job: rescue the rescue chamber.

The crew gave the chamber enough weight to return toward the bottom. Diver Squire went down after dark. He tried to unshackle the jammed line, but it was too tight. At 9:22 he cut it. The deck winch tried the other line, the retrieval cable. Under the load, its wire strands began to break. The crew eased off and lowered the chamber again. Just one small strand of the retrieval cable remained intact.

Replacing the wire seemed the next answer. Clayton descended with a fresh line but became fouled among the cables and had to be brought up. Duncan tried next. Tangled lines and trouble with his lights stopped him too. Ten people were still inside the chamber, including its two operators. Each failed attempt left the damaged wire as the only usable link.

Pull harder and the last strand might break. Push out too much ballast water and the chamber could rise uncontrollably, perhaps striking Falcon itself. The rescuers chose to make the chamber almost, but not quite, buoyant enough to float on its own.

Shortly after midnight, operators blew water from the ballast tank in three measured stages: first thirty seconds, then fifteen, then fifteen. Men on Falcon tested the damaged wire by hand. Each small adjustment reduced the burden on it, while the cable still restrained the chamber. They were not trying to pull harder. They were making the weight that needed pulling smaller.

At last the chamber began climbing again. It did not need an intact winch cable to carry all of its weight; its controlled buoyancy now supplied most of the lift.

At 12:23 on the morning of May 25, the chamber finally reached the surface. Two minutes later its hatch opened. The last eight survivors emerged, along with the operators who had stayed with them.

Thirty-three people survived the sinking and the rescue. Twenty-six others had already been lost when the submarine flooded. Neither the success of the chamber nor the skill of the divers erased that distinction.

There was also one unanswered question. The survivors had come from the forward section. Could there still be someone alive in an isolated room toward the rear?

Later that day, the Navy sent the chamber down again to inspect the after torpedo room. It was a fifth descent, but not a fifth group of survivors. Water entered from the flooded compartment. No one was found alive there. The rescue count remained thirty-three.

After the people were safe, a much heavier recovery began. Divers attached air-filled pontoons to lift Squalus herself. On an early attempt the hull rose, slipped free of its chain rigging, and sank again. It took months of further work to bring the submarine into Portsmouth on September 13. Repaired and renamed Sailfish, she returned to service in 1940.

Salvage could bring the hull back. It could not bring back the twenty-six who had died in the flooded stern. And the most important return had happened months earlier: at 12:25 on May 25, the last eight survivors had stepped out of a chamber that had almost become another wreck.
