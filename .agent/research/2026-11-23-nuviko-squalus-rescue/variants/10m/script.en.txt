How could a submarine crew survive a night on the ocean floor when their boat could not rise? How could a metal chamber carry them back without making them swim through seventy metres of seawater? And why did the last rescue trip almost end in another disaster?

On May 23, 1939, the American submarine Squalus was making a test dive off the New Hampshire coast. She was a new boat, not a vessel returning from combat. A valve in the main engine's air-intake system failed to shut. The control was supposed to be closed, but water entered through an opening that remained exposed to the sea.

The rear compartments began flooding. The crew closed watertight doors. Behind some doors, twenty-six people died. On the other side, thirty-two naval personnel and one civilian remained alive. The hull settled on the bottom, about 240 feet below the surface.

The division mattered. A submarine has no single open interior where every crew member can walk from bow to stern after an accident. Closed doors can isolate flooding, but they can also leave people separated permanently. The survivors had air and space for the moment. They had no means of bringing the submarine to the surface.

One of the submarine's first useful actions happened after she had already sunk. The men released a smoke signal. Squalus's sister submarine, Sculpin, searched the water above and helped locate the wreck. Finding the exact position was the beginning of a rescue, not the end of one.

A vessel on the surface could not just lower a rope and pull thirty-three people up. The wreck was too deep for an ordinary escape. Bringing each person through the sea would have exposed them to pressure and cold, while the submarine's escape route still had to be found and opened.

The Navy had been working on another answer for years. In 1927, the submarine S-4 sank, and attempted rescue efforts failed. After that loss, Charles Momsen and other Navy specialists developed equipment intended to connect a rescue vessel to a submarine that could no longer surface. They did not have proof it would rescue a crew from a real wreck at this depth. In May 1939, they were about to find out.

The device was known as the McCann rescue chamber. Imagine a short steel room lowered from a ship on a cable. Its lower section could be drawn against a submarine's hatch and sealed. Water in the connection could be displaced, allowing an operator to open a dry passage into the boat. People could walk through that passage, climb inside, and be carried back up together.

The idea sounds simple until you try to use it in the open ocean. First, the rescue ship has to stay over a wreck it cannot see. A diver must reach the submarine, find the proper hatch and prepare it. The chamber has to descend along the correct line, come down evenly, and meet a surface that will hold a seal. Then the operators must arrange the pressure and open the passage without letting the sea rush in.

The rescue ship Falcon arrived with the equipment. Divers went down to establish a connection to Squalus's forward escape hatch. The men inside could not see each step of this work. What they knew was that somebody was now trying to reach them from above.

By May 24, almost a day had passed since the sinking. The chamber made its first working descent. It settled against the hatch. The operators checked the connection, cleared the way through, and opened the passage. For the first time, a group of survivors could leave the submarine without entering the sea.

Seven men came up on the first trip.

That would have been a remarkable rescue by itself. But there were twenty-six more to bring home, and the chamber was not a permanent tunnel. Every group meant another descent, another docking, another opening and closing of hatches, and another ascent to the ship.

The second trip brought nine people up. The third brought nine more. By late afternoon, twenty-five survivors were on Falcon, with eight still in Squalus. These repeated journeys also gave the men below fresh contact with the rescue crew and helped restore air in the forward compartment. Each return showed that the procedure could work again.

The remaining eight included Squalus's commanding officer. They entered the chamber on the fourth trip, along with the operators already riding in it. The hatches closed. The chamber left the submarine's hull and began rising toward the ship.

This was supposed to be the last familiar climb.

Instead, the motor driving the downhaul gear stalled. The chamber's movement stopped at roughly 155 feet below the surface. A cable on its reel had jammed. It could neither continue up normally nor simply return to the rescue ship.

The men on Falcon now had a second job: rescue the rescue chamber.

A diver descended and cut the line that was holding it. That removed the jam, but it did not solve the lift. The separate retrieving cable carried the chamber's considerable weight. As the crew tried to recover it, the wire began to strand and fail. The operators had to lower the chamber again rather than let the situation turn into an uncontrolled break.

Only a tiny part of that retrieval cable remained intact. The rescue ship was connected to the chamber, but the connection could no longer carry its normal load.

There were two easy answers, and both were dangerous. Pull harder, and the last wire could part. Blow all the water out of the chamber's ballast tanks, and the chamber could become too buoyant. It might accelerate toward the surface too quickly for the crew to manage.

The rescuers had to solve a problem of weight, not strength.

The chamber carried adjustable ballast water. With enough water inside, it was heavy and needed the cable to lift it. Remove too much, and it would try to rise on its own. But somewhere between those extremes was a point where the water almost supported the whole chamber.

The men on Falcon began directing short bursts of air into the ballast system to force out small amounts of water. They tested the line by hand after each adjustment. A thirty-second release was followed by shorter ones. The chamber grew lighter in the water, without being allowed to fly upward.

A few feet above the bottom, then slowly farther up, the fragile wire was no longer doing all the work. It was mostly keeping the chamber under control while buoyancy helped it rise.

At 12:23 on the morning of May 25, the chamber finally reached the surface. Two minutes later its hatch opened. The last eight survivors emerged, along with the operators who had stayed with them.

Thirty-three people survived the sinking and the rescue. Twenty-six others had already been lost when the submarine flooded. Neither the success of the chamber nor the skill of the divers erased that distinction.

There was also one unanswered question. The survivors had come from the forward section. Could there still be someone alive in an isolated room toward the rear?

Later that day, the Navy sent the chamber down again to inspect the after torpedo room. It was a fifth descent, but not a fifth group of survivors. Water entered from the flooded compartment. No one was found alive there. The rescue count remained thirty-three.

Another operation then began. The Navy decided to recover the submarine itself, not merely leave the wreck at the bottom. Lifting a damaged vessel presented different problems from retrieving a chamber. Salvage crews had to secure the hull and use pontoons and lifting gear; the recovery was neither immediate nor smooth.

On September 13, months after the accident, Squalus was brought back to the Portsmouth Navy Yard. The boat was repaired, renamed Sailfish, and returned to service in 1940. A submarine that had once been the setting of a rescue would sail again during the Second World War.

But that second life should not make the first accident sound harmless. Twenty-six people died in compartments the rescuers could not reach in time. The salvage returned the machine, not those people.

The part of the operation that changed what future crews could hope for took place on the fourth rescue trip. The chamber had already proved it could reach the seabed, seal to a hatch, and lift people. Then a cable failed, and the device meant to save them nearly trapped them again.

The last ascent worked because the crew changed the job they asked the cable to do. Instead of using it to lift a heavy chamber, they made the chamber almost buoyant and used the weakened line to guide it. Thirty-three people came home because of the equipment. The final eight came home because the rescuers understood when the equipment had to be used differently.
