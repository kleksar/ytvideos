How can a submarine be visible above the sea, surrounded by rescuers, and still lose ninety-nine people? What stopped those inside from reaching the air just metres away?

On June first, 1939, the British submarine HMS Thetis was making a trial dive in Liverpool Bay. There were 103 men aboard, including shipyard workers and naval observers.

The boat was slow to submerge. As the crew checked its weight and balance, they tested whether a forward torpedo tube contained seawater. A small test tap gave no warning. The inner door was opened.

Water burst through. The outer opening was already connected to the sea. Men retreated from the flooded bow and secured a door farther back, but the submarine sank to the seabed.

The crew used compressed air to lighten the stern. By the next morning, its rear end stood above the waves. A searching warship found it. This should have been their way out.

Four men escaped through a special chamber, two at a time. One told rescuers that the people below were still alive. More than ninety remained inside.

The stern, however, was not a ready-made doorway. Cutting into it at that height risked flooding the occupied compartments. Strong tides limited diving, and an attempt to lift it farther broke its cables. As the tide rose, the visible stern disappeared.

No one else escaped. Ninety-nine died.

The later inquiry exposed the original trap: the test tap had been blocked by protective enamel. It had given a reassuring answer to the wrong question.

Thetis was eventually raised and returned to service under another name. But that did nothing for the people left inside when the stern was still above the water.
