Why did a small test tap help sink a brand-new submarine? And why couldn't rescuers reach ninety-nine people after finding its stern above the water?

The answers are connected, but they aren't the same mistake.

On June first, 1939, HMS Thetis left the British shipyard at Birkenhead for trials in Liverpool Bay. Submarines are tested before the navy accepts them. On this trip there were not just sailors aboard. Engineers, inspectors and other civilian workers had joined them. One hundred and three men went down together.

The boat had been troublesome during earlier trials. This time the crew needed to check how it behaved underwater. At about twenty to two in the afternoon, Thetis began her dive.

It did not settle properly. The crew investigated why the bow was so buoyant. One possibility was that water had not entered a torpedo tube as expected. Each tube had a door inside the submarine and another opening to the sea. Opening the inner door was safe only if the sea was shut out.

There was a simple check: open a tiny test tap and see whether water came through.

The tap gave them no water. The men proceeded to open the inner door of tube five.

Then the sea came in.

The outer end of that tube was open. Water surged into the forward spaces. Men tried to shut watertight doors while the bow tipped down. They had to retreat, leaving two compartments flooded. By the time they sealed off the remaining interior, the submarine had settled on the seabed, roughly forty metres below the surface.

That should have been the end of the dive, not the end of the crew. They still had air in the sections behind the closed door. They released marker buoys and used compressed air to force water out of ballast tanks. The stern grew lighter and rose.

Above the waves, the escorting tug had lost track of the submarine. Its markers were not immediately recognised. Thetis was supposed to return by late afternoon, but did not. Aircraft and warships were sent to search.

Only the next morning did HMS Brazen find the submarine. The rear of the hull stood some five metres out of the water.

Imagine arriving at the scene. The wreck is not entirely hidden. You can see part of it. Rescue crews are there. Surely they can cut a hole in that exposed steel?

The first survivors emerged through an escape chamber instead. Captain Harry Oram and Lieutenant Frederick Woods came up wearing Davis breathing equipment. Later, Leading Stoker Walter Arnold and shipyard engineer Frank Shaw made it out too.

Four men. Oram reported that the others had been alive when he left.

But the exposed section was dangerously close to the water. Cutting through at that level could open the occupied hull to the sea. Rescuers tried to raise the stern first, so that an opening could be made safely above the waves.

They were fighting the tide as well as the weight of the boat. A strong current restricted the divers to short periods. The first attempt to lift the stern failed when the cables broke. The water rose. The end that had been visible disappeared beneath it.

Inside, the air was deteriorating. The Navy later reported severe carbon monoxide poisoning among the men who had escaped. A further attempt to move the submarine also failed. Divers eventually reported sounds they thought might be tapping from within, but an attempt to get air into the compartment would take too long.

No more people came out. Ninety-nine of the 103 died.

After the submarine was recovered, investigators could finally inspect the tiny tap. Its passage was plugged with enamel used to protect the inside of the torpedo tube. No water had come out because the hole was blocked, not because the tube was dry.

The mistake was not just that someone opened a door. A safety check had produced a false answer, and the rescue plan depended on getting into a hull that the tide would not leave exposed.

Thetis was raised, repaired and renamed HMS Thunderbolt. It later served in the Second World War and was lost again in 1943.

The submarine could be recovered. The ninety-nine people trapped inside her could not.
