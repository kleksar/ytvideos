How did a test meant to keep seawater out of a submarine lead to its sinking? And why couldn't rescuers reach the people inside when they could see the stern above the waves? Four men escaped from HMS Thetis. The attempts to save her explain why so few made it.

On June first, 1939, Britain was preparing for a possible European war. A new Royal Navy submarine was still being tested before entering service. HMS Thetis had been built at Cammell Laird in Birkenhead. Earlier trials had found problems with her machinery and steering, so her final diving tests were taking place in Liverpool Bay.

A submarine trial is not staffed like an ordinary patrol. Naval inspectors, shipyard specialists and other civilians came aboard to observe equipment they had worked on. Together with the crew, 103 men went to sea. A tug followed Thetis to keep other traffic clear of the diving area and to maintain contact.

Around twenty to two that afternoon, the submarine began to descend. But the test was not going smoothly. She seemed too light at the bow. The men needed to establish why the planned water balance was wrong.

The torpedo officer, Lieutenant Frederick Woods, faced conflicting information. The submarine's records said the two lower torpedo tubes had been filled with water. But there was disagreement about whether they ought to be full. He went to check.

Each tube has an outer door to the sea and an inner door to the submarine. A small tap on the inner door allows the crew to check for water before opening it. The outer door must also be shut.

A little water came from tube six. Nothing came from tube five. Woods checked the indicators for the outer doors and believed they were closed.

With another sailor, Hambrook, he inspected the tubes. The first four were dry. When they reached number five, its inner door was stiff. As it began to open, a little water appeared.

Then the door burst open, and the sea came pouring into the submarine.

The outer cap was open. The water injured Hambrook, and Woods helped him out. Behind them, the men tried to shut a heavy watertight door. As the bow dropped, they had to pull the door uphill.

They couldn't secure it in time. They retreated farther back, where another group was trying to shut a second door. Men were still coming through. Closing the door would cut them off; leaving it open risked flooding the rest of the boat.

Eventually the men got through, and the second door was closed. Two compartments were flooded. Thetis struck the seabed at roughly forty metres.

The first question now was not how to escape. It was whether the boat could be raised.

The crew tried compressed air. On a submarine, this air can push water out of ballast tanks and make the hull buoyant again. But with two forward spaces flooded, the boat could not simply float free. The stern was lighter than the bow, which remained down in the seabed.

They released marker buoys. They also tried to communicate with the surface. Yet the impact had damaged their signalling equipment, and the escorting tug was no longer directly over them. It had followed the submarine's expected underwater course. The emergency markers were not recognised immediately.

That meant valuable hours passed before rescuers could locate the hull.

Thetis had been due to surface by late afternoon. When she did not, the Royal Navy launched a search. An aircraft eventually spotted a marker, and a destroyer continued looking through the night.

Inside, the crew tried to stop the flood at its source. The forward escape chamber could also be used as an air lock into the flooded section without opening the door protecting the rest of the boat.

First Lieutenant Chapman entered the chamber. While water was rising inside it, he signalled that he was in pain. The others had to empty the chamber again.

Woods volunteered to try, with Petty Officer Mitchell accompanying him. They planned to use a rope to stay in contact. Mitchell could not continue as the chamber filled. Woods tried with another companion, but that attempt also had to stop. None of the three attempts even reached the flooded section.

The trapped men had two flooded compartments and far more people aboard than on a normal patrol. They had lost the air in those compartments, and now they could not regain them.

The crew found another possibility: lighten the stern until it reached the surface. If they could get an exit close enough to the air, perhaps people could leave without waiting for the whole submarine to be raised.

By morning, the stern had risen above the sea. When the destroyer HMS Brazen found Thetis, the rear end was roughly eighteen feet out of the water.

The effort to raise the stern had brought part of the submarine out of the water. The rear escape hatch, however, was still submerged. Men would have to climb through the inclined hull to reach a chamber near the stern and then make their way to the surface using breathing equipment. The chamber had to be prepared again after each group. They could not all leave together.

Captain Harry Oram decided to go up and help organise the rescue before he knew the submarine had been found. Woods joined him. They reached the surface, and Oram reported that the people inside had still been alive when he left, although conditions were worsening.

The next group tried to use the same chamber. This time, four people went in together, but did not get out. Men had to be brought back inside. The exact sequence of deaths during the escape attempts was still uncertain in the first official report.

Another pair tried. Walter Arnold, a naval stoker, helped Frank Shaw, a shipyard engineer, with his breathing equipment. Shaw later recalled struggling to open the hatch. Eventually they succeeded.

Both reached the surface. Now four of the 103 men were out. No other group would succeed.

Meanwhile, the rescue ships were looking at the exposed stern. Could they cut their way in?

Not at the waterline. That could let seawater pour into the air-filled parts of the hull. To make a safer opening, they needed to lift the stern farther above the surface.

This introduced a different problem. Liverpool Bay has powerful tides. Divers could work effectively only at slack water. The rescue vessels had to hold an angled steel submarine against the moving sea while trying to pull it higher.

A salvage vessel hauled at the stern. The cables broke.

The tide rose. Thetis swung in the current, and the part of the hull that had been visible slipped beneath the surface again.

A doctor who examined the survivors reported serious effects of carbon monoxide poisoning. The rescuers tried another lift before the current eased. By then the stern was losing buoyancy, and they couldn't raise it.

They tried again, passing a wire around the submerged hull. The current moved the submarine and the wire slipped off. It carried away the marker the rescuers had been using to locate Thetis. After hours of work, they had to search for the submarine all over again.

They found her late that evening. More divers arrived. One later reported hearing something that might have been tapping inside the hull. That did not establish that someone was deliberately signalling.

The rescuers investigated whether they could get air to that part of the submarine. The work would take several changes of tide; they concluded it could not be done in time. The rescue operation gave way to recovery. Ninety-nine people died.

For the families there was still the question of why the inner door had been opened at all. The first account to Parliament left that unresolved. A public inquiry was announced, but one piece of evidence could not be examined until the submarine was recovered: tube five itself.

When investigators reached it, they found protective enamel obstructing the tiny test tap. The enamel was meant to protect the metal inside the tube. Instead, it had prevented water from appearing at the tap. Nothing coming out did not mean the tube was dry.

The outer door was another question. Woods had believed its indicator showed it closed. After the recovery the indicator was found near the open position. How the outer door had come to be open could not be conclusively established. The obstruction explained the false test without explaining every step leading to the accident.

The Navy subsequently introduced a precaution known as the Thetis clip, intended to prevent the inner door from opening freely against water pressure. The submarine itself was repaired and later served as HMS Thunderbolt.

The recovered hull finally supplied the answer that the men at the torpedo tube had missed. But there had been no safe way to cut into that same hull while the rescuers could still see it above the sea.
