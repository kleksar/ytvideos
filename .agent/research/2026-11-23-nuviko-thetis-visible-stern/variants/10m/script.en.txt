If a submarine's stern is sticking out of the sea, why can't rescuers open it? And how can an empty-looking torpedo tube contain enough water to sink an entire boat? The loss of HMS Thetis answers both questions, but only after two separate failures unfold.

On June first, 1939, Britain was preparing for a possible European war. A new Royal Navy submarine was still being tested before entering service. HMS Thetis had been built at Cammell Laird in Birkenhead. Earlier trials had found problems with her machinery and steering, so her final diving tests were taking place in Liverpool Bay.

A submarine trial is not staffed like an ordinary patrol. Naval inspectors, shipyard specialists and other civilians came aboard to observe equipment they had worked on. Together with the crew, 103 men went to sea. A tug followed Thetis to keep other traffic clear of the diving area and to maintain contact.

Around twenty to two that afternoon, the submarine began to descend. But the test was not going smoothly. She seemed too light at the bow. The men needed to establish why the planned water balance was wrong.

Among the things they checked were the torpedo tubes at the front of the boat.

Each tube had an outer opening into the sea and an inner door facing the crew. The two must never be opened together. When the outer opening is shut, a sailor can open the inner door and work on the tube. When the outer opening is open, the inner door is the only barrier holding back the sea.

So the inner doors had a small test tap. Before opening a door, you opened that tap. If water came out, the tube was flooded, and the door stayed closed.

The crew checked tube five. No water came from its test tap. They began to open the inner door.

Then an enormous weight of seawater forced its way in.

The outer bow cap was open. The men had believed the tube was dry. It wasn't.

Water rushed through the forward torpedo compartment. The bow dropped sharply and the submarine began to fall. Men tried to shut the nearest watertight door. It was heavy, needed multiple catches and became increasingly difficult to secure as the boat tipped downward. People were still escaping through the doorway. They eventually retreated farther back and shut a second barrier.

It kept most of the boat from flooding, but two forward spaces were lost. Thetis struck the bottom at roughly forty metres.

The first question now was not how to escape. It was whether the boat could be raised.

The crew tried compressed air. On a submarine, this air can push water out of ballast tanks and make the hull buoyant again. But with two forward spaces flooded, the boat could not simply float free. The stern was lighter than the bow, which remained down in the seabed.

They released marker buoys. They also tried to communicate with the surface. Yet the impact had damaged their signalling equipment, and the escorting tug was no longer directly over them. It had followed the submarine's expected underwater course. The emergency markers were not recognised immediately.

That meant valuable hours passed before rescuers could locate the hull.

Thetis had been due to surface by late afternoon. When she did not, the Royal Navy launched a search. An aircraft eventually spotted a marker, and a destroyer continued looking through the night.

The men inside were not waiting idly. They tried to preserve air and power. One possible way to remove the floodwater was to get back into the submerged forward section and shut the torpedo tube. A diver from inside would have to pass through a small flooded chamber to reach it.

One attempted entry had to be stopped. Another attempt, with a rope to guide the man back, could not complete the job either. The sea outside the tube was still open. Recovering the boat from within was becoming less likely.

The crew found another possibility: lighten the stern until it reached the surface. If they could get an exit close enough to the air, perhaps people could leave without waiting for the whole submarine to be raised.

By morning, the stern had risen above the sea. When the destroyer HMS Brazen found Thetis, the rear end was roughly eighteen feet out of the water.

From the outside, that must have seemed encouraging. The submarine was visible. A warship had arrived. There was a hull to hold on to.

But a visible stern was not an open doorway.

Thetis had a dedicated escape chamber. It could be flooded so that the water pressure inside matched the pressure outside. A man using Davis breathing equipment could then open the hatch, rise to the surface and be picked up.

Captain Harry Oram and Lieutenant Frederick Woods went first. The two men came up as the rescue vessels converged. Oram had valuable information for the officers above. The rest of the men, he reported, were still alive when he left.

Later, Leading Stoker Walter Arnold and Frank Shaw, an engineer from the shipyard, escaped as well.

Four men had made it.

Getting the rest out through the chamber would take repeated cycles, each requiring flooding, opening and draining a confined space. The men inside were growing weaker. One attempt to send a larger group through the chamber failed, with fatal consequences. The remaining people could not simply rush out together.

Meanwhile, the rescue ships were looking at the exposed stern. Could they cut their way in?

Not at the waterline. That could let seawater pour into the air-filled parts of the hull. To make a safer opening, they needed to lift the stern farther above the surface.

This introduced a different problem. Liverpool Bay has powerful tides. Divers could work effectively only at slack water. The rescue vessels had to hold an angled steel submarine against the moving sea while trying to pull it higher.

A salvage vessel hauled at the stern. The cables broke.

The tide rose. Thetis swung in the current, and the part of the hull that had been visible slipped beneath the surface again.

That was the second catastrophe. The first was the flood inside the torpedo compartment. The second was the loss of a possible way into the occupied boat.

The Navy made another attempt with salvage gear. It too failed. Doctors had examined the four survivors and reported grave effects of carbon monoxide poisoning. Time was running out. Divers later reported sounds that they thought could be tapping from within the boat, although that was not proof that an organised signal was being sent. Supplying fresh air to that compartment would take longer than the rescuers believed the men could survive.

Eventually, rescue operations gave way to recovery. No fifth man came to the surface. Ninety-nine died.

The wreck was not left there forever. Thetis was salvaged and investigated. That made it possible to answer a question the men at the torpedo tube could not answer on June first: why had the water test appeared safe?

Investigators found that the hole in the test tap of tube five was plugged with the protective enamel applied inside the tube. The tap was supposed to warn of seawater. Instead it was blocked. The absence of a trickle had been treated as evidence that the tube contained no water.

But the investigation uncovered more than paint.

The outer bow cap had been open, and exactly how it came to be in that position could not be established conclusively. The mechanical indications were not straightforward to interpret. The heavy watertight door had slowed the effort to contain the flood. And the routine check had not revealed that the tiny opening was blocked. Several protections had failed in sequence.

Afterward, the Navy introduced a device often called the Thetis clip, intended to prevent an inner torpedo-door hatch from opening freely against pressure from a flooded tube.

The story also reached a courtroom. Some relatives sought documents from the shipbuilder in their negligence claims. The Admiralty argued that disclosing certain submarine plans and records would harm the public interest. In 1942 the House of Lords upheld that claim of privilege. The disaster had become a case about access to evidence as well as the loss itself.

There is one final turn. Thetis was repaired and commissioned again, this time as HMS Thunderbolt. The same hull served during the Second World War. In March 1943 it was lost at sea again, with everyone aboard. Historical sources differ on the precise circumstances of that second loss.

The first disaster was caused by more than one blocked hole. It was a chain: a misleading check, an open sea connection, doors that could not contain the flood quickly enough, a search that took hours, and an escape route that rising water took away.

The most painful detail is also the clearest. The rescuers could see part of the submarine. They could reach four of its men. But they could not reach the ninety-nine left inside.
