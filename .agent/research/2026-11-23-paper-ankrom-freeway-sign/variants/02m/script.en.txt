In August 2001, a man in a hard hat climbed above a Los Angeles freeway and attached two new road signs. California hadn't sent him.

Just after a tunnel on the 110 freeway, the exit toward northbound Interstate 5 came up on the left. Artist and professional sign painter Richard Ankrom had missed that turn himself. He thought the big overhead sign needed an I-5 shield and the word NORTH.

Instead of waiting for the state, he made the panels without permission. They had to pass for government equipment, not freshly painted artwork. Ankrom checked California's sign standards, matched the aluminum and green background, and found the old round reflectors used on nearby signs.

He prepared a fake work order, dressed for a road job, and brought cones and a ladder. Friends filmed as he climbed up and fastened the panels. Real state road workers were nearby. By Ankrom's account, nobody challenged him.

For months the additions looked like ordinary directions. Then Ankrom showed his film in 2002, revealing exactly who had changed the sign.

The transportation agency warned that working above traffic without authorization was dangerous. It hadn't approved the installation. But officials chose to leave the accurate directions where they were.

Then came the second surprise. In 2009, contractors replaced the whole sign. Ankrom tried to recover the pieces he'd signed, but they had already been crushed at a recycler.

The replacement was installed by the state. It still showed northbound Interstate 5. The artist lost his original work, while drivers kept the information he'd supplied.

One version had been installed by the state. The other had only looked that way.
