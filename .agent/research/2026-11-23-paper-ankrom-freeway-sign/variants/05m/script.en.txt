Why did California's highway officials leave an illegally installed sign over a busy freeway? How did an artist make it convincing enough to fool the people responsible for the road? And why did the state eventually destroy the original, only to keep its idea?

To understand that, picture downtown Los Angeles in 2001. A driver heads north on the 110 freeway, intending to join Interstate 5. The interchange comes immediately after a tunnel. The needed lane is on the left. By the time you recognize it, you may already have passed it.

Richard Ankrom knew that problem personally. He had missed the turn. He was an artist, but he also painted signs for a living. When he looked at the overhead highway sign, he saw room for two missing pieces: a red, white and blue Interstate 5 shield and a little green plaque marked NORTH.

He could have written a letter about it. Instead, he decided to make the pieces himself and install them without permission.

The difficult part wasn't just getting up there. The additions had to look as though California's transportation department had made them years earlier.

Ankrom found the state's sign manual and compared its instructions with an existing sign he could inspect. He cut the shapes from aluminum, matched the green background, and worked out the lettering. But the older freeway signs had small round reflectors fixed to their faces. He couldn't simply paint those on.

He found a supplier and explained that he needed the reflectors for a film project. That was true, as far as it went. He planned to film the whole installation.

Before installation day, he signed the backs of the pieces. He also prepared a fake work order, gave his truck the name of a plausible-sounding company, and dressed for a road job. A hard hat, orange vest and traffic cones completed the picture.

At sunrise on August 5, 2001, Ankrom and his friends arrived near the sign. The friends took up positions with cameras. He set out cones, carried his ladder to the structure and climbed up with the aluminum pieces.

Actual Caltrans workers were nearby. Ankrom later recalled being watched, but nobody challenged him. The man in the safety vest appeared to be doing his job.

He fastened the Interstate shield and the NORTH panel onto the existing sign and climbed down. Cars continued past. Nothing dramatic happened.

That was precisely what made the stunt work.

For roughly nine months, the signs hung over traffic. To motorists, they were ordinary directions. Ankrom edited his footage into a short documentary. When the film and local reporting revealed what he'd done in 2002, the story spread well beyond Los Angeles.

Now the state had a public problem. It could remove a useful direction because it had been installed illegally. Or it could leave the unauthorized object above a live highway.

Caltrans publicly warned that this was dangerous work and that its crews followed safety procedures. It did not endorse the stunt. But officials chose to leave the addition in place.

Eight years passed. The artist's sign was still telling drivers where the road went.

Then, in 2009, a routine sign-replacement project brought it down. Ankrom tried to recover the piece he had signed. He followed the trail from the contractor to a recycling company. Too late: the old aluminum had already been crushed into a bale.

This time the transportation department installed a completely official sign. It included the same northbound I-5 information his unauthorized panels had supplied.

Ankrom lost the artwork he had made with his hands. But the correction was still hanging over the road.

The state didn't preserve his original. It preserved what the original had fixed.
