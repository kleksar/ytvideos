How could a handmade addition to a California freeway sign pass for government equipment? Why would transportation officials keep it after discovering who put it there? And how did the artist lose his original work while the state preserved the directions he had invented?

On a northbound drive through downtown Los Angeles, the 110 freeway offers a particularly awkward choice. A tunnel interrupts the view. After the tunnel, the turn toward northbound Interstate 5 comes up on the left. A driver who expects a normal right-hand exit can miss it before understanding what's happened.

Richard Ankrom once missed it. At the time, he was just another motorist who needed to get somewhere else. Years later, living and working in downtown Los Angeles, he studied the overhead sign that should have warned him. The large green panel had space for the information he needed, but it lacked a prominent northbound I-5 marking.

This wasn't an unfamiliar problem to him. Ankrom was a working sign painter as well as an artist. He knew that drivers read signs at speed. The question was how to correct an official sign without being the official who was allowed to correct it.

He began with research rather than paint.

The transportation standards were available to consult. Ankrom studied the manual, then inspected another freeway sign he could reach without stepping onto the highway. He wanted the additions to match not just the meaning, but the physical language of the existing structure: its lettering, proportions, colors, materials, and age.

The work required two separate pieces. One would carry the familiar blue, red and white Interstate 5 shield. Above it, another would say NORTH. Together, they would tell a driver which direction the left-hand junction actually served.

He cut aluminum to shape. He selected the correct lettering and worked on the green background. Even that wasn't enough. The existing freeway signs came from an earlier generation and had round button-like reflectors. Newly painted metal with the wrong surface would stand out.

Ankrom tracked down the old-style reflectors. When the supplier asked why he needed them, he explained that he was making a film. It was a carefully incomplete answer: the film would document the sign, but the sign had to be installed above live traffic first.

He also aged the finish. A freshly painted addition would look suspicious against decades of dirt and smog. A light gray glaze took some of the new shine away. Then he signed the back of the pieces with a marker, as he might sign a painting.

That signature seems like an odd choice for an object he wanted nobody to recognize. But it mattered to him that this was an artwork with an author, not merely an anonymous act of repair. He planned to reveal it later, on film.

Planning the installation was its own task. He needed to reach the sign from an overpass, carry the parts, fix them in place, and leave without blocking freeway lanes. He recruited friends to record the process from different positions. He made a false work order that could pass for paperwork if someone questioned him. His truck carried a deliberately official-looking name: Aesthetic De Construction.

He cut his hair. He bought a white hard hat and an orange safety vest. And he obtained traffic cones, the sort of small detail that tells a passerby, before they've read anything, that maintenance is underway.

At first, he considered doing the job at night. But daylight, he reasoned, would make his disguise more convincing. A person working in an orange vest under the morning sun looked like someone who belonged there.

On August 5, 2001, he drove to the overpass near the sign, with his friends and their cameras ready.

He had put some of his equipment in position ahead of time. Now he set up the cones and carried the ladder to the structure. He climbed onto the narrow access area and began fastening the Interstate shield and NORTH panel to the existing sign.

In a later interview he recalled how carefully he controlled his pace. Working too fast could look wrong; dropping a tool could be dangerous. He concentrated on handling each piece while freeway traffic moved below.

There were genuine state road workers nearby. He later heard that they had noticed him and watched for a while. But they did not come over to challenge him. From the road, the uniform, cones, truck and ladder all told the same believable story.

He finished, came down, and drove away.

The film captured an act that was meant to disappear into daily routine. And for a while, it did. There was no public unveiling. No ribbon. Drivers simply saw clearer directions.

For months, Ankrom edited the video. The sign could have stayed anonymous. Instead he chose to show how an apparently ordinary piece of government equipment had been made and installed by someone with no authority to touch it.

In May 2002, local reporting followed the film's appearance. National television crews came to interview him. This was a reveal the artist had arranged for, not an inspector catching him on the bridge. The sign had worked because it looked routine. Now its origin was the story.

California's transportation department was asked to explain how a stranger had altered a sign above live traffic. It warned that such work was dangerous and that authorized crews followed safety procedures. Nobody should treat the fact he got away with it as permission.

There was a second question: should Caltrans take the addition down? The panels supplied directional information that the original overhead sign had lacked. After examining the work, officials left it in place. A spokesperson even joked in an interview about a job application for Ankrom.

The distinction mattered to the decision. Caltrans could recognize accurate information without approving the unauthorized method. A good result was not a license to climb above a freeway. And for motorists who had missed the left-hand turn, the information did not change depending on who had fastened it there.

Ankrom's project came to be called Guerrilla Public Service. Most drivers would never know the name. For them it was simply a shield marked Interstate 5 and a small panel reading NORTH.

The sign remained in place through the rest of the decade. Then the sign vanished for a reason that had nothing to do with the 2002 publicity.

In 2009, contractors were replacing the overhead signs along that stretch of the 110 freeway. The larger panel that carried Ankrom's two additions was removed along with the other old equipment. For eight years his work had survived being publicly exposed as unauthorized. Now an ordinary replacement contract threatened it.

Ankrom learned of the change through an online report. He went to check the roadway and found a new sign in place. The original he had signed was gone.

He began tracing the old metal. First he had to find which contractor had removed the sign. That led him to an employee who thought he had set aside the piece. The object the employee had saved was not Ankrom's. His precaution of signing the reverse had not helped, because the right panel had never been checked.

He followed the trail to a recycling company. By then the actual original had been crushed into a compressed block of aluminum with other discarded road signs. It was no longer something he could take home and hang on a wall.

According to Ankrom's account in a 2009 interview, he even tried to recover the block. The company would not sell it to him. The signed panel had survived the years above moving traffic, only to disappear into a routine stream of recycled material. That is the part of the story his film could preserve but his signature could not prevent.

The road, however, had received a new official sign. On that replacement were the same directions toward northbound Interstate 5 that he had added in 2001. Later accounts describe the corrected approach information appearing on other nearby signs too, though this story does not establish any measured effect on crashes or congestion.

Ankrom lost the physical work he had made. The state installed its own equipment and kept the directional correction. Those are two different outcomes, not evidence that officials endorsed his dangerous climb or authorized the original installation.

There had been two signs with the same message: one made by an artist who pretended to belong to a road crew, and the next made through the state's normal replacement program. The forged addition did not become official by being left alone. Its information became official when the replacement took its place.
