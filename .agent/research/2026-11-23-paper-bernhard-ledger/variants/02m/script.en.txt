A British five-pound note looked genuine. A bank ledger said otherwise.

In 1943, a forged note passed through a British bank in Morocco. The paper felt right. The printing looked right. Even the tiny details were convincing. But the serial number belonged to a note already recorded as paid.

The fake came from a hidden printing workshop inside Sachsenhausen concentration camp in Germany. Nazi security officers had forced Jewish prisoners who understood engraving, printing and banking to make British money.

British notes at the time looked surprisingly plain: black printing on white paper. But their paper had a particular feel, and the designs carried intentional tiny flaws. The prisoners copied the watermark, the signatures and those flaws. They even rubbed and folded fresh notes until they looked used.

This wasn't a clever business run by willing counterfeiters. It was forced work in a concentration camp. The prisoners' skills were the reason their captors kept them alive and working.

The original plan had been to undermine British currency. Instead, the Nazis used forged pounds to pay spies and buy supplies. Millions of notes were made.

Yet one detail resisted copying. The forgers could not reproduce the Bank of England's numbering system, so they reused numbers from real notes. A clerk in Morocco checked a ledger. The note had apparently been paid before. That was the clue the printing could not conceal.

Britain withdrew larger notes from circulation. In 1945 the prisoners were moved to Austria, where American troops liberated them. One survivor, Adolf Burger, later described the operation.

It was a factory built to make convincing money, and a prison where people were forced to make it. Its most revealing flaw was not in the ink or the paper. It was in an ordinary bank record.
