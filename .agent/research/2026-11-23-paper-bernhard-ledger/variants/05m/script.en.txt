How could a banknote be copied so accurately that even banking checks could be fooled? And why did one ordinary ledger catch what the paper and printing did not?

In the early 1940s, a British five-pound note was a large piece of white paper with black printing on one side. There was an image of Britannia, a signature, a watermark and a long serial number. It didn't look like a modern, colourful banknote. Yet reproducing it was an extraordinarily demanding job.

Nazi intelligence had begun trying to forge British pounds under a project called Operation Andreas. The original aim was to destabilise Britain's currency by flooding the country with fake money. Then the project changed. In 1942, SS officer Bernhard Krüger was put in charge of producing forged notes that could be spent abroad to buy supplies and pay agents.

He didn't recruit ordinary printers. The SS searched concentration camps for Jewish prisoners with skills in engraving, typography, paper and banking. A group of 142 was assembled in Sachsenhausen, near Berlin. They worked in an isolated workshop inside the camp, under guard. Their expertise made them useful to the SS; it did not make them free.

Now the real problem began. Copying the drawing wasn't enough. The Bank of England's paper had a particular texture and watermark. The design contained tiny intentional irregularities, some so small that someone unfamiliar with them might think they were printing mistakes. Those imperfections were security features.

The prisoners examined authentic notes and reproduced about 150 such marks. They engraved plates and experimented with paper until a new sheet could pass for a familiar British one. Then came a strange finishing step: freshly printed money looked too clean. Workers rubbed and folded the notes to give them the wear of circulation.

There was an encouraging test for the people running the operation. According to the Sachsenhausen memorial, an agent took forged notes to Switzerland, and a check involving the Bank of England treated them as genuine. The counterfeiters had copied the appearance convincingly enough.

But a dangerous weakness remained. The operation could reproduce the printing; it could not reliably reproduce the Bank of England's numbering system. The forgers reused serial numbers from genuine notes.

In 1943, a counterfeit passed through a British bank in Morocco. A clerk consulted a ledger of notes already paid. The serial number was there. He had found the mistake without needing to spot anything wrong with the engraving.

By then the fake pounds were not simply a proposal to damage Britain's economy. They were being used in transactions to fund German intelligence and purchase supplies. The Bank of England reacted by withdrawing notes above five pounds from circulation. That response would outlast the war.

Inside Sachsenhausen, the work continued. The scale was enormous: surviving institutional records place its total face value at roughly 135 million pounds. That is the amount printed on the notes, not proof that this much money was successfully spent.

In early 1945, the SS moved the imprisoned workers from Germany to Austria as the war turned against the regime. The operation did not restart there. American troops liberated the prisoners in early May. Among the survivors was a Slovak Jewish printer, Adolf Burger, who later wrote about the workshop.

Many notes were reportedly dumped in Lake Toplitz. Some continued circulating for years. In 1959, reports described divers recovering counterfeit notes from the lake, although early newspaper estimates of their value varied wildly.

The Bank of England did not bring back a ten-pound note until 1964. The replacement belonged to a more sophisticated, colourful generation of designs. The forged money had failed to deliver its original plan of collapsing Britain's currency, but it had left a lasting mark on the bank's response.

And there is another part of the result that cannot be read from a banknote alone. The quality of the fakes was produced by prisoners who had no choice about making them. The ledger exposed the crime of counterfeiting. The surviving accounts exposed the human reality of the workshop.
