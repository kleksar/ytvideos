How could fake British pounds pass a check connected to the Bank of England and still be exposed by a bank clerk in Morocco? And what happened to the imprisoned people forced to make those notes after the fraud was discovered?

To follow both questions, start with the banknote itself.

During the Second World War, Britain's familiar paper money looked nothing like today's colourful designs. A note was a broad piece of white, cotton-based paper. On one side, black printing showed the value, a signature, a serial number and a small drawing of Britannia. The reverse was blank. No colour-changing patches or elaborate printed scenes. The simplicity was deceptive.

Bank of England notes had been refined over decades. Their paper had a distinctive feel and watermark. The engravings contained tiny intentional defects, different for different denominations. Someone casually copying the image might remove those imperfections. A convincing forger had to put them back.

The first Nazi plan was called Operation Andreas. Planning began in 1939; the Bank of England dates the first programme to 1940. German security officers wanted enough false pounds to undermine Britain's currency. They also needed proof the counterfeits would survive inspection. The Sachsenhausen memorial describes an early trial: an agent carried forged notes to Switzerland, and a check involving the Bank of England's serial numbers treated them as genuine.

That sounds like a victory. But the available account does not describe every record consulted in Switzerland. It establishes that these notes were accepted in that test, not that every subsequent transaction could escape detection. The distinction will matter when a different bank opens a different book.

In 1942, the programme was revived for a more practical purpose. The SS wanted forged pounds to pay foreign agents and purchase supplies. The officer placed in charge was Bernhard Krüger. The operation took his name: Bernhard.

Krüger needed people who knew more than how to operate a press. The SS searched concentration camps for Jewish prisoners with experience in engraving, typesetting, paper and banking. A total of 142 were brought into the operation at Sachsenhausen concentration camp, near Berlin.

The workshop was isolated from the rest of Sachsenhausen. The memorial describes single beds and civilian clothes for the prisoners there, conditions somewhat better than in much of the camp. Yet the SS still controlled whether they worked, where they lived and whether they survived. The memorial states that illness, failure or the project's end could mean death. Skill did not remove that threat; it made the prisoners indispensable to the people threatening them.

Inside, the technical work was painstaking. Every authentic British note was a specimen to study. The prisoners examined the tiny drawing of Britannia and the precise weight of its lines. They matched lettering, the shapes of signatures and the way banknote paper behaved when bent.

An engraved plate could reproduce the drawing. It could not create a genuine-looking watermark without the right paper. The makers had to copy the paper and the print together. The goal was a note that felt ordinary when someone picked it up, not a spectacular imitation in a display case.

The inspectors paid particular attention to what looked like mistakes. The Bank of England had introduced tiny irregularities as security marks. The counterfeiters identified about 150 of them and reproduced them in printing plates for five-, ten-, twenty- and fifty-pound notes.

After printing came another problem. An untouched stack of money looked as though it had come from a new press, which it had. But real cash travelled from hand to hand. The notes were passed around, rubbed and folded with dirty hands so that the result looked used. The printed detail and the physical wear now told the same story: ordinary money already in circulation.

The result was striking: a note with the right paper, deliberately imperfect lines and the appearance of ordinary use. But there was a second kind of information a note carried. It was printed in ink, yet its meaning depended on records held outside the workshop.

This became the operation's dangerous weakness.

Each Bank of England note had a serial number. The numbers followed an internal system that the counterfeiters had tried to work out. According to the Bank of England's own museum, they never fully cracked it. Instead, they copied numbers from real notes.

Reusing a number was different from copying a watermark. No amount of careful printing could tell the workshop where every genuine note with that number had already been recorded. The earlier Swiss acceptance was real, but it did not certify the entire future history of each number. The surviving accounts do not explain precisely which records were consulted in that trial.

In 1943, a counterfeit passed through a British bank in Morocco. Its appearance raised no decisive objection. But the bank maintained leather-bound ledgers recording the serial numbers of banknotes withdrawn from circulation. A clerk checked the number and found it was already listed as paid.

This bank was checking something different from the shape of a watermark. The ledger showed that a note with that serial number had already been paid. The records do not identify the Swiss test as an identical paid-note-ledger check. So there is no need to invent a mysterious change in the quality of the forgery: the documented result depends on what was checked, and what the clerk found in 1943.

The figure later recorded for the operation was enormous. According to secretly kept statistics cited by the Sachsenhausen memorial, the face value printed exceeded 135 million pounds. But a denomination is not a receipt. The number tells us how much the printed notes claimed to be worth, not how many were successfully spent or what loss Britain suffered.

The SS used forged pounds to pay foreign spies and buy supplies. That made the counterfeits useful to the regime even without the original plan to flood Britain's economy. Detection in Morocco did not immediately stop the machines inside Sachsenhausen. The next question was no longer just whether the money could be trusted. It was what a bank could withdraw—and what would happen to the workers the SS still held captive.

The Bank of England withdrew notes worth more than five pounds from circulation. The forged five-pound notes were not all removed by that measure, and the underlying problem was larger than a single suspicious piece of paper. For years afterward the bank delayed the return of the higher denominations. But a policy change in Britain did not free anyone working at the camp.

By late 1944 the prisoners were printing in two shifts, according to the memorial. Outside, the war was moving toward Germany's defeat; inside, the workshop continued to serve the SS. The people making the money could not decide to close the operation or walk away from it. A technically successful print run did not make the next day safer for them.

Then the war approached Germany. In February 1945, the SS transferred the prisoners to Mauthausen and later to the Redl-Zipf subcamp in Austria. The presses did not resume production. The prisoners were moved again, to Ebensee. American forces liberated them in early May, with the Bank of England recording 6 May 1945.

Among the liberated prisoners was Adolf Burger, a Slovak Jewish printer. He later wrote about the counterfeiting workshop. His testimony gives the surviving notes a context they cannot supply on their own: their makers were not participants in a profitable scam, but people forced to put their expertise at the service of their captors.

Some false pounds continued to surface after the war. Reports later linked lost counterfeit notes to Austria's Lake Toplitz. When divers recovered notes in 1959, news reports disagreed sharply about the amounts. The sensational totals from those headlines are not a reliable measure of how much forged money was spent.

The ten-pound note returned in 1964, the twenty-pound note in 1970, and the fifty-pound note in 1981. The new designs were more colourful and complex. Those dates are the long institutional aftermath of a problem first made visible by an ordinary bank record; they are not evidence that the counterfeit operation ruined Britain's currency.

The ledger in Morocco supplied one kind of evidence: a note had claimed a serial number already recorded as paid. The surviving testimony supplied another: who had been forced to make these notes, and what happened to them when the presses stopped. The printing could deceive the eye. Neither the bank's records nor the prisoners' history could be copied onto a fresh sheet of paper.
