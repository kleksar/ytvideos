If a counterfeit banknote looks perfect, what can a bank do to detect it? How did a Nazi printing operation produce millions of convincing British pounds inside a concentration camp? And why did one bank ledger expose the flaw that all the engravers had missed?

The answer begins with a note that looked almost too simple to counterfeit badly.

During the Second World War, Britain's familiar paper money looked nothing like today's colourful designs. A note was a broad piece of white, cotton-based paper. On one side, black printing showed the value, a signature, a serial number and a small drawing of Britannia. The reverse was blank. No colour-changing patches or elaborate printed scenes. The simplicity was deceptive.

Bank of England notes had been refined over decades. Their paper had a distinctive feel and watermark. The engravings contained tiny intentional defects, different for different denominations. Someone casually copying the image might remove those imperfections. A convincing forger had to put them back.

The first Nazi effort began before the war was fully under way. German security officers planned a scheme called Operation Andreas. The initial idea was to create forged British currency on such a scale that introducing it into Britain could destabilise the economy.

But an idea about crashing a currency is not the same as having a useful supply of banknotes. Paper, ink, printing plates, watermarks and bank clerks were separate obstacles. A false note had to survive not only a glance across a counter but routine handling and record checks.

In 1942, the programme was revived for a more practical purpose. The SS wanted forged pounds to pay foreign agents and purchase supplies. The officer placed in charge was Bernhard Krüger. The operation took his name: Bernhard.

Krüger needed people who knew more than how to operate a press. The SS searched concentration camps for Jewish prisoners with experience in engraving, typesetting, paper and banking. A total of 142 were brought into the operation at Sachsenhausen concentration camp, near Berlin.

The workshop occupied an isolated part of the camp. Guards kept the prisoners apart from other inmates to preserve secrecy. The prisoners were permitted a few conditions better than those elsewhere in the camp, including beds and civilian clothing. But those details are not evidence of freedom or kindness. Their captors controlled their lives, and the prisoners understood that illness, failure or the ending of the project could mean death.

Inside, the technical work was painstaking. Every authentic British note was a specimen to study. The prisoners examined the tiny drawing of Britannia and the precise weight of its lines. They matched lettering, the shapes of signatures and the way banknote paper behaved when bent.

A printing plate could reproduce lines. It could not reproduce a watermark unless the paper itself was also made properly. The operation experimented with paper until the notes matched the look and feel of genuine currency. The unusual challenge was not making something impressive. It was making something unremarkable enough to be accepted immediately.

The inspectors paid particular attention to what looked like mistakes. The Bank of England had introduced tiny irregularities as security marks. The counterfeiters identified about 150 of them and reproduced them in printing plates for five-, ten-, twenty- and fifty-pound notes.

Think about the direction of the work. A normal printer tries to remove defects. These prisoners had to imitate defects, because a perfect-looking line in the wrong place would make the note less convincing.

After printing came another problem. An untouched stack of money looked as though it had come from a new press, which it had. But real cash travelled from hand to hand. The notes were passed around, rubbed and folded with dirty hands so that the result looked used. The printed detail and the physical wear now told the same story: ordinary money already in circulation.

There was evidence that the scheme might succeed. The Sachsenhausen memorial describes a test in Switzerland: an agent took false notes abroad and a verification involving the Bank of England treated them as genuine. As a demonstration of paper and printing, it was formidable.

It did not prove that every bank check would be defeated. The operation still had one unresolved problem.

Each Bank of England note had a serial number. The numbers followed an internal system that the counterfeiters had tried to work out. According to the Bank of England's own museum, they never fully cracked it. Instead, they copied numbers from real notes.

That shortcut could be invisible when two notes were far apart. If one was checked against a record of notes that had already been paid, however, the duplication became an alarm.

In 1943, a counterfeit passed through a British bank in Morocco. Its appearance raised no decisive objection. But the bank maintained leather-bound ledgers recording the serial numbers of banknotes withdrawn from circulation. A clerk checked the number and found it was already listed as paid.

The mistake was not in the drawing of Britannia, or the watermark, or how worn the edges felt. It was the evidence of two supposedly genuine notes sharing a history only one note could have.

That discovery exposed the limitation of a remarkable counterfeit. You can reproduce a banknote's appearance; you cannot make a second copy inherit the original note's transaction record.

The operation was still large enough to matter. The Sachsenhausen memorial reports secretly kept production statistics showing more than 135 million pounds in face value. That is a staggering number, but it must be read carefully. Face value measures the denominations printed on paper. It does not mean the SS converted or spent every note, nor does it establish the amount of harm to the British economy.

The original plan to flood Britain was not carried out as imagined. The forged pounds instead helped pay intelligence agents and acquire supplies. In other words, a project proposed as economic warfare became a way of financing secret wartime activity.

The Bank of England reacted by removing notes above five pounds from circulation. Britain's monetary system had to respond to an attack in which the fake paper was good enough that simply looking more carefully at the picture was not an adequate defence.

Meanwhile the people producing the notes remained prisoners. By late 1944, the workshop operated in two shifts. The work could sustain their captors' scheme but offered its workers no control over what happened next. Even a highly skilled printer could not bargain away the danger imposed by the SS.

Then the war approached Germany. In February 1945, the SS transferred the prisoners to Mauthausen and later to the Redl-Zipf subcamp in Austria. The presses did not resume production. The prisoners were moved again, to Ebensee. American forces liberated them in early May, with the Bank of England recording 6 May 1945.

One survivor was Adolf Burger, a Slovak Jewish printer. Long after the war, he wrote about what happened inside the factory. His account matters because a preserved forged note can show craftsmanship, but it cannot by itself tell us what it meant to make that note under armed coercion.

The money had a strange afterlife. Some counterfeit notes continued to appear for years. Many were thought to have been thrown into Austria's Lake Toplitz, perhaps along with printing equipment. In 1959, newspapers reported boxes of fake pounds recovered by divers. Early reports inflated estimates of the value, while later accounts offered much lower figures. That disagreement is another reminder not to mistake a dramatic headline for a reliable total.

A single counterfeit note also survived in museum collections, including the Bank of England Museum and Sachsenhausen. On display, its paper and printing are evidence of the work's technical quality. Its origin is evidence of the system that forced people to do it.

The effects on British money lasted beyond the war. The ten-pound note did not return until 1964, the twenty-pound note in 1970 and the fifty-pound note in 1981. The new designs were more colourful and complicated than the old white notes.

The operation did not collapse the British economy. It did force a response to convincing counterfeits, while leaving the surviving prisoners to tell a story far larger than money.

And that is why the Morocco ledger is such a powerful ending. The forgers had learned to copy the image, the texture, the watermark, the deliberate imperfections and even the wear of everyday use. What they had not copied was the life the original banknote had already lived.

A clerk found that life in an account book. The rest of the story, including the prisoners who made the money, could not be told by the banknote alone.
