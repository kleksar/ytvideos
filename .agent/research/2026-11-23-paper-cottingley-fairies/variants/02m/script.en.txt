Five photographs of the Cottingley fairies were genuine photographs. The fairies weren't.

In 1917, two cousins in Yorkshire took a camera down to a stream. Frances stood among dancing fairies in one picture. In another, a small winged figure appeared beside Elsie.

The camera recorded something on its glass plates. And that was enough to start a much bigger story.

Elsie's mother later showed the pictures to people interested in the supernatural. Then the photographs reached Arthur Conan Doyle, the author who had invented Sherlock Holmes. He believed they offered evidence of fairies.

The girls were asked for more proof. In 1920 they received new cameras and specially marked photographic plates, intended to make tampering easier to spot. They produced three more pictures.

Experts could look for changes made to the negative. But there was a simpler way to fool a camera: put something artificial in front of it.

The fairies had been drawn, cut from cardboard and fixed among the plants with hatpins. There was no need to alter the photographs afterward.

In 1983, more than sixty years after the first pictures, the cousins acknowledged that the images had been staged. They still disagreed about one of the five: Frances maintained that the last photograph was genuine, while Elsie said all five were fabricated.

The pictures didn't prove fairies existed. They showed what a camera can record, and what people can decide that record means.
