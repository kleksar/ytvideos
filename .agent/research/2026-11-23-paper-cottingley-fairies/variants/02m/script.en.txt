In 1920, a sixteen-year-old wrote to Edward Gardner with a simple suggestion: the fairies in those photographs might be cardboard cutouts. Yet the creator of Sherlock Holmes was treating the pictures as evidence.

Three years earlier, cousins Frances Griffiths and Elsie Wright had borrowed a camera beside a Yorkshire stream. One photograph showed Frances near dancing fairies. Another placed a little winged figure beside Elsie.

The photographs left the family. Some photographic examiners found no clear signs of familiar tricks such as double exposure or darkroom alterations. Arthur Conan Doyle believed the pictures supported the existence of fairies.

Then Gardner raised the stakes. He gave the girls new cameras and photographic plates with hidden marks, making tampering harder to conceal. They produced three more fairy pictures.

But a mark on a plate cannot show what stood among the plants.

Elsie had drawn the figures. The cousins cut them out and fixed them in place with hatpins. The camera recorded cardboard in the grass without any need to alter the negatives. The teenager had guessed the kind of trick involved, although the letter alone did not prove it.

In 1983, Elsie explained the method in a letter. She said all five images were fabricated. Frances still insisted the fifth was genuine; that claim was never independently established.

The photographs really recorded what was in front of the lens. The mistake was expecting a camera to tell its viewers what the figures were.
