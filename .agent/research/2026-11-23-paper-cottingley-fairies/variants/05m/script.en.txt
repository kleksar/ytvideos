How did two cousins turn fairy photographs into a national argument? What did the experts actually test? And why did a sixteen-year-old suggest an explanation that the cameras could never rule out?

It began beside a stream in Yorkshire in 1917. Frances Griffiths said she had seen fairies there. Her older cousin, Elsie Wright, helped make photographs that seemed to back up the story.

In one picture, Frances sat near a group of dancing little figures. In another, Elsie appeared beside a winged gnome. Both girls and the strange figures occupied the same ordinary garden scene. If you were shown the prints without knowing their origin, where would you even start checking?

At first, these were family pictures. But in 1919 Elsie's mother brought them to people interested in fairies and spiritual phenomena. The images travelled beyond Cottingley and reached Edward Gardner, a prominent supporter of such claims.

Gardner took them to photographic specialists. Some examiners saw no convincing signs of double exposure or alteration in the darkroom. That was a finding about the photographic materials, not a finding that tiny living creatures had stood in the garden. In the public discussion, the difference became easy to lose.

Arthur Conan Doyle became interested. The man who had invented Sherlock Holmes was also deeply involved in spiritualism. He wanted to discuss the images in The Strand magazine. The pictures had gone from two girls' garden to a famous writer's desk, and the question was no longer just what the family believed.

Then came the test that ought to have settled it.

In 1920, Gardner supplied the cousins with new cameras and photographic plates bearing hidden marks. If someone swapped or tampered with the plates, the marks might help expose it. The girls returned with three more photographs. Now there were five, taken in two separate periods, and the second group had appeared under tighter controls.

But one person was asking a different question. A sixteen-year-old wrote to Gardner in 1920 suggesting the fairies might be cardboard cutouts. It was only a suggestion, not proof, and the available account does not show that Conan Doyle ever saw the letter. Yet it pointed toward something none of the marks on the photographic plates could measure.

The cousins had put the trick in front of the lens.

Elsie drew fairy figures, using illustrations as models. The girls cut the drawings out and secured them among plants with hatpins. A genuine exposure captured those little shapes next to real people, real grass and real water. No second exposure or altered negative was necessary.

That explains the two rounds of photographs. An examiner could inspect the original plate and find no darkroom alteration. Gardner could supply an entirely new camera and still get a picture of a staged scene. The extra controls tested the plates, while the decisive question concerned the objects being photographed.

Doyle published and supported the pictures. Not everyone agreed with him; people doubted them from the beginning. But the story was no longer a private joke. The photographs circulated as evidence, and the girls grew up while others continued arguing about what they had seen.

Decades later, photography writer Geoffrey Crawley revisited the images and their history. In 1983, Elsie wrote to him describing the paper figures and the method of staging them. The museum still preserves her letter, alongside cameras and other material connected with the photographs.

The two cousins did not agree on every picture. Elsie said all five were fabricated. Frances maintained that the fifth was genuine. Her insistence remains part of the record, but it is not independent proof of fairies.

The surprise is not that every expert was fooled. Some people had proposed the right physical explanation back in 1920. The surprise is how much attention went into checking the photograph while the objects inside the photograph escaped the same question.

Two girls made figures out of paper, and a real camera took their picture. The camera did its job. The interpretation was made by people.
