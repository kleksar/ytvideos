How could two girls photograph fairies, persuade famous adults to believe them, and keep the secret for more than sixty years? And what exactly did the experts check when they examined the pictures?

The story begins beside a stream in Yorkshire in 1917. Frances Griffiths and her older cousin Elsie Wright spent time near Cottingley Beck, at the bottom of the family garden. Frances had been talking about seeing fairies there. Adults did not take her explanation seriously.

The cousins borrowed a camera. One photograph showed Frances with several little figures dancing in front of her. A second showed Elsie beside something that looked like a winged gnome.

At first this was a family matter. The photographs had not been doctored after they were taken, which made them a surprisingly effective trick. The girls had placed the figures in the scene itself.

But the family photographs did not stay in the family.

In 1919 Elsie's mother took the images to a meeting of people interested in fairies and spiritual phenomena. The pictures travelled through that circle and reached Edward Gardner, a prominent believer in the supernatural. He asked photographers to examine them.

The scrutiny seemed impressive. There was a real photographic plate, not simply a drawing passed off as a photo. Experts looked for double exposure, darkroom manipulation and marks of editing. Some said they could find no evidence of that kind of trick. This was not the same as proving the figures were living creatures.

Then Arthur Conan Doyle took an interest. The creator of Sherlock Holmes was also a committed spiritualist. He wanted photographs for an article in The Strand magazine, and the Cottingley pictures appeared to offer them.

For a moment, consider the gap between those two roles. Doyle had invented a detective famous for noticing what others missed. Yet with these photographs he accepted the idea that figures on a photographic plate must have existed as fairies in front of the lens.

Gardner visited the cousins in 1920. They were given new cameras and photographic plates with hidden marks designed to expose interference. Under that extra scrutiny, they produced three more fairy photographs.

That development mattered. The first two images might be dismissed as a one-off family joke. Five photographs, taken at different times, appeared to tell a continuing story. The new pictures strengthened the confidence of believers, even though no outsider watched the actual scenes being arranged.

Doyle published the photographs and argued for their significance. Other observers remained doubtful. The debate was not a clean contest between science and superstition. A photographic negative could be genuine while the scene it recorded was arranged.

That was the trick. Elsie had drawn the figures, using fairy illustrations as models. The cousins cut them out, fastened them among plants with hatpins, and photographed them as though they were alive. A search for retouching would miss the crucial action. Nothing had to be added to the negative.

Decades passed. In the early 1980s, photographic writer Geoffrey Crawley revisited the case. In 1983 Elsie wrote to him about the construction of the figures. Both cousins acknowledged staged pictures.

They did not agree on every detail. Frances continued to insist that the fifth photograph was real; Elsie said all five were faked. That disagreement is part of the record, not a mystery solved by the documentary evidence.

The surviving cameras, prints and letters now make it possible to follow the photographs from an ordinary stream to a national argument. The answer is not that cameras in 1917 were incapable of telling the truth. They did tell the truth about the light that reached their plates.

The error was asking the camera to decide what the objects were.

Two cousins had made paper fairies, photographed them for real and watched adults turn that photograph into evidence of something else.
