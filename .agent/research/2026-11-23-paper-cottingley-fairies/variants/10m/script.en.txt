Why did a famous detective novelist accept photographs of fairies as evidence? Why did a fresh set of cameras make the claim look stronger? And how could a confession, more than sixty years later, leave one photograph in dispute?

The pictures began in a place that hardly looked like the centre of a national controversy: a small stream behind a family garden in Cottingley, Yorkshire.

In 1917, Frances Griffiths spent time there with her older cousin, Elsie Wright. Frances spoke about fairies near the water. Adults had no reason to accept that as an explanation for what she was doing by the stream. The cousins answered with a camera.

The first picture showed Frances in the grass with a group of dancing fairy figures. The next placed a small, winged gnome beside Elsie. They looked like photographs of people and creatures occupying the same patch of ground, rather than illustrations added around a portrait.

That distinction would become the centre of the case. For the girls had indeed taken photographs of objects positioned in front of the lens. What those objects were was another question.

At first their two plates were a private family curiosity. There was no national investigation, no famous novelist and no contest of experts. The girls had made images that seemed to support a story adults had refused to believe. What would turn this small joke into something much bigger was not an improvement in the pictures. It was the route the pictures took after they left the household.

In 1919, Elsie's mother brought them to people interested in fairies and the spiritual world. The Theosophical Society offered an audience willing to take such subjects seriously. The photographs circulated and eventually reached Edward Gardner, a prominent believer in their possible significance.

Gardner did not merely look at them and say he liked them. He approached people with photographic expertise. This made the images appear to have passed an objective test. The test, however, was narrower than the claim people wanted it to settle.

A photograph can be altered by exposing the same plate twice, manipulating a print or working on a negative. A technician can look for clues to some of those operations. But an unaltered photographic plate is not a sworn statement about what the things in front of the camera actually were.

A toy, a drawing, a costume, a scale model and a living creature can all leave an image on a plate. The camera records reflected light. It does not label the objects. That problem mattered here because the eventual answer was sitting among the plants.

Before it was known, a more famous name joined the story.

Arthur Conan Doyle was known around the world for creating Sherlock Holmes. He was also deeply interested in spiritualism and wanted to examine accounts of extraordinary phenomena. He was preparing material for The Strand magazine. The Cottingley photographs reached him as images that might support the existence of fairies.

It is tempting to turn this into a joke about a man who invented a clever detective but could not solve an obvious mystery. That misses the mechanism. Conan Doyle did not invent the photographs, and other people had already looked at them. To him, their apparent resistance to a familiar form of photographic trickery was significant.

The mistake was treating the absence of one kind of manipulation as positive evidence for a very different explanation. The photographs could be technically genuine and still be completely misleading.

In 1920, Gardner went to Cottingley to meet the cousins. Now the issue was not just what had happened three years earlier. Could the girls make new pictures, under a more controlled arrangement?

They received new cameras and photographic plates with hidden identifying marks. The intention was to make it easier to notice interference or substitution. A new set of pictures taken with these materials would appear harder to dismiss than an old family print.

The girls returned with three more photographs.

This should have been the moment when the story either collapsed or became compelling. There were now five images, separated in time, with a second group produced after people had started asking questions.

But there was an important limit to that arrangement. The marked plates were a check on the photographic materials. They did not give anyone a continuous view of what the girls put in front of the camera. No independent observer stood beside them throughout the staging of each frame.

The tests made it harder to substitute a plate. They did not make it harder to place a cutout among the flowers.

Some photographic examiners reported that they could not find conventional evidence of trickery. That finding was often treated as a broader endorsement. Others expressed doubts, and the public discussion never produced a universal technical verdict that fairies existed.

Conan Doyle nonetheless wrote about the pictures for The Strand. The case had escaped the family garden and become public. The writer who had made Holmes famous was now helping give a far less conventional claim a large audience.

The photographs were also visually persuasive for reasons that are easy to miss a century later. A little figure could be sharply outlined, close to a real person, and surrounded by the same grass. It needed no elaborate printing effects. The camera provided its normal perspective, exposure and texture, making separate cardboard shapes appear to share one physical scene.

The material for the figures was ordinary paper or cardboard. Elsie drew fairy shapes, taking inspiration from existing illustrations, including pictures associated with Princess Mary's Gift Book. The cousins cut out the figures and held them in place among the plants with hatpins.

There was no need to paint fairy wings onto a photograph. The wings were already there when the shutter opened.

That explains why a search for alterations to the negative could fail. There was no hidden double exposure to discover. The photograph was a faithful record of a staged arrangement.

It also explains why receiving new cameras and marked plates did not solve the problem. Those precautions concerned the camera, not the scene.

This is not to say that everyone believed the girls. Some observers doubted the pictures. Nor should the affair be remembered as a contest between two perfectly informed sides. The adults who endorsed the pictures had their own interests in spiritual questions. The girls had their own practical ability to make convincing models. The relationship between those people, rather than any unprecedented camera technology, kept the story moving.

The timing mattered too. These photographs reached a public living with the aftermath of the First World War, when claims about spiritual contact and unseen worlds had an audience. That context helps explain why such pictures would attract serious attention. It cannot prove what any particular reader thought on seeing them.

After the articles, the pictures became a part of the story of Conan Doyle's interest in spiritualism. He expanded his writing about fairies into a book in 1922. For readers who wanted evidence, the photographs were ready-made objects to point to. For skeptics, the same pictures raised the question of whether expert examination had started with the wrong question.

And the two girls grew older while the photographs stayed famous.

There was no immediate public confession ending the debate in 1920. Instead the case persisted for decades, which made the original joke much harder to separate from everything people had built on it.

In the early 1980s, photography writer Geoffrey Crawley examined the images and the surrounding claims in detail. The investigation helped bring the physical method into the open.

In 1983, Elsie wrote to Crawley describing how the figures had been made. The cousins acknowledged that they had staged photographs using cutouts. The surviving correspondence and drawings allow historians to trace the practical work that had been hidden behind claims of fairy appearances.

But the two women did not tell precisely the same story about every image.

Elsie said all five photographs were fabricated. Frances continued to maintain that the fifth, a picture without the same obvious posing beside a person, was genuine. Her belief was a claim, not a verified independent observation of living fairies. Acknowledging that disagreement is more accurate than turning the confession into a perfectly neat answer.

The broad explanation, however, no longer rests on guesswork. Paper cutouts were used for the staged photographs. The Science Museum Group preserves cameras connected to both phases, along with prints and correspondence. These are material objects, not merely a later legend that the story might have happened.

That is why the case remains useful even after its trick has been explained. A viewer could have held a genuine print, seen no evidence of retouching, and still mistaken a cardboard figure for a living creature.

The failure was not that the camera recorded false light. The failure was that people asked a photograph to make a judgment it could not make.

Two cousins needed drawings, hatpins and a borrowed camera. Adults added the investigation, the publications and the certainty. More than sixty years later, the explanation returned to the little stream where the lens had first seen the figures.

No darkroom alteration was necessary. The fairies had been made before the shutter clicked.
