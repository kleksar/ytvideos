How could a car pass America's pollution test, then release many times more pollution on the way home? And why did a factory recall fail to fix it?

In 2014, researchers at West Virginia University took three certified diesel cars onto real roads. They carried instruments that could measure exhaust while the cars were moving.

The results were striking. Two Volkswagens produced far more nitrogen oxides on the road than their certification standard allowed. Yet both cars had tested below that limit on the official laboratory cycle. The third car generally stayed within the standard on most routes, except for a difficult rural route.

Why were the two cars so different?

California's regulators asked Volkswagen to explain. The company offered technical explanations and began a voluntary software recall in December 2014. Fresh tests showed that the update had only a limited effect.

The regulators expanded their tests. Then Volkswagen needed approval to sell its next model year of diesel cars. That approval was held back while the regulators demanded an explanation.

In September 2015, Volkswagen acknowledged that its software recognized emissions testing. During a test, the cars used stronger pollution controls. In ordinary driving, those controls worked differently.

The laboratory instruments had not needed to report a false number. They were measuring cars that changed their behavior when tested.

Volkswagen later pleaded guilty in the United States. Among the safeguards in a 2017 civil agreement was a requirement for the company to conduct in-use testing with the same kind of portable equipment that had helped uncover its deception.

The road test that exposed the cheat became part of the remedy.
