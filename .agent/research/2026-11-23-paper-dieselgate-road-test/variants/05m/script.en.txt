Why would two diesel cars produce far more pollution outside a testing laboratory than inside it? And how did the investigators establish that the difference was deliberate?

In an American car dealership in the early 2010s, a customer could choose a diesel car for its fuel economy. The customer could not look at the exhaust and tell how much nitrogen oxide it contained. The government's certification system was supposed to establish that the car met the required emissions standards.

In 2014, a research group at West Virginia University was measuring three certified diesel cars for the International Council on Clean Transportation. The team wanted to know how the vehicles actually performed in everyday use.

Inside a laboratory, a car can drive on rollers without moving down a road. A prescribed sequence of speeds and stops makes the test reproducible. The university researchers used portable instruments as well. They could measure exhaust while a car traveled through a city, along a highway, or up a hill.

The three cars did not produce similar results.

On the different road routes in the study, one Volkswagen produced between fifteen and thirty-five times the nitrogen oxide standard used in its certification. The other produced between five and twenty times the standard. The third diesel car generally stayed within the limit on most routes, although it also exceeded the standard on a rural route with uphill and downhill sections.

Then came the finding that made this more than a story about difficult driving conditions. Both high-emitting cars had produced results below the limit on the official laboratory cycle.

Nitrogen oxides, often called NOx, are not carbon dioxide. They are pollutants that contribute to smog and harmful air pollution. A fuel-efficient car can still produce far too much of them.

The researchers had measured a discrepancy. They had not established its cause merely by measuring it. That was the next problem for the regulators.

California's Air Resources Board and the federal Environmental Protection Agency pursued the findings. Volkswagen offered technical explanations. There was an apparent way forward. If a fault was causing the excessive emissions, the company could correct it and the cars could be tested again.

In December 2014, Volkswagen began a voluntary recall to update the cars' software. The regulators now had a chance to test not just the original vehicles, but the manufacturer's proposed solution.

The update produced only limited improvement. The discrepancy persisted. California's regulators expanded their investigation, running additional tests and examining why the cars' diagnostic systems did not explain the excessive emissions.

Then the next model year became important. Volkswagen needed new certification to sell its 2016 diesel vehicles in the American market. The regulators withheld approval while demanding an adequate explanation for the older cars.

In September 2015, Volkswagen acknowledged the presence of software designed to recognize emissions testing. During a test, the cars operated with stronger emissions controls than under ordinary driving conditions.

The testing equipment had not simply been fed a false number. Its readings could be perfectly real at the time of the test. The vehicle itself was behaving differently. A laboratory technician could follow the correct procedure and still fail to discover the deception.

On September eighteenth, the EPA issued a notice of violation concerning certain two-liter diesels. The investigation later reached additional vehicles.

For the people who had bought the cars, the certification they had relied on had become a financial problem. A 2016 American settlement worth up to fourteen point seven billion dollars provided for consumer remedies, environmental mitigation and investment in zero-emission vehicles. Eligible owners could choose a buyback or, where available and approved, a modification under the settlement's terms.

The criminal case was separate. In 2017 Volkswagen pleaded guilty, and a federal court imposed a two point eight billion dollar criminal fine.

But the response was not only about money. A civil agreement also required Volkswagen to conduct in-use emissions testing with the same type of portable equipment that had helped uncover its deception.

Think back to the three cars traveling on ordinary roads. The university researchers had taken the measuring instruments out of the laboratory because they wanted to see what happened outside it. Now that kind of measurement was part of the manufacturer's obligations.

The road test that had exposed the cheat had become part of the remedy.
