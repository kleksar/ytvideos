Could an emissions test be performed correctly and still allow a manufacturer to sell cars that violated pollution standards? What would expose the difference between a laboratory result and the truth about a car? And why would the manufacturer's own attempt to repair the cars become an important part of the investigation?

Start with a buyer in the United States a few years before the scandal. A person who wanted a diesel car could compare its fuel economy with the gasoline cars on the lot. But another important characteristic was not visible through a showroom window or easy to check on a test drive.

One group of pollutants produced by combustion is called nitrogen oxides, or NOx. They contribute to smog and can harm human health. They are different from the carbon dioxide emissions people often discuss when comparing cars' climate impact. Good fuel economy alone does not establish that a car has low nitrogen oxide emissions.

That is one reason a buyer relies on the certification system. A manufacturer demonstrates compliance with the relevant standards before offering a new model for sale.

In a laboratory, a car can run on rollers. It goes through a prescribed sequence of speeds and stops while instruments measure what comes out of the exhaust. A standardized cycle allows engineers to compare vehicles under controlled conditions. Each measurement is meaningful because the conditions are known.

But what happens after the laboratory doors open?

The International Council on Clean Transportation commissioned researchers at West Virginia University to measure three certified diesel vehicles in actual use. Rather than fixing all their instruments in a laboratory, the researchers carried portable measuring equipment with the cars. They recorded emissions over routes that included city driving, highways and rural terrain. One car traveled nearly four thousand kilometers between California and Washington State.

Before selecting the cars for road testing, the researchers checked their electronic diagnostic systems for signs of engine or emissions-control problems. None showed relevant fault codes or other anomalies. They were testing relatively low-mileage vehicles, not selecting worn-out cars known to be defective.

The first important result was a contrast within that small group.

One of the two Volkswagens produced roughly fifteen to thirty-five times the nitrogen oxide standard used in its American certification across the road routes in the report. The other produced approximately five to twenty times the standard. These figures are results from particular tests, not a claim that every affected car emitted the same amount on every trip.

The third diesel car generally produced results at or below the standard on most of the routes. It was not perfect: on a rural route with uphill and downhill sections, it exceeded the standard substantially.

That third vehicle matters. Conditions on an actual road are different from those on a laboratory cycle. Traffic, gradients and engine load can all affect emissions. An elevated reading on one route does not automatically prove that a manufacturer has installed illegal software. If all three cars had shown similar results, the discrepancy might have called the testing conditions or the standard laboratory cycle into question in a different way.

But here two cars produced unusually high road readings while a third generally did not. There was a reason to investigate the behavior of those two cars in particular.

The researchers also had laboratory results. Two of the vehicles underwent testing at California's certification facility in El Monte. Both of the high-emitting cars had nitrogen oxide results below the applicable limit on the prescribed certification cycle.

The difference could not simply be dismissed as a pair of cars that were always unable to control their emissions. Under a particular set of conditions they had demonstrated that they could do so.

At that stage, however, the university team had not proved a criminal conspiracy. A component could have malfunctioned. A control system might have responded incorrectly to some conditions. The team had established an important discrepancy; explaining the discrepancy was a separate job.

California's Air Resources Board, or CARB, and the federal Environmental Protection Agency pursued the findings.

There was an earlier part of the story that would only become clearer through the investigation. In the account published by the United States Department of Justice in 2017, Volkswagen's work on a new diesel engine for the American market began in 2006. The engineering project had to address stricter nitrogen oxide requirements and produce a vehicle that customers would want to buy. The development effort instead led to software that changed how the engine operated when it recognized official emissions testing.

The distinction between changing the engine's behavior and falsifying an instrument's display is crucial. During a test, an instrument could accurately measure the exhaust produced at that moment. The problem was that the manufacturer presented those readings as evidence of compliance while its vehicles operated with different pollution controls in other circumstances.

That explanation had not yet been established when California's regulators first pursued the university findings. Volkswagen offered a series of technical explanations for the excessive emissions.

Those explanations created an opportunity to test a proposed solution. If the excessive pollution resulted from an ordinary fault, a suitable fix should improve the behavior of the vehicles under the conditions where the fault occurred.

In December 2014, Volkswagen began a voluntary recall to update the software in affected cars. The regulators could now examine how the vehicles behaved after the company's intervention. The recall was not just an administrative event. It was another chance to find out whether the proposed explanation could account for the observed measurements.

CARB's follow-up tests showed limited improvement. The discrepancy had not disappeared. Regulators broadened their testing and examined why the cars' diagnostic systems failed to explain the excessive emissions.

The initial measurements had shown a problem with the cars. This new round of testing showed that the company's proposed solution did not adequately account for it.

Then the ordinary annual process of certifying new vehicles gave the regulators leverage. Volkswagen wanted to sell new diesel models for the 2016 model year. It needed the regulators' approval. CARB and the EPA held that approval back while demanding an explanation for the previous findings.

In September 2015, Volkswagen acknowledged that its vehicles contained software designed to recognize emissions testing. During those tests, the cars used stronger emissions controls. Under ordinary driving conditions, the controls operated differently.

The portable equipment had not discovered a broken laboratory instrument. It had measured cars outside the conditions in which they had been designed to produce their favorable certification results.

On September eighteenth, the EPA issued a notice of violation concerning certain two-liter diesel vehicles. The agency said that affected cars could emit nitrogen oxides at up to forty times the standard during normal operation. That is an upper figure, not a claim that every car always produced forty times the limit. Subsequent investigations covered additional vehicles and engine types.

For owners, there was a problem beyond the harm to air quality. A diesel car might still start, accelerate and appear to work perfectly well. Its owner had no reason to have installed equipment to verify its exhaust before buying it. Yet the owner's decision had been based on representations that could no longer be trusted.

In 2016, a partial American settlement worth up to fourteen point seven billion dollars established several remedies. Eligible owners of affected two-liter vehicles could sell their cars back under the terms of the settlement. If a modification was approved by regulators, eligible owners could instead have the car modified and receive compensation. The agreement also provided funds to mitigate excess pollution and invest in zero-emission vehicle infrastructure and initiatives.

These were civil remedies, not a single criminal fine. The criminal case proceeded separately. In 2017 Volkswagen pleaded guilty to three criminal counts. A federal court imposed a criminal fine of two point eight billion dollars.

It would be easy to end the story there, with the largest dollar figures on screen. But one of the less spectacular provisions of a 2017 civil agreement goes right back to the original experiment.

Volkswagen was required to perform in-use emissions testing with the same kind of portable measuring system that had helped reveal its deception.

A laboratory cycle is valuable because it produces comparable measurements under known conditions. An additional way to check a vehicle on the road is valuable for another reason: it tests whether those controlled results tell us enough about the car in ordinary use.

The university researchers had taken their instruments onto the road to investigate that question. Their measurements exposed a discrepancy, and the regulators' investigation established why it existed. The same type of measurement then became part of the manufacturer's obligations.

The laboratory instruments had not needed to lie. What mattered was the difference between the reality the cars presented during a test and the reality the portable instruments could measure after they left.

That was the small change in the experiment that made the large deception visible.
