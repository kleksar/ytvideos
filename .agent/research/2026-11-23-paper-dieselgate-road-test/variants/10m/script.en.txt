Could an emissions test be performed correctly and still allow a manufacturer to sell cars that violated pollution standards? What would expose the difference between a laboratory result and the truth about a car? And why would the manufacturer's own attempt to repair the cars become an important part of the investigation?

A buyer in an American dealership could compare fuel economy, prices and the way a car felt on a test drive. But there was no easy way to check what came out of the exhaust. Nitrogen oxides, or NOx, are pollutants that contribute to smog and can damage health. Even a car that uses very little fuel can produce a lot of them.

Before a model could go on sale, its manufacturer had to demonstrate compliance with emissions standards. A laboratory could put the car on rollers and measure its exhaust through a prescribed sequence of speeds and stops. That gave regulators repeatable results. In 2014 a university team decided to measure what happened beyond the rollers.

The International Council on Clean Transportation commissioned researchers at West Virginia University to measure three certified diesel vehicles in actual use. Rather than fixing all their instruments in a laboratory, the researchers carried portable measuring equipment with the cars. They recorded emissions over routes that included city driving, highways and rural terrain. One car traveled nearly four thousand kilometers between California and Washington State.

Before selecting the cars for road testing, the researchers checked their electronic diagnostic systems for signs of engine or emissions-control problems. None showed relevant fault codes or other anomalies. They were testing relatively low-mileage vehicles, not selecting worn-out cars known to be defective.

The first important result was a contrast within that small group.

One of the two Volkswagens produced roughly fifteen to thirty-five times the nitrogen oxide standard used in its American certification across the road routes in the report. The other produced approximately five to twenty times the standard. These figures are results from particular tests, not a claim that every affected car emitted the same amount on every trip.

The third diesel car generally produced results at or below the standard on most of the routes. It was not perfect: on a rural route with uphill and downhill sections, it exceeded the standard substantially.

The third car gave the investigators a useful comparison. Hills and traffic can change emissions, and the road routes were not identical to a certification test. A high reading alone could not establish illegal software. Yet the difference between these three cars was difficult to ignore.

The researchers also had laboratory results. Two of the vehicles underwent testing at California's certification facility in El Monte. Both of the high-emitting cars had nitrogen oxide results below the applicable limit on the prescribed certification cycle.

The difference could not simply be dismissed as a pair of cars that were always unable to control their emissions. Under a particular set of conditions they had demonstrated that they could do so.

At that stage, however, the university team had not proved a criminal conspiracy. A component could have malfunctioned. A control system might have responded incorrectly to some conditions. The team had established an important discrepancy; explaining the discrepancy was a separate job.

California's Air Resources Board, or CARB, and the federal Environmental Protection Agency pursued the findings.

Volkswagen offered technical explanations for the high readings. That gave the investigators a way to challenge the company's account. They could measure the same problem again after a repair.

In December 2014 Volkswagen began a voluntary software recall. The California regulators took the updated vehicles back into the laboratory and onto the road. The update helped only a little. The cars still produced excessive emissions, and the testing team could not find an adequate explanation in the company's proposed causes or the onboard diagnostics.

The problem now involved more than the original cars: a repair offered by the manufacturer had not resolved the discrepancy. The regulators expanded their investigation.

Then the ordinary annual process of certifying new vehicles gave the regulators leverage. Volkswagen wanted to sell new diesel models for the 2016 model year. It needed the regulators' approval. CARB and the EPA held that approval back while demanding an explanation for the previous findings.

In September 2015, Volkswagen acknowledged that its vehicles contained software designed to recognize emissions testing. During those tests, the cars used stronger emissions controls. Under ordinary driving conditions, the controls operated differently.

The investigators could now explain why the laboratory numbers had looked convincing. Those readings did not have to be falsified: the software made the car behave differently when a test was taking place.

The Justice Department later described how the scheme originated during development of a diesel engine for the American market, a project that began in 2006. The company faced stricter nitrogen oxide requirements while trying to make a car attractive to buyers. The software was used to circumvent the requirements, and the scheme was concealed from regulators. The university's road measurements had exposed a discrepancy; the subsequent investigation established deliberate conduct.

On September eighteenth, the EPA issued a notice of violation concerning certain two-liter diesel vehicles. The agency said that affected cars could emit nitrogen oxides at up to forty times the standard during normal operation. That is an upper figure, not a claim that every car always produced forty times the limit. Subsequent investigations covered additional vehicles and engine types.

For owners, the problem was now practical. A car could still start and accelerate normally. But what would it take to bring it into compliance?

A week after the public notice, the EPA announced additional checks for software designed to circumvent emissions testing. Volkswagen was also required to propose a new repair for the affected two-liter cars.

In November 2015 the company submitted its plan. On January twelfth, 2016, California rejected it. The proposed repairs lacked sufficient technical detail, and the regulators said the company had not adequately addressed the effects on emissions, performance and safety.

So the first software update had failed to resolve the measurements, and even after the admission there was still no acceptable repair plan for these vehicles.

The American settlement announced in June 2016, worth up to fourteen point seven billion dollars, gave eligible owners an alternative. They could sell their cars back under the agreement, or receive an approved modification and compensation if one became available. Money was also allocated for environmental mitigation and zero-emission investments.

Then the distinction between a proposed repair and an approved one became visible in an unexpected place: the transmission.

In May 2017 the regulators approved a modification for certain two-liter diesel Passats from the 2012 through 2014 model years with automatic transmissions. California estimated that about ten thousand six hundred cars in the state were covered. Around five hundred and ninety manual-transmission cars in the same group were left out because Volkswagen had not demonstrated the necessary compliance.

Two customers could own cars from the same model years and still face different repair options. In September the regulators rejected the proposed modification for those manual Passats. This did not mean that every Volkswagen with a manual gearbox was impossible to repair. It meant that this particular proposal had not earned approval.

The distinction mattered because the manufacturer could no longer simply tell owners that an update had fixed the problem. It needed to demonstrate an acceptable result for the vehicles in question.

The criminal case was separate from the consumer agreement. In 2017 Volkswagen pleaded guilty and received a two point eight billion dollar criminal fine.

The most revealing consequence, though, was a requirement in a separate 2017 civil agreement. Volkswagen itself would have to measure emissions from cars in use with the same kind of portable equipment that had helped expose the deception.

Picture the original experiment: a car leaves the rollers, but the instrument goes with it. Years later, the manufacturer had an obligation to make that kind of measurement itself. The road had become part of the remedy.
