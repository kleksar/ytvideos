The magazine had a world-changing discovery. The paper inside its new notebooks was about to prove otherwise.

In April 1983, Stern, a major German news magazine, presented what it called Adolf Hitler's private diaries. The black notebooks were supposed to cover the years before and during the Second World War. Stern had paid millions of German marks for them.

There was a dramatic origin story. Reporter Gerd Heidemann said the books had been recovered from a crashed aircraft at the end of the war, then smuggled out of East Germany.

The supposed supplier was a man calling himself Konrad Fischer. His real name was Konrad Kujau. He sold Nazi memorabilia and had been making forged documents.

As more diaries arrived, the number of volumes grew. So did the money. The magazine pressed ahead with an international announcement.

On April 25, Heidemann showed the notebooks to reporters in Hamburg. Stern began publishing extracts. But doubts about the historical details were already growing.

Then investigators examined the material. The paper and other physical clues did not belong to the wartime period the diaries claimed to describe. The Federal Archives announced on May 6 that they were fakes.

Kujau had not uncovered Hitler's private writing. He had created it.

Both Kujau and Heidemann were later convicted. The magazine's reputation took the damage, and the apparent sensation became a warning about what happens when an enormous story moves faster than verification.

There was a darker problem, too. Decades later, researchers reading the forged text found passages falsely presenting Hitler as unaware of, or opposed to, the persecution and murder of Jews. They were not harmless private anecdotes. They were a distortion of history.

A newsroom had been ready to rewrite the past. A physical examination of ordinary notebooks finally stopped it.
