A newsroom in Hamburg was preparing to rewrite the history of Nazi Germany using sixty black notebooks. Nobody had yet established that the notebooks were real.

In 1983, the magazine Stern was buying what it believed were Hitler's private diaries. Its reporter, Gerd Heidemann, repeated a supplier's story: the books had survived a plane crash at the end of the war and been smuggled out of East Germany.

The supplier called himself Konrad Fischer. His real name was Konrad Kujau, a dealer who had made forged Nazi-era documents.

The offer grew from roughly twenty-seven notebooks to more than sixty. The price climbed into the millions. But another set of supposed Nazi documents, submitted for testing, had already raised an alarm. Investigators found postwar paper in some of those samples, and Heidemann learned the result before publication. Those samples were not the diaries. They were a reason to examine the diaries properly.

On April 25, Stern displayed its collection to the world press. Soon after, experts studied the notebooks themselves. Their materials and content didn't fit the claimed dates. On May 6, the German Federal Archives announced the diaries were fakes.

Kujau hadn't rescued them. He had written them.

That ended the claim of a spectacular discovery. But it didn't end the story of what was written.

In 2023, broadcaster NDR made the forged diaries searchable and worked with historians to examine them. They found repeated attempts to portray Hitler as unaware of, or opposed to, the Nazi persecution and murder of Jews. That portrayal is false. Hitler led the Nazi regime responsible for the Holocaust.
