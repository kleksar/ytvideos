A music CD from a store could hide software on your Windows computer.

In 2005, Sony BMG wanted to stop people copying certain albums. The discs still played music. But on Windows computers with autorun enabled, they could also start a copy-protection installer.

Security researcher Mark Russinovich found something strange while testing a tool that detects hidden software. Files and settings on his own computer were disappearing from ordinary listings. He traced them to the copy protection on a Sony BMG CD.

This wasn't merely a player that limited copying. One system, called XCP, used a rootkit: software that hid certain files and processes from Windows tools. Its hiding trick was broad enough for unrelated malicious programs to exploit.

Sony BMG sold another system too, called MediaMax. Researchers found that some of its components could be installed before a customer accepted a license agreement. Saying no did not necessarily keep them off the computer.

Then came the supposed solution. Sony offered ways to remove the software, but researchers found that the removal tools introduced security holes of their own.

The company recalled the affected XCP discs. In a 2007 settlement with the US Federal Trade Commission, it agreed to clearer disclosures, consent rules, exchanges, and remedies for certain customers.

So the scandal wasn't simply that a company tried to stop copying. It placed risky code on customers' machines, made that code hard to see, and even struggled to remove it safely.
