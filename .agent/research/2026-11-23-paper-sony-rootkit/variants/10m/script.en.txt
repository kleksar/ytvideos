How could a music CD hide software on a computer? Why did a refusal on a license screen sometimes fail to stop installation? And how could an official removal tool become a new security problem?

In 2005, buying an album often meant taking home a plastic case and a silver disc. You put the CD into a Windows computer, played the songs, perhaps tried to move them to a portable player. A customer was buying music, not shopping for computer software.

Sony BMG was selling some albums with digital rights management, or DRM. The systems tried to restrict copying and certain forms of playback. On Windows machines with autorun enabled, inserting one of those discs could launch an installer. The music and the program reached the computer together.

This was not one program on every Sony disc. Researchers later examined two separate families, XCP and MediaMax, made by different vendors. Their failures were different. That difference is the key to what happened next.

The first discovery began on a security researcher's own computer. In late October 2005, Mark Russinovich was testing RootkitRevealer, a tool he had helped develop. It compared ordinary Windows listings with another view of what was on the system.

The two views did not agree. Files and registry entries were present but absent from the usual lists. Russinovich traced the discrepancy to a Sony BMG CD he had purchased: Get Right With the Man, by Van Zant. He published his findings on October 31.

The protection package was XCP, developed by First 4 Internet. The disc had presented a license agreement that Russinovich accepted. That matters: the scandal was not that XCP had never displayed any agreement. It was that the agreement did not plainly disclose what its hidden components would do.

XCP used a driver that altered what Windows reported when programs asked for lists of files and processes. A particular name prefix made entries disappear from ordinary view. This was rootkit-style concealment, not merely a refusal to copy a song.

And the rule did not check whether the hidden item belonged to XCP. Other software could adopt the same prefix and vanish from those listings. A system installed to protect music could make unrelated malicious software harder to see. Researchers distinguished that capability from claiming that every purchaser had been infected.

For the customer, even the first step of finding the protection had become harder. But the deeper problem appeared when investigators examined a different family of discs.

MediaMax, developed by SunnComm, did not rely on the same concealment technique. Researchers showed that components of some MediaMax versions could reach a Windows computer before the person had accepted the license agreement.

The order is important. A screen asking for permission appeared, but the computer could already have received software. Clicking Decline did not necessarily undo that earlier step. The customer had made a choice on the screen; the machine did not always return to its prior state.

Researchers also documented transmission of information about use of the protected music. The Federal Trade Commission later raised questions about listening information and marketing. That was a privacy concern separate from XCP's hiding trick. Neither finding means every disc used the same methods.

By then the story had changed. A CD designed to restrict copying had created a question about control of the computer itself. And finding the unwanted software did not mean the owner could remove it safely.

A normal uninstaller can usually be downloaded and run. The early XCP procedure, documented by Princeton researchers, was something else. First came an online form asking for personal details. Then a wait of several days for an email. Then another form and an installed component. Then another wait and a final link.

Even after that, the first removal tool was tied to one computer and worked only for a limited time. If XCP was installed again, for example after reinserting a protected CD, the same tool would not necessarily remove it. The customer could have to request a new one and repeat the sequence.

This is the first removal reversal: identifying the software did not restore control. The official exit depended on repeated requests and on code supplied by the companies whose software the customer was trying to remove.

Public complaints led to less restricted removal tools for XCP and MediaMax. But both vendors chose browser-based ActiveX components to deliver the new uninstallers. Those components introduced a second, more serious reversal.

For MediaMax, researchers found that the removal component could remain installed after the music protection was gone. It accepted instructions about where to obtain code without adequately checking the destination or the downloaded content. A malicious website could exploit that behavior to run code on the computer.

The XCP removal component had a comparable flaw. Researchers demonstrated a proof-of-concept using a specially prepared download. This was a demonstrated vulnerability, not evidence that every consumer who used an uninstaller had been attacked.

Think about the sequence without any jargon. A purchased disc brought in software. The user found a way to remove it. The removal page installed a browser control. The unwanted music protection could disappear, while a new dangerous component stayed behind.

That is why the problem was not fixed merely by offering an Uninstall button. The person trying to clean up the first installation could be left with a new exposure, even after apparently finishing the repair.

The findings attracted responses beyond the original researchers. Microsoft said its security software would detect and remove XCP. Consumer advocates pressed for discs to be exchanged and for help to remove the code safely.

In November 2005, Sony BMG stopped producing affected XCP discs and announced a recall. A recall could take discs out of shops; it could not reach into every Windows computer and remove software already there. That gap between recalling a physical product and repairing its digital effects became part of the remedy.

US state authorities announced a settlement in December 2006. In January 2007 the Federal Trade Commission announced a separate proposed consent settlement. Those were different proceedings, not one criminal conviction.

The FTC said customers had not received adequate notice of playback restrictions and security risks, and that uninstalling the software was unreasonably difficult. The proposal required clearer disclosures and consent, effective removal methods, exchanges of covered discs and specified reimbursement for eligible repair costs. It also addressed information collected about listening habits.

One detail of that remedy shows how long the consequences could outlast a purchase. The proposed federal agreement called for repair patches and removal tools to remain available for two years. Customers needed help with computers, not just replacement albums. A settlement was not an admission by Sony BMG of every alleged violation.

The whole episode started with an effort to control copies of music. Researchers found that determined users could still get around the protection, while ordinary buyers inherited extra software and risk. The protection's limits and its side effects were badly out of balance.

The strongest image is not a hacker at a keyboard. It is a familiar CD case, followed by two different unwanted installations, then a removal page that could leave another problem behind. The buyer had paid for the music; getting the computer back was suddenly part of the price.
