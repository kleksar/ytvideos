## Three competitive applications

### A. Van Meegeren (chosen)
- Audience value: an accused seller must prove he himself manufactured what experts called a Dutch masterpiece. Concrete internal reversal: a celebrated attribution makes the owner's legal predicament worse.
- Hook opening proposed: “How could a painter escape an accusation of selling Dutch heritage to the Nazis by confessing to an art fraud?” First visual: two paper gallery labels on the same painting, one ‘Vermeer’, one ‘Van Meegeren’.
- Development: Göring acquisition → trace to artist → confession not believed → Rotterdam museum previously celebrated another forgery → how old support/resin worked → live painting + chemical evidence → charge changes → museum keeps the painting.
- Payoff: the fraud conviction and authentic museum relabeling; story about authority and evidence, not an invented last-second pardon.
- Strong objection: TED-Ed told essentially this legal-reversal story already in an accessible 5m animated video; fresh English demand is **UNVERIFIED**. Avoid any claimed new exclusive facts, originality, or sanitized heroism.

### B. Operation Bernhard (close second)
- First line: “A British bank found a pound note whose number had already been paid out.” First paper image: identical printed serial number beside a leather ledger.
- Stakes: prisoners forced to produce forged money; Bank of England changed notes, fake notes reportedly dumped in a lake; liberation by US troops on 6 May 1945.
- Development/payoff: failed numbering scheme → 1943 detection in Morocco → British withdrawal of notes above £5 → prisoners moved and liberated → banknote-design aftermath. Sources directly from issuing bank. 
- Strong objection: extremely serious prisoner experience needs sensitivity and primary testimony, not a cheerful visual prank; YouTube result spread is not strong in checked examples (Historic Mysteries 2025 1,128 views; See U in History Short 2022 6,920), no matching creator baseline. Still a meaningful competitor, not rejected permanently.

### C. HMS Dreadnought visitors hoax (third)
- First line: “The Navy welcomed foreign princes aboard its flagship. One of them was a writer in a fake beard.”
- Development: false telegram → disguises and assumed royal status → guards and officers receive party → fear rain/beards reveal them → press and parliamentary controversy.
- Payoff: Navy ceremonial access exposed as fallible; a dated Cole letter corroborates visit/behavior, reported in 2012; US Naval Institute contextualizes event.
- Strong objection: racial disguise/colonial stereotypes central to original prank, cannot replicate casually; under one paragraph the reversal is already known, ten-minute script risks repetition.

Selection judgment: A has a concrete legal consequence *after* the trick, layered proof through painting and chemistry, and an enduring tangible object. B is arguably greater historical significance and may beat A for Nuviko, but demands a different humane tone and more prisoner testimony. C is visually striking but shorter. A's crowded public retellings are a genuine drawback; this is a tentative editorial selection, **not** verified Paper Detour demand.
