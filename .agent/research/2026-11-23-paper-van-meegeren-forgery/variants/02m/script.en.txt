The police thought a Dutch painter had sold a national treasure to one of Hitler’s most powerful men. His defense was strange: he said the painting was worthless as a Vermeer—because he had painted it himself.

In 1945, investigators traced a painting in Hermann Göring’s collection back to Han van Meegeren. It had been sold as a newly discovered work by Johannes Vermeer, a celebrated Dutch artist. Selling a genuine masterpiece to the occupying Nazis could bring a much more serious charge than cheating an art buyer.

So Van Meegeren admitted the fraud. Investigators didn't simply take his word for it. And there was a problem bigger than the painting in Göring's collection.

Years earlier, a Rotterdam museum had proudly acquired another supposed Vermeer, The Supper at Emmaus. Experts had praised it as an important discovery. Van Meegeren now claimed that was his work too.

He had invented a scene that looked like a missing part of Vermeer's career. He used old materials and a modern resin to make fresh paint behave like aged paint. An expert's confidence had done the rest.

To demonstrate his claim, Van Meegeren painted another picture in a Vermeer-like style while under observation. Investigators also examined the disputed works and found evidence of modern materials.

The charge involving the sale of Dutch heritage to the enemy was dropped. In 1947, he was convicted of forgery and fraud and sentenced to a year in prison. He died before serving that sentence.

And the Rotterdam museum? It still holds The Supper at Emmaus, no longer as a Vermeer, but as a painting by the forger who exposed himself.

The label tells the truth now. Imagine what it said when the museum first bought the painting.
