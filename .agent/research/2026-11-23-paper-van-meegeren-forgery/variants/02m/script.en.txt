In the late 1930s, a museum in Rotterdam acquired The Supper at Emmaus as a lost painting by Johannes Vermeer. A respected expert supported the discovery. The museum displayed it as a masterpiece.

Then, in 1945, investigators found another supposed Vermeer in the collection of Nazi leader Hermann Göring. They traced its sale to a Dutch painter named Han van Meegeren.

They suspected him of helping transfer valuable Dutch art to the occupying enemy. His defense made everything stranger: the painting was a forgery, he said, and he had painted it himself. He also claimed the celebrated Rotterdam picture was his.

Now he faced an unusual problem. Proving he was a fraud could undermine the more serious accusation. But why should investigators believe a confession that conveniently helped the suspect?

Van Meegeren had painted a religious scene that seemed to fit a missing part of Vermeer's career. He worked with old materials and added a modern resin to harden fresh paint. The surface could pass checks that an ordinary new painting might fail.

Under observation, he painted another Vermeer-like religious picture. That demonstrated his ability, but it did not prove he had made the earlier works.

The stronger evidence came from the paintings themselves. Specialists detected modern material in their paint. A seventeenth-century artist could not have used a resin invented centuries later.

The allegation about selling genuine Dutch heritage to the enemy fell away. In 1947, Van Meegeren was convicted of forgery and fraud and sentenced to a year in prison. He died before serving it.

Rotterdam kept its painting. Today the museum lists Van Meegeren as its creator, not Vermeer. The canvas is the same; the attribution isn't.

Before that name was corrected, though, the museum had been telling a very different story.
