How could a painter escape an accusation of selling Dutch heritage to the Nazis by confessing to an art fraud? And why would a museum resist believing the confession?

When the Second World War ended, Allied investigators found a painting in Hermann Göring's art collection. It had been sold as a lost work by Johannes Vermeer, a seventeenth-century Dutch painter whose surviving pictures were treasured. The trail led back to an artist named Han van Meegeren.

If the painting really was a Vermeer, the sale raised a serious question: had Van Meegeren helped transfer Dutch cultural property to one of the Nazi regime's most powerful men?

His answer was the opposite of what investigators expected. The painting, he said, was not a Vermeer at all. It was his own forgery.

That answer could remove the basis for the more serious accusation. But it also meant admitting to a different crime. And the authorities had no reason to accept such a convenient confession without proof.

The problem went far beyond Göring's collection.

Before the war, Rotterdam's Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen had bought a painting called The Supper at Emmaus. It showed figures gathered around a table, in the kind of religious subject associated with an early phase of Vermeer's career. The respected specialist Abraham Bredius had endorsed it. The museum believed it had acquired an extraordinary discovery.

Van Meegeren now said he had painted that supposed masterpiece as well.

He had not copied a known Vermeer. He had made up a new painting and offered viewers something they wanted to believe existed: a previously unknown work that seemed to explain a gap in the artist's development. A claim like that was hard to test by comparing it with an exact original, because there was no original to compare.

He also understood a more practical obstacle. Fresh oil paint doesn't behave like paint that has been drying for hundreds of years. Contemporary examinations sometimes used alcohol or a heated needle to test the surface. Recently applied paint could react in ways old paint would not.

Van Meegeren mixed a synthetic resin, Bakelite, into his paint and hardened the surface. He also worked with old materials to make the whole object seem older. The illusion wasn't simply in the picture's subject. It was in the physical surface that an examiner could touch.

That was his advantage in the 1930s. It would eventually become evidence against him.

At first, his confession created another problem for the museum. A celebrated work in its collection would have to be relabelled as a modern fake. The museum's own history records that this picture was once purchased as a Vermeer and was recognised as a forgery only after the war.

Investigators asked for more than an explanation of old techniques. Under observation in 1945, Van Meegeren painted another religious scene in a manner meant to resemble Vermeer. It was a demonstration of what he could make, not the same picture he had sold.

A separate technical examination supplied a different kind of proof. Specialists identified modern material in the disputed paintings. Resin invented centuries after Vermeer's lifetime could not have been on a genuine seventeenth-century canvas as part of its original paint.

The conclusions changed the case. The allegation concerning the transfer of genuine Dutch heritage to the enemy was dropped. Van Meegeren was instead prosecuted for forgery and fraud. In 1947, a court sentenced him to one year in prison. He died that December before serving it.

There is a tempting ending to this story: a clever patriot fools Göring and escapes. But the documents don't support turning Van Meegeren into a straightforward wartime hero. He profited from deception, and his dealings during the occupation were more complicated than that legend allows.

The actual ending is stranger. The museum did not throw its expensive mistake away. It still owns The Supper at Emmaus. The painting has become an example of the very mistake its first label concealed.

The canvas did not change when the attribution changed. What changed was the story people were willing to believe about the person who painted it.
