What happens when a museum pays for a masterpiece that never existed? How can a painting fool both an art historian and a physical examination? And why, after the war, did its maker have to convince the police that he was a forger?

The picture is called The Supper at Emmaus. Christ sits at a table breaking bread, surrounded by other figures. It looks like a religious painting from the seventeenth century. In the late 1930s, Rotterdam's Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen acquired it as a major work by Johannes Vermeer. The celebrated expert Abraham Bredius had supported that attribution. For a museum collecting Dutch masters, it was a remarkable acquisition.

But the picture was not by Vermeer. And the person who knew that most certainly did not correct the museum.

He was Han van Meegeren, a Dutch painter who had learned that inventing a convincing past could be more profitable than selling a painting under his own name. The drama that exposed him did not begin inside the museum. It began after the Second World War, in a collection belonging to Hermann Göring.

Allied investigators were tracing works found among art held by one of the Nazi regime's most powerful men. One painting had been sold to Göring as a newly discovered Vermeer: Christ and the Adulteress. The trail led back through dealers to Van Meegeren. If it were genuine, the sale suggested that precious Dutch cultural heritage had been transferred to the occupying enemy.

Van Meegeren offered a defense that sounded even less credible than the sale itself. He admitted that the Vermeer was fake. He said he had painted it. Then he made the claim much larger: other works celebrated as masterpieces, including Rotterdam's Supper at Emmaus, were his creations too.

He needed investigators to believe his guilt on one set of charges to escape a more serious allegation. They had reason to be suspicious. A confession that conveniently turns a potentially grave offense into art fraud is not proof by itself.

To understand how he could make such a claim, go back to the problem art experts faced before the war.

Vermeer had left a small, incomplete-looking body of known work. His early style did not always resemble the quieter domestic scenes for which he is now famous. This made the idea of a missing religious painting plausible to at least some experts. Van Meegeren didn't need to reproduce a known picture and invite a side-by-side comparison. He could make a new scene and allow the supposed discovery to fit a theory about Vermeer's development.

When Bredius accepted The Supper at Emmaus, the attribution encouraged others to see it as a masterpiece too. The museum bought it. The painting was displayed and discussed under the famous name. Scholars had already invested not only money but also their reputations in the decision.

It wasn't enough, however, to paint something that looked old. A real seventeenth-century picture would have physical history: support, pigments, hardened oil and cracks. An ordinary modern painting might fail simple tests of its surface.

At the time, some examiners tested paint with alcohol or a heated needle. Newly dried oil paint could be softened or marked. Van Meegeren sought to make the surface respond like paint that had aged for centuries. He worked over old canvases, chose pigments associated with the period and mixed a modern resin into the paint. He also used heat to harden the result and imitate age cracks.

There is an irony here. He was deliberately introducing a modern chemical into his paint to make it pass for old paint. In a limited examination, the surface might appear reassuringly hard. Under later chemical scrutiny, that same resin would betray the picture.

The period materials did not make the image authentic either. An old canvas is still just an old canvas. A new painting on top of it remains new. And a pigment that resembles seventeenth-century colour is no guarantee of who applied it.

Van Meegeren's particular invention was an image people were ready to accept, attached to an object that behaved enough like an antique. The forger did not have to deceive every possible future scientific test. He had to deceive the tests and assumptions available at the time of sale.

Years passed. Then came the German occupation, the sale associated with Göring and the end of the war. When Van Meegeren confessed, the Rotterdam painting became a problem as large as the one in the Nazi collection. If he was telling the truth, the museum's celebrated discovery was an expensive modern forgery.

Some experts resisted the idea, even after the confession. That reaction matters: the difference between a genuine Vermeer and a fake wasn't simply a label on a card. For years, scholars had praised the picture's composition, colour and spiritual character. If they had been wrong about the painter, they would have to reconsider all that praise as well.

Van Meegeren therefore had to provide evidence beyond his own story. Investigators found material associated with his work. In 1945 he painted a new religious picture in a Vermeer-like manner while witnesses observed. The demonstration showed he could create a convincing imitation. It did not, by itself, prove that every painting he named had been made by him.

The stronger answer came from the objects themselves.

A specialist commission included experts from the Netherlands, Belgium and Britain. They examined the disputed paintings and identified a modern binding material in the paint. The substance was based on phenol-formaldehyde resin, introduced centuries after Vermeer's death. Chemical tests could identify its presence throughout the questioned works. This was not a matter of whether the figures looked graceful or the colours looked right. An original seventeenth-century painting could not have been made with that modern material.

Their examination also supported a broader conclusion: a painting could be visually persuasive and still contain anachronistic evidence in its physical layers.

The change in opinion was striking. Before Van Meegeren's exposure, critics had described The Supper at Emmaus as one of Vermeer's finest paintings. Afterward, observers began identifying weaknesses in the same composition and faces. The paint hadn't rearranged itself. A different attribution had changed the way people looked.

In 1947, Van Meegeren went to court on forgery and fraud charges. The accusation based on the sale of authentic cultural heritage to the enemy had fallen away. He was sentenced to a year in prison, but died that December without serving the sentence.

This was not a simple story of a patriotic trickster humiliating a Nazi. Van Meegeren profited enormously from his fakes, and historical accounts complicate the image of him as an uncomplicated wartime hero. His confession was also a defense of his own interests. Those facts don't make the fraud less remarkable; they explain why his later legend needs care.

The effects on art history continued. The case encouraged more rigorous explanations for attributions and a greater role for scientific testing. It did not mean that chemistry could answer every question about a painting. Even experts still have to distinguish an artist's own variations from a pupil's work, a restoration or a sophisticated imitation. But they could no longer treat a confident eye as the whole case.

The story acquired one further twist. Another painting attributed to Van Meegeren, a copy of a scene by Dirck van Baburen held by the Courtauld in London, remained a subject of investigation many years later. Testing in the twenty-first century detected Bakelite-related material and helped confirm the attribution. The very ingredient meant to disguise modern paint had become a signature for identifying its maker.

Return to Rotterdam. The museum has kept The Supper at Emmaus in its collection. It openly identifies Han van Meegeren as the artist. The painting still draws interest, not because it was secretly made by Vermeer, but because it exposes what can happen when desire, expertise and physical evidence point in different directions.

The object that began as a convincing 'missing Vermeer' ended as a document of a different history: the history of people who believed it.

And that leaves the museum's original question waiting in the same frame: whose masterpiece are we looking at?
