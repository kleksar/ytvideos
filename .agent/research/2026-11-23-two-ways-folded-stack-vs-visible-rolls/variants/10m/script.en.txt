When I pack for a trip, I want two things that sound compatible: to finish packing quickly, and to find the right shirt without pulling the whole suitcase apart. Are those actually the same problem? Would folding or rolling save me more time if I lived out of the bag for several days? And what would make the winner change?

Here is my suitcase and eight T-shirts. I will compare two layouts using the same clothes, the same case, and the same definition of success: the total time to pack, then take out a chosen shirt on three different mornings. I will not claim to have measured an actual suitcase. Everything you hear next is a declared model. The numbers are assumptions we can replace, not facts about anyone's hands.

A is a flat stack. I fold all eight T-shirts and place them in one pile. The top shirt is immediately reachable, but a shirt lower down may require moving the others. B is eight rolls arranged so that each can be seen and lifted on its own. I am not claiming B takes less room. This test is about access time, not capacity.

Before we race, I need a fair rule for choosing shirts. If I know on packing day exactly what I will wear each morning, I can arrange the folded pile in that order. Then the comparison is nearly over before it begins. So for our first case I do not know tomorrow's choice in advance. On each morning, any shirt still in the bag is equally likely to be requested. A shirt that has been worn leaves the suitcase; the others stay in their original relative order.

The action costs are simple. Folding one shirt takes five seconds; making one roll takes nine. Retrieving the target itself takes two seconds in either layout. If a folded shirt is underneath others, I move each shirt above it aside in three seconds and put it back in another three. I count both directions. Otherwise I would quietly give the stack free labor. I also don't assign a mystery penalty to rolls: a visible roll needs no preliminary moves.

At the start, A looks unbeatable. Eight shirts times five seconds is forty seconds. B needs eight times nine, or seventy-two. That's a thirty-two-second lead for folding before I even close the case. If I were measuring packing alone, the answer would already be A.

But my task doesn't stop there. On the first morning, the folded stack contains eight shirts. The target might be at the top with nothing in the way. Or it might be at the bottom with seven shirts above it. For equally likely positions from top to bottom, the average is three and a half shirts in the way. Each obstructing shirt takes three seconds out and three back, six in total. Three and a half times six is twenty-one seconds, plus two to take my shirt. That first retrieval averages twenty-three seconds.

After that, seven shirts remain. The average number above the target falls to three, so the second retrieval is eighteen seconds of moving and two of taking: twenty. On the third morning six shirts remain. Two and a half above the target means fifteen seconds of moving, plus two: seventeen.

Now I can finish the first race. The folded stack costs forty to pack, then twenty-three, twenty and seventeen to access three shirts. Add them: one hundred seconds. The rolls take seventy-two to pack and two seconds per retrieval. Seventy-eight seconds. Under this model, rolling wins by twenty-two seconds even though it started thirty-two behind.

I want to check what that result really means. I did not film eight shirts and discover that a roll was twenty-two seconds faster. I calculated the average cost of reaching unknown items in a specified layout. There is a difference. If my first shirt happens to be on top of the stack, the retrieval takes just two seconds. If it is at the bottom, I need to move seven shirts out and back: forty-two seconds, then two to take it. That one retrieval would take forty-four seconds. The average lies between those extremes; it is not what must happen on any particular morning.

What if I am only away for one night? Keep everything identical but change the number of shirt requests to one. The folded method uses forty seconds to pack plus twenty-three for the expected first search: sixty-three seconds. The rolls use seventy-two plus two: seventy-four. For a one-night task, folding is faster by eleven seconds.

Try two requests. The folded method needs forty plus twenty-three plus twenty: eighty-three seconds. The rolls need seventy-two plus four: seventy-six. One request favors folding; two already favor rolling. I can point to the exact reason for the flip. Rolling charges me thirty-two extra seconds before the trip. The first access saves twenty-one seconds compared with searching the stack. That's not enough. The second saves eighteen more. Now the savings add up to thirty-nine, more than the initial price.

This is a more useful result than saying one packing style always wins. It tells me where the break-even point lies, under stated rules. It also tells me which assumptions matter.

Consider the cost of moving a shirt. Maybe I can lift a thin pile much faster than three seconds per item. For three unknown requests with eight starting shirts, the average number of shirts that get moved aside is three and a half plus three plus two and a half. Exactly nine. Each one gets moved twice, so there are eighteen moves. If each move costs three seconds, that's fifty-four seconds of rearranging.

The folded total can therefore be written as forty seconds of packing, six seconds to grab three shirts, and eighteen times the per-move cost. At three seconds per move, that's one hundred. At two seconds per move, it's eighty-two. Rolls still take seventy-eight, so they win, but only by four. At one and a half seconds per move, the stack takes seventy-three and wins by five. The equality point is about one point seven eight seconds per move. I don't know whether your hands are faster than that. The model shows what to time rather than pretending everyone behaves alike.

The number of shirts matters too. Suppose I pack only four, keeping every per-shirt and per-move timing the same and still drawing three unknown shirts on separate mornings. The folded method starts at twenty seconds. With four shirts, the average obstructions across three searches are one and a half, one, and a half: three shirts moved aside in total on average. Six moves at three seconds cost eighteen, and three grabs cost six. Forty-four seconds overall. Rolls need thirty-six seconds to pack and six to retrieve: forty-two. The gap has shrunk to just two seconds.

Make it three shirts with three retrievals. Folding costs fifteen to pack. Across the three searches the average obstruction count is one, half, then zero. Twice those one and a half obstructions at three seconds gives nine, and grabbing the shirts adds six. The folded method totals thirty. Rolls take twenty-seven to pack and six to retrieve: thirty-three. Now folding wins. No new physics appeared; the extra prep cost of rolling has less time to pay off.

There is still a bigger practical objection than any formula. Real people can arrange folded clothes vertically, use packing cubes, or put tomorrow's outfit right on top. Those are sensible strategies, but they are not the flat-stack A that I timed here. My comparison cannot establish that rolling beats every kind of folding. It compares one stack against separate visible rolls. If I change what A is allowed to do, I need another fair test.

I also left out compressibility, wrinkles, fabric type, zipper strain, and how long it takes to restore a real suitcase. This is deliberate, because pretending to know those values would make the arithmetic look scientific while actually weakening it. A tidy-looking suitcase alone won't tell me how long the next search takes. That depends on which shirt I need and how I arranged the others.

If I wanted to turn it into a physical experiment, I would keep the same eight shirts, same case and same retrieval sequence. I would time folding, rolling, moving and replacing each shirt, repeat several runs, and report the spread instead of one convenient stopwatch result. I would also test an organized folded layout as a third competitor, not secretly swap it in after seeing A lose.

So what should I do before my next trip? If I'll unpack everything on arrival, quick folding makes sense here. If I don't know my outfits and will pull one shirt at a time over several mornings, separate rolls can save effort, even though rolling takes longer at the start. If I already know each day's outfit, arranging the stack intelligently could change everything. The model's winner belongs to a specific task, not to a universal travel hack.

My own packing choice would begin with that question: am I packing to finish packing, or packing to find things later?
