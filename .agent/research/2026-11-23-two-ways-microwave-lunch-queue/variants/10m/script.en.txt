How can the same microwave run for fifteen minutes in two different queues, yet one queue gives the average person a hot lunch almost seven minutes earlier?

I want to test an everyday question: is it better to keep the order in which people arrive, or let the quickest meals use the microwave first?

I'm not filming a real lunchroom or measuring food. I'm building a repeatable model with four imaginary people and one microwave. All four arrive at exactly the same time, and every container is already prepared. One has a ten-minute heating cycle, two have two-minute cycles and one has a one-minute cycle. These are assumed durations for a scheduling example, not instructions for reheating actual food.

The machine can heat only one container at a time. Nobody can interrupt a cycle halfway through, and there's no gap between cycles. Everyone starts waiting at the same zero on my clock.

My two rules are simple. In A, first come means first served. The original line happens to be ten, two, two, one. In B, I rearrange those exact same four meals from shortest to longest: one, two, two, ten. No one gets an extra microwave; I change no heating duration.

Before choosing, I must decide what winning means. I choose average time until each person's food is ready. That's different from how long the appliance is running, and different from the moment the last person finishes. An average matters because four people each experience their own clock.

Which order would you choose?

Let's run A. I start the ten-minute container. By the five-minute mark, nobody has a ready meal. Not even the person whose container needs only sixty seconds.

When the first cycle ends at minute ten, one person can take lunch. The next two-minute cycle ends at twelve. The third ends at fourteen. The one-minute meal finally comes out at fifteen.

The clock for each person reads ten, twelve, fourteen or fifteen minutes from arrival until food ready. Adding those four personal times gives fifty-one minutes. Divide by four and I get twelve minutes and forty-five seconds.

That isn't fifty-one minutes of electricity. It's fifty-one combined minutes of people's time, much of it overlapping. The machine itself ran for fifteen minutes.

Now I reset the same scene. All four people arrive together again. Every meal keeps its original heating time. The only alteration is the order.

In B, the one-minute cycle finishes at minute one. The first two-minute cycle finishes at three. The second finishes at five. The ten-minute cycle runs last and completes at minute fifteen.

At minute five, three people have their meals ready. Under A, none did. Yet at minute fifteen, both schedules are complete.

This time, the four personal clocks read one, three, five and fifteen. Their sum is twenty-four minutes. Divide by four: the average is six minutes.

So the shortest-first queue wins my declared metric by six minutes and forty-five seconds per person. The difference is huge, but it doesn't come from making the microwave run faster. The sum of heating cycles is still fifteen minutes. I've changed who bears the waiting.

I could stop there and make a confident video about why you should always let quick meals jump the line. But that would conceal something important.

Look at the person with the longest meal. Under A, their food was ready at minute ten. Under B, it is ready at fifteen. That person loses five minutes. Everyone is not better off.

Look at the one-minute person. Their meal was ready at fifteen under A and at one under B. They gain fourteen minutes. The other two each gain nine minutes: one moves from twelve to three, the other from fourteen to five. Three people gain a combined thirty-two minutes; one person loses five. That's why the total across people falls by twenty-seven, and the average falls by six minutes and forty-five seconds.

This isn't a trick in the arithmetic. It is what an average hides. I can improve the group score and make an individual outcome worse.

Now I add a constraint that a real person might care about. Suppose the long-meal owner has an eleven-minute break remaining. The heating cycles don't change. The order in A puts their meal at minute ten, inside the break. The order in B puts it at fifteen, four minutes after their break ends.

If I judge the same schedules by whether that person's deadline is met, A succeeds and B fails. If I judge by average food-ready time, B still wins. I am not claiming two winners under one secret metric. I have named two different goals, and the results answer different questions.

Maybe that sounds like a special exception. To see why the original average result is so dependable under its assumptions, I can shrink the problem to two lunches.

Imagine only a ten-minute meal and a one-minute meal. Long first: the first person gets lunch at minute ten and the other at minute eleven. Together, their completion times add up to twenty-one.

Swap them. The short meal is ready at minute one and the long meal at minute eleven. The sum is twelve. The same eleven minutes of microwave work have passed, but the combined personal time falls by nine.

Notice the trade. The short meal finishes ten minutes earlier, while the long meal finishes one minute later. That gives nine minutes less combined personal time, even though the microwave did exactly the same work.

The same reasoning works for two neighboring meals in a bigger queue. If a longer meal is immediately ahead of a shorter one and both are waiting, switching their order cannot push the very last finish any later. It reduces the sum of their completion times by the difference between their durations. Repeating that swap puts all the shorter jobs in front. That is the reason the shortest-first rule minimizes average completion time in this particular single-machine, simultaneous-arrival model.

It's not a magic rule for every office.

For example, maybe everyone didn't arrive together. Suppose the person with the ten-minute meal arrives alone, starts heating at noon, and three shorter lunches appear five minutes later. I can't suddenly put the long meal on pause and insert the others if my rule forbids interrupting a cycle. At minute five the machine is already busy. The clever rearrangement I showed earlier isn't available.

Or maybe a heating label is just an estimate. If I guess that a container takes one minute and it actually needs five, I might place it ahead of others for the wrong reason. The model assumes I know the durations. Without those inputs, I cannot claim the same exact completion times.

What if everyone takes half a minute to remove a container and put in the next one? If each person incurs that same handling time regardless of position, both schedules get extra waiting, but I haven't given one rule special treatment. The shorter-first advantage remains a scheduling effect, though its exact displayed times must be recalculated. If changeover time differs between containers, I need to include those differences explicitly.

And an average is only one way to describe fairness. The last meal in either schedule is ready at minute fifteen. The total heater time stays fifteen. The maximum wait before anyone's own heating starts is fourteen minutes in A, because the one-minute meal is last; in B it's five, because the ten-minute meal is last. Those numbers might matter to a group, but they answer a different question from the average time until eating.

Even when the arithmetic is clear, I wouldn't silently reorder people standing at the microwave. The first person may have a short break. Someone may need to follow their own food's safe heating instructions, which my model does not replace. And someone may simply not agree to step aside.

What can I actually carry into a workplace? First, if four people arrive at once and agree that average time to ready food is the goal, I can explain why short cycles first produces six minutes instead of twelve minutes and forty-five seconds. Second, if a specific person has a deadline, I should account for it before moving them to the back. Third, if meals arrive over time or heating estimates are uncertain, I must rerun the model rather than announce that one queue rule always wins.

The microwave never changed. The fifteen minutes of heating never changed. What changed was how those minutes were shared.

And that's why, standing in front of the same four imaginary lunches, I can't decide the order from the container labels alone. I ask what we're trying to protect: the group's average, or somebody's deadline. In this model, B wins the average; A gets the long meal ready before minute eleven. Both statements are true. The choice belongs to the people waiting.
