If I buy a smart plug to stop my television wasting electricity, will I save money? What if the smart plug uses more power than the television does while it waits? And what would have to change for the purchase to make sense?

My room looks perfectly ordinary. The television screen is black, but the standby light remains on. I picture a smart plug behind the cabinet: one scheduled switch that cuts the TV's electricity for most of the day. I want to spend less without walking behind the furniture every night. Yet the smart plug is itself a small powered device. It has to listen, keep time, and switch a relay. That tiny detail can reverse my conclusion.

I am going to compare two methods from the same starting point. Method A: leave one device connected, with its usual standby power while unused. Method B: connect the same device through a smart plug and schedule it off whenever I am not using it. Both methods deliver the same four hours of actual use. Both use the same electricity tariff. The metric is energy drawn from the wall over one full day, not the price on the packaging and not how modern the setup looks.

I haven't measured any of this in my home. Every wattage in the comparison is an illustrative input that I choose openly. I can calculate what the assumptions imply, but I cannot pretend the calculation was an observed experiment.

First I need to separate power from energy. A watt tells me how fast electricity is being used right now. A watt-hour tells me how much energy accumulates over time. One watt for ten hours is ten watt-hours. This matters because a very small standby draw can persist for many hours, whereas a bigger appliance may be used for only a short period.

I draw a timeline: four hours watching, twenty hours with the TV unused. That is twenty-four hours in both methods. During the four hours of viewing, the television itself consumes the same energy no matter which socket it is connected to. I don't have to invent its screen-on wattage. That shared energy cancels from my A-versus-B comparison.

For method A, I set a hypothetical efficient television's standby power to 0.3 watts. I am not saying my actual TV measures 0.3. I chose a plausible low value for the model, and the ENERGY STAR specification for qualifying TVs includes low standby-power requirements. A specification is a limit, not an individual reading.

Twenty hours at 0.3 watts gives six watt-hours of standby electricity in a day. I write six beside A. This is the maximum standby energy that my smart plug can avoid in this setup. The plug cannot save more than the TV would have consumed in those unused hours, unless I start changing something else about the system.

Now I put the smart plug in method B. Here is my crucial assumption: with its output switched off, the plug itself consumes 0.7 watts to stay ready. With its output switched on during viewing, its electronics use 0.9 watts. Real plugs vary; these are model inputs, not a manufacturer's claim or a measurement from a power meter.

When the plug cuts the television's supply for twenty hours, it spends 0.7 times twenty, or fourteen watt-hours, simply waiting. During the four hours when it lets me watch TV, its own overhead adds another 0.9 times four, or 3.6 watt-hours. I add those together: 17.6 watt-hours a day.

The television's normal viewing energy is identical on both sides, so the comparison is six watt-hours of avoidable standby versus 17.6 watt-hours of the plug's overhead. With these assumptions, installing the smart plug adds 11.6 watt-hours a day. The energy-saving gadget loses to doing nothing.

I don't want to exaggerate that result. Eleven point six watt-hours per day is not a frightening bill. Over 365 days it is about 4.23 kilowatt-hours. At a hypothetical twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour, that is only about one dollar and six cents in additional electricity over a year. My surprising result is about the direction of the difference, not an invented financial disaster.

The plug also costs money to buy, but purchase price belongs to a separate question. Right now my energy-only metric says the plug loses. If I include its purchase price too, it certainly cannot rescue this first example's financial savings. I should not secretly swap between watt-hours, annual cost, and convenience just to make one method win.

Before declaring smart plugs pointless, I change one clearly announced input: the device. In a second scenario, the same socket controls an old audio receiver that I assume draws eight watts when not in use. Everything else remains fixed. I still use it for four hours, leave it idle for twenty, and use the same 0.7-off / 0.9-on smart plug. I also assume this particular receiver can safely be disconnected from mains power.

In method A, an idle eight-watt receiver consumes eight times twenty: 160 watt-hours. In method B, the plug still adds 17.6 watt-hours while preventing those 160. The device's normal listening power once again cancels between both methods.

This time method B saves 142.4 watt-hours in one day. Over a year, that is just under 52 kilowatt-hours. At the same assumed tariff, the energy saving is about thirteen dollars annually. The two models have not broken their own rules. A new idle power value has changed which method uses less.

I can now locate the exact dividing line instead of deciding by instinct. The smart plug's daily overhead is 17.6 watt-hours. I divide that by the twenty hours I would otherwise leave the appliance in standby. The threshold is 0.88 watts.

Under this exact schedule and this exact plug model, a device below 0.88 watts idle draws less energy if I simply leave it in standby. A device above 0.88 watts idle would let the plug save electricity. At 0.88 watts, the two methods tie on energy. I cannot apply that threshold blindly to an entire house. It comes from the powers and hours I selected.

What happens if I use the television for eight hours instead of four? I have to declare a new timeline before calculating: eight hours of use and sixteen unused hours. The plug now consumes 0.7 times sixteen, plus 0.9 times eight. That totals 18.4 watt-hours. Dividing by sixteen gives a new break-even standby power of 1.15 watts. More use leaves fewer hours in which the plug can avoid standby consumption, while it continues drawing its own power.

The efficient 0.3-watt TV still fails that test. The hypothetical eight-watt receiver still passes. But the boundary has moved. This is why an annual saving printed without the actual usage hours may be misleading.

I should also test the plug rather than treating its assumed consumption as a law of nature. Suppose another hypothetical controller draws only 0.1 watts while off and 0.2 watts while on. Back with four hours on and twenty off, that means two watt-hours plus 0.8: just 2.8 watt-hours of overhead. Against a 0.3-watt TV using six standby watt-hours, that more efficient controller would finally save 3.2 watt-hours a day. The same TV can produce the opposite winner if the controller changes.

That is a sensitivity check, not a claim that I found a real plug with precisely those numbers. I would need the model's reliable measured off and on consumption, ideally measured at the wall with equipment suitable for small loads, before I used them in a buying decision.

Now for a different question: would the expensive plug pay for itself? Assume the original model, the eight-watt receiver, the four-hour schedule, and the twenty-five-cent electricity price. The calculated annual saving is about thirteen dollars. If I assume a fifteen-dollar purchase price, the arithmetic says the purchase needs a little over one year to earn its price back through electricity savings alone.

That payback ignores failures, replacement costs, changing electricity rates, changes in how often I use the receiver, and any extra devices required for a smart-home setup. I am not quoting a store price or promising that payback in an actual house. It is a conditional result. A plug already owned for convenience has a different upfront-cost question from buying one purely to save electricity.

There is a more important check before I ever schedule a hard power cut. Some electronics use standby time for software updates, network functions, recordings, or display maintenance. Interrupting that work may be inconvenient or harmful. I need the actual appliance's manual. A low number on my calculation does not authorize switching off a fridge, medical equipment, a router needed for the smart home, or any other device that must remain powered.

In fact, if the appliance has a genuinely effective built-in power-saving setting, that may change the entire reason for adding a second device. An ordinary accessible wall switch might also eliminate standby consumption without adding a powered controller, though it would not give me the same remote scheduling convenience. Those are useful outside options, but they are not secretly a third contender in the two-method calculation I just showed.

So if I wanted to act on this at home, I would write down the device's actual unused hours, check which standby modes are safe to interrupt, and measure the load's true idle draw. Then I would measure the smart plug's draw with its output both on and off. Finally I would run the two daily totals and, separately, any purchase-cost calculation at my electricity tariff.

The answer to my original question is no universal slogan. With a low-standby television and one assumed plug, method A wins. With a power-hungry receiver and the same plug, method B wins. With a much more efficient plug, even the television could reverse. The turning point isn't the word 'smart' printed on a box. It is the energy the plug can prevent compared with the energy the plug itself requires.

My decision is to check the numbers and the safety constraints before buying anything. When a device claims it will save standby power, the question I need answered is very specific: what is it consuming just to switch something else off?
