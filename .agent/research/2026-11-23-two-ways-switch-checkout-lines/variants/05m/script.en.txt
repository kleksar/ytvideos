Is a faster-moving checkout line actually worth switching to? And how much delay would make me regret staying where I am?

I have just two people in front of me. The other cashier has four. I stay with the shorter line, but I keep looking sideways. One customer leaves the other register. Then another starts paying. My own line has barely moved.

Here is the part I could not see while standing there: a line can move more often and still take longer to clear.

I am going to test two choices in a simple illustrated schedule. These seconds are assumptions I set before the comparison, not readings from a store. Both versions start at the same instant with the same shoppers, the same baskets, and no new arrivals. Each register serves one person at a time. Neither cashier stops for an unannounced problem.

The line I joined has two people ahead of me. The first takes ninety seconds and the next takes sixty. My small basket takes forty-five seconds. The other line has four customers, each taking forty seconds, and my basket would still take forty-five there.

Which would you choose at the one-minute mark: stay, or move?

At sixty seconds, the other cashier has already finished one person and is twenty seconds into the second. At my cashier, the first person still needs thirty seconds. That visual difference is genuine inside the model. It just does not tell the whole story.

I draw the remaining work rather than counting people who have already left. Ahead of me in my own line: thirty seconds for the first shopper, then sixty for the second. That is ninety seconds. Add my forty-five and I am out after another hundred and thirty-five seconds. From entering the queue to leaving: three minutes and fifteen seconds.

Now I reset the scene to that same one-minute mark. I switch to the other line. Ahead of my new position, customer two has twenty seconds left. Customers three and four still need forty each. There are one hundred seconds of work before my own basket. Add forty-five for me. My finish is three minutes and twenty-five seconds from the same starting point.

The register that visibly moved first still leaves me ten seconds later.

I walk across in five seconds. That doesn't automatically become an extra five seconds at the end. During my walk, the cashier keeps serving the customers already ahead of me. Provided I join before they finish, those five seconds overlap with waiting time. Counting both would be charging for the same time twice.

Here is a more interesting question. How badly would my original line have to slow down to change the answer?

The other line finishes my basket at three twenty-five. My original line finishes at three fifteen. I have a ten-second margin. If an extra delay in front of me is exactly ten seconds, both choices tie. If that delay is longer, switching wins, assuming the other line stays on schedule.

I can test that without pretending a price check actually happened. In a second, explicitly changed scenario, the shopper in front of me needs an additional twenty-five seconds for a price check. The new finish for staying is three forty. Switching still ends at three twenty-five, fifteen seconds earlier. The winner really does flip—but only because I changed and disclosed one condition.

But there is a second risk I didn't test yet. What if the other line changes while I am crossing?

I reset both registers to the original schedule, with no price check. At second sixty I start walking. The five-second crossing is still the same. Now I openly add a new customer: at second sixty-three, someone with a forty-second basket joins the other queue. They arrive before I do, so they stand ahead of me.

The original four customers finish at second one hundred and sixty. That newcomer finishes at second two hundred. I leave at second two hundred and forty-five: four minutes and five seconds. My original line would still have got me out at three fifteen. This time my switch costs fifty seconds.

Move the new arrival to second sixty-seven, *after* I join, and they wait behind me. My exit is back at three twenty-five. The new customer and their basket are unchanged. Only the order of arrival decides who waits.

These are three separate model runs, not three surprises smuggled into one real checkout: stay wins in the first, a declared price check makes switching win in the second, and an earlier arrival makes switching lose badly in the third.

In a real shop, I don't know anyone's remaining seconds. I can look at baskets, payments already underway, and the place I would actually take after switching. And the minute I've already waited is gone either way; I shouldn't stay just to justify it, or switch to punish it.

The useful habit isn't to follow the line that just moved. It is to ask what is actually left between me and the exit.
