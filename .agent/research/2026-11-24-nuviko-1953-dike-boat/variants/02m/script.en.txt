Why did a Dutch mayor send a cargo boat toward a collapsing dike? And why wasn't that boat enough to stop the flood?

On February 1, 1953, a storm surge overwhelmed dikes in the Netherlands. Near the town of Nieuwerkerk aan den IJssel, water broke through a barrier protecting a densely populated region of low-lying land.

Men threw sandbags into the opening. The water carried them away.

Mayor Vogelaar ordered the use of Arie Evegroen's eighteen-metre grain boat. Another boatman, Cor Heuvelman, went with him. They took a rowing boat in case their vessel was swept through the opening.

The hole was about fourteen or fifteen metres across. The cargo boat was just long enough to span it.

Heuvelman advised Evegroen to bring the bow against the bank. Men on the dike held a rope attached to the bow. The stern swung around, and the current pushed the hull into the gap.

But the boat didn't make a watertight seal. The workers still needed sandbags. The damaged dike could no longer support trucks, so Hannes van Vliet brought supplies by water aboard another cargo boat.

Together, they closed the breach.

In 1958, the Netherlands opened its first Delta Works storm surge barrier on the same river. Instead of repairing a break while the water was rushing through, operators could now shut the river against a dangerous surge.

A cargo boat had temporarily done the work of a floodgate. Five years later, the river had a floodgate of its own.
