What can a cargo boat do that a truckload of sandbags cannot? And why would two of the men who helped save a Dutch dike have to wait until 2009 to receive public recognition?

The Netherlands, January 31, 1953. A fierce northwesterly storm was driving North Sea water toward the coast. It coincided with a particularly high astronomical tide. Water was already unusually high on the Hollandse IJssel, a tidal river running through South Holland.

Three inland cargo vessels sought shelter in a harbour near Nieuwerkerk aan den IJssel. One was an eighteen-metre boat called the Twee Gebroeders, owned by Arie Evegroen.

Early the next morning, the water had cut a gap in the nearby Groenendijk. On the far side of that embankment lay low-lying land. If the gap widened, a disaster already spreading across the country could become much worse in the region.

The people working on the dike tried throwing sandbags into the opening. Evegroen later recalled that the current simply carried the bags inland. Before anybody could make a lasting repair, they needed something much larger to slow the water.

Mayor Vogelaar came to the boatmen. He wanted a cargo vessel placed in the breach itself. Of the three vessels, two were much larger than Evegroen's. The small boat was the one chosen.

Its length was only a few metres greater than the width of the opening. If it went into the gap lengthwise instead of across it, that narrow margin would be useless. The boatmen brought a rowing boat as an escape option.

Cor Heuvelman, a twenty-two-year-old boatman, joined Evegroen. Years later, Heuvelman described the manoeuvre. They approached against the wind, brought the bow up to the bank, and handed a rope to people waiting on the dike. Those people held the bow in place.

The stern turned toward the opening. Evegroen used the engine, and the moving water pressed the hull against the broken embankment. The ends of the boat caught on the remaining sections of the dike.

Now they had something solid to work behind.

In his own later account, Evegroen recalled getting away in the rowing boat and returning to switch off the engine. The opening was not completely sealed. Sand was needed to weigh the vessel down, and bags were needed to fill the spaces around it.

There was another problem. Trucks could no longer use the damaged dike safely. Another boatman, Hannes van Vliet, supplied the workers from the water on the Onderneming II. His boat was also a place where exhausted helpers could get warm. The operation went on until the gap was closed.

The 1953 flood killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands. Nobody can tell us precisely how many more would have died if this particular dike had failed completely. What is clear is that the people behind it escaped a much larger local inundation.

Evegroen received recognition for the rescue. In 1983, he unveiled a monument at the site. But the other two boatmen had not received the same public recognition. They were absent from that ceremony.

On January 7, 2009, almost fifty-six years after the flood, Heuvelman and Van Vliet each received a silver medal from the Dutch Carnegie Hero Fund. The story of the dike was reissued with their contributions included.

Meanwhile, the Netherlands had built a permanent answer to the problem. Work on a storm surge barrier across the Hollandse IJssel began in 1954. The first gate entered service in 1958. Unlike a fixed dam, the barrier could let normal shipping through and close when the river threatened the land.

Today a steel gate can be lowered between tall concrete towers. On that Sunday in 1953, there was no gate to lower. There was a small cargo boat, a rope held by people on the bank, and a second vessel bringing the sand needed to turn a boat into a barrier.
