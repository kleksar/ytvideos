Why did the mayor of a Dutch town order a man to put his own cargo boat into a collapsing dike? What stopped the vessel from being carried away? And why did the enormous flood barrier built after that night have to leave room for ships?

At the end of January 1953, a storm over the North Sea was pushing water toward the Netherlands. The winds came from the northwest. The surge arrived when the tides were already unusually high.

Much Dutch land was enclosed by dikes. In some places, the water outside the embankment could stand higher than the ground on the inside. Such a dike was part of the system that made a low-lying, inhabited landscape possible. When water overtopped it or broke through it, the difference in elevation gave the flood somewhere to go.

On the Hollandse IJssel, a tidal river in South Holland, the boatmen could see how strange the water was behaving. In a later interview, Cor Heuvelman and Hannes van Vliet recalled seeing exceptionally high water even at a time when it should have been low. The incoming tide was still to come.

Several cargo vessels moved across the river into the harbour at Kortenoord, near Nieuwerkerk aan den IJssel. They were there to find shelter, not because they were already planning to act as part of a dike.

One of the vessels was the Twee Gebroeders, an eighteen-metre grain boat belonging to Arie Evegroen. Two others were much larger inland ships.

The storm was breaking through the Netherlands' defences. Along the Hollandse IJssel, the dike at Ouderkerk failed. Near Nieuwerkerk, another section began to give way. Water was flowing over the unpaved embankment in the early morning; by around half past five there was a substantial gap.

People working at the breach were throwing sandbags into the flow. In Evegroen's later recollection, the bags disappeared inland almost as soon as they went in.

This was a particularly dangerous place for the water to get through. The embankment was part of the protection around an extensive low-lying region, including densely populated parts of South Holland. A historical account from the Zeeland archives puts the population protected by the wider dike system at about three million. That figure describes the region at risk, not a count of people who would necessarily have died that morning.

There was no time to build a new dike across the opening. But there were three cargo vessels within reach.

Mayor Vogelaar ordered one of them to be used as a plug. The two larger vessels were judged unsuitable. The order fell on Evegroen's eighteen-metre boat.

The breach was around fourteen or fifteen metres wide, depending on the account. A hull that could span that distance had to be put across the opening, rather than pointed into it. There were only a few metres of extra length to play with.

Evegroen did not go alone. Heuvelman, then twenty-two, offered to help him. They brought a small rowing boat with them. If the grain boat disappeared into the breach, they intended to have another way off it.

Heuvelman later described how they brought the bow toward the bank against the wind. The idea was to make one end of the hull a fixed point. A rope passed from the bow to the people standing on the dike. They held it there.

The stern then came around toward the opening. Evegroen recalled using the propeller to turn the vessel. The current helped push the long side of the hull into the gap. Its ends caught on what was left of the embankment.

For the people on the bank, the situation was now different. They no longer had to throw a little bag into a wide, unobstructed flow. They had a large object taking up most of the opening. But it was still a floating cargo boat, and there were spaces around it.

Evegroen recalled leaving in the rowing boat and then going back to shut off the engine. The men on the bank still had an entire repair to carry out.

They needed sand to weigh the boat down, and bags to stop the water getting past the ends. They couldn't simply send trucks back and forth along the weakened dike. Transport was becoming almost as important as the manoeuvre itself.

This was where the Onderneming II came in. Its skipper was Hannes van Vliet. He and his family helped carry sand by water. A later account of the monument's history describes repeated journeys from the local sand supplier to the breach. The men working outside in the cold could also warm themselves aboard the vessel.

The sand in and around the grain boat did the work that loose bags in an open current had failed to do. The breach was closed.

Elsewhere, the disaster was still unfolding. A second high tide came that afternoon. More than one hundred and fifty breaches had opened in the southwestern Netherlands during the disaster. The death toll in the Netherlands reached 1,836.

Closing the dike at Nieuwerkerk could not undo what had already happened in Zeeland or elsewhere. But it prevented the situation on this stretch of river from becoming another major inundation.

The government now had to solve the same problem before the next storm, rather than during it. In May 1953, the Delta Commission recommended a storm surge barrier on the Hollandse IJssel. Construction work began the following year.

Why not just dam the river permanently? Because the same waterway that could carry a storm surge inland also carried cargo vessels and received water pumped off low-lying land. A permanent closure would interfere with both.

Instead, engineers built a structure with large gates suspended above the shipping channel. In ordinary conditions, vessels could travel beneath them. A lock alongside the barrier provided another route for navigation when the gates were down.

The project had its own version of the problem faced by the men with the sandbags. As a gate descended, water would have less room to pass underneath. In that narrowing space the current could become strong enough to wash away material supporting the structure.

The builders reinforced the muddy riverbed and constructed specially shaped thresholds. They also diverted the river slightly during the works so that shipping could continue.

In October 1958, the first Delta Works barrier opened. It had one operating gate. According to the Flood Museum, that original gate had to be lowered near the change of tide, when the current was weakest. A second gate came later, in the 1970s, and newer techniques made closure possible even while the water was flowing.

The engineering achievement was large enough to have its own monuments and anniversaries. But the story of the cargo boat was not yet finished.

Evegroen was honoured in 1977. Thirty years after the flood, in October 1983, he unveiled a bronze memorial at the site of the breach. Heuvelman and Van Vliet were not there.

It was only in January 2009 that the two other boatmen received their own public recognition. At a ceremony in Nieuwerkerk, each received a silver Carnegie Hero Fund medal. The account of the rescue was republished to acknowledge what they had done.

On the Hollandse IJssel today, an eighty-metre-wide steel gate can stand between a storm surge and the inland river. In 1953, an eighteen-metre cargo boat had to stand between the water and a broken dike.

The reason the smaller barrier held was not just that its length matched the opening. It had to be steered across it, held in place, weighed down, and sealed. The boatman at the controls could not have finished those jobs alone.

One ship became the most visible part of the rescue. The rope, the workers, and the second ship supplying sand made that rescue possible.
