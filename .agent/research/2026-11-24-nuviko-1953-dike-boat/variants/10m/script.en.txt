Why did a Dutch mayor requisition a small cargo boat to save a dike? What could keep it from being swept through the breach? And how did the two boatmen who helped make the rescue possible end up waiting until 2009 for their medals?

On the last evening of January 1953, a northwesterly storm was driving the waters of the North Sea toward the Netherlands. The tide was unusually high. In South Holland, water from the sea could travel up the Hollandse IJssel, a tidal river bordered by dikes.

Two boatmen, Cor Heuvelman and Hannes van Vliet, later remembered looking at the river when the tide should have been low. It was already exceptionally high. And the next high tide was still ahead.

Heuvelman's family boat was undergoing repairs in Ouderkerk. It was one of three cargo vessels that crossed the river and took shelter in the harbour at Kortenoord. The smallest was an eighteen-metre grain boat, the Twee Gebroeders, belonging to Arie Evegroen.

The land behind the dikes included towns, factories and farmland lying below the surrounding water. The dike had to hold even when the water was higher than the ground on the other side.

Early on February 1, water was overtopping the Groenendijk near Nieuwerkerk. According to a local historical account, a former drainage sluice had been removed there, and the clay replacing it had settled. At the vulnerable spot, water began cutting away the embankment.

By the time the opening was roughly fourteen or fifteen metres across, men were trying to throw sandbags into it. Evegroen later remembered what happened to those bags: the current took them inland.

Someone would have to put a much larger obstruction across the water before the men could finish the repair.

Local history credits a sand merchant, Leen Boer, with suggesting the use of Evegroen's boat. He knew the vessel because it had once belonged to his business. It had since been lengthened. Mayor Vogelaar went to requisition a ship.

There were three to choose from. According to Heuvelman's later account, the two larger vessels were each thirty-eight metres long and too unwieldy for the operation. The little boat Evegroen had brought in to escape the storm was the one they needed.

Heuvelman was twenty-two. In his account of the rescue, he said that he had not even known Evegroen before that morning. He offered to go with him anyway. The men took a rowing boat along, so they would have a way to leave the larger vessel if it was carried through the gap.

The problem was not simply getting the boat there. A ship eighteen metres long could cover a breach fourteen or fifteen metres wide only if the hull lay across the opening. If the bow went straight in, the vessel could pass through.

At the dike, Heuvelman advised Evegroen to approach against the wind and bring the bow up to the embankment. Heuvelman threw a line to the people waiting on land. They held it.

Now one end of the boat was secured. The stern turned toward the breach. Evegroen recalled using the propeller, and the moving water pressed the side of the hull into the opening. The boat caught on the surviving edges of the dike.

The men got off in the rowing boat. But there was still an engine running in the gap. According to their later accounts, Evegroen climbed back aboard to stop it: the propeller was disturbing the dike they were trying to preserve.

They had blocked much of the flow, but the boat was still afloat and water could still get past it. The vessel needed ballast. The spaces between the hull and the embankment needed filling.

The ordinary means of delivering a truckload of sand was no longer available. Heavy trucks could not safely use the weakened dike. So the authorities requisitioned a second boat, the Onderneming II.

Its skipper was Hannes van Vliet, the other boatman who had watched the river rise the evening before. He was twenty-nine. In his recollection, another skipper had already been arranged for the vessel, but Van Vliet decided to take it himself. His brothers Henk and Aart went with him.

Their cargo came from the sand business on the river. They carried supplies to the breach, unloaded and went back for more. The boat also had a heated cabin. Workers who had been out in the storm could come inside to get warm, and Van Vliet provided hot coffee.

The crew of the first boat had used the current to get the hull into place. The second crew had to keep moving in those same conditions. Local accounts describe them continuing to bring supplies to other endangered sections after their work at the breach.

At the Groenendijk, sand weighed down the Twee Gebroeders and workers packed the remaining gaps with sandbags. Once the gaps were closed, a temporary barrier made from the cargo boat and the sand finally held the river back.

Elsewhere, the flood was still unfolding. Another high tide arrived that afternoon. Across the Netherlands, 1,836 people lost their lives in the disaster. The wider dike system protected a region with millions of inhabitants; that does not tell us how many people would have died if this repair had failed.

For Evegroen, there was a practical problem after the water receded. The Twee Gebroeders returned to service, but, according to Van Vliet's later recollection, its engine kept failing. Evegroen went back to repair yards repeatedly. He waited about a year for money from the disaster fund, and the water authority ultimately paid for a replacement engine.

Meanwhile, the Dutch government was planning a permanent solution to the danger they had faced. A fixed dam across the river would also block ordinary shipping and the drainage of the low-lying land. The Delta Commission recommended a movable barrier in May 1953, and construction began the next year.

Even the permanent structure had to deal with the force of moving water. As a gate descended, the current could accelerate through the space beneath it and scour the riverbed. Engineers reinforced the bed and shaped the thresholds to withstand that flow. They provided a navigation lock alongside the gates.

The first barrier of the Delta Works opened there in October 1958. The original gate had to be closed near the turn of the tide, when the current was weaker. A second gate and improved techniques followed in the 1970s.

By then, a single name had become associated with the emergency repair: Arie Evegroen. He received a royal honour in 1977. In October 1983, he unveiled a monument on the dike. Heuvelman and Van Vliet were not at that ceremony.

Decades later, the two men gave their account of what had happened. Their roles were included in a reissue of the story of the rescue. On January 7, 2009, in Nieuwerkerk's town hall, the local mayor presented each of them with a silver medal from the Carnegie Hero Fund.

The first boat had blocked the current. The young man who threw the rope and the people who held it had made that manoeuvre possible. The second boat had brought the material that kept the first from floating away and sealed the gaps around it.

Look at the river today and you can see a steel gate about eighty metres across. It can be lowered between its towers before a storm surge reaches the inland river. In 1953, the boatmen and the workers had to create their own barrier after the water had already broken through. They did it with two cargo boats, a rope and sand.
