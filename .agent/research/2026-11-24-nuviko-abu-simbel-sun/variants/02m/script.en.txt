Workers cut a temple in Egypt into pieces, but they also had to move the path of the sunlight inside it.

Abu Simbel was carved into a sandstone cliff more than three thousand years ago. Four enormous figures of Ramses the Second guarded the entrance. Behind them, a passage led to four smaller figures deep inside the rock.

The builders had arranged the temple so the rising sun could reach the inner chamber at certain times of the year.

In the nineteen sixties, Egypt was building the Aswan High Dam. The lake behind it would cover the cliff and the temples beside it.

An early rescue plan proposed lifting both temples on enormous jacks. Instead, engineers chose to take them apart.

They built a temporary dam to hold back the rising water. Then they removed the rock above the chambers. The sandstone was so fragile that they used hand saws for important cuts.

The walls, ceilings and statues were divided into more than a thousand blocks. The workers numbered them and carried them to higher ground.

Now they needed to put them back together. At the new site, a small error in the direction of the entrance could send the sunlight onto the wall instead of the statues.

They recreated the passage in its original orientation and built concrete domes over the chambers. Rock piled over the domes formed a new hillside.

The temples reopened in nineteen sixty-eight, about sixty-five metres higher.

And today, in February and October, the morning light still reaches three figures at the back. The fourth, Ptah, remains in shadow.

Those statues are ancient, but the mountain surrounding them is largely a modern construction. Why did the engineers have to recreate more than just the stones?
