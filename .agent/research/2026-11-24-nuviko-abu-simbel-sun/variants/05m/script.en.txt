How do you move a temple that was carved into a mountain? And how do you make sure that the sun will still find the same statues inside it?

In southern Egypt, four colossal figures sit in front of a temple built for Ramses the Second. Each is about twenty-one metres high. Walk between them and you enter a series of halls cut out of the rock. At the far end, four figures sit together in a small sanctuary.

The great temple at Abu Simbel was designed so that the changing position of the rising sun could send a shaft of light all the way inside. Next to it stood another rock-cut temple, built for the goddess Hathor and the king's wife, Nefertari.

For over three thousand years, these buildings had occupied the same cliff above the Nile. In the twentieth century, the danger came from a construction site far downstream.

Egypt was building the Aswan High Dam to supply electricity and improve its agriculture. Behind the dam, the river would become a vast reservoir. The old entrance to Abu Simbel would disappear beneath the surface.

Egypt and Sudan appealed for help, and in nineteen sixty an international campaign began. Saving a building made of separate stones was one thing. Abu Simbel had no such joints. The walls and ceilings were part of the mountain itself.

An early proposal involved separating the two temples from the cliff, enclosing them in concrete and lifting them with giant hydraulic jacks. That was the scheme being promoted in nineteen sixty-one. In the end, another solution was adopted: divide the temples into pieces and reconstruct them above the reservoir.

The water could not wait for the reconstruction. A temporary barrier, hundreds of metres long, was built to protect the site while the lake was filling. Workers covered the carved faces with sand and put steel supports inside the chambers.

Then they began stripping away the rock above the roofs. They could not simply blast the mountain apart. The sandstone was porous, and the vibrations and broken rock could damage the parts that had to survive.

Even the width of a saw cut mattered. A thick cut through a carved figure removes part of the original figure. Once the pieces are joined again, that missing stone cannot be put back. For important cuts, workers used hand saws that removed less material.

The roofs, walls and facades were divided into blocks. More than a thousand pieces of the temples had to be identified and put aside until their new home was ready. Steel lifting bars helped workers move the heavy stone safely.

At the new site, roughly sixty-five metres above the original one, the workers had to recover the relationship between every piece. The statue, the doorway and the far end of the sanctuary all had to line up again. The engineers worked to a placement tolerance of about five millimetres.

And they had another job that no ordinary moving company would have: rebuilding the hill.

The ancient rooms had been surrounded by natural rock. If engineers simply piled a mountain's worth of stone directly over the reconstructed ceilings, they would put those ceilings under an enormous load. Instead, two large concrete domes supported the artificial hillside. Seen from outside, the temples could once again look as though they had been carved into a cliff.

The new complex was inaugurated on September twenty-second, nineteen sixty-eight.

But the most revealing demonstration is inside the Great Temple. In February and October, sunlight travels through its halls and reaches the sanctuary. It illuminates Ramses, Amun-Ra and Ra-Horakhty. The fourth figure, Ptah, associated with the underworld, remains in shadow.

The rescue preserved more than the outlines of the buildings. It preserved the arrangement that gave them their remarkable effect. The sun never had to be moved. The engineers moved the architecture in such a way that the sunlight could still do its work.

Visitors can walk out of the darkness of the sanctuary into the daylight and look up at what seems to be the ancient cliff. Behind that cliff is another monument: a piece of twentieth-century engineering built around the work of the ancient Egyptians.
