What would you do if an ancient temple was about to disappear under a lake, but the temple itself was part of a mountain? Would you protect it where it stood, lift the mountain, or cut the stone apart? And after moving it, how would you know that you had preserved the way the temple actually worked?

To see the problem, walk into the Great Temple of Abu Simbel.

On the face of the cliff, four colossal seated figures of Ramses the Second surround a doorway. They are about twenty-one metres tall. Beyond the doorway lies a series of chambers cut into sandstone. At the end of those chambers is a much smaller room, with four seated figures.

Here the direction of the building matters. When the sun rises at particular times of the year, the light passes through the entrance and the halls to the rear of the temple. As the direction of sunrise shifts through the seasons, the sun can no longer illuminate the inner statues in quite the same way.

Just north of the Great Temple stood another sanctuary, dedicated to Hathor and Queen Nefertari. Both temples were carved in the thirteenth century before Christ. At Abu Simbel there were no existing joints along which to take the walls apart. The walls were part of the cliff.

In the nineteen fifties, Egypt planned the Aswan High Dam to store water for agriculture and generate electricity. Its reservoir would flood the temples and land far into Nubia. Egypt and Sudan appealed to UNESCO in nineteen fifty-nine, and an international rescue campaign began the following year.

One possibility was to protect the temples in place. That would mean maintaining a barrier between the rock and the reservoir for as long as the lake existed. In nineteen sixty-one, the international appeal promoted a different solution: enclose each temple in concrete and raise the entire mass on hydraulic jacks. The carved interiors could remain intact, but the weight and the supporting structure would make this a formidable project.

Egypt eventually chose a plan developed by Swedish engineers. The workers would actually saw through the ancient temples, then rebuild them high above the river. That meant finding a way to cut a carved face without losing its features.

A temporary barrier was built between the Nile and the temples. It was about three hundred and seventy metres long and twenty-five metres high. Behind it, the crews could work on the cliff while the reservoir was rising. Sand protected the exterior carvings, and steel scaffolding supported the ceilings inside.

Next came the removal of the stone above the rooms. A building with open space around it can be rigged for a lift. These temples first had to be uncovered from the top. The crews used bulldozers and pneumatic tools to take away the surrounding rock, while instruments monitored the vibrations. The engineering archive describes reducing the layer of rock around the monuments to about eighty centimetres before cutting the actual temples.

As the roofs were exposed, stonecutters could work on the actual temple. Specialists with experience cutting marble came from Carrara in Italy. They cut the decorated surfaces by hand, limiting the material lost to a few millimetres, and used mechanical saws where the cuts would be hidden. A cut through a carved face removes stone permanently. When the pieces come together, the workers still have to restore the original distance between the features.

The resulting pieces were still enormous. Some weighed twenty or thirty tonnes. Each had to be supported as it was freed, lifted without damage and labelled so it could be found when the reconstruction reached that particular place. Steel bars were used to help handle the pieces.

The work started inside, with the roofs. Later the sand protection was removed and the facades were dismantled. When it was time to move the entrance, even the great seated figures had become pieces in a much larger collection. The numbered pieces were laid out across a depot of about forty-four thousand square metres. At the depot, workers strengthened the backs with concrete and fitted steel anchors for the reconstruction. The carved surfaces were still ancient stone; their hidden backs now had to function as part of a modern building.

The work required a temporary settlement for crews and their families, with dining halls, shops and even a mosque. As cutters were still dismantling the old cliff, other crews worked on the new foundations about sixty-five metres higher. One temple site was disappearing while the other was taking shape.

The new temple needed more than a faithful entrance. Suppose the entrance were turned slightly to one side, but every statue in the inner chamber were exactly where it belonged relative to the others. The sculptures would look correct to a visitor. The beam of sunlight, however, would approach from the wrong direction. Across the length of the temple, even a small change in angle can make a noticeable change in where the light lands.

The engineers therefore had to control both the position of the individual blocks and the orientation of the whole structure. The placement tolerance was about five millimetres. Surveyors checked the new positions with a theodolite. The facades rose on supporting frames, with the enormous figures assembled piece by piece. Workers joined the stones and finished the seams by hand.

The next problem was above their heads. At the original site, the mountain surrounded the halls. On the plateau, the temple roofs had been reconstructed from separate blocks. The engineers built large reinforced concrete domes above the two temples so that the new hill would be supported by those structures rather than simply resting on the ancient ceilings. Then they covered the domes with material from the excavations, creating hills that resembled the original setting.

The two temples were inaugurated on September twenty-second, nineteen sixty-eight. The great figures looked down from their new cliff. But had the temple's most unusual feature survived?

Today, on the twenty-second of February and October, dawn light reaches the inner sanctuary. Three figures are illuminated: Ramses, Amun-Ra and Ra-Horakhty. Ptah remains in shadow. Popular stories associate the ancient dates with the king's birthday and coronation, but those exact dates are not established. The illumination demonstrates the survival of the temple's geometry, not the accuracy of a claim about the king's calendar.

The work at Abu Simbel was part of a much larger effort. At Philae, another temple complex was moved to a higher island. In all, twenty-two monuments and complexes were rescued. UNESCO credits the Nubian campaign with helping lay the groundwork for the World Heritage Convention.

But the entire landscape could not be restored. Nubian communities had to leave homes that the reservoir would cover. The rescue of the temples did not undo that loss. At Abu Simbel, the original riverbank and the old setting were gone. What survived was the carved stone and the relationship between its rooms and the sky.

From the outside, the figures of Ramses still sit in a cliff. Behind them is a twentieth-century structure. And at the back of the temple, the sunlight falls on three ancient faces, just as the restored architecture was intended to allow.
