What would you do if an ancient temple was about to disappear under a lake, but the temple itself was part of a mountain? Would you protect it where it stood, lift the mountain, or cut the stone apart? And after moving it, how would you know that you had preserved the way the temple actually worked?

To see the problem, walk into the Great Temple of Abu Simbel.

On the face of the cliff, four colossal seated figures of Ramses the Second surround a doorway. They are about twenty-one metres tall. Beyond the doorway lies a series of chambers cut into sandstone. At the end of those chambers is a much smaller room, with four seated figures.

Here the direction of the building matters. When the sun rises at particular times of the year, the light passes through the entrance and the halls to the rear of the temple. As the direction of sunrise shifts through the seasons, the sun can no longer illuminate the inner statues in quite the same way.

There is another temple a little to the north. It honours the goddess Hathor and Queen Nefertari, the wife of Ramses. At its entrance the queen is shown on the same scale as her husband. That is unusual in ancient Egyptian royal imagery. Both temples were made in the thirteenth century before Christ. They were not objects that could be picked up and put on a ship. They were rooms hollowed out of a cliff.

For thousands of years, the Nile had flowed below the temples. Then Egypt decided to build a dam that would change the river.

In the nineteen fifties, a much larger dam at Aswan was planned. It would store water and generate electricity. Farmers could use the stored water rather than rely entirely on the changing river. But the water behind the dam would stretch far south into Nubia, across land in Egypt and Sudan.

The temples were only a small part of what stood in its path. There were archaeological sites along the Nile, and there were living communities whose homes lay in the area to be flooded. The ancient monuments could sometimes be rescued; moving a community was a very different and costly matter.

In nineteen fifty-nine, the Egyptian and Sudanese governments asked UNESCO to help protect the monuments. An international campaign began the following year. There was work to do throughout the valley: excavating and documenting sites, recovering objects and deciding which structures could be saved.

At Abu Simbel, the scale of the temples made the choice especially difficult. The most straightforward idea in principle was to keep water away from them. A permanent barrier might protect the cliff, but the site would be surrounded by a reservoir. Water could still find its way through or around the structure, and the sandstone would have to endure these conditions for as long as the reservoir remained.

The other major proposal was almost the opposite. Instead of trying to keep the water down, the engineers would move the temples up.

In nineteen sixty-one, the international appeal described a plan to cut around each temple, enclose it in concrete and raise it with large hydraulic jacks. The advantage is easy to understand. If the interior stays together as one structure, the carved walls and the alignment of the chambers need not be broken apart. But this would mean lifting enormous masses of rock, with immense supporting structures beneath them. The project needed funding and time that were not unlimited.

Neither scheme became the final operation. Egypt adopted a plan developed by Swedish engineers: cut the temples into separate blocks and reassemble them on the plateau.

Imagine opening a book to a picture and cutting the page into sections. If a thin piece of paper is removed between the sections, they will no longer fit together exactly. On a small picture the defect is visible. At Abu Simbel, the equivalent could be a scar across a face that had survived for three thousand years.

The engineers had to divide the monument while losing as little original material as possible. And they had to start while construction of the dam continued.

A temporary barrier was built between the Nile and the temples. It was about three hundred and seventy metres long and twenty-five metres high. Behind it, the crews could work on the cliff while the reservoir was rising. Sand protected the exterior carvings, and steel scaffolding supported the ceilings inside.

Next came the removal of the stone above the rooms. A building with open space around it can be rigged for a lift. These temples first had to be uncovered from the top. The crews used bulldozers and pneumatic tools to take away the surrounding rock, with very limited use of explosives because of the delicate sandstone.

As the roofs were exposed, stonecutters could work on the actual temple. Specialists with experience cutting marble came from Carrara in Italy. Important cuts were made with narrow hand saws, rather than removing the greater amount of stone that powered saws could have taken away.

The resulting pieces were still enormous. Some weighed twenty or thirty tonnes. Each had to be supported as it was freed, lifted without damage and labelled so it could be found when the reconstruction reached that particular place. Steel bars were used to help handle the pieces.

The work started inside, with the roofs. Later the sand protection was removed and the facades were dismantled. When it was time to move the entrance, even the great seated figures had become pieces in a much larger collection. The blocks were laid out in a storage area covering several hectares.

The old cliff was being stripped of the architectural spaces and surfaces that made it a temple. Meanwhile, at a site about sixty-five metres higher, the engineers prepared to give those spaces a new home.

The new temple needed more than a faithful entrance. Suppose the entrance were turned slightly to one side, but every statue in the inner chamber were exactly where it belonged relative to the others. The sculptures would look correct to a visitor. The beam of sunlight, however, would approach from the wrong direction. Across the length of the temple, even a small change in angle can make a noticeable change in where the light lands.

The engineers therefore had to control both the position of the individual blocks and the orientation of the whole structure. The placement tolerance was about five millimetres. They joined the stones with reinforcement and filled the gaps between them, completing the surfaces by hand.

The next problem was above their heads. At the original site, the mountain surrounded the halls. On the plateau, the temple roofs had been reconstructed from separate blocks. The engineers built large reinforced concrete domes above the two temples so that the new hill would be supported by those structures rather than simply resting on the ancient ceilings. Then they covered the domes with material from the excavations, creating hills that resembled the original setting.

The two temples were inaugurated in their new location in September nineteen sixty-eight. Other monuments in the campaign were still being rescued. At Philae, for example, the sanctuary of Isis was moved to another island. Across the entire campaign, twenty-two monuments and complexes were saved. Abu Simbel and the other Nubian monuments were inscribed on the World Heritage List in nineteen seventy-nine.

That international success does not mean the entire old Nile landscape was saved. The reservoir covered the original sites and many Nubian settlements. The temple was one thing the twentieth century could carry forward from that lost landscape.

The evidence is visible at dawn on the twenty-second of February or October. Light enters the Great Temple and reaches three figures: Ramses, Amun-Ra and Ra-Horakhty. The figure of Ptah remains in shadow. There are popular stories claiming the ancient dates were the king's birthday and coronation. Those exact dates are not established, and the achievement does not depend on that claim.

What the light shows is that the ancient arrangement still functions at the new site. The engineers did not need to reproduce the entire ancient mountain, nor bring back the river below it. They had to understand what the mountain did for the temple, preserve the carved stone, and reconstruct the spaces in the right relation to the sun.

From the outside, the figures of Ramses still sit in a cliff. Behind them is a twentieth-century structure. And at the back of the temple, the sunlight falls on three ancient faces, just as the restored architecture was intended to allow.
