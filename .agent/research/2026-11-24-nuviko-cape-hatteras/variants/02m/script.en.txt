They put the Cape Hatteras Lighthouse on steel rollers and moved it nearly three thousand feet.

In 1870, this brick tower stood about fifteen hundred feet from the Atlantic. But storms kept taking sand from the ocean side of the island and depositing it farther inland. By 1999, the shore was almost at the lighthouse.

There was another problem underground. The tower rested on pine timbers submerged in fresh water. If the sea reached that foundation, the wood could decay.

A seawall might protect the tower for a while. But as the beach disappeared around it, the lighthouse could end up on an island of its own.

After years of debate, the National Park Service decided to move the whole light station. Many people feared the brick tower wouldn't survive the journey.

Workers removed sections of its buried foundation and replaced them with temporary supports. They inserted steel beams, raised the tower six feet with hydraulic jacks and slipped rollers underneath.

The track ran across sand. If one side sank farther than the other, the tower could tilt. So the team divided the lifting system into three independently controlled zones. Sixty sensors tracked how the structure behaved.

The lighthouse advanced in short pushes. On July ninth, twenty-three days after the move began, it reached its new foundation.

Even the keepers' houses had been moved ahead of it. The whole station was back in its original arrangement, with a broad stretch of land between it and the ocean.

Two hurricanes postponed the relighting. Then, on November thirteenth, the beacon finally shone again.
