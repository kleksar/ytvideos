What do you do when the ocean reaches the bottom of a lighthouse? Can you lift an entire brick tower without pulling it apart? And where do you put it when the island itself is moving?

Off the coast of North Carolina lies a narrow chain of sandy islands called the Outer Banks. Ships passing Cape Hatteras have to navigate around the shallow waters of Diamond Shoals. A tall lighthouse there helps them recognize where they are.

In 1870, the new brick tower stood around fifteen hundred feet from the Atlantic. It was built on a bed of pine timbers submerged in fresh water. Above the wood lay heavy masonry. As long as the surrounding sand and fresh water remained in place, that foundation could support the tower.

But the coast did not stay in place. During storms, water swept across the island, shifting sand from the ocean side toward the sheltered waters on the other side.

In the nineteen thirties the waves came so close that the Coast Guard moved the beacon to a separate steel tower. The beach eventually recovered enough for the light to return in 1950. It seemed there was a way to keep the old lighthouse after all.

Then the ocean came back. Sand was pumped onto the beach, and barriers were built out from the shore to retain sand. In 1980, a storm washed away the foundation of an even older lighthouse nearby.

The National Park Service considered relocating the brick tower, then dismissed the idea as impractical. Instead, it chose a protective seawall. But there was a serious drawback. The wall could defend a particular patch of ground while the coastline continued moving around it.

A study in 1988 recommended relocation. People who opposed the move had a reasonable objection. A lighthouse built from masonry isn't designed to roll over uneven ground. A bad move could destroy the structure that everyone wanted to preserve.

It took another independent study, published in 1997, and congressional funding for the relocation to go ahead.

By 1999 the shore had come within about fifteen feet of the tower. The workers had to begin underneath it.

They dug around the base and removed the buried granite in sections, replacing it with temporary supports. Steel beams were worked into the space beneath the masonry until they formed a large carrying frame.

Hydraulic jacks gradually raised that frame six feet. At each stage, the load could rest on stacked timbers while the jacks were reset. Finally, there was enough room to put steel rollers and rails beneath the structure.

The team prepared a route across the sand using compacted material and steel mats. Even then, the weight of the lighthouse could make the ground settle unevenly. The vertical hydraulic jacks were divided into three zones, so the operators could adjust the support on different sides.

Sixty sensors measured changes in the structure and the loads beneath it. Separate jacks pushed the carrying frame along the rails, around five feet at a time. Sections of track were taken from behind the moving tower and laid ahead of it.

On June seventeenth, the lighthouse began its journey. Waiting at the other end were the keepers' houses, the oil house and other parts of the station, already relocated.

Twenty-three days later, the tower reached its destination. The steel frame was dismantled in stages while the load was transferred to a permanent foundation of reinforced concrete and masonry.

The buildings were arranged just as they had been before. The tower now had roughly the same separation from the sea that it had when it was new.

But a lighthouse has one more job to do. Its light had been turned off in March, and a ceremony to switch it back on in September was postponed because of Hurricanes Dennis and Floyd.

On November thirteenth, 1999, the beacon finally returned to service. Ships could once again use it to find their way past the cape.

The engineers had saved the lighthouse by letting the island change. They hadn't tried to keep the Atlantic in its old place.
