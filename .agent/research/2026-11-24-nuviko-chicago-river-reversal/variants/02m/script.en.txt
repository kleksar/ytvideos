What do you do when your city's drinking water and its sewage end up in the same lake? And what if your solution sends the problem toward another city?

In nineteenth-century Chicago, Lake Michigan supplied drinking water. The Chicago River also carried waste toward that lake. Moving the water intake farther offshore helped, but it did not eliminate the danger.

Chicago's next answer was to change where the river went.

There was a divide between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi River system. Beyond it, the land descended toward the Des Plaines River. Engineers could give the water a new route downhill by cutting through that divide.

Work began in 1892. The new canal stretched for twenty-eight miles. Chicago was now preparing to send water away from Lake Michigan, instead of letting its dirty river empty into it.

Downstream communities objected. As legal opposition mounted, workers hurried to open the canal. In early January 1900, they removed the last barrier. Water could now flow from the lake toward the Mississippi system.

Missouri took Illinois to the United States Supreme Court, alleging that Chicago's sewage endangered St. Louis. One experiment even detected a bacterial tracer from upstream near the city's water intake.

But the court found the evidence insufficient to establish that Chicago had caused the alleged harm. It dismissed the case in 1906 without prejudice.

Chicago had protected its source of drinking water by changing the route of its wastewater. The river reversal was an engineering victory for the city. Whether it was a victory for the people downstream was a different question.
