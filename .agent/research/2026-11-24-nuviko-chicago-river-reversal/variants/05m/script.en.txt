Why would a city spend years making a river run in the opposite direction? Why did its neighbors try to stop the project? And how did the dispute end up involving bacteria and the United States Supreme Court?

Imagine standing on the shore of Lake Michigan in nineteenth-century Chicago. The lake looks enormous. It is the city's source of drinking water. Now walk over to the mouth of the Chicago River. This is also where the city's waste has been heading.

The problem is not difficult to understand, but escaping it is. The engineer Ellis Chesbrough helped Chicago build a tunnel beneath the lake so the city could collect water farther offshore. The workers dug from both ends, meeting underground. The new intake gave Chicago access to water away from the polluted shore. Yet outbreaks of disease continued. The lake was still receiving waste.

The geography around Chicago offered another possibility.

The city sits near the divide between two enormous drainage systems. One carries water through the Great Lakes. The other carries it toward the Mississippi. The terrain beyond the divide descends toward the Des Plaines River. If engineers could open a sufficiently large channel across that boundary, gravity could send water from Lake Michigan through Chicago and out the other side.

In 1889, the Sanitary District of Chicago was established. Construction of the new canal started in 1892. Workers cut a route twenty-eight miles long. The project cost approximately thirty-three million dollars at the time.

Picture the finished route. Lake water enters the Chicago River, travels along its South Branch and enters the new canal. From there it reaches the Des Plaines, the Illinois River, and eventually the Mississippi. The river near the lake can now carry waste away from Chicago's drinking-water source.

Look at the other end of that route, however. St. Louis also depends on river water.

Chicago had not found a place where its waste simply vanished. It had connected its drainage to a system other communities already used. Opposition to the project sharpened, and its opening became a race against possible legal intervention.

In early January 1900, only a barrier separated the canal from the water that would enter it. Accounts describe workers struggling with the frozen ground before the last obstruction was removed. January second is commemorated as the official date of the reversal. Water from Lake Michigan could now take the new course.

The legal dispute was not over.

Missouri brought a case against Illinois. It argued that sewage from Chicago was threatening the health of St. Louis. Illinois disputed that conclusion. The downstream rivers already received sewage from other communities, and the question was whether the specific danger Missouri alleged could be attributed to Chicago.

One experiment introduced a bacterial tracer into the upstream system. It was not the organism that causes typhoid. Samples later identified the tracer near the St. Louis water intake. This was striking evidence that the new connection could carry material an extraordinary distance.

But the experiment did not establish how much disease-causing material survived the journey, or whether it was responsible for illnesses in St. Louis. The court was being asked to restrain another state, and it required convincing evidence of the alleged injury.

In February 1906 the Supreme Court dismissed Missouri's case without prejudice. That did not amount to a finding that the river water was safe. It meant that the evidence in this case was insufficient.

A map of Chicago tells two stories. Near the lake, the arrows point away from the source of drinking water. Follow those arrows across the state, and they point toward other communities.

Chicago had accomplished something extraordinary. Instead of waiting for the river to take waste toward its water supply, the city had constructed a route that carried it away. The same connection that made the solution possible also created a responsibility beyond Chicago's borders. That was the part no excavation could resolve.
