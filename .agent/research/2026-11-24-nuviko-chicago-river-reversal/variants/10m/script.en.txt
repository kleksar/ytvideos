Why did Chicago build a canal to make its river flow away from Lake Michigan? How did it persuade people farther downriver to let it happen? And why did a discovery in the water at St. Louis still fail to stop Chicago in court?

In the middle of the nineteenth century, Chicago needed both clean drinking water and streets that did not fill with sewage. The land was so low that burying sewers beneath the existing streets would put them below the river. Engineer Ellis Chesbrough had a different idea. His crews laid sewers above the old ground level, then built higher streets over them. Some doorways ended up below the pavement. Building owners who could afford it had their properties lifted.

The sewers drained into the river, and the river emptied into Lake Michigan. In other words, the better Chicago became at collecting waste, the more urgently it needed to keep that waste away from the place where it collected drinking water.

Chesbrough moved the water intake about two miles offshore. Crews digging beneath the lake started at opposite ends and met underground. The tunnel delivered water from farther out, but typhoid outbreaks continued. Sewage could still reach the lake. What if Chicago gave the river a different outlet altogether?

A short distance southwest of Chicago lies the boundary between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi watershed. Beyond that divide, the ground drops toward the Des Plaines River. The obstacle was the land in between. Cut a channel through it and water from Lake Michigan could descend into the other basin, taking the city's wastewater along with it. No pump would have to lift the entire river over a ridge.

Chicago had attempted earlier changes to the movement of water, including a partial reversal in the nineteenth century. The 1900 project was the larger, permanent-scale intervention that would send substantial quantities of lake water into the drainage system leading toward the Mississippi.

But Chicago had to get permission before it could start digging. Earlier attempts to create an agency for the project had stalled in the Illinois legislature. Communities elsewhere in the state were concerned about the wastewater that would be sent their way.

A commission including Chicago mayor John Roche and state legislators offered something besides drainage: a canal that could carry steamboats. Downstate communities would gain a transport route linking the Great Lakes with the Mississippi system. Illinois passed the law in 1889. Within the proposed district, more than seventy thousand voters supported its creation; fewer than three hundred opposed it.

Construction began in 1892. The twenty-eight-mile route crossed clay and solid rock. Workers excavated those different materials with different equipment, and camps sprang up along the site. The work was dangerous. It was also costly: approximately thirty-three million dollars. Yet even after the excavation, the water could not follow its new course until the barriers separating it from the river were opened.

Consider the route that the new canal offered. Water could move from Lake Michigan into the Chicago River. From the South Branch it could travel through the canal toward the Des Plaines River. The Des Plaines joins another river to form the Illinois, which in turn reaches the Mississippi.

The new flow would carry Chicago's waste away from its intake. The shipping route, which helped secure support for the canal, also meant that other towns were connected to the city's drains.

Follow the Mississippi downstream and you approach St. Louis. That city also drew water from the river system. The new canal did not destroy Chicago's waste or guarantee that the material became harmless. It directed it toward rivers that others depended on. Communities downstream had a reason to challenge the project.

By the beginning of January 1900, officials faced the possibility of an order stopping the project. One historical reconstruction describes trustees arriving at the remaining earthen barrier to help remove it, only to find the soil frozen. A contractor finally opened a passage with excavation equipment. Accounts differ on the exact operation; January second is the date the district commemorates, while the later court opinion dates the canal's opening to January seventeenth. We do not need to settle the sequence of every opening ceremony to see what mattered: the new current had been established before Missouri could stop the discharge.

Missouri went to the United States Supreme Court against Illinois. The state alleged that sewage discharged by Chicago presented a danger to people taking water from the Mississippi system near St. Louis.

Missouri had a difficult case to make. Chicago's wastewater had to travel 357 miles to St. Louis. Other settlements discharged sewage along the way. And the Illinois River looked clearer after the canal opened, as lake water diluted its contents. Looking at the water alone could not tell anyone whether it carried typhoid.

The two sides hired experts. They disagreed over how long the organism responsible for typhoid could live in river water and how long the journey would take. Missouri's case rested in part on showing that material from Chicago could reach the St. Louis intake while still alive.

So investigators tried tracing the water with a different bacterium that could be distinguished from the organisms usually found in the river. Weeks later it was detected at the intake in St. Louis. The experiment supplied evidence of a connection that no map alone could prove.

Illinois challenged what that finding actually meant. Only a few of the tracer organisms were detected, and the tracer was somewhat more resilient than the typhoid organism. Experiments presented by Illinois suggested that typhoid bacteria might survive for a much shorter time; Missouri disputed the conditions of those experiments. The two sides had produced evidence about different things under different conditions.

There was another problem: Missouri cited rising typhoid deaths after the canal opened, but the city had also experienced a much worse year before it opened. The experts disagreed about other sources of infection and the way deaths had been classified. The judges could not treat a change over time as proof of one particular cause.

The Supreme Court considered the evidence and the arguments about other sources of pollution. In February 1906, it dismissed the case without prejudice. Missouri had not produced sufficient proof for the relief it sought. The ruling did not establish that untreated river water was safe, and it did not make further claims impossible.

The judgment did leave one practical issue on the table. St. Louis needed protection from contamination, wherever it originated. Chicago, meanwhile, eventually began treating wastewater rather than relying only on its new route and dilution. The sanitary district completed its first treatment plant in 1922.

More than a century later, the connection has another consequence. The waterway joins two basins that were once separated by a natural divide. Boats can use it, and living organisms can also move through interconnected waters. The direction in which water normally flows does not guarantee that a fish can move only in that direction.

In 2026 the United States Army Corps of Engineers announced continuing work on the Brandon Road Interbasin Project. Its purpose is to reduce the risk of invasive carp reaching the Great Lakes. Measures planned for the waterway include acoustic and bubble deterrents. A contract for initial defenses was announced that April. This was continuing work, not proof that the threat had been eliminated.

The builders had promised a new route for water and boats. A century later, engineers are working on ways to keep that route open without letting invasive fish pass into the lake. Chicago changed which communities shared a waterway. The work of deciding what can safely travel through it never really ended.
