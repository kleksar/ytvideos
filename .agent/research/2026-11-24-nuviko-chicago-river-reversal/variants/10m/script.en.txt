Why did Chicago decide that it needed a river flowing away from Lake Michigan? How could engineers make water cross the boundary between two great river systems without lifting it over a mountain? And what happened when a city farther downstream argued that Chicago's solution was making its own water dangerous?

The first clue is in a glass of water. For nineteenth-century Chicago, the vast lake beside the city was an extraordinary resource. The city could draw water from it and distribute that water to homes and businesses. The difficulty was that the same lake lay at the end of another route through the city: the Chicago River.

As the city grew, the river carried waste toward the lake. Industrial activity added to that burden. The water supply and the city's drainage were connected by geography even though they served opposite purposes. Bringing water into a city is useful only if the water is safe to drink.

One early response was to reach farther out into the lake.

Ellis Chesbrough, an engineer working on Chicago's water infrastructure, helped develop a tunnel beneath Lake Michigan. Instead of collecting water close to the shore, the city could obtain it from an intake about two miles offshore. The tunnel was dug from opposite ends until the two sections met. This was a substantial engineering accomplishment in its own right.

But the new intake did not make the lake immune to pollution. Disease outbreaks continued. The city had increased the distance between its intake and the place where waste reached the lake. It had not broken the connection between them.

To understand the more ambitious solution, step back from the lake and look at the continent.

Water falling on one side of a drainage divide may travel toward an entirely different ocean from water falling on the other. Such a divide does not have to be a dramatic mountain range. Near Chicago, the boundary between the Great Lakes and the Mississippi River basin could be crossed by excavation.

Southwest of the city, the terrain descends toward the Des Plaines River. Around Joliet, it is considerably lower than Lake Michigan. That difference in elevation offered an enormous advantage. Engineers would not have to carry every gallon of water uphill. They needed to give it an open route along which it could descend.

Chicago had attempted earlier changes to the movement of water, including a partial reversal in the nineteenth century. The 1900 project was the larger, permanent-scale intervention that would send substantial quantities of lake water into the drainage system leading toward the Mississippi.

In 1889 the Sanitary District of Chicago was established. The construction of the new canal began in September 1892. Its twenty-eight-mile route was eventually completed at a cost of approximately thirty-three million dollars.

For an engineer, the route was not just a line on a map. The ground between the lake basin and the lower rivers was an obstacle. The excavation had to provide a continuous connection across it. The project was useless if a final barrier still separated the two bodies of water. Only after the channel was open could gravity do the work that justified the excavation.

Consider the route that the new canal offered. Water could move from Lake Michigan into the Chicago River. From the South Branch it could travel through the canal toward the Des Plaines River. The Des Plaines joins another river to form the Illinois, which in turn reaches the Mississippi.

When lake water came into Chicago along this route, it could carry the city's wastewater in the opposite direction from its drinking-water supply. The city would no longer depend on maintaining a safe separation within the same lake between the inflow of waste and the intake of water.

That was the benefit Chicago saw. But the route had a second significance, visible on a map that extended beyond the city.

Follow the Mississippi downstream and you approach St. Louis. That city also drew water from the river system. The new canal did not destroy Chicago's waste or guarantee that the material became harmless. It directed it toward rivers that others depended on. Communities downstream had a reason to challenge the project.

As the opening approached, the prospect of a court order made the final work urgent. Different accounts describe the precise opening operation somewhat differently. They agree that the work was rushed in early January 1900 and that Chicago established the new flow before legal action could prevent it. Accounts describe frozen ground at the remaining barrier. January second became the official date associated with the reversal.

Water entering the new channel was the payoff of years of construction. But the physical connection was not the end of the story. It was the beginning of a dispute about what that connection meant.

Missouri went to the United States Supreme Court against Illinois. The state alleged that sewage discharged by Chicago presented a danger to people taking water from the Mississippi system near St. Louis.

Think of what Missouri needed to establish. It was not enough merely to draw a line from Chicago to St. Louis. There were other towns on that line and on the rivers feeding it. Those towns also discharged waste. Illinois argued that the new flow was diluted and that other sources could account for the danger Missouri alleged.

There were really several questions. Could material entering the system upstream reach St. Louis? If so, could the organisms that cause disease survive the journey in sufficient quantities? And could illnesses in St. Louis be attributed to this particular source rather than other contamination?

An experiment produced an arresting piece of evidence. Investigators used a bacterial tracer, an organism distinct from the one that causes typhoid. It could be identified in samples. After its introduction upstream, the tracer was eventually detected in the downstream system, including near the St. Louis intake.

The result was a powerful illustration of physical connection. It made the downstream city's objection much easier to visualize. Water that entered the system in one place could bring something to another place that was far away.

But a tracer experiment had a limitation. The fact that this organism could reach the intake did not establish that the organisms responsible for typhoid survived in the same way. It did not establish the size of the hazard or identify Chicago as the cause of specific illnesses.

The Supreme Court considered the evidence and the arguments about other sources of pollution. In February 1906, it dismissed the case without prejudice. Missouri had not produced sufficient proof for the relief it sought. The ruling did not establish that untreated river water was safe, and it did not make further claims impossible.

Chicago's engineering and Missouri's legal case had therefore answered different questions. The canal demonstrated that a city could redirect its drainage. The court considered whether the evidence justified restraining Illinois because of the alleged downstream injury.

More than a century later, the connection has another consequence. The waterway joins two basins that were once separated by a natural divide. Boats can use it, and living organisms can also move through interconnected waters. The direction in which water normally flows does not guarantee that a fish can move only in that direction.

In 2026 the United States Army Corps of Engineers announced continuing work on the Brandon Road Interbasin Project. Its purpose is to reduce the risk of invasive carp reaching the Great Lakes. Measures planned for the waterway include acoustic and bubble deterrents. A contract for initial defenses was announced that April. This was continuing work, not proof that the threat had been eliminated.

The project poses a problem that would have sounded familiar to Chicago's original engineers: how can people keep the benefits of a connection while preventing an unwanted consequence from traveling through it?

Look once more at the map. On one side, the canal allowed Chicago to direct wastewater away from the lake it drank from. On the other, it connected a vast river system to the Great Lakes. The same opening appears in both pictures.

Chicago's achievement was not that it made a river obey an impossible command. It recognized a difference in elevation and built a route that water could follow. The city changed the geography of its own water supply, and it changed the relationship between communities that shared the connected waters.

That is why the story of the river reversal does not finish at the moment the barrier gives way. A new route can solve the problem at its entrance while creating a new problem at its exit. The hardest part is deciding what should be allowed to travel through it.
