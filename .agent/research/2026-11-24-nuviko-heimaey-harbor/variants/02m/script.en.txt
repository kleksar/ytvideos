How did an Icelandic fishing town fight a volcano with seawater? And why did the town's future depend on keeping one narrow passage open?

On January 23, 1973, a fissure opened beside the town on Heimaey. A storm the day before had brought the fishing fleet back to port. Now those boats carried thousands of people to the mainland.

The eruption continued. Lava moved toward the harbor entrance, threatening the very route the islanders had used to escape. Without that harbor, the town could lose its fishing industry.

Firefighters sprayed seawater onto the advancing rock. The outside hardened, but hot lava kept moving behind it. Ordinary hoses could not reach far enough across the growing mass.

A dredging ship arrived with powerful pumps. Workers used a bulldozer to carry steel pipes onto the lava itself. As the ground moved, pipes broke. They extended the line until water could reach farther across the flow.

More pumps arrived from abroad. Crews fed water through flexible plastic pipes and sprayed the cracks in the lava. The water turned to steam, removing heat from inside the rock and building a thicker, solid barrier.

The town still suffered terrible damage. Roughly a third was destroyed, and lava reached the rear rooms of a fish-processing factory.

But the harbor stayed open. Cooling helped restrict the lava, although the eruption itself also weakened and changed course. Nobody can know exactly what would have happened without the pumps.

By mid-July, fishing boats were coming home. The passage that had carried people away was still there to bring them back.
