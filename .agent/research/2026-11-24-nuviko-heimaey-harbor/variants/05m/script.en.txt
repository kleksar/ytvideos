How can you build a defense out of the lava that's coming to destroy your town? And why were the people of one Icelandic island so determined to keep a gap between two shores?

Heimaey, off the south coast of Iceland. On January 22, 1973, the weather was so bad that fishing boats had to come back to the island. By evening, the storm had eased.

During the night, the ground split open near the town. Fire fountains rose along a fissure about a mile long. The police and fire brigade called people out of their homes.

There was already an evacuation plan for a volcanic eruption. It relied on ships coming from the mainland, which could take many hours. But the fishing fleet was waiting in the harbor. Within hours, almost five thousand civilians had been taken off the island.

That solved the first problem. The second was whether they would have a town to come back to.

Fishing boats returned to collect equipment and stocks of fish from the factories. Even that job had an unexpected complication: workers had left with the keys and paperwork for the storage facilities. The boats stayed away at night in case conditions got worse.

The volcano grew into a new mountain, Eldfell. Lava began moving toward the town and along the coast toward the entrance to the harbor.

The islanders built banks of loose volcanic material with bulldozers. But lava broke through the defenses. It cut electrical cables and one of the water pipes linking the island to the mainland. The passage into the harbor grew dangerously narrow.

The port was the center of the island's fishing business. It wasn't enough to rescue the boats. They needed somewhere to bring their catch.

A physicist named Thorbjorn Sigurgeirsson had seen how water could harden flowing lava on a nearby volcanic island. In February, firefighters began spraying seawater onto the advancing front on Heimaey.

This was not an attempt to extinguish the volcano. Cool the moving rock enough and it becomes solid. If that solid rock can hold back the hot material coming from behind, it becomes a barrier made from the lava itself.

The first pumps had a problem. As the lava accumulated, its front grew taller. Much of the water simply ran off. On March first, a dredging ship called Sandey arrived. Its pumps could send water much higher.

To reach farther inland, crews took a bulldozer up onto the moving lava. A layer of volcanic debris provided some insulation. They hauled up heavy steel pipes and connected them, only for the movement underneath to break the joints. They kept extending the pipeline, working through clouds of steam.

The harbor front could be cooled more effectively now, but another branch of lava was advancing into town. At the end of March, it destroyed dozens of homes.

More powerful pumps arrived from the United States. Their water was sent through long plastic pipes. The pipes survived because water moving through them helped keep them cool. Crews directed water into openings in the rock instead of letting it all run off the edge.

The difference was visible in later drill holes. Near the harbor, the cooled rock extended about fifteen meters down. In an uncooled area, molten lava was encountered at five meters.

The eruption continued for months. Lava forced its way into the back of a fish-processing factory before stopping. The harbor never closed. Cooling had limited the advance in important places, but natural changes in the eruption also played a role. Exactly how much damage the cooling prevented remains uncertain.

By July, the island had gained more than two square kilometers of land, while roughly a third of the town had been destroyed. Crews and returning residents cleared ash from the streets and homes.

By the middle of that month, most of the displaced trawler fleet was back in its home port. Forty families had returned, too. The harbor that had made their evacuation possible was once again a place where they could make a living.
