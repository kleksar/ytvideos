Can you defend a town from lava by turning that lava into a wall? How do you get water onto a moving field of hot rock? And how do you tell whether your defense worked, or the volcano simply ran out of energy?

On the island of Heimaey, a harbor sits between steep hills and connects the town to the sea. Fishing crews bring their catch to the waterfront, where factories process it for export. If the harbor becomes inaccessible, the problem isn't limited to a few damaged buildings. The island loses a major part of its reason for being inhabited.

On Monday, January 22, 1973, a storm forced much of the fishing fleet to return to port. The weather improved by evening. People were back at their usual activities. The local cinema was open, and accounts from that night describe an ordinary evening.

There had been small earthquakes deep beneath the region. Instruments on mainland Iceland had registered them, but with only two working stations involved, the exact source was difficult to establish. The town had no clear warning of what was about to happen.

Early the next morning, a fissure opened near the eastern edge of town. It extended for about sixteen hundred meters. Lava fountains rose near fields and houses.

The islanders had an evacuation plan drawn up in 1964, after an eruption at another island nearby. Under that plan, ships would have to come from the mainland. Their expected arrival could be fifteen to twenty-four hours after an eruption began.

Now the storm had already brought the fishing boats home. They were available to carry residents away. Aircraft also took people off the island. By midmorning, almost five thousand civilians had escaped.

In the space of a few hours, the harbor had become a rescue route. Before long, it would become the object of a rescue operation itself.

The first work on the island was not all about lava. Some people had left expecting a brief absence. Boats went back for equipment and stored fish. Factory workers had taken their keys and documents with them, slowing access to the supplies. Horses were ferried to the mainland, while sheep were carried away in aircraft. The fishing crews avoided staying overnight at the island.

Every journey back depended on the island's remaining access routes. Meanwhile, ash and fragments of rock were falling on buildings. Crews cleared roofs to reduce the risk of collapse. The eruption gradually concentrated around a growing cone, later named Eldfell.

Bulldozers began building banks of loose volcanic debris in the path of the lava. But part of the new cone gave way, overwhelming some defenses. Lava advanced toward the town and north along the shore. The island lost electrical cables and one of its two underwater water pipes.

The entrance to the harbor was getting smaller. If lava bridged the opening, the fishing boats could no longer reach their own factories. Keeping the sea passage open had become essential to a permanent return.

A physicist, Thorbjorn Sigurgeirsson, knew of an interesting effect from the nearby eruption that had formed Surtsey. Where water removed enough heat from lava, it hardened. On Heimaey, firefighters tried to exploit that effect deliberately.

The basic idea was to take heat from a small but important part of the flow. There was no need to cool the entire volcano. If enough of the advancing rock became solid, the material coming from behind would have to accumulate or find another path.

There was an important limitation. Lava can look solid on top while remaining hot and mobile inside. Spraying the face with water does not necessarily remove enough heat to resist the pressure of the material behind it.

The initial pumps could supply roughly a hundred liters a second. The front of the flow eventually stood about twenty meters high in places. Water ran down its side before reaching the upper surface. The crews needed a way to send it above and behind that wall.

On March first, the dredger Sandey arrived. It had equipment normally used to move sand. Now the ship was pumping seawater toward the lava. Its output was estimated at about four hundred liters a second under the working conditions.

But even this stream could miss the most useful spot. So the engineers tried taking the water supply onto the lava field.

A bulldozer climbed over the rock, where fallen volcanic material provided some insulation. It began clearing a road. The crew brought steel pipes more than half a meter wide onto the field. Each joint required dozens of bolts.

The pipework had been designed for a ground that stayed in one place. Here it did not. As the lava shifted, sections broke and had to be repaired. Steam made it harder to see where to work. The line was eventually extended about two hundred meters onto the lava, so water could be delivered beyond its leading edge.

At the harbor, a thicker block of hardened rock formed. The flow behind it could still press forward and pile up. Cooling one branch did not make the other branches stop.

Late in March, a separate stream overran part of the town. Homes disappeared under the advancing rock. More pumping equipment had been requested, but getting the machinery to the island, connecting it and running pipes out to the front all took time.

The first American equipment arrived by air on March twenty-sixth, followed by more deliveries. It was transferred to the island by sea. Engineers changed the planned location of the pumping station because the lava threatened the original wharf.

The new system was split into four separate units to improve reliability. Starting on March thirtieth, they were brought online in stages. Together, they supplied about a thousand liters of water a second, in addition to pumping elsewhere.

Plastic pipes were surprisingly useful. The water inside them helped prevent them from overheating, while their flexibility made them better able to withstand movement than the earlier steel lines. Workers connected long lengths at the wharf and hauled them up toward the flow.

The water could then run into cracks in the lava. As the rock cooled and fractured, it gave water access below the surface. Steam carried heat away. That was how the crews could build a deeper obstruction rather than merely cool a thin outer skin.

Afterward, drill holes provided a way to look inside. At a cooled site near the harbor entrance, the effect extended about fifteen meters down. At an uncooled site, molten rock was found five meters below the surface. The temperature there was still over a thousand degrees Celsius in July.

Those measurements showed that artificial cooling could change the interior of the flow. They did not tell anyone exactly where the lava would have stopped if the operation had never taken place.

The big advances into the town slowed at the beginning of April. At a waterfront fish factory, lava reached the rear rooms before coming to a halt. The harbor itself remained navigable. A third of the town was lost, and the remaining neighborhoods still needed to be cleaned and repaired.

Even Sigurgeirsson acknowledged that the precise amount of damage prevented could not be known. A visiting geologist, Alan Morgan, also believed changes in the lava supplied by the volcano might have contributed substantially to the halt. The defense worked on rock at particular locations; the outcome for the whole island also depended on the eruption.

By early July, Eldfell had ceased erupting. The water pumps were shut down in stages, ending on July tenth. Millions of tons of seawater had been used. Much of the new land was still hot underneath.

Volunteers and residents removed ash from the roads, dug out gardens and washed buildings. By the middle of July, most of the trawler fleet that had been working from elsewhere had returned to Heimaey. Forty families were back as well.

The island had grown by more than two square kilometers. Where homes and streets had stood, a new lava field remained. But the boats could still pass through the harbor entrance. They had carried the islanders out when the eruption began. Now they could come home, unload their catch and give the town a way to live again.
