A man listening to a homemade radio in Russia picked up a distress call from a crashed airship that its own support ship had missed.

In May 1928, Umberto Nobile and fifteen other men flew the Italia over the North Pole. On the way back, their cabin struck the ice and broke away. The rest of the airship rose again and disappeared with six men still aboard.

Nine men survived on the ice. Among the wreckage they found food, a tent and a portable radio they had brought in case they landed to do scientific work.

Radio operator Giuseppe Biagi rebuilt the damaged antenna and called for help. He could hear broadcasts from Rome, more than four thousand kilometres away. Yet the ship waiting for him in Svalbard, only a few hundred kilometres away, couldn't establish contact.

Three men set off across the ice to seek help. Biagi kept calling. On June third, Nikolai Schmidt heard the message near Arkhangelsk, roughly nineteen hundred kilometres away.

How could the radio work over that distance and fail over a much shorter one? Shortwave signals can return to Earth far beyond the point where the weaker surface signal dies out. A later reconstruction suggests this gap and disturbed conditions high in the atmosphere hindered contact.

The ship finally established communication. A Swedish pilot flew Nobile out, then crashed on his return. He too had to be rescued.

On July twelfth, the Soviet icebreaker Krasin picked up two of the men who had left on foot, then reached the five remaining at the tent. Eight of the original crew had survived.

Who finally heard their call when the expedition's own ship had failed to hear it?
