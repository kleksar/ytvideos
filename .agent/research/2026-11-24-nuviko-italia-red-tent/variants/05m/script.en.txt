Why could nine men stranded in the Arctic hear the news from Rome, but not get their own rescue ship to hear them? And why did the first successful evacuation from their camp leave another man stranded on the ice?

In May 1928, an Italian expedition set off from Ny-Ålesund in Svalbard aboard the airship Italia. Its commander, Umberto Nobile, had flown across the North Pole two years earlier. This time he had also brought scientists and equipment for a possible landing on the polar ice.

The men reached the Pole, but the journey home was much harder. They flew into headwinds, using more fuel than expected. On May twenty-fifth the airship came down. The cabin hit the ice and separated from the great envelope that held the lifting gas. The lighter part rose into the air again, carrying six men away. They were never found.

One man died in the crash. Nine survived on the ice, along with Nobile's dog. Both Nobile and another man, Natale Cecioni, were badly injured. The survivors gathered what they could from the debris. There was chocolate, concentrated food made with dried meat and fat, a tent, navigation equipment and, crucially, a little radio.

That radio had not been packed exclusively for emergencies. If the airship had landed for scientific work, the party on the ice would have needed a means of communication. Now the scientific landing equipment was keeping its owners alive.

The operator, Giuseppe Biagi, repaired the damaged antenna and began sending distress calls. The expedition's support ship, Città di Milano, was waiting in Svalbard. If Biagi could reach it, he could tell the rescuers where to look.

He had one reason to believe the radio was working. He could pick up broadcasts from Italy, over four thousand kilometres away. Sometimes he could hear the ship as well. He was receiving news about the missing expedition while sitting in the middle of its wreckage. But the ship could not reliably pick up his calls.

The men were drifting with the ice. Three of them, the Swedish meteorologist Finn Malmgren and two Italian navigators, left camp to try to reach land.

Then, on June third, a Russian amateur named Nikolai Schmidt picked up a distress signal on a receiver he had built himself. He was near Arkhangelsk, around nineteen hundred kilometres away. He raised the alarm. Communication between the tent and the ship began a few days later.

The strange difference in distance has a possible physical explanation. Some shortwave energy travels near the ground, but another part can bend back towards Earth in the upper atmosphere. Between the two reception areas there can be a gap. A retrospective scientific study suggests that this gap, along with disturbances in the upper atmosphere, was a major obstacle. The weak transmitter, improvised antenna and noise at the receiving station made matters worse. The radio was not simply broken.

Knowing where the men were was only the next step. Planes could drop supplies, but landing on a sheet of drifting ice was another matter entirely.

On June twenty-third, a Swedish pilot, Einar Lundborg, managed to land. According to the Norwegian historical account, Nobile asked him to take the injured Cecioni first. Lundborg insisted on taking Nobile. The commander left with his dog. His departure later became a subject of fierce criticism.

Lundborg went back for more men. His plane crashed on the ice. Instead of another survivor leaving the camp, the others now had another castaway. A second Swedish pilot eventually got Lundborg out, but changing ice conditions prevented further flights from evacuating the expedition's men.

On July twelfth, the Soviet icebreaker Krasin found the two surviving men from the party that had left on foot. Malmgren had died. Later that day the icebreaker reached the five men at the tent.

Eight members of the original sixteen came home alive. The international rescue had also claimed lives, including that of the explorer Roald Amundsen, whose aircraft disappeared during the search.

Biagi's little radio had worked. It just hadn't worked over the one path they needed most. The signal that the ship missed reached a stranger far away, and the stranger helped turn an invisible camp on moving ice into a place rescuers could find.
