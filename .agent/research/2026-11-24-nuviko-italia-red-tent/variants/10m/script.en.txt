How could a radio call from men trapped on Arctic ice reach a stranger almost two thousand kilometres away, yet fail to reach their own ship? And when rescuers finally knew where to look, what kept the men on the ice for weeks longer?

In 1926, Umberto Nobile had crossed the North Pole in the airship Norge with Roald Amundsen and Lincoln Ellsworth. Two years later he returned with an Italian expedition aboard the Italia. This time the scientists had brought equipment for measurements during flight and for a possible stop on the polar ice. That distinction would matter when the men lost their airship.

They took off from Ny-Ålesund in Svalbard on May twenty-third, 1928, and reached the Pole. The flight back into headwinds consumed more fuel than planned. On May twenty-fifth they could no longer keep the airship in the air. The precise chain of failures that brought it down remains disputed.

When it struck the ice, the cabin separated from the upper part of the craft. There was still enough lifting gas for that part to rise without the weight of the cabin. Six men were carried away with it, including the physicist Aldo Pontremoli. No one ever found them.

Ten men had come down onto the ice. One, the mechanic Vincenzo Pomella, died. Nine were alive, some with serious injuries. Nobile and Natale Cecioni could not simply walk away from the site. The survivors searched the debris for what they could use.

They recovered a tent and food estimated to last forty-five days. The scientific party had also brought the instruments and tables to calculate its position from the sun. They could work out where they were when conditions allowed, but the result would not remain true indefinitely: their camp was on drifting sea ice. They needed to send updated positions to somebody who could reach them.

Among the equipment was a portable transmitter called Ondina 33, brought along for the planned scientific landing. The antenna had been damaged, but the radio itself survived. The operator, Giuseppe Biagi, rebuilt an antenna and started calling for the support ship Città di Milano, waiting in King's Bay in Svalbard.

Biagi had a schedule. At five minutes to every odd-numbered hour he transmitted and waited for the ship to answer, the arrangement established before the accident. He was sending with about five watts of power. The ship's transmitter was rated at ten thousand watts, so it was possible for Biagi to hear it without being heard in return.

Then Biagi heard a broadcast from Rome. It carried news of the missing expedition. The people the announcer was talking about were listening more than four thousand kilometres away, among the pieces of their own aircraft. Their ship was only about three hundred and fifty kilometres from the camp, yet there was no reliable way to reply to it. One reported reception of a distress call aboard the ship is disputed.

The broadcasts from Rome gave Biagi something concrete to try. He had been calling on one frequency, so he tried the frequency on which he could hear Italy. For a while there was still no useful answer.

The radio was not the only way the men tried to find help. Finn Malmgren, Filippo Zappi and Adalberto Mariano left the camp to walk towards land. The others, including the badly injured, stayed with the tent and radio.

On June third, nine days after the crash, Nikolai Schmidt picked up the distress call near Arkhangelsk. His receiver was homemade. He had heard Biagi on the frequency the operator had adopted after listening to Rome. Schmidt passed on the news, and around June sixth the tent and ship began exchanging messages. Even then, contact came and went.

The four places now form a strange picture. Rome could reach the camp, the camp could reach the area around Arkhangelsk, and the nearby ship struggled to hear it.

A shortwave signal can travel along the Earth's surface until it becomes too weak to use. Another part can travel upwards and return to the ground far away, bent by an electrically charged part of the atmosphere. In between, there can be a gap in reception. A powerful station far away may be audible while a much closer receiver gets nothing from a weak transmitter.

Researchers who reconstructed the radio conditions decades later found that, at the frequency Biagi was using, that gap could cover much of Svalbard but leave the region near Arkhangelsk within reach. And there was another obstacle to the surface route: the signal had to travel across ice, land and mountains. The damaged antenna and changing conditions high in the atmosphere made it harder still. That reconstruction cannot establish what happened to each individual message, but it explains why moving the receiver closer was not necessarily the answer.

There were two practical uses for the scientific equipment in camp. One was to locate the men and keep in contact. The other emerged in the same days when that first contact was finally established. The physicist František Běhounek had recovered an instrument for measuring radiation. Its chamber needed to be electrically charged, and the available batteries could not do the job. According to his own later account, Cecioni supplied a piece of fleece. Běhounek rubbed an amber cigarette holder with it to obtain a charge.

Between June sixth and ninth, Běhounek recorded twenty-five observations on the ice. For other measurements he lowered the instrument into the sea, where the moving ice limited how long it could stay submerged. He was carrying out a part of the scientific expedition on the site of its disaster. Those movements of the ice posed a much bigger problem for anyone trying to land an aircraft.

Meanwhile an international search had grown around the missing crew. Norwegian, Swedish, Italian and Soviet rescuers took part, among others. Amundsen joined the effort aboard a French flying boat. It disappeared on the journey from Tromsø to Svalbard; searchers later found parts of the aircraft but never its crew.

In June, the Italian pilot Umberto Maddalena located the camp and dropped supplies. A radio position could guide a plane close enough to find the tent, and a supply drop did not require a runway. To take an injured man away, a pilot had to land, and then have a suitable surface left for takeoff.

On June twenty-third, Swedish pilot Einar Lundborg landed. The badly injured Nobile was evacuated, along with his dog. A Norwegian historical account records that Nobile had asked for Cecioni to be taken first, but Lundborg insisted on Nobile. The commander would later face harsh criticism over this decision.

Lundborg returned for another passenger and crashed. Now the rescuers had one more man to recover. The men still at the tent had contact with the outside world, but the plane beside them could not take them anywhere.

While the air rescue was stalled, Biagi and the other men were still working on the radio. They lengthened the rebuilt antenna and in early July used lower frequencies, around six megahertz for transmissions. The radio records show a stable link at last. By changing the equipment on the ice, they had achieved something the first nine days of calling had failed to produce. On July sixth a second Swedish pilot managed to take Lundborg away in a lighter plane. It did not establish a reliable route for the five expedition members still at the tent.

By then a ship approaching the camp had another task as well. There were still the men who had set out on foot, whose fate was separate from that of the group around the radio. On July twelfth the Soviet icebreaker Krasin found Zappi and Mariano alive. Malmgren had died during their journey. Later that day Krasin reached the five at the tent. Almost seven weeks had passed since the cabin hit the ice.

Eight of the sixteen men aboard the Italia survived. Běhounek also brought his observations home, and scientific work from the expedition appeared in print in 1929, including work credited to colleagues who had died. The radio and instruments had been packed so men could go down onto the ice and return to an airship. The airship was gone. Instead it took a stranger with a homemade receiver, an altered antenna and an icebreaker to help bring the men and their work home.
