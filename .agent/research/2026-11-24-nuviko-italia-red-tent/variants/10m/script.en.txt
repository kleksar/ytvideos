Why did a distress call from the Arctic reach a homemade radio nearly two thousand kilometres away, while the expedition's own ship missed it? How did a scientific expedition end up conducting research on the very ice where its men were waiting to be rescued? And why did bringing them home take so much longer than finding their camp?

In 1926, Umberto Nobile had crossed the North Pole in an airship called Norge with the Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen and the American Lincoln Ellsworth. Two years later, Nobile was preparing another polar flight, this time with an Italian expedition and an airship called Italia. It was also a scientific undertaking. Among the men aboard were specialists in weather and physics. There were instruments for making observations in flight and others for use if they could stop on the ice.

They took off from Ny-Ålesund in Svalbard on May twenty-third, 1928, and reached the Pole. The flight back into headwinds consumed more fuel than planned. On May twenty-fifth they could no longer keep the airship in the air. The precise chain of failures that brought it down remains disputed.

When it struck the ice, the cabin separated from the upper part of the craft. There was still enough lifting gas for that part to rise without the weight of the cabin. Six men were carried away with it, including the physicist Aldo Pontremoli. No one ever found them.

Ten men had come down onto the ice. One, the mechanic Vincenzo Pomella, died. Nine were alive, some with serious injuries. Nobile and Natale Cecioni could not simply walk away from the site. The survivors searched the debris for what they could use.

They had a tent and enough recovered food for an estimated forty-five days. A tent that had been intended for a scientific stop was now a home on the sea ice. They gathered sextants, chronometers and the tables needed to work out their position when the sun was visible. There was no fixed piece of land under their shelter. The location they calculated could change as the ice drifted.

Among the equipment was a portable transmitter called Ondina 33, brought along for the planned scientific landing. The antenna had been damaged, but the radio itself survived. The operator, Giuseppe Biagi, rebuilt an antenna and started calling for the support ship Città di Milano, waiting in King's Bay in Svalbard.

The transmission power of the little set was about five watts. The ship's transmitter was rated at ten thousand. That did not mean communication in both directions would work. In fact, Biagi was getting signals the ship was not getting from him.

He could listen to broadcasts from Rome. The news was about an expedition that had disappeared. Rome was more than four thousand kilometres from the survivors, yet it was coming in clearly. The much closer ship, a few hundred kilometres away, had only intermittent, disturbed contact in the other direction. There was one disputed report of a distress call being heard aboard it, but no dependable link.

Biagi had originally called on a frequency of nine point one megahertz. He also tried nine point four, the frequency on which he heard the Rome station. Day after day there was no useful reply.

At this point it was possible for the people in the tent to know that a search was under way, without being able to tell the searchers where they were. Three members of the expedition, Finn Malmgren, Filippo Zappi and Adalberto Mariano, decided to make for land on foot. They left the others with the radio.

On June third, a young radio amateur named Nikolai Schmidt picked up the call near Arkhangelsk. He was about nineteen hundred kilometres from the tent and had built his own receiver. He passed on word of what he had heard. Around June sixth the tent and the ship in Svalbard began to make contact, although reception was still unreliable.

Why did Schmidt's receiver work where the ship's equipment had failed?

Imagine a radio wave that travels a short distance along the surface and fades. Another part of the same signal travels upwards. At a suitable frequency, the electrically charged region of the atmosphere can bend it back towards Earth. The first place where that part can be received may be a long way from the transmitter. Between those two areas lies a region where neither path gives usable reception.

That gap changes with frequency and with conditions in the upper atmosphere. The equipment in 1928 could not tell the men what that region looked like over the Arctic. Decades later, a reconstruction using historical geomagnetic observations and modern models suggested that the distance gap and disturbances in the ionosphere significantly affected the signal. Other obstacles included the low power of the camp's set, the unstable antenna and radio interference around the ship. It is not possible to replay every call or assign the entire delay to a single cause. But the reception in Russia need not mean that the support ship was simply failing to listen.

The survivors continued to improve their antenna. By early July, the radio records show a much clearer link at lower frequencies. The camp could receive updates and pass on information needed by the rescue operation.

Something else was happening at the tent while the search went on. The Czech physicist František Běhounek had recovered an instrument designed to measure how radiation ionizes air inside a sealed chamber. He wanted to continue the scientific work. But the expedition's batteries were insufficient for charging his instrument. In his own later account, he found a way around that problem using an amber cigarette holder and a piece of fleece supplied by Cecioni. Rubbing them together produced the electrical charge.

He went out repeatedly to read the instrument. He even lowered it into the sea for some observations, although moving ice limited the time it could remain underwater. Between June sixth and ninth he recorded twenty-five measurements on the ice. The scientific purpose that had brought the radio and tent onto the airship was still alive in the middle of the rescue.

Meanwhile an international search had grown around the missing crew. Norwegian, Swedish, Italian and Soviet rescuers took part, among others. Amundsen joined the effort aboard a French flying boat. It disappeared on the journey from Tromsø to Svalbard; searchers later found parts of the aircraft but never its crew.

An Italian pilot, Umberto Maddalena, located the camp in June and dropped supplies. But finding a tent from the air and getting a plane onto the ice were very different operations. The ice could move or break, and conditions could deteriorate between one flight and the next.

On June twenty-third, Swedish pilot Einar Lundborg landed. The badly injured Nobile was evacuated, along with his dog. A Norwegian historical account records that Nobile had asked for Cecioni to be taken first, but Lundborg insisted on Nobile. The commander would later face harsh criticism over this decision.

Lundborg returned for another passenger and his aircraft crashed. He was now one of the people waiting for help. On July sixth, another Swedish pilot managed to remove him in a lighter aircraft. That did not open a reliable route for the others. Five expedition members were still at the camp.

The solution came from the sea rather than the air. On July twelfth the Soviet icebreaker Krasin found Zappi and Mariano, two of the three men who had set out across the ice. They were alive. Malmgren had died during their journey. Later the same day the ship reached the five remaining at the tent. The men had spent nearly seven weeks on the ice.

Eight of the sixteen men who left Svalbard in the airship survived. Běhounek brought his scientific results back; his expedition measurements appeared in print in 1929, including work credited to colleagues who had died. There was no way to bring back the lost part of the airship or the rescuers who had disappeared.

The original scientific landing never happened as planned. Instead, a tent, instruments and a portable radio meant for that landing survived a crash. The radio's signals went farther than the camp's own ship, and a stranger who heard them helped make the rescue possible.
