Bag Rothstein wasn't a passenger. It was a name used to reserve an empty seat in first class.

The man sitting next to that seat had paid a fortune to fly for the rest of his life.

In 1987, Steven Rothstein bought a lifetime pass from American Airlines for two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. Two years later, he paid another hundred and fifty thousand for the right to bring a companion on each flight.

For more than twenty years, he was one of the airline's most frequent travelers.

But in the booking system, some of his companions were people who didn't exist. One name, Steven Rothstein Junior, appeared at least forty-one times in just a few months.

In April 2004, the airline told him he couldn't use the companion benefit to keep the seat next to him empty. He stopped making reservations under that particular name.

Four years later, American canceled his entire lifetime pass. Rothstein sued. He argued that the airline had allowed his booking practices for years, and that taking away the pass was unfair.

There was something important in the contract: American could cancel the pass for fraudulent use. But could the airline really decide that for itself?

A federal judge ruled in 2011 that the company still had to act in good faith. And even without deciding the disputed issues around other bookings, the judge found that the fake companion reservations were sufficient grounds for cancellation.

Rothstein lost his contract claim.

He hadn't reached a maximum number of flights. The unlimited pass had a different limit: a companion seat was for a companion. And in the records of the dispute, there was a particularly revealing name.
