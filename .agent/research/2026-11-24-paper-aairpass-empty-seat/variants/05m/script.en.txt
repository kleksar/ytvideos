How can an airline cancel a ticket that's supposed to last until the day you die? And why did a seat with nobody in it matter more to a judge than the number of flights the owner had taken?

In 1987, a financier named Steven Rothstein made a deal with American Airlines. Instead of buying another ticket each time he needed to travel, he paid two hundred and fifty thousand dollars up front. For that price, he could fly in first class on American for the rest of his life.

He had asked question after question before signing. Some of the answers even made it into the agreement. He negotiated special treatment for his wife: she could count as his companion when she took the flight immediately before or after his.

Two years after the original purchase, Rothstein paid another hundred and fifty thousand dollars for the companion benefit. Now the total was four hundred thousand. He could reserve a first-class seat for himself and another for someone traveling with him.

And Rothstein really used the pass. For more than twenty years, he was among the airline's most frequent flyers. His daughter later recalled that he could finish a business trip without her even realizing he'd left.

Then something strange showed up in the reservations. There were seats booked for Steven Rothstein Junior. And there were seats booked for a passenger named Bag Rothstein.

These were invented names. Rothstein acknowledged using them to keep a seat beside him empty. He'd used the Junior name at least forty-one times between December 2003 and April 2004.

Think about what the airline had sold him. It had promised to carry two people when he traveled with a companion. It hadn't explicitly sold him two seats to use however he liked.

In April 2004, the airline told Rothstein that booking an empty seat this way wasn't allowed. He stopped using that particular Junior name. But the story didn't end with the warning. American also investigated other reservations, including bookings for companions who might not actually travel.

The company canceled his pass in December 2008. One day he had the right to reserve a first-class flight for life. The next time he wanted to book a flight without that pass, he'd have to find another way to pay for it.

Rothstein took American to court. His argument wasn't simply that he loved flying. He said the company had tolerated his practices for years. And he had evidence that an airline sales representative had described fraudulent use much more narrowly when he bought the pass.

That brought the dispute back to a few lines of the original agreement. Paragraph twelve let American revoke the pass if it determined the customer had used it fraudulently. The written contract also said earlier communications didn't change the signed terms.

But that didn't mean the airline could take the pass away for absolutely any reason. The federal judge applied Texas law and explained that the company still had an obligation to act in good faith.

Some of the bookings remained disputed. American itself acknowledged that the agreement didn't specifically address speculative reservations or require advance notice when a passenger wouldn't turn up. So what if the judge put those arguments aside?

That's what made the invented names so important. The judge reasoned that Rothstein had bought the right to take a companion, not the unconditional right to reserve two seats for himself. Calling an imaginary passenger a companion was sufficient for the airline to invoke the cancellation clause in good faith.

On June thirtieth, 2011, the judge ruled for American on Rothstein's breach-of-contract claim. The airline's separate claims against him weren't resolved by that decision, and it wasn't a criminal verdict.

It also wasn't a finding that Rothstein had flown too often. He had paid for unlimited flights. His problem was that one of the benefits he used to make those flights more comfortable had a condition attached to it.

There is no need to calculate the price of every flight he ever took to understand why the lifetime deal ended. American had sold him passage for himself, and passage for a companion. In court, the difference between those two promises came down to an empty seat.
