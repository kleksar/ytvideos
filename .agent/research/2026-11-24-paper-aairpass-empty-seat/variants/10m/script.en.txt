What does a lifetime airline ticket actually buy you? If the airline decides it wants the ticket back, can it simply cancel the deal? And how did a passenger who never existed become part of the answer in a dispute over a four-hundred-thousand-dollar pass?

In 1987, Steven Rothstein was a financier who traveled frequently. He entered into an extraordinary agreement with American Airlines. In exchange for two hundred and fifty thousand dollars, paid in advance, he received the right to travel in first class on American for the rest of his life.

American received the money at once, while Rothstein depended on the airline continuing to fly for decades. He had considered its prospects before buying. And he did something that would matter years later: he negotiated the terms rather than simply accepting the printed deal.

He questioned the sales representative about the details before signing. He even negotiated changes to the agreement. One was a special arrangement for his wife. If she traveled on the flight just before or just after his, she could be treated as his companion, even though she was not actually sitting next to him.

Two years later, Rothstein paid another hundred and fifty thousand dollars for the companion feature. With both payments, he had spent four hundred thousand dollars. The deal covered unlimited first-class flights for him, and allowed him to reserve a place for one companion per flight, subject to the applicable rules.

In the airline's booking records, however, the name of one of his companions looked unusual. The passenger was called Bag Rothstein. Another was listed as Steven Rothstein Junior. Years later, under questioning in the lawsuit, Rothstein acknowledged that both were invented names. He had reserved those seats to keep the one beside him empty.

For more than twenty years, Rothstein put the agreement to extensive use. He became one of American's most frequent travelers. His family later described how naturally flying had become part of his working life.

The airline had records of at least forty-one reservations under the Junior name in a period of just a few months, from December 2003 to April 2004. Rothstein would later challenge the employee's evidence for that number. The judge accepted it: the employee had relied on flight records that the airline kept in its regular business, and those records had been produced in the case.

In April 2004, American warned Rothstein that his pass did not entitle him to reserve an empty seat this way. After that, he stopped booking under the Junior name. The warning was important, because it put a specific disagreement on the record while Rothstein still held the pass.

American continued examining his reservation history. The airline identified other patterns that it treated as fraudulent. It objected, for instance, to speculative reservations for companions, substitutions at the airport, bookings it considered incompatible with one another, and certain cancellations or failures to turn up.

Some of those reservations raised a different problem. Imagine putting your brother's name on a seat because you hope he can come, and then discovering that he cannot. American objected to speculative bookings, but acknowledged that the written agreement did not expressly address them or require advance notice when a passenger would not show up. That left open a question that the made-up names did not: what had been agreed about a real person who might never get on the plane?

In December 2008, the airline terminated Rothstein's pass. He no longer had the privilege he had paid to exercise for life.

He sued American for breach of contract. American responded with claims of its own. And that brought both parties back to a document written when he first became an unlimited-pass customer.

Rothstein had an unusual advantage when he challenged the cancellation. The salesman who had handled his original purchase gave a written declaration in his support. According to that declaration, the salesman had explained fraudulent use much more narrowly: the pass holder letting another person use his pass. That would be very different from reserving a seat beside himself.

Remember the special arrangement for his wife. It showed that Rothstein could ask for an exception and get it written into the deal. The judge found that the signed contract superseded earlier communications and that changes required a writing signed by both parties. The salesman's explanation had not become such a term.

Paragraph twelve gave the airline the right to revoke a pass for use it determined to be fraudulent. The clause also described forfeiture without a refund. Rothstein challenged the provision from several directions.

Rothstein argued that this provision handed the airline far too much power. It had already collected four hundred thousand dollars. If the company alone could decide what counted as misuse, what stopped it from taking away the service at will? The judge found a limit in the law of Texas, which governed the agreement. American had to use its discretion in good faith.

Rothstein had a third argument. American had dealt with him for many years. Its conduct, he argued, showed how both sides understood the terms. If the company had accepted his way of booking for so long, why should it now be treated as grounds to cancel the pass?

But another provision of the agreement said that a failure to enforce a contractual right did not mean American had surrendered that right. And again, the contract could not be changed simply by one party's practices over the years.

Now the judge had to apply those rules to the reservations themselves. Some additional facts supplied by American were treated as admitted because Rothstein had not filed the required response. But the judge did not need that procedural advantage to settle his contract claim. She could set those facts aside, along with the disputed speculative bookings, and ask whether the admitted use of fictitious names was enough.

Yes. The fabricated names were not a hypothetical problem. Rothstein had acknowledged them. A companion seat, in the judge's reasoning, came with the condition that there actually be a companion. The airline had not sold him a separate unlimited pass for a second seat.

On June thirtieth, 2011, the federal court granted American judgment on Rothstein's breach-of-contract claim. The ruling did not establish that every disputed booking was fraudulent. The judge found that the fictitious companion bookings were enough to support the airline's decision in good faith.

Rothstein had also attacked the no-refund provision as an improper contractual penalty. The judge separated the right to stop providing the service from questions about the money. Winning on the first question did not give American an automatic award on its own claims.

American said that on roughly half a dozen specific flights, it had lost the opportunity to sell seats that Rothstein had reserved for companions he did not intend to bring. It alleged losses of about fifty-eight thousand six hundred and fifty dollars on those flights. That figure concerned the counterclaims, not the cost of all his travel, and no court had awarded it to the airline.

Both sides asked the judge to rule on those remaining claims. By then, American's parent company was in bankruptcy. In February 2012, the judge declined to decide the motions for the time being. A trial could cost substantial money over a comparatively small claim. She gave American time to choose where to pursue it and encouraged the parties to revisit settlement.

Then the airline dropped its remaining counterclaims. The parties filed a joint stipulation in September 2012, and the court dismissed the claims with prejudice. The airline's specific loss allegation was not turned into a damages award in this case. Rothstein still did not have his pass back.

In January 2014, he appealed. The Seventh Circuit's mandate dated July eighteenth of that year left the district court's judgment in place without a full new trial. In November the appellate court also refused a request to file a late petition for rehearing.

For the airline, the remaining money claims had disappeared. For Rothstein, the judgment that ended his flying privileges still stood. His daughter was writing about the loss of the pass as late as 2019. The case had not established a maximum number of journeys or a total price for his decades of flying.

He had bought first-class travel for the rest of his life and paid extra to take someone with him. The company had even agreed to a special exception for his wife. But the judges did not find a promise of an extra seat whenever he traveled alone. After years of litigation, that was still the distinction that cost him his lifetime ticket.
