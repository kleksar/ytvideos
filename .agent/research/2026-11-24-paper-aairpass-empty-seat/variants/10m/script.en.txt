What does a lifetime airline ticket actually buy you? If the airline decides it wants the ticket back, can it simply cancel the deal? And how did a passenger who never existed become part of the answer in a dispute over a four-hundred-thousand-dollar pass?

In 1987, Steven Rothstein was a financier who traveled frequently. He entered into an extraordinary agreement with American Airlines. In exchange for two hundred and fifty thousand dollars, paid in advance, he received the right to travel in first class on American for the rest of his life.

For an ordinary traveler, the price of a ticket is a decision made before each flight. Rothstein was making that decision for decades at a time. The airline got the money immediately. He would receive the service over the years that followed.

That arrangement depended, of course, on the airline still being there to carry him. The court would later note that Rothstein had considered the company's future viability when he made the investment. This wasn't an impulse purchase at an airport desk.

He questioned the sales representative about the details before signing. He even negotiated changes to the agreement. One was a special arrangement for his wife. If she traveled on the flight just before or just after his, she could be treated as his companion, even though she was not actually sitting next to him.

Two years later, Rothstein paid another hundred and fifty thousand dollars for the companion feature. With both payments, he had spent four hundred thousand dollars. The deal covered unlimited first-class flights for him, and allowed him to reserve a place for one companion per flight, subject to the applicable rules.

For more than twenty years, Rothstein put the agreement to extensive use. He became one of American's most frequent travelers. His family later described how naturally flying had become part of his working life.

There was a difference, though, between what the airline had sold him and what the names in its reservation records suggested he was doing.

One passenger was called Steven Rothstein Junior. Another was called Bag Rothstein. In a deposition years later, Rothstein acknowledged that they were fictional names he had used for companion bookings.

The purpose of those reservations was to secure an empty seat beside him. The airline's records showed the Junior name used at least forty-one times between December 2003 and April 2004.

This was not a dispute over a passenger flying once with a friend, or a traveler discovering at the last minute that a relative couldn't come. Those are situations in which a real person might be expected to take a reserved place. Here the companion had never existed.

In April 2004, American warned Rothstein that his pass did not entitle him to reserve an empty seat this way. After that, he stopped booking under the Junior name. The warning was important, because it put a specific disagreement on the record while Rothstein still held the pass.

American continued examining his reservation history. The airline identified other patterns that it treated as fraudulent. It objected, for instance, to speculative reservations for companions, substitutions at the airport, bookings it considered incompatible with one another, and certain cancellations or failures to turn up.

Not every one of these objections was equally straightforward. The agreement didn't expressly address speculative bookings, and American acknowledged that it didn't specifically require advance notice of a no-show. That distinction would eventually become central to the case.

In December 2008, the airline terminated Rothstein's pass. He no longer had the privilege he had paid to exercise for life.

He sued American for breach of contract. American responded with claims of its own. And that brought both parties back to a document written when he first became an unlimited-pass customer.

Paragraph twelve gave the airline the right to revoke a pass for use it determined to be fraudulent. The clause also described forfeiture without a refund. Rothstein challenged the provision from several directions.

First, he argued that such a forfeiture was an improper contractual penalty. For the judge, this raised two different questions. Could the airline stop providing the service? And if it could, did that settle how much money either party should receive?

Imagine a company that terminates an agreement and then demands compensation for what happened before the termination. Whether it had grounds to stop performing its side of the deal doesn't, by itself, calculate those damages. The judge applied that distinction here. The right to terminate could be considered separately from the question of money.

Rothstein also argued that the provision gave American an excessive amount of power. The company was effectively deciding for itself whether the customer had violated the deal. If a lifetime pass can be taken away whenever the seller dislikes what the buyer is doing, what is the lifetime promise worth?

There was another piece of evidence in Rothstein's favor. The sales representative who had sold him the pass gave a declaration saying he had described fraud in narrower terms, as someone else using Rothstein's pass.

The court noted, however, that Rothstein had negotiated successfully and had specifically asked about fraudulent use. The signed agreement superseded earlier communications, and changes had to be made in writing with both parties' agreement. The sales representative's narrower explanation had not been made part of the written bargain.

Even then, the judge did not give American an unconditional right to cancel. The agreement was governed by Texas law, and the judge held that the airline had to exercise its discretion in good faith.

Rothstein had a third argument. American had dealt with him for many years. Its conduct, he argued, showed how both sides understood the terms. If the company had accepted his way of booking for so long, why should it now be treated as grounds to cancel the pass?

But another provision of the agreement said that a failure to enforce a contractual right did not mean American had surrendered that right. And again, the contract could not be changed simply by one party's practices over the years.

Now consider the position the judge faced. Some of the airline's evidence involved bookings for people who might or might not have joined a flight. There were also additional facts that the court treated as admitted because Rothstein had not responded to them in the required procedural form. Could the judge reach a decision even without relying on those additional facts?

Yes. The fabricated names were not a hypothetical problem. Rothstein had acknowledged them. A companion seat, in the judge's reasoning, came with the condition that there actually be a companion. The airline had not sold him a separate unlimited pass for a second seat.

On June thirtieth, 2011, the federal court granted American judgment on Rothstein's breach-of-contract claim. The ruling did not establish that every disputed booking was fraudulent. The judge found that the fictitious companion bookings were enough to support the airline's decision in good faith.

That answered the question of whether the company was entitled to terminate. But the fight over money had not ended. The airline alleged losses of about fifty-eight thousand six hundred and fifty dollars in its counterclaim, relating to specific flights. That was an allegation, not a court award.

Then the balance of the dispute shifted again. Before the court ruled on later motions concerning the counterclaims, American's parent company entered bankruptcy proceedings. In February 2012, the judge declined to decide those motions at that point, giving the airline time to consider where to pursue its claims and encouraging settlement discussions.

The available appeal docket shows that Rothstein appealed in January 2014. It doesn't tell us the final outcome of the appeal. What we can say is that the 2011 ruling established why American was permitted to cancel, and his daughter was still writing about the lost pass in 2019.

When we see a headline about an airline selling an unlimited ticket and regretting it, the natural question is how much the passenger must have cost the company. But the answer to why this man's ticket could be taken away didn't require a total of all the flights he'd ever taken.

He had paid for a lifetime of travel for himself. He had paid extra to share that travel with other people. In court, the decisive distinction was that an empty chair was not another person.
