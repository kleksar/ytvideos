In 2005, a British student offered to sell every pixel on a blank web page.

Alex Tew needed money for university. He made a square a thousand pixels wide and a thousand pixels tall. A dollar per pixel, with a hundred dollars as the smallest purchase. Buyers could put an image there and link it to their own website.

His first customer was a friend. Then his brother bought space for his go-kart business. A press release brought in news coverage, and visitors came to see how much of the page had been sold. Advertisers followed those visitors.

Soon there were only a thousand pixels left. Tew put them on eBay. Instead of a thousand dollars, they fetched thirty-eight thousand one hundred. His total sales topped a million.

Then the page went down.

Someone had demanded money from Tew and overwhelmed his website with traffic. He refused to pay. A security company helped bring the page back in exchange for publicity.

The colorful billboard survived. But there was another problem that nobody could fix just by keeping Tew's website online.

In 2017, a study found that links attached to three hundred and forty-two thousand pixels no longer worked. Another hundred and forty-five thousand pixels sent visitors to different domains or pages selling domain names.

Nearly half the billboard's area pointed somewhere broken or changed. You could still see the ads, even when you could no longer visit the shops behind them.

So how did all those little ads end up together on one page?
