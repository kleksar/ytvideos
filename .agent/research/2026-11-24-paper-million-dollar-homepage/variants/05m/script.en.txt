How did a student turn a blank web page into more than a million dollars? And why, years later, could you still see the advertisements without being able to visit many of the websites behind them?

In August 2005, Alex Tew was twenty-one and preparing to study business at university in England. He faced a bill for his first term's fees and accommodation. He wanted a way to make money before classes began.

He opened a website called the Million Dollar Homepage. In the middle was an empty square, one thousand pixels wide and one thousand pixels high. Multiply the two numbers and you have a million pixels. Tew put a price on each one: one dollar.

You couldn't buy just a single dot. The smallest space was ten by ten pixels, costing a hundred dollars. Buy several adjoining blocks and you could display a larger image. Clicking the image took visitors to your own website.

But there was an obvious problem. Why pay to put an advertisement on a page nobody has heard of?

A friend bought four hundred pixels to promote his beatboxing website. Tew's brother used another part of the grid for his go-kart business. Now at least there was something on the page to look at.

Tew sent out a press release, and the BBC picked up the story. People had a reason to visit even if they weren't interested in any of the ads. They could watch the square fill up and see whether this student would really manage to sell the whole thing.

Those visitors made the advertising space more valuable. New buyers brought more color to the page, and the growing mosaic gave the next visitor another reason to come and look.

There were dating businesses, shops selling model trains, and even a yarn mill in New England. Tew started with a page so small that friends could help fill it. Now he needed friends to help maintain it while he gave interviews.

By January 2006, just one thousand pixels remained. At the original price, they would bring in one thousand dollars. But these were also the last available spaces on a page that had become famous.

Tew listed them on eBay. On January eleventh, the bidding ended at thirty-eight thousand one hundred dollars. Add that to the nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand he had already sold, and the gross sales came to one million, thirty-seven thousand, one hundred dollars.

Yet there was a problem with collecting the money and walking away. Tew had promised to keep the advertisements on the site for at least five years.

Around the time of the auction, he received demands for money. When he refused, the page was overwhelmed with traffic and went offline. The demand eventually reached fifty thousand dollars.

A web security company offered protection in exchange for publicity. The site came back, and the final advertiser got its space. Tew had sold the entire square.

Now imagine returning twelve years after the site launched. The grid still looks full. But each little picture has two parts: the image stored on Tew's page and the address it opens. Keeping one does not guarantee the survival of the other.

In 2017, the links on the page were examined. More than five hundred were unreachable. Almost another five hundred redirected visitors to a different domain or a place where domain names were sold.

The broken addresses accounted for three hundred and forty-two thousand pixels of advertising space. Redirects covered another hundred and forty-five thousand. Together, that's nearly half the area.

And even some of the links that still opened a page no longer appeared to show what their advertisers had originally intended.

The student had managed to keep his billboard online. What he couldn't preserve by himself was the world of small websites that made the billboard useful. The very same collection of little advertisements that once made people curious enough to click had become a record of places they could no longer visit.
