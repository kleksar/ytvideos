What made people pay for tiny dots on a computer screen, until a twenty-one-year-old student had collected over a million dollars? What happened when his page went offline just as he finished selling it? And what was left of that million-dollar billboard when someone tried following all its links years later?

In August 2005, Alex Tew was about to start a business course at Nottingham University in England. According to Tew, his first term's fees and accommodation came to seven thousand pounds. He was looking for an idea that could bring in money before he accumulated student debt.

He had already tried selling recordings of a beatboxing event online. This time, he wanted an idea that was easy to explain and would attract attention outside the small market of people who knew his previous work.

He came up with a name that did most of the explaining for him: the Million Dollar Homepage. He put a thousand-by-thousand-pixel square on a web page. Each pixel was offered for one dollar. If the whole square sold, that was a million dollars.

A hundred pixels was the smallest order, forming a little square ten pixels on each side. A buyer could purchase neighboring squares to make a larger space, submit a picture, and attach a link to it. Tew committed to keep the ads available for five years.

Imagine trying to sell that first hundred-dollar space. If you're advertising a shop, you don't just want somewhere to display your logo. You want potential customers to see it. And an almost empty website with no established audience doesn't offer much of that.

Tew started with people he knew. His first customer bought four hundred pixels for a beatboxing website. Another part of the grid went to his brother, who advertised a go-kart business.

Now Tew had something he could show people: a website with a very strange goal and a few businesses that had already put money into it. He sent out a press release. When the BBC covered the idea, it helped bring people to the page.

The site wasn't selling a new kind of advertising technology. What it had was a simple story that visitors could check for themselves. How far had the student got? How many spaces were still empty? Who on earth was paying for them?

That last question was particularly useful to the advertisers. A person might never have searched for a New England yarn mill on the internet. But one of those mills bought a small space in the mosaic. Suddenly its picture was sitting right where curious visitors were looking. Other buyers included dating services and sellers of miniature trains.

There was a feedback loop. Every article about Tew could bring in new visitors. Those visitors created a reason for businesses to spend a hundred dollars on a tiny image. Each sale brought the million-dollar goal a little closer and gave journalists another development to report.

It wasn't possible to know how many additional sales each buyer actually made because of that little picture. Some may have received very little useful traffic. But the speed at which the advertising spaces sold showed that people were willing to pay to be part of the page.

Tew had to get friends to help look after the website while he answered questions from the media. In November, he and his brother traveled to the United States for interviews. By the beginning of January, the thing that made his idea possible, the fixed size of the square, had become its biggest limitation. Once the last space was filled, there would be nothing else to sell.

He decided to change the way he sold the final thousand pixels. Instead of asking the same one-dollar price, he listed them on eBay.

A business buying one of the early spaces had been taking a chance on a virtually empty page. The winner of this auction would be buying the last available area on a page whose name was already known. On January eleventh, 2006, the auction ended at thirty-eight thousand one hundred dollars.

The original ninety-nine point nine percent of the square had brought in nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand dollars. That last tenth of one percent brought in nearly forty times its original price. Altogether the sales reached one million, thirty-seven thousand, one hundred dollars, before expenses and taxes.

Tew had solved the problem of finding buyers. He still had to deliver what he had sold.

In the days around the auction, messages arrived threatening to disable his website unless he paid. He refused. The people behind the demand eventually asked for fifty thousand dollars. The site stopped working under a flood of traffic.

This was a problem for Tew's customers as well as for him. They had paid for ads that were supposed to remain accessible. The grid might be sold out, but the one page on which all those transactions depended could no longer be reached.

A web security company agreed to help protect the site in return for being mentioned by Tew. The page came back and the last buyer received its pixels. The identity of the attackers was not conclusively established.

With the page sold out, Tew eventually left university. In December 2006 he launched another project called Pixelotto. It addressed an awkward feature of his first business: there was no new inventory once the square was full. This time he offered ad space for two dollars per pixel and proposed a large prize for a visitor, followed by a fresh round of advertising.

The second project did not reproduce the original result. In January 2008, a technology publication reported that the eventual prize was one hundred and fifty-three thousand dollars, well short of the proposed million-dollar prize. That isn't a verified figure for Tew's personal earnings. It does show why the first page's success shouldn't be treated as a formula that automatically works whenever somebody draws a grid.

Meanwhile, the original website was still there. Its customers' websites, however, were changing.

In 2014, a published analysis counted six hundred and seventy-five failed links out of just over three thousand tested. The check classified pages that did not load quickly enough as failures. But some addresses that passed the test had changed hands. They led to parked domains with advertising, rather than to the old businesses.

That was a different kind of disappearance. Imagine a shop closing but its sign remaining fixed to a public noticeboard. The old address might still point to a building. It doesn't mean you can buy anything from the shop that put up the sign.

In July 2017, a separate study examined two thousand eight hundred and sixteen links occupying almost the entire grid. It classified five hundred and forty-seven as unreachable and another four hundred and eighty-nine as redirecting to different domains or domain resale pages.

Counting the links doesn't tell us how much of the mosaic that represents. A small ten-by-ten ad and a huge picture each count as one address. So the study also measured the area associated with those links.

Three hundred and forty-two thousand pixels pointed to inaccessible destinations. Another hundred and forty-five thousand pointed to redirected destinations. The two categories together accounted for nearly half the million-pixel square. And even among the reachable links, many no longer appeared to serve their original purpose.

That was a snapshot from 2017, not a measurement of today's web. It also didn't mean that half the advertisers' money had been wasted. An ad might have brought customers for years before its owner's website closed.

But if you wanted to preserve this little piece of internet history, it was no longer enough to save an image of the colorful grid. You would also need historical copies of the pages the images once opened. Some of those copies may survive in web archives. Others may be gone.

Tew's project had made a million dollars because it turned a collection of unrelated ads into something people wanted to see. Twelve years later, the same collection showed just how much of that early internet had changed. The pictures were still there, almost like signs on the windows of a street whose shops had moved away.
