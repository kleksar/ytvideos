How did a student sell an empty web page for more than a million dollars? Why did he find himself facing a fifty-thousand-dollar demand just as the last space sold? And what happened to the businesses behind the pictures he had promised to keep online?

In August 2005, Alex Tew was about to start a business course at Nottingham University in England. According to Tew, his first term's fees and accommodation came to seven thousand pounds. He was looking for an idea that could bring in money before he accumulated student debt.

Tew had already sold recordings of a beatboxing event over the internet. For this project, he needed people who had never heard of that event to understand the idea immediately.

He came up with a name that did most of the explaining for him: the Million Dollar Homepage. He put a thousand-by-thousand-pixel square on a web page. Each pixel was offered for one dollar. If the whole square sold, that was a million dollars.

A hundred pixels was the smallest order, forming a little square ten pixels on each side. A buyer could purchase neighboring squares to make a larger space, submit a picture, and attach a link to it. Tew committed to keep the ads available for five years.

Imagine trying to sell that first hundred-dollar space. If you're advertising a shop, you don't just want somewhere to display your logo. You want potential customers to see it. And an almost empty website with no established audience doesn't offer much of that.

Tew started with people he knew. His first customer bought four hundred pixels for a beatboxing website. Another part of the grid went to his brother, who advertised a go-kart business.

Now Tew had something he could show people: a website with a very strange goal and a few businesses that had already put money into it. He sent out a press release. When the BBC covered the idea, it helped bring people to the page.

Visitors could follow the progress without buying anything. The more of the square that sold, the more interesting the next visit became. It was also a place where very different businesses could appear side by side.

A yarn mill in New England spent a hundred dollars on the minimum order. It got a tiny picture in the same mosaic as dating services and model railway dealers. Later, a business called Pixellance bought enough adjoining blocks to make the biggest image on the page. At the original price, that picture occupied ten thousand eight hundred dollars' worth of space. Remember that name.

Every sale gave the press something new to report, and every new reader was a potential visitor to the page. A business could buy an audience that other businesses were helping attract. The billboard itself had become the attraction.

We don't know what those visits were worth to each business. But the orders kept coming in, and Tew had to get friends to maintain the site while he dealt with interviews.

Tew and his brother went to the United States for interviews in November. By New Year's Day, nearly the entire square was sold. There were still people who wanted to buy space, but only one thousand pixels remained. If he opened a second page, it would no longer be the one page everyone was following.

On January first, Tew put the final thousand pixels on eBay. A buyer would get the only space left, even if someone else had paid a dollar per pixel for an identical area just months earlier.

The price climbed so high that, on the eve of the sale, the Washington Post was reporting bids of more than a hundred and forty thousand dollars. That was not money Tew had received. When the auction closed on January eleventh, the successful bid was thirty-eight thousand one hundred dollars.

The last block alone sold for over thirty-eight times its original price. Add that payment to the nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand dollars from the earlier sales, and the total is one million, thirty-seven thousand, one hundred dollars, before costs and taxes.

There was one thing Tew couldn't do with a sold-out billboard: turn it off. He had promised his buyers five years of advertising, and every single picture depended on the same page being accessible.

Around the auction he began receiving threats. He refused to pay. The demand eventually reached fifty thousand dollars. In a later interview, Tew recalled that his hosting company had warned him that the site was unusually slow. He initially put it down to the volume of visitors. Then the page stopped loading.

For the people whose ads had just become famous, this was a blackout of their shared billboard. Anyone attempting to see the last purchase or click on a hundred-dollar picture faced the same unavailable page. The customers' own sites did not have to fail for their advertising to disappear.

A web security company agreed to help protect the site in return for being mentioned by Tew. The page came back and the last buyer received its pixels. The identity of the attackers was not conclusively established.

Afterwards Tew left university and, in December 2006, tried selling pixels again. This time Pixelotto offered them at two dollars each and proposed a large prize, after which a new round of advertising could begin. A January 2008 report put the eventual prize at a hundred and fifty-three thousand dollars rather than the million envisaged. The exact business proceeds are unknown. What mattered for the first billboard was its one-off event: there was no guarantee of an equally interested crowd a second time.

The original site remained online. But the links in the mosaic were beginning to tell a different story.

In 2014, an analysis found six hundred and seventy-five failures among just over three thousand links it tested. The test had a time limit for loading. Some addresses that did open had already been repurposed. A successful connection was therefore not proof that the original destination still existed.

In 2017, a researcher visiting the site found that even some of its own menu items led to missing pages. The colorful square itself, though, was still there. So he examined the links attached to the pictures, including the largest one.

That ten-thousand-eight-hundred-dollar Pixellance graphic was still visible. The site named in it appeared to be defunct. A photograph of the billboard would preserve the large picture, but it would not tell you what the visitor once saw after clicking it. The problem was now visible even in the biggest ad.

The researcher tested two thousand eight hundred and sixteen links and then did something more revealing than simply counting the failures. Each link was tied to a particular area of the grid. A hundred-dollar square and the biggest picture should not count equally if the question was how much of the billboard had changed.

In the 2017 snapshot, inaccessible addresses occupied three hundred and forty-two thousand pixels; another hundred and forty-five thousand led elsewhere. Together they represented nearly half the entire square. Even among the links that opened, the researcher found many that appeared to have lost their original purpose.

The two studies used different methods, so they cannot tell us a precise rate at which the page decayed. Nor can they tell us how much money buyers made from their ads while those links still worked.

If you wanted to reconstruct what a visitor could do in 2006, you would need more than the billboard. You would have to recover the old sites it pointed to, and the links between them. Archived copies may help, but we do not know how many of the original destinations can be restored.

Tew had put together a website that was worth a million dollars because people wanted to look at its ads and follow them somewhere. In 2017 you could still find that enormous Pixellance graphic and see exactly where its buyer's space had been. What the buyer once wanted you to see on the other side of that picture was a different matter.
