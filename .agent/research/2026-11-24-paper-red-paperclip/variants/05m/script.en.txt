Why would someone with a year of free rent exchange it for a few hours with a rock star? And why would a Canadian town give that same man a house in exchange for a movie role?

In July 2005, Kyle MacDonald was living in Montreal. He remembered a game he had played as a child: you start with a small object and see what you can exchange it for. With each new object, you approach someone else.

Kyle took a red paperclip from his desk and put its picture on the internet. The idea was simple enough for anyone to join. You didn't need to buy a ticket, and you didn't have to make him a cash offer. You just had to have something he would accept.

Two women in Vancouver offered him a novelty pen shaped like a fish. A woman in Seattle wanted the pen and had a sculpted doorknob. A camping stove came next, then a portable generator.

Now Kyle had something much more expensive than the paperclip. And he was about to discover why that could be a problem.

The generator was bulky. It wasn't easy to find someone who needed it. During a trip to New York, a complaint about its smell brought firefighters to his accommodation, and the generator was temporarily confiscated.

He got it back, but the deal that finally ended this stage of the project wasn't for another large machine. It was for a beer keg, a neon sign and an agreement that the keg would be filled. Kyle turned those three things into a package he called an instant party. In his blog, he admitted that the parts probably wouldn't fetch as much money as his generator.

That was a change in the project. Rather than always looking for a larger object, Kyle could offer something people might enjoy doing. A Quebec radio and television personality took the party in exchange for a snowmobile. A magazine offered a trip. Part of the trip was exchanged for a truck.

The truck became a recording contract. A musician in Phoenix had housing she could rent out and wanted the chance to record. Kyle exchanged the contract for a year's rent.

By now the project was attracting newspaper, television and radio coverage. An unusual offer could reach thousands of people. Kyle's next trade demonstrated just how much that mattered.

A restaurant manager in Phoenix wanted the free rent. She offered an afternoon with her boss, who happened to be Alice Cooper. Kyle accepted.

Then came an offer from a KISS fan with a collection of memorabilia. Kyle picked a snow globe. Readers who had watched him get as far as housing complained that he was throwing away his progress.

But he had been contacted earlier by Corbin Bernsen, an actor and director making a film. Bernsen wanted to offer a paid speaking part in exchange for something from Kyle's project. And Bernsen had a collection of thousands of snow globes.

Kyle sent him a picture. Bernsen wanted this particular globe. The apparent step backwards gave Kyle exactly what he needed for the next exchange.

When the movie role appeared on the blog, it caught the attention of Bert Roach, an economic development officer in Kipling, Saskatchewan. What could a little town do with a movie role? It could give somebody a chance to audition in front of a real director. And it could invite the public to the place where the famous paperclip challenge would finish.

The council acquired an empty rental house and offered it to Kyle in exchange for the role. He accepted. The town held its talent contest, and a local resident, Nolan Hubbard, subsequently appeared in Bernsen's film.

On July 12, 2006, exactly a year after Kyle began, the exchange for the house was completed. The town later put up a giant red paperclip and continued attracting visitors. Bernsen even returned to make another movie there.

Try to price that last deal as two ordinary goods on a secondhand market and it doesn't make much sense. But Kyle wanted somewhere to live, Bernsen wanted an object for his collection, and Kipling wanted people to come to town.

The house wasn't the reward for a paperclip that had somehow become more valuable. It was the last stop in a chain of people who had very different things to gain.
