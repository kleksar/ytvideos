Why did Kyle MacDonald give up a year's rent when he was trying to get a house? Why did he spend almost two months trying to get rid of one of his trades? And what did the town that gave him a house receive in return?

In July 2005, Kyle and his girlfriend were living in rented accommodation in Montreal. He put a photograph of a red paperclip on the internet. It was a game he remembered from childhood: exchange a small object for another one, then see what the next person would offer. He was willing to travel for the deals.

Two women in Vancouver answered. They had a pen shaped like a fish. Kyle traveled to make the exchange. The pen was unusual enough to attract the attention of a sculptor in Seattle. She had made a doorknob with a face on it, and wanted the pen.

A man in Massachusetts offered a camping stove for the knob. Years later Kyle explained that the buyer had a particular use for it: replacing a knob on an espresso maker. The little handmade object had found a person who could use it. The buyer didn't have to share Kyle's ambition or want the next thing in the chain.

In California, a Marine who wanted that type of stove offered an electrical generator. The red paperclip was gone, but Kyle now had a useful machine that could make electricity.

That was where the neat game stopped working.

A portable generator is much harder to carry across the country than a pen.

Kyle traveled to New York to make the next trade. A smell from the generator prompted complaints where he was staying. Firefighters took the machine away. Suddenly he couldn't even offer the object on which his entire project depended.

He was able to recover it and arrange the exchange. His customer wanted the generator for use outside the city. In return, Kyle received a keg, a neon beer sign and a promise that the keg could be filled later.

At the exchange, they powered the neon sign with the generator. Both sides could see what they were getting.

Kyle's blog recorded that it had taken fifty-three days to get from the generator to the next exchange. It also recorded something more important: he didn't think the party equipment would necessarily sell for as much as the generator.

If this was supposed to be a ladder of rising prices, he had stepped down a rung. But the new package was easier to describe to people who weren't looking for a specific machine. He was offering the ingredients for a party. He also told his readers that he was now open to trading experiences and promises, not just physical objects.

That gave the project a wider range of possible offers.

A Quebec media personality had a snowmobile he was willing to exchange. Kyle took it. It was winter in Canada, and a snowmobile magazine offered a travel package in exchange for the machine.

The publicity was beginning to change the kinds of deals he could make. The magazine could promote its activity to people following the project. A travel reservation, although it weighed nothing, could be valuable to someone else. Kyle's next exchange turned a portion of the trip into a box truck.

The next offer came from a recording studio. Kyle handed over the truck and got a contract to record music.

One such musician, Jody Gnant, had a property in Phoenix, Arizona, with space available to rent. She agreed to exchange a year of rent for the recording opportunity.

It looked as if Kyle was now one step away from his goal. He had reached the point where his next trade could conceivably involve a piece of property. Instead, a manager at a restaurant in Phoenix offered him a few hours with her employer. He was Alice Cooper.

To her, the rent was valuable. To a fan, the chance to meet a famous musician could be valuable. Kyle gave up the rent.

Then a man with KISS memorabilia contacted him. Among the collector's possessions was a snow globe. Kyle chose it.

The readers following his blog had seen the paperclip become a truck and then a place to live. Now it had become a souvenir. Some of them responded with disbelief and anger.

There was a reason Kyle's readers couldn't see. During a radio appearance in Los Angeles, his story had reached actor and director Corbin Bernsen. Bernsen offered a paid speaking role in his upcoming film. According to Kyle's account at the time, he deliberately kept the offer off his blog. He wanted to find something the actor would genuinely accept in exchange rather than take the role as a favor.

Kyle found out that Bernsen was a collector of snow globes. When the KISS fan offered that globe, Kyle had a possible buyer in mind. He sent Bernsen a picture, and Bernsen agreed to the exchange.

According to Kyle, this was the only time during the project that he had the next trade lined up in advance. To the people following his progress, the reason for the apparent setback only became clear when the next deal was made public.

The movie role created a different problem. A role has no single resale price, and Kyle wasn't selling tickets to see the movie. He needed a participant who wanted to use the opportunity rather than keep it on a shelf.

In Kipling, Saskatchewan, economic development officer Bert Roach saw the project as a way to get attention for his community. He took the idea to the council. A town could turn one movie role into a local event: invite people to audition, bring in a director, and give visitors a reason to come.

The council acquired an unoccupied rental house on Main Street and offered it to Kyle. The town was spending actual money. Its development officer said the purchase price was below the usual local house price of about fifty thousand Canadian dollars, without disclosing the amount. The exchange also required the town to put up a giant red paperclip. Once the role was used, visitors would still have something to come and see.

On July 12, 2006, a year after the experiment started, Kyle completed his fourteenth trade.

The project didn't end when Kyle got the keys. On July 12, 2007, exactly a year after the house trade, the promised landmark was unveiled. Auditions took place in 2007 too. Bernsen chose a local resident, Nolan Hubbard, who went to Hollywood and appeared in Donna on Demand.

Later Bernsen returned to Kipling and made a feature film called Rust, with townspeople among the performers. The original prize gave one person a chance to work in a movie. Now filmmaking had come to the community. And in 2025, a local news report described visitors still stopping at the giant paperclip and the house, which had changed owners and become a café.

None of this means you can go outside with a paperclip today and reproduce the same outcome. The trades involved travel, time, a growing audience, and people who saw an advantage in participating. Kyle had no guarantee that the next person would appear.

The first paperclip left Kyle's hand in 2005. Twenty years later, visitors were still coming to see a giant version of it in Kipling. The town had exchanged a house for the chance to become part of a story that people wanted to see for themselves.
