How could nearly every household in one village win the same lottery? What did the only man without a winning ticket do when he saw his neighbors celebrating? And what changed in the village once the money reached their bank accounts?

In 2011, Sodeto was a small farming community in northern Spain. The surrounding fields were suffering from drought, and the economic crisis had made it difficult for families to keep up with their debts. Some farmers had borrowed money to modernize their irrigation systems.

Every Christmas, a local women's association sold lottery shares to raise funds for its activities. This was a familiar arrangement: part of the payment went to the association, while the rest bought a fraction of a ticket.

Spain's Christmas lottery worked differently from a draw with one unique ticket for the jackpot. Many tickets carried the same five-digit number, and groups could divide their tickets into smaller shares. In Sodeto the women distributed shares of number 58268.

They visited their neighbors before the draw. One address, however, was left out. It belonged to Costis Mitsotakis, a Greek filmmaker who had moved into a converted barn beyond the village. He didn't seek out a share himself.

On December twenty-second, the winning number was announced in Madrid. It was 58268.

A standard twenty-euro ticket bearing that number paid four hundred thousand euros. Even a small share was now worth a substantial amount of money.

The mayor, Rosa Pons, broadcast the news to the village. People came into the square, where the celebration quickly spread. What might have been a single household's good fortune had reached almost every household.

Costis heard about the commotion from a friend on a train. He went to the square, fetched his camera and recorded the people celebrating. They asked him how many shares he had. He hadn't bought any.

At first he didn't understand the scale of their winnings. Once he did, his own situation had become the exception that would make headlines around the world.

But there was another story unfolding right in front of his camera. The villagers had to decide what to do with the money.

Banks sent representatives. Salespeople offered luxury cars. A Jaguar appeared in the square, but several residents had more pressing uses for their winnings. A new tractor could be used every working day. An improved irrigation system could save hours of labor. Paying off the loans already taken out to modernize the farms could make those investments affordable.

Many residents used their money for precisely those things. Others renovated their homes, bought cars or traveled. The village didn't simply turn into a community of people who had retired.

And the money didn't stop at the doors of the winners.

Costis had previously been unable to find a buyer for some of his land. The day after the win, a neighbor called and offered to buy it. Another neighbor called the following day. Costis could have tried to make them compete, but said he didn't want to create resentment in a small community.

He was also receiving a new kind of opportunity. His footage of the celebration could become the beginning of a film about what happens after a whole village becomes wealthy. A trailer appeared in 2012, and he continued collecting interviews.

The project took far longer than announced. In 2017, he was still living near Sodeto and talking about how the money had affected relationships in the village. He thought people had become somewhat more focused on their own households. One of the women responsible for the winning shares saw it differently: the farmers still worked, and many families no longer had the burden of debt.

In December 2025, Costis said the documentary was still in progress. There is no need to imagine a final premiere or a fortune from the film to understand what he got out of that day. He had found a subject to keep returning to.

And the neighbors who had missed his house in 2011 had changed their routine. They now put aside a share for him. Costis said he paid for it.

The lottery was over long ago. The women still knew which house not to forget.
