United Airlines asked a passenger for permission to use his complaint video to train its own employees.

A year earlier, that same passenger couldn't even get the airline to pay for a guitar repair.

In March 2008, Canadian musician Dave Carroll was flying to Nebraska with his band. During a connection in Chicago, a passenger spotted baggage handlers tossing guitar cases. The band's bass player saw his own instrument being thrown.

Carroll tried to tell airline employees what was happening. He was directed to deal with it in Omaha.

The group arrived late at night. Carroll's case looked fine from the outside. At soundcheck the next day, he discovered that the bottom of his Taylor guitar had been smashed.

The repair cost twelve hundred dollars.

He tried making a claim in Halifax, where his trip had started. The local partner airline documented the damage but denied responsibility. United sent him from one office to another. At one point he was even asked to bring the guitar back to Chicago for inspection.

After nine months, he couldn't get twelve hundred dollars in flight vouchers.

So he made a music video with his friends and posted it on YouTube.

He'd hoped to reach a million views in a year. By July tenth, 2009, the video had already passed a million.

United contacted him with an apology and an offer. This time Carroll declined and suggested a charitable donation instead. United gave three thousand dollars to a jazz education institute.

The airline had gone from refusing his request to making a request of its own.

United didn't just want to settle a complaint about a broken guitar.
