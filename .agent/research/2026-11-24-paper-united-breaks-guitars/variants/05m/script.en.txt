How did a musician turn a twelve-hundred-dollar repair bill into a complaint heard around the world? And why did the airline he complained about end up asking to use his video?

Dave Carroll played in a Canadian band called Sons of Maxwell. In March 2008 the group had a week of performances booked in Nebraska. They were flying from Halifax to Omaha, changing planes in Chicago.

While they waited to get off the first plane, a woman near Carroll noticed guitar cases being thrown outside. The bass player looked through the window and recognized his own instrument. Carroll began trying to get an airline employee to intervene.

Inside the aircraft he was directed to an agent on the ground. That didn't get him anywhere. At the gate he was told to take the matter up in Omaha, where the band was heading anyway.

They got there late at night. Carroll could see no employee to report to, and his guitar's outer case showed no obvious damage. He went to the hotel.

The following day, the band was setting up to play. Carroll took out his Taylor guitar and discovered the damage to its body.

This wasn't an instrument he'd bought for that tour. He'd used it for years to write and record music. A repair could make it playable again, but replacing its sentimental value was another matter.

When the group returned to Omaha at the end of the week, Carroll brought the problem to United. He was told to begin the claim back in Halifax.

That sounded simple enough. But in Halifax, the airline's local partner was Air Canada. A representative there inspected the guitar and created a claim, then denied responsibility for the damage attributed to United.

Now Carroll had a reference number. He could give it to United on the phone, but getting the company to retrieve the claim and resolve it was another problem. Eventually someone at the Chicago baggage office asked him to bring the instrument in for inspection.

He lived in Halifax, Canada. The guitar would need another trip just to be examined by the company.

Carroll tried another office. He faxed the information, waited, called again and was told it hadn't arrived. When the employee looked for it, she found it. By the time he called back, the number was no longer in service.

Months went by. Carroll paid twelve hundred dollars to repair the Taylor himself. It could be played again, although, in his judgment, it wasn't quite the same guitar.

United's eventual response listed reasons for refusing the claim, including the delay in reporting the damage in Omaha and the involvement of Air Canada.

Carroll tried one last compromise. Instead of insisting on a cash reimbursement, he asked for flight vouchers worth the cost of the repair. He could use them for future trips. That request was refused too.

That's when Carroll proposed something that had nothing to do with the airline's claim forms. He would write three songs about the experience, make videos and try to get a million views in a year.

He and his friends made a comic music video. They could turn the airport into a small set and tell the story without another call to the baggage department. On July sixth, 2009, he put the first video on YouTube.

Within days he reached the target he'd given himself a year to achieve. Television interviews followed. Taylor Guitars, the company that made the damaged instrument, responded as well.

And United called.

The airline apologized and offered compensation. Its customer solutions director also asked whether the company could use the video for internal training.

Carroll declined the offer and suggested that the money go to charity. United eventually donated three thousand dollars to the Thelonious Monk Institute of Jazz.

You may have heard that his song cost the airline a hundred and eighty million dollars in stock market value. That causal claim isn't established. The real reversal needs no such number.

For months Carroll had been trying to persuade United to recognize a problem. Now a senior representative of United wanted permission to show that very problem to its own employees.

And Carroll still had the guitar he'd paid to repair.
