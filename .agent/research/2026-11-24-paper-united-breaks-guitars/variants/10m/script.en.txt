What happens when an airline refuses to pay for a damaged guitar and its owner decides to make a song about it? Why did the company eventually ask for permission to show the song's video to its staff? And did that musician really wipe out a hundred and eighty million dollars in stock market value?

The story starts with a working band, not a marketing department.

Dave Carroll was a Canadian musician playing with Sons of Maxwell. They had engagements in Nebraska and were due to spend a week traveling between performances. On March thirty-first, 2008, they flew from Halifax to Omaha with a connection at Chicago's O'Hare airport.

After the first flight landed, the musicians were waiting to leave the plane. A passenger noticed something through the window. People handling baggage outside were throwing guitar cases. The band's bass player looked out and saw his own instrument being thrown. Carroll understood that his guitar had been handled that way too.

He was carrying a Taylor acoustic guitar he'd owned for more than ten years. It had cost thirty-five hundred dollars. He had used it on recordings and to write songs. For a musician going to a series of performances, a damaged instrument is a problem even if the eventual repair bill is affordable.

Carroll attempted to alert a flight attendant, who directed him to an agent outside. He approached the person he had been directed to. She didn't resolve the matter. At the gate, another employee raised the issue of a baggage waiver. Carroll said he hadn't signed one. In the end, he was directed to the people in Omaha.

His connecting flight arrived late. By his account, he couldn't see anyone available from the airline, and he could not see damage to the outside of the protective case. He took it to the hotel.

The next day, when the group was checking its sound, he opened up his guitar and found that the bottom of the body had been smashed.

That detail about the case matters. Carroll had already complained about the way instruments were being handled. But he had not yet established that his own guitar had been damaged. By the time he found the damage, he had left the airport. He would now need the airline to connect two separate events: something reported during a connection and something discovered at a performance the following day.

There was no time during the tour to simply return to the airport in Omaha whenever a baggage representative became available. A week later, when the band was back there to fly home, he explained the problem to United. He was told that his claim belonged at the airport where the trip had started.

In Halifax, he was dealing with Air Canada, United's local partner. He called United and was directed back to the airport with the guitar. An Air Canada employee looked at the damage and created a claim. Air Canada denied responsibility, on the basis that the damage attributed to United employees in Chicago wasn't Air Canada's to pay for.

Carroll now had something concrete to work with: a claim number. He called United. Its representatives had trouble finding the claim in their system. On subsequent calls it would eventually turn up, but finding the claim didn't result in payment.

Several months after the flight, he was directed to the baggage office in Chicago. One proposed solution was to bring the damaged guitar into that airport for inspection.

Imagine being told to take your broken guitar from Canada to another country so that somebody could look at the damage that had already been recorded at your local airport. The proposed inspection might have made sense if Carroll had lived in Chicago. It was of little practical help to him at home.

He was sent on to another baggage office, this time in New York. Someone asked him to fax his information. He sent the fax. After weeks without a reply he called and learned the employee hadn't seen it. She looked for it and found it. He was asked to call back after a couple of days. When he did, the number had been disconnected.

By now Carroll had the guitar repaired at his own expense. Twelve hundred dollars had gone into making it playable. For the rest of the dispute, the question was no longer who would fix the instrument, but who would bear a cost he had already incurred.

United finally sent him a denial. Carroll's account gives several reasons he was offered: he hadn't reported his damaged guitar when he arrived in Omaha, he hadn't reported it within twenty-four hours there, and Air Canada had already denied a claim. His earlier attempts to alert employees in Chicago had not produced the evidence the process required.

There was one last possibility. Carroll offered to accept flight vouchers instead of money. He was a touring musician; a credit for future travel could compensate him for the repair without asking the airline for a cash payment. United refused.

At this point he stopped treating the problem as something to be solved by a better phone call. He announced that he'd write three songs and make three videos about what had happened.

The format was a natural fit for him. A musician who could perform a song for an audience already had a way to tell a story in a few minutes. He could put an airline's name in the title, give a particular incident a beginning and an ending, and let people pass the video to somebody else who had never met him.

He made the first video with friends. The action on screen was comedy, a reenactment, not footage recorded on the day his guitar was damaged. That distinction is worth making. An entertaining retelling helped the complaint travel, but it wasn't a second eyewitness recording of what had happened outside the airplane.

On July sixth, 2009, he released the first song. His original goal was a million views over the following year. On July tenth he was on American television, and the video had already passed a million. Taylor Guitars reported more than three million views by July seventeenth.

The guitar manufacturer was now part of the story. It could help a musician whose damaged instrument bore its name. United, meanwhile, was being asked publicly about a claim it had previously refused.

The airline's customer solutions director, Rob Bradford, called Carroll to apologize. United offered compensation, but Carroll declined. He suggested donating the money instead. The company ultimately gave three thousand dollars to the Thelonious Monk Institute of Jazz.

The call included a second request: could United use his video to train employees?

That didn't mean every part of the claims process had been fixed. We know the airline asked to use the video; we can't infer how many employees actually saw it or whether the company prevented similar cases afterward. What changed in this particular dispute was clear enough. Carroll was now in a position to answer a request from United, instead of continually being redirected by it.

There is an even bigger claim often attached to the story: that the song caused the airline to lose a hundred and eighty million dollars in market value. A change in the price of shares during a viral event doesn't identify the cause. Airlines were already under financial pressure in 2009, during a recession. Without establishing what the airline's share price would have done in the absence of the video, you cannot assign the movement to this particular complaint. The donation and the call are documented actions; the supposed stock market punishment is not a demonstrated result of the song.

Carroll did go on to complete the other two songs. He also continued playing music and later wrote a book about his experience. In a 2012 interview he described the opportunities that had followed the video, including work as a speaker. The guitar was still part of his life.

It would have been perfectly possible to tell this story as a spectacle about an airline losing millions. But the tangible outcome is much closer to the original problem.

A musician had an instrument that no longer played quite the way it once did. He'd paid to have it repaired. No new video could undo that damage. What he could change was who got to hear about it.

And the airline that had refused to cover the repair bill ended up asking whether it could show its own employees the song about that refusal.
