How did a musician get an airline to take a twelve-hundred-dollar repair seriously after nine months of refusals? And why did that airline eventually ask to show his complaint to its own employees?

Dave Carroll was a Canadian musician playing with Sons of Maxwell. They had engagements in Nebraska and were due to spend a week traveling between performances. On March thirty-first, 2008, they flew from Halifax to Omaha with a connection at Chicago's O'Hare airport.

As the band waited to get off the first plane, a passenger noticed guitar cases being thrown outside. The bass player looked out and saw his own bass being tossed. Carroll believed his Taylor had been thrown as well. He began looking for someone who could intervene.

He was carrying a Taylor acoustic guitar he'd owned for more than ten years. It had cost thirty-five hundred dollars. He had used it on recordings and to write songs. For a musician going to a series of performances, a damaged instrument is a problem even if the eventual repair bill is affordable.

Carroll attempted to alert a flight attendant, who directed him to an agent outside. He approached the person he had been directed to. She didn't resolve the matter. At the gate, another employee raised the issue of a baggage waiver. Carroll said he hadn't signed one. In the end, he was directed to the people in Omaha.

His connecting flight arrived late. By his account, he couldn't see anyone available from the airline, and he could not see damage to the outside of the protective case. He took it to the hotel.

The next day, while the group was setting up for a performance, he took the guitar out of its case. The bottom of its body had been smashed.

He had tried to warn employees in Chicago, but he hadn't yet seen the damage to his own guitar. By the time he could show it to anyone, he was already away from the airport. That gap would come back to haunt his claim.

The band had left Omaha that morning and would not return for seven days, Carroll recalled. On his way home he finally showed up at United's airport desk. He was told to file the claim where the journey had begun.

In Halifax, he was dealing with Air Canada, United's local partner. He called United and was directed back to the airport with the guitar. An Air Canada employee looked at the damage and created a claim. Air Canada denied responsibility, on the basis that the damage attributed to United employees in Chicago wasn't Air Canada's to pay for.

Carroll now had something concrete to work with: a claim number. He called United. Its representatives had trouble finding the claim in their system. On subsequent calls it would eventually turn up, but finding the claim didn't result in payment.

Several months after the flight, he was directed to the baggage office in Chicago. One proposed solution was to bring the damaged guitar into that airport for inspection.

The inspection would require another international trip, although the damage had already been recorded in Halifax.

He was sent on to another baggage office, this time in New York. Someone asked him to fax his information. He sent the fax. After weeks without a reply he called and learned the employee hadn't seen it. She looked for it and found it. He was asked to call back after a couple of days. When he did, the number had been disconnected.

Carroll went back to the original phone number. He says the claim was difficult to find again, and he was once more told to take the guitar to Chicago. By six months after the flight, he had paid twelve hundred dollars for the repair. The guitar was playable, but he felt it had lost some of what made it special.

This time a customer service manager offered to arrange contact from Chicago. A letter arrived about a month later, saying someone would get in touch, but with no name or contact information. Another month passed before an email denied the claim. Carroll recalled being told that he'd reported the damage too late in Omaha, that Air Canada had already refused the claim, and that United still needed to inspect a guitar that had now been repaired. He asked for a supervisor; the exchange ended without a payment.

There was one last possibility. Carroll offered to accept flight vouchers instead of money. He was a touring musician; a credit for future travel could compensate him for the repair without asking the airline for a cash payment. United refused.

In his final reply, Carroll told the airline what he intended to do instead: record three songs and make a video for each. He planned to let people watch them free and vote for their favorite, with a goal of reaching a million views in a year.

He enlisted friends to make the first video. The musicians acted out their airport experience as a comedy. The complaint was now easy to pass around, although the reenactment was not actual footage of the baggage incident.

On July sixth, 2009, he released the first song. By July tenth he was on American television and the video had already passed a million views. In four days the first song had reached the goal he'd set for the year.

By July seventeenth Taylor Guitars reported more than three million views. The manufacturer announced that Carroll and a bandmate would be visiting its factory and invited journalists to meet them. The second song was already written, with video production underway. The company whose name was on the broken instrument was now offering the musicians a very different reception.

The airline's customer solutions director, Rob Bradford, called Carroll to apologize. United offered compensation, but Carroll declined. He suggested donating the money instead. The company ultimately gave three thousand dollars to the Thelonious Monk Institute of Jazz.

The call included a second request: could United use his video to train employees?

The airline was no longer asking Carroll to present the damaged guitar. It was asking to present his version of the complaint to its staff. We know it made that request; whether the video actually became part of training is not established here.

You may have heard that the video cost United a hundred and eighty million dollars in market value. Stock prices move for many reasons, and airlines were under financial pressure during the recession. There is no established way to attribute that figure to this song. The apology, offer and donation are documented outcomes.

Carroll kept his promise and released the other two songs. By 2012 he had also written a book and was speaking at conferences. He told a music magazine that people at social media conferences knew the song well, while at music events it could be treated as a novelty. It had brought him a new audience, but being known for that one song was not the same thing as being known for his whole musical career.

He had acquired two new Taylor guitars, he said in that interview. But the original instrument was still with him. He played it and sometimes took it along when he traveled by car. The repair bill remained part of the story, and the guitar was still something he could make music with.

And the airline that had refused to cover the repair bill ended up asking whether it could show its own employees the song about that refusal.
