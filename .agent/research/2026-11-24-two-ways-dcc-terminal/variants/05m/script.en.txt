I have a hotel bill for two hundred euros, but the card machine offers me a second price in dollars. Can choosing the currency on my own bank account actually make the room more expensive? And can I work out which button is cheaper before I pay?

I'll run both choices through the same model. I'm traveling with a dollar-denominated card, the hotel has already charged the room to my bill in euros, and there's no difference in the room, the taxes, or the way I pay off my card. I'm comparing the total dollar cost of completing the purchase.

The terminal offers two hundred euros or two hundred and thirty-one dollars. Pick a side: A is the dollar button, B is the euro button. I've got a hypothetical card rate of one dollar and ten cents per euro. For this first comparison, the card has no extra foreign purchase fee.

If I choose A, I'm accepting the exchange rate offered by the payment provider at the hotel. It works out to one dollar and fifteen and a half cents per euro. I see the converted price before I approve it. The charge reaching my card is two hundred and thirty-one dollars.

If I choose B, I pay the same two hundred euros. The conversion now goes through my card's payment arrangements instead. At my assumed rate, the same bill becomes two hundred and twenty dollars.

That's eleven dollars less. The first option's conversion was five percent more expensive than the modeled card conversion. Neither option changed the cost of the room in euros; it changed who converted the payment.

Now I take that card into town. For the model, I buy an eighty-euro dinner, a twenty-euro museum ticket and a forty-euro taxi ride. I assume both exchange rates stay the same and each terminal offers the same proportional markup. All four purchases add up to three hundred and forty euros, including the hotel.

In the dollar route, the four converted charges total three hundred and ninety-two dollars and seventy cents. In the euro route, they total three hundred and seventy-four dollars. The difference is eighteen dollars and seventy cents. If I had set aside four hundred dollars, I'd have just seven dollars and thirty cents left with A, but twenty-six dollars left with B.

Then I check the card's terms. What if it adds three percent to foreign purchases? It's possible to think paying in dollars must avoid that fee. In fact, the fee can depend on where the purchase takes place or who the merchant is, not simply on the currency.

I rerun the hotel bill assuming the same three percent applies to both methods. A comes out at two hundred and thirty-seven dollars and ninety-three cents. B comes out at two hundred and twenty-six dollars and sixty cents. The bank fee didn't make the terminal's exchange rate any better.

There is another thing to consider. If I choose the local currency, the exact exchange rate applied by my card may not be the one I see when I check my phone. It can depend on when the payment is authorized or processed and on my card's terms.

Suppose I keep the quoted two hundred and thirty-one dollars fixed, remove the extra card fee from both routes, and change only the card rate. At one dollar and fifteen and a half cents per euro, the two prices are identical. If that rate turns out to be one dollar and sixteen cents, my euro payment costs two hundred and thirty-two dollars. In that different model, the dollar button wins by one dollar.

That's not a prediction of exchange rates. It's the point where this particular comparison changes.

Before paying on a real trip, I'd check my card's foreign purchase terms and compare its estimated conversion with the terminal's offer. I'd read the currency and amount on the receipt, too. If I'm offered conversion, declining that service isn't the same as refusing to pay the hotel; I can still choose to pay its local price.

For the starting model, I'd take B and keep the eleven dollars. Over the day, the savings become eighteen dollars and seventy cents. What mattered wasn't which currency felt familiar. It was the complete cost of converting and paying the bill.
