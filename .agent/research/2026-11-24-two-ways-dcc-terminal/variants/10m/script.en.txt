I can see the dollar amount on a hotel card machine before I approve a payment. That sounds useful. But could the option that makes my travel budget easier to understand also make the same purchases more expensive? What happens when I add my own card's fees? And is there a point at which choosing dollars actually makes sense?

I'm going to follow one modeled travel day, from the hotel desk to the last taxi, and compare two ways of paying the same bills. I won't actually make the purchases. I'll state the amounts, choose the exchange rates, and keep the rules the same for both routes so that the result can be checked.

I'm traveling in a country where prices are in euros. My card account is in dollars. At the hotel desk, I have a bill for two hundred euros. It already includes everything I owe for the room. I have four hundred dollars set aside for today's modeled purchases.

The payment terminal displays two options: pay two hundred euros or pay two hundred and thirty-one dollars. I can recognize the dollar figure without reaching for my phone. That is option A. Option B is the local currency. Which would you choose?

My only measure of success is the number of dollars the four purchases ultimately cost. I'm not comparing different hotels, cards, rewards, or rooms. I'm paying the same amounts to the same businesses. At first, I'll give the card an assumed conversion rate of one dollar and ten cents for each euro and no additional fee.

The person who receives the hotel payment needs euros, so one way or another my dollars have to cover that amount. The two buttons determine which payment arrangement converts the bill.

I take A. The hotel payment provider converts the euros into dollars before the purchase reaches my card account. It offers me an exchange rate of one dollar and fifteen and a half cents per euro. I know the converted price on the terminal: two hundred and thirty-one dollars. This kind of offer is called dynamic currency conversion.

I reset the scene. Same hotel bill, same card. This time I choose B and authorize two hundred euros. The transaction goes through the card's arrangements for conversion to dollars. Using the rate I've put into the model, two hundred euros becomes two hundred and twenty dollars.

The difference is eleven dollars. I haven't got a nicer room for the extra eleven dollars. I haven't paid for breakfast or late checkout. I have paid for the same room through a more expensive conversion.

I can express the difference as a percentage. The extra eleven dollars is five percent of two hundred and twenty. This is a comparison with my modeled card rate, not a measured markup at a real hotel or an official reference rate.

I have the choice to see the dollar figure immediately, which is useful when I am counting money in a currency I understand. But I now have a price for that convenience. In this example it's eleven dollars at the hotel desk.

Next I want to see whether one payment makes much difference to my day. I leave the room and go to a restaurant, a museum and a taxi. For each purchase, I'm going to give the terminal the same proportional markup as the hotel and keep the modeled card rate constant. I don't claim these merchants actually make those offers.

Dinner costs eighty euros. On the dollar route, the terminal converts it to ninety-two dollars and forty cents. On the euro route, my card converts it to eighty-eight dollars. That's four dollars and forty cents left in my budget.

Then a museum ticket costs twenty euros. It becomes twenty-three dollars and ten cents through the terminal or twenty-two dollars through my card. I have another dollar and ten cents in savings. A taxi costs forty euros, which comes out at forty-six dollars and twenty cents or forty-four dollars.

The receipts don't look dramatic individually. But I can put all four of them on the table, including the hotel's. The local bills total three hundred and forty euros.

I add the dollar route receipts: three hundred and ninety-two dollars and seventy cents. I add the euro route receipts: three hundred and seventy-four dollars. That leaves seven dollars and thirty cents of my four-hundred-dollar budget on side A, compared with twenty-six dollars on side B. The exact same day has cost eighteen dollars and seventy cents more on A.

Now I look for a reason that result might be misleading. I've assumed that my card doesn't add a fee to foreign purchases. Many card terms can be different. I'll use a second scenario where the same card adds three percent to every one of these overseas purchases, regardless of the currency in which the merchant sends the charge.

Notice why the last part matters. A fee on an overseas transaction and a fee for converting currency are not necessarily the same thing. A purchase from a foreign merchant may still trigger the former even if its price arrives at the issuer in dollars. I need the actual rules of my card, not just its currency.

Back at the hotel, the dollar option goes from two hundred and thirty-one dollars to two hundred and thirty-seven dollars and ninety-three cents. The euro option goes from two hundred and twenty dollars to two hundred and twenty-six dollars and sixty cents. Both got more expensive. The extra foreign purchase fee doesn't remove the earlier conversion markup.

If I apply the three percent to each of my four modeled purchases and round each final charge to the nearest cent, the dollar route comes to four hundred and four dollars and forty-eight cents. That's over my budget. The euro route comes to three hundred and eighty-five dollars and twenty-two cents. The same room, dinner, museum visit and taxi are still covered.

What if I also need cash? I'll keep this separate from my four purchase totals. Suppose I withdraw one hundred euros with a debit card and the ATM adds a four-euro operator fee to the amount converted. For the demonstration, my debit card also charges a flat three-dollar withdrawal fee either way.

The terminal converts one hundred and four euros at its modeled rate, which is one hundred and twenty dollars and twelve cents. Add three dollars: one hundred and twenty-three dollars and twelve cents. If I decline the terminal's conversion and allow the card to convert the same one hundred and four euros, it's one hundred and fourteen dollars and forty cents, then three dollars on top. The total is one hundred and seventeen dollars and forty cents.

I haven't removed the ATM fee by choosing euros. I changed the conversion cost. Actual ATMs and cards can charge fees differently. I would read the withdrawal conditions rather than treat these amounts as a real tariff.

Finally, I return to the original hotel payment with no additional card fee. The terminal quotes two hundred and thirty-one dollars and I leave that offer unchanged. Now I vary only the exchange rate my card ultimately uses.

A card rate of one dollar and fifteen and a half cents per euro makes the two totals equal. Any lower rate in dollars per euro favors paying the euro bill, given these inputs. If the actual applied rate is one dollar and sixteen cents, B costs two hundred and thirty-two dollars and A is cheaper by one dollar. The direction can change. A rate that looked good on my phone isn't necessarily the rate used by my card.

Before an actual trip, I would check the terms for my card, including when its exchange rate is determined and whether foreign purchases in my account currency still incur fees. At the terminal, I'd compare the converted offer with the card estimate, read the choice on the screen, and keep the receipt showing what I agreed to pay. If I don't understand an offer or didn't agree to the currency shown, I'd ask for clarification before confirming or contact the issuer about a disputed charge.

In the original model, I leave the hotel having spent two hundred and twenty dollars rather than two hundred and thirty-one. At the end of the four purchases, the choice has kept eighteen dollars and seventy cents in my budget. The local currency wins in that model; it isn't a guarantee for every card or every trip.

The two buttons are easy to understand once I know what they control. The question isn't whether I like euros or dollars. It's who does the conversion, at what rate, and what else the card will charge me.
