I have enough range to get home with one big charging session. But would I actually arrive earlier if I stopped twice? I want to find the point where an extra stop stops being worth it.

Imagine I'm parked at a fast charger in an electric car. I have a two-hundred-and-eighty-kilometer drive left. My battery is at twenty percent, and I've decided I want to arrive home with twenty percent, too. I won't trade away that reserve just to make one plan look faster.

There's another fast charger directly on the route, a hundred and sixty kilometers from here. The first leg uses forty percentage points of my battery. The last hundred and twenty kilometers use thirty. I assume a constant driving speed and no traffic difference between the two runs.

I'll compare the time from this station to my driveway, including charging and the time I spend entering and leaving each station. This is an invented route and a simplified charging model, not a measurement of a real car. Choose A, charge once and drive straight home, or B, charge twice along the way.

I'll start with A. Since I'm using seventy percentage points on the road and want twenty left at the finish, I need to charge to ninety.

At first the battery fills quickly. In this model I gain two percentage points every minute between twenty and sixty. After twenty minutes, I'm at sixty percent. That's enough to reach the next station, but not enough to skip it.

From sixty to eighty, the charging rate drops to one point per minute. Twenty more minutes. Now I only need another ten points, but the speed drops again, to half a point a minute. So that little final section takes as long as the entire first forty points.

After sixty minutes of charging, the battery shows ninety. Add five minutes for getting into position, connecting, disconnecting and leaving. I drive the first leg, pass the second station at fifty percent, and reach my driveway at twenty.

My stops took sixty-five minutes. With two hours and forty-eight minutes of driving, I arrive after three hours and fifty-three minutes.

Now I reset the car. Same battery, same road, same conditions. With B I stop charging as soon as I have sixty percent. After the five-minute overhead, I'm on my way in twenty-five minutes.

I use forty points on the first leg and reach the second station with twenty left. Now I only need another thirty points to complete the trip with my reserve intact. I charge to fifty. At two points per minute, that takes fifteen minutes. Another five to get in and out, and I'm back on the road.

When I reach home, I still have twenty percent. The driving time didn't change. But my total charging time was only thirty-five minutes. Even with two lots of five minutes at the stations, I arrive after three hours and thirty-three minutes. Twenty minutes earlier than with one stop.

The extra stop wasn't free. I just saved more time charging than I spent making it.

Now I want to know what can destroy that advantage. I reset again, but this time I make the second charger slower. It can only deliver enough power to add one percentage point per minute. I still need thirty points, so that session now takes half an hour instead of fifteen minutes. My original twenty-minute lead shrinks to five.

If I also wait ten minutes for a plug at that slower station, B now takes five minutes longer than A. I could have been home sooner by passing it.

Even at the original fast station, twenty extra minutes of waiting would erase the advantage, and twenty-five would make the one-stop route faster by five minutes. I can work out that threshold before leaving the first station.

Real cars don't change charging speed in three neat steps. Temperature, the battery, the station and my actual energy use all matter. I also need to know the next charger is available and compatible. If it isn't, I can't just copy the two-stop plan and hope my reserve will cover the missing distance.

My takeaway isn't to leave every charger at sixty percent. On this route, that was enough for the next leg. I'd plan with the car's actual charging curve and available chargers, keep the same arrival reserve for both options, and include the time I'd spend off the road. One extra stop is worth making only when the time it saves is greater than the time it adds.
