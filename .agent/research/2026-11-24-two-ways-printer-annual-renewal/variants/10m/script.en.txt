My printer spends most of its life doing nothing. Then I have a batch of forms, a set of instructions, or a project to print, and suddenly I need dozens of pages. If I pay for ink by subscription, should I choose a small monthly plan or buy a year's worth of pages in advance? I want to find out how the timing of my printing changes the answer.

I will use the published United States offers for HP Instant Ink, not an invented discount. The two smallest subscription plans give me an interesting comparison because their advertised page allowances add up to the same number over twelve months.

This is a calculated example, not a purchase or a measurement from a printer. I assume the same eligible printer, the same cartridge service, the same page sequence and the same starting point after any free trial. There is no saved allowance at the start. I assume listed prices do not change, automatic renewal is enabled, and I don't change or upgrade either plan during a comparison. Paper, tax and printer purchase are outside the model.

I will measure subscription charges attributable to printing periods inside my chosen time window. I will also keep track of the pages remaining. That second number matters because paying for a new subscription period does not mean I have already used its contents.

Here are the two choices. A gives me ten pages per month for a dollar ninety-nine. B costs twenty dollars and ninety-nine cents in advance and gives me a hundred and twenty pages for the annual period. If I simply multiply the monthly price by twelve, A comes to twenty-three dollars and eighty-eight cents. The annual price is two dollars and eighty-nine cents lower. I'd be tempted to choose B. Which would you pick?

I'll start with a year where I print a little most months. Ten pages in month one. Ten in month two. I follow that schedule through month eleven. Nothing carries over on A because I use all ten pages each month. B's remaining balance falls to ten.

If I print nothing in month twelve, my subscription charges are twenty-three eighty-eight on A and twenty ninety-nine on B. B wins. Those ten unused annual pages won't carry forward into the next yearly period. The smaller bill was real within the model, even though I didn't use every page I paid for.

Now I return to the same starting conditions, but a new batch of forms arrives on the first day of month twelve. I print twenty pages instead of none. On A, I use the month's ten included pages. HP's offer describes extra page blocks costing two dollars, with ten to fifteen pages per block depending on the plan. I only need ten extra pages, so one block is sufficient either way. A's twelve monthly charges plus the block come to twenty-five eighty-eight.

B starts that day with ten pages remaining. I print the first ten and the balance hits zero. On the annual plan, running out of pages can end the annual billing period before its twelve months have passed. With automatic renewal on, I get a new period. At the unchanged model price, another twenty ninety-nine is due. That gives me a hundred and twenty new pages, ten of which I use to finish my forms.

I have now printed the same hundred and thirty pages on both sides. But B's charges total forty-one ninety-eight, compared with twenty-five eighty-eight on A. The plan that looked cheaper is now sixteen dollars and ten cents more expensive over my first twelve months.

I wouldn't describe that whole second payment as a penalty. In B's new period I have a hundred and ten pages left. If the printer is going into a cupboard and won't be used again, buying that allowance may not help me. If the printer will be used, the calculation is unfinished in a different sense: my budget has paid in advance for printing that can happen later.

To see how much that matters, I'll extend the same example. My new annual period began at the start of month twelve. From month thirteen through month twenty-two, I print ten pages each month. In month twenty-three I print five. That is another hundred and five pages, bringing my overall total to two hundred and thirty-five. The new annual allowance still has five pages available. It hasn't exhausted its page limit, and I stop counting before the next normal renewal.

On A, none of those extra months needs another paid block. Twenty-three monthly payments plus the original two-dollar block add up to forty-seven seventy-seven. B still costs forty-one ninety-eight. Now B is cheaper by five dollars and seventy-nine cents.

Both outcomes are consistent. The first year has a larger charge on B because it includes the next period. Over this longer interval, I have used almost all of the second allowance, and the balance changes. A family trying to stay within this year's budget could reasonably care about the first number. Someone who expects to keep the printer could care more about the second. I wouldn't silently change the scoring window just to make one side win.

There is also a reason not to assume that twelve times ten pages works the same way as a hundred and twenty pages in a single pool. Let's start an entirely new year. This time I leave the printer idle for eleven months and then print a hundred and ten pages in a project in month twelve. The total is exactly the same as in my first, evenly spread example.

The monthly plan allows unused pages to roll forward, but the ink rollover balance is capped at three times my monthly allowance. At the start of the second month I have ten saved pages. At the start of the third I have twenty. By the fourth I have thirty. The balance cannot keep growing indefinitely. When I reach month twelve, my thirty saved pages and ten new pages cover forty of the project.

I need another seventy. If the blocks contain fifteen pages, I need five; if they contain ten, I need seven. At two dollars per block, that's ten to fourteen dollars added to my ordinary twenty-three eighty-eight. A costs between thirty-three eighty-eight and thirty-seven eighty-eight. The exact extra block size should be checked in the account.

B has the full hundred and twenty pages available because nothing has been printed in this annual period. I print the project and still have ten left. It costs twenty ninety-nine. The same total page count has a very different result for A, simply because I printed in a different month. This is the part I would put in my own calendar: not just how many pages I need, but when I need them.

What if I only need the printer for three months? I start fresh again, print ten pages per month, and stop after the third billing period. A's three payments are five dollars and ninety-seven cents. B has already charged twenty ninety-nine. HP's published annual terms do not offer a refund for cancellation after the initial thirty-day window. I have not assumed that the unused allowance disappears immediately; I am measuring the money committed to those three months of work, with no more work planned. In that particular situation, the extra pages don't serve my chosen task.

In real life I could change plans, disable automatic renewal before exhaustion, compare ordinary replacement cartridges, or look at the separate pay-as-you-print offer. Those are different decisions with different inputs. Turning off renewal means I can't assume a fresh annual allowance will appear to finish my next batch. I haven't compared the cost of the alternative printing method. I'd also check the exact eligible plan in my account, because these are published offers, not a guaranteed personal quote.

So here's what I would do before paying. I'd estimate my busiest printing month, not just my average. I'd check the number of pages I could still print before renewal, the date of that renewal, and the extra-block price. If I was close to the annual limit, I'd decide whether I actually wanted to buy another period. And I'd decide how long I expected to keep printing before comparing totals.

The monthly plan and annual plan each have situations where they win in this model. The surprising bill is not explained by the ink in ten pages. It's explained by buying access to the next period. Once I know which period I'm paying for, I can choose the subscription that fits the work I really expect to do.
