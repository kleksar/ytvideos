I have a train to catch, and the booking screen is asking for eighty pounds. I can buy the ticket now, or divide the journey into two purchases. Can I save money without travelling on a slower train? And what happens if the train doesn't stop where my first ticket ends?

Let's put both options on the same fictional British railway. I haven't booked anything. The fares and stations are invented for this model, but I'm going to follow the ordinary split-ticket rule.

I'm travelling from West to Harbour. Between them are Junction and Park. My train calls at both. I want standard class, no railcard discount, and a ticket valid on this exact train. I'll count the total amount I pay, including any booking fee. No fees in the first round.

Pick A if you would buy one ticket from West to Harbour. Pick B if you would look for a cheaper combination of tickets covering that exact ride.

I'll start with A. I get one ticket for eighty pounds. I can walk straight to the train and use it for the whole journey.

Now I open the same route in B and check the fare from West to Junction. Twenty-eight pounds. From Junction to Harbour, another twenty-seven. I add them together before buying either ticket. Fifty-five pounds.

I haven't changed the train, departure, destination or class. I've just bought two valid portions of the same journey. I save twenty-five pounds.

Why can that work? The price of the whole journey isn't necessarily calculated by adding up the prices of every shorter journey inside it. The ticket offers can be different. A shorter fare could have different availability or restrictions, so a particular combination can be cheaper. I still have to check that both tickets allow the train I've chosen.

When I reach Junction, I don't have to step out onto the platform and get back in just to start using the second ticket. The train calls there, and both portions are covered. If my reservations are for different seats, I may have to move, but I don't need to change trains.

Now I give myself a slightly harder problem. Suppose I prefer a faster service along the same route, with the same modeled through price. It stops at Park but goes straight through Junction.

The fifty-five-pound combination disappears from my valid options. A cheap ticket to a station where this train doesn't call won't work for this ordinary split.

There's another pair I can look at. West to Park costs forty-six pounds, then Park to Harbour costs nineteen. Both are valid on this express. My total is sixty-five pounds. A still costs eighty, so B wins again, this time by fifteen.

That's a useful difference. I didn't have to abandon the idea of splitting just because the first split point failed. I looked at the stops on the actual train.

I go back to my original stopping service for the last round. The through fare changes to forty-nine pounds. The cheapest valid split is still fifty-five. Now A wins by six. I'm comparing the new prices at the same moment, not pretending I bought the first ticket yesterday and the second today.

One more thing can change the decision even without a change in train fares. Suppose my original eighty and fifty-five pound prices are back, but the service selling the split tickets adds a fee. At five pounds extra, B still costs sixty. At twenty-five pounds extra, both methods cost eighty. If B alone has a fee greater than twenty-five pounds, it loses. Those fees are hypothetical, not an estimate of any real company's charges.

So I don't have to believe that split tickets are always cheaper. I need to compare the final prices of tickets that actually cover the journey, including fees and restrictions. In the first round the split saves twenty-five pounds; on the express it saves fifteen; when the through price drops, a single ticket is cheaper.

My destination never changed. I just learned to check the stops and the price before pressing buy.
