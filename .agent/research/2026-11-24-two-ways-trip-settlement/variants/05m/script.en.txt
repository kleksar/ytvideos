At the end of a weekend trip, I want to get our money settled without turning the group chat into a second job. Can four friends clear every bill with fewer payments, even if two of us both owe money and are owed money? And what happens if one receipt wasn't actually shared by everyone?

I'm going to use a small made-up ledger so I can compare both methods without moving any real money. I count separate payments between friends, not the original payments to the shops. I assume one currency, no fees, and that everyone can pay everyone else.

There are four of us. I put a hundred and sixty dollars on my card for the accommodation. Maya pays a hundred and twenty for the rental car, Leo covers eighty dollars of groceries, and Nina buys forty dollars of snacks. For now, we agree that all four expenses are shared equally.

I have two ways to get the money back. In A, I settle the balance between every pair of friends. In B, I calculate how much each person should pay or receive overall. Pick one before I start moving the arrows.

In A, I divide the accommodation bill into four parts. Maya, Leo and Nina each owe me forty. But I also owe Maya thirty for the car. Instead of paying her thirty and asking for forty back, I ask her to send me ten.

That seems efficient already. I repeat it with Leo. He owes me forty for the accommodation, and I owe him twenty for the groceries, so he sends me twenty. Nina owes me forty, and I owe her ten, so she sends me thirty.

I now have my sixty dollars back. But the others aren't finished. Leo owes Maya ten after their two bills are offset. Nina owes Maya twenty and owes Leo ten.

Notice what's happening to Leo. He sends out thirty dollars and then gets ten back. His actual contribution to the settlement is twenty dollars, but three payment notifications have his name on them.

When all six transfers are done, everybody has spent a hundred dollars. The calculation is correct. The extra payments are not an error. They're a consequence of the rule I chose: settle separately with each person.

I reset the ledger. In B, I first add up the receipts. Four hundred dollars, split four ways, means a hundred each. I paid sixty above my share and Maya paid twenty above hers. Leo still needs to pay twenty and Nina needs to pay sixty.

I match the amounts. Nina can cover my entire sixty, and Leo can cover all twenty of Maya's balance. Two payments finish the job. In this example, two is also the fewest possible: two different people need to send money, so I can't finish with just one person-to-person transfer.

I check the outcome before trusting it. I paid a hundred and sixty and received sixty, so I've spent a hundred. Maya paid a hundred and twenty and received twenty. Leo paid eighty and sent twenty. Nina paid forty and sent sixty. Exactly the same four final costs as A.

Now imagine we catch a mistake before either settlement begins. The forty dollars of snacks were only for Nina and me. I keep the other three receipts unchanged and split that last one between the two of us instead.

My share and Nina's are now a hundred and ten each. Maya and Leo only owe ninety each. I should receive fifty, Maya should receive thirty, Leo should send ten and Nina should send seventy.

I can't simply reuse the two-payment plan. Nina can send me fifty, send Maya twenty, and Leo can send Maya the remaining ten. B now needs three payments. The pair-by-pair method still needs six. The simpler route survives the correction, but the exact number changes.

That's why I would settle the agreed balances after checking the receipts, not replace the receipts with a list of transfers. I can show every friend what they originally paid, what their share was, and why the new payment route leaves them with precisely that cost. If anyone disputes a bill or can't use a proposed route, I resolve that before sending money.

For these four friends, I can reduce six payments to two under our first agreement, or six to three under the corrected one. I'm not getting a cheaper holiday. I'm just avoiding unnecessary payments between us.
