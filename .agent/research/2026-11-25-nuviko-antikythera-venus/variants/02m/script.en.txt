One corroded gear with sixty-three teeth may explain how an ancient Greek machine modelled centuries of Venus's motion.

In 1901, sponge divers recovered pieces of bronze from a shipwreck near the island of Antikythera. Among the objects brought to Athens was a mass of corroded metal. It had once belonged to a mechanism small enough to fit inside a wooden box.

The gears were so badly corroded that pulling them apart would destroy evidence. X-rays let researchers count teeth they couldn't see from the outside. Then, in 2005, three-dimensional scans revealed more of the machine's internal structure and hidden Greek writing.

The back of the mechanism was an astronomical calculator. One dial followed a calendar based on the Moon. Another marked months in which eclipses could occur.

But most of the front was gone. The surviving inscriptions described a display of the planets, and among the numbers were four hundred and sixty-two years for Venus.

Why would anyone need a cycle that long? Venus doesn't return to exactly the same pattern in the sky every year. A much longer cycle gives a better approximation of its repeating motion.

In 2021, a team proposed a system of gears that could represent that cycle. A crucial part of their solution was a surviving sixty-three-tooth wheel. Their design also had to fit the remaining supports and the limited space inside the box.

Nobody has recovered the lost front assembly. The model is a possible solution, not proof that every reconstructed part is correct. So what real piece of the original gives researchers a way to test that idea?
