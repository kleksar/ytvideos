What was an ancient Greek machine doing with a cycle that lasted four hundred and sixty-two years? And how can a single surviving gear help us reconstruct the parts that are missing?

In 1900, sponge divers found an ancient shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera. During the recovery, which continued into 1901, they brought up statues and other objects. Among the material sent to Athens were pieces of bronze that had corroded together.

A gear became visible in the fragments. That changed the problem completely. An ancient object that had looked like a lump of metal was now evidence of a mechanical instrument. The original mechanism probably dated to the second or first century BC.

It had been enclosed in a wooden case, roughly the size of a large book, though considerably thicker. There were scales, pointers and Greek inscriptions. The difficulty was that many of the gears were still buried inside the corroded fragments. If someone tried to separate them, the very evidence needed to explain the instrument might be lost.

In the twentieth century, researchers including Derek de Solla Price and the Greek radiographer Charalambos Karakalos used X-rays to investigate the interior. They could count hidden teeth and work out relationships between some of the gears. The object was becoming recognizable as an astronomical calculator.

A new investigation in 2005 used three-dimensional X-ray imaging and detailed surface photography. Instead of simply looking through a fragment, researchers could examine different depths inside it. More of the inscriptions became legible.

The back of the mechanism gave them two particularly useful answers. One spiral dial followed a cycle of nineteen years. In that time, the Moon completes almost exactly two hundred and thirty-five cycles of its phases. A calendar could use that relationship to keep lunar months aligned with the seasons.

Another spiral had a cycle of two hundred and twenty-three lunar months. That is the Saros, a period after which similar eclipse conditions recur. Marks on the dial indicated possible eclipses. The machine even accounted for the shift in time of day from one Saros to the next.

So a substantial part of the ancient machine could now be explained. The mystery was on the other side.

Most of the front mechanism had disappeared, but the surviving writing described a display of the planets. And there were two striking numbers: four hundred and sixty-two years for Venus and four hundred and forty-two for Saturn.

Think about what a planet looks like from Earth. Against the stars, it normally appears to travel in one direction, but at certain times it seems to turn back. A simple pointer that rotates steadily cannot reproduce that motion. The maker needed a way to combine rotations and make the resulting movement change direction.

The researchers had the inscriptions, a few physical remains from the front, and very little room in which to fit the missing gears. Among those remains was a separate fragment containing a wheel with sixty-three teeth.

In 2021, a team at University College London proposed a reconstruction in which that wheel played a crucial role in the long Venus cycle. Their model combined gear trains with a system of concentric tubes, so different planetary indicators could move around the same centre. The model was also designed around surviving supports on the original fragments. The numbers in the writing were no longer isolated facts: they became constraints on a possible mechanical solution.

The front display might have brought the motions of the heavens together on one face. But there is an important difference between a design that fits the evidence and a machine recovered intact. Much of the bronze is missing. The exact original arrangement and whether all the proposed parts could have been made with ancient techniques remain open questions.

The Antikythera mechanism did not have to run for four hundred and sixty-two years. That number described a relationship used to represent Venus's repeating motion. A person could turn the mechanism to calculate a different date.

An object once buried in a shipwreck thus preserves two things: an ancient calculation and part of the hardware that performed it. Two thousand years later, the surviving wheel is helping researchers work out how the rest might have fitted together.
