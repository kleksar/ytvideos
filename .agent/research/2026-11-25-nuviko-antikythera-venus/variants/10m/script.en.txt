Why would a machine built more than two thousand years ago need a number as large as four hundred and sixty-two years? How did its makers use gears to calculate the sky? And how much of that machine can we really reconstruct from a handful of corroded fragments?

In 1900, sponge divers working near the Greek island of Antikythera discovered the remains of an ancient ship. The recovery continued into the following year. It was an archaeological find with extraordinary objects, including bronze and marble statues. Among the material sent to Athens were pieces of metal that had corroded together during their long stay underwater.

A gear was discovered in the fragments. It was a small part of a much larger problem. This was not just a decorated bronze object. Someone in antiquity had built a mechanism with interlocking wheels.

The instrument probably dates to the second or first century BC. Its surviving parts point to a wooden case about thirty-three centimetres high, eighteen centimetres wide and ten centimetres deep. A person could have held the box in front of them. The original instrument had dials and writing on its surfaces. Today only a fraction of it survives, broken into dozens of pieces.

The problem was how to examine a device whose gears were locked inside layers of corrosion. Counting teeth would be essential, but a gear that appeared complete from one direction could be hiding another wheel directly underneath. Taking everything apart would have risked destroying the instrument.

Early investigators debated what the device had done. Was it related to navigation or to instruments for representing the heavens? Before they could choose between such explanations, they needed to understand what was connected to what.

In the twentieth century, the historian of science Derek de Solla Price and Greek radiographer Charalambos Karakalos used X-rays to examine the fragments. The resulting images revealed teeth and wheels that the surface alone could not show. Price published a major reconstruction in the nineteen seventies. It established the seriousness of the mechanism as a mechanical astronomical calculator, even though later work would change important parts of his explanation.

The reason teeth matter is that a gear can turn an astronomical relationship into a mechanical one. Imagine two wheels, one with twenty teeth and one with forty. If the small wheel turns twice, the larger wheel turns once. Link several pairs together and you can obtain much more complicated relationships. That is an illustration of the principle, not a claim about those particular numbers inside the ancient box.

The X-ray pictures revealed a great deal, but there were still problems. Different objects inside a fragment could overlap in a flat image. Later researchers, including Michael Wright and Allan Bromley, investigated ways of recording internal structure at different depths.

In 2005, an international team examined the fragments with more advanced three-dimensional X-ray imaging and detailed surface photographs. A hidden layer of the instrument could be inspected without physically removing the metal above it. The same investigation brought more of the Greek inscriptions into view.

One major result was an explanation of the back of the mechanism. It contained two large spiral scales. The upper one was connected to the relationship between the Moon and the calendar year.

Imagine a calendar that counts months by watching the Moon change from new to full and back again. Twelve of those months make a year shorter than the cycle of the seasons. If you never adjust the calendar, its months gradually move away from the seasons they once marked.

But over nineteen solar years, the Moon completes almost exactly two hundred and thirty-five cycles of its phases. That gives a way to arrange extra months in a lunar calendar. On the back of the Antikythera mechanism, a spiral scale represented this nineteen-year cycle. Its pointer could move through the sequence of months. A smaller dial also connected the calendar to the cycle of athletic festivals in the Greek world.

The other large spiral dealt with eclipses. The Moon has to be in the right phase and also in the right part of its tilted orbit for an eclipse to occur. After two hundred and twenty-three lunar months, those conditions return to a similar arrangement. This is the Saros cycle.

The lower spiral had markings for months in which an eclipse could occur. It also had a problem that any eclipse calculator based on the Saros had to confront. The cycle does not end after a whole number of days. It contains about a third of an extra day. An eclipse in the next cycle therefore comes at a different time of day, roughly eight hours later on the clock. A smaller dial supplied corrections for successive cycles. That does not mean the instrument could tell everyone on Earth that they would see the same eclipse from their own town. It was a calculator of recurring astronomical conditions, not a modern map of eclipse visibility.

The rear scales told a remarkable story on their own. The front of the box raised a different question.

Most of the gearwork for the front display had disappeared. The inscriptions, however, included descriptions of the planets and numerical cycles. For Venus the text gave four hundred and sixty-two years. For Saturn it gave four hundred and forty-two.

These were clues to the design, not instructions to wait four centuries. The planets move around the Sun, and we watch them from a moving Earth. Venus can appear on one side of the Sun in the sky and later on the other. The apparent movement of planets against distant stars is complicated. At times a planet appears to go backwards.

To build a mechanical display of this movement, a designer could combine steady rotations into a changing apparent position. Think of a point on a small circle whose centre is itself moving around a larger circle. The combined movement can be different from either rotation on its own. Ancient Greek astronomy used geometrical ideas of this kind. The challenge was to express them through actual bronze parts.

The long planetary periods in the inscriptions were useful because they describe approximations to recurring patterns. A short period might give a tolerable answer for a while, then drift as the mechanism is advanced through more years. A longer and more accurate relationship can reduce that drift. Gearing must translate the relationship into ratios of whole numbers of teeth.

But many arrangements of gears could produce astronomical relationships. The researchers needed to test their choices against the surviving hardware.

One fragment contained a wheel with sixty-three teeth, along with other metal parts. Another part of the instrument preserved features such as supports and bearings. A reconstruction could not simply ignore the existing pieces in order to make its arithmetic work. It had to find a place for them and leave room for the rest of the device.

In 2021, a team at University College London published a proposed mechanical model for the front display. In their design, the sixty-three-tooth wheel played a crucial role in implementing the four-hundred-and-sixty-two-year Venus relationship. They also proposed gear trains for the other planets and a system of concentric tubes. Those tubes would let different indicators move independently around a common centre, even though the gearing driving them was crowded together behind the display.

That is where the physical puzzle and the astronomical puzzle meet. A calculation of Venus on paper is not enough. It has to be converted into a mechanism that accommodates an actual wheel from the wreck, works with other surviving features and fits the dimensions of a box.

The front would have represented the heavens through moving indicators rather than a modern screen. Turning the instrument to another date would change their positions. If the proposed model is right in its essential features, the ancient maker found a way to bring an entire set of planetary motions together in a remarkably compact instrument.

Still, the 2021 proposal is not the recovery of the original missing assembly. Its authors had to reconstruct parts that no longer exist, and the feasibility of manufacturing all the proposed components with ancient methods was not established by that publication. An elegant fit to the surviving evidence gives us a model to examine and test. It does not give us a photograph of the mechanism before the ship sank.

That distinction makes the surviving gear more interesting, not less. The shipwreck preserved the number in the writing and a piece of the mechanical problem. Researchers can now investigate whether the two belong to the same design.

The object has moved from an unidentified mass of corroded bronze to a partially decoded ancient astronomical machine. Its rear dials reveal how its maker represented calendars and eclipse cycles. Its missing front has become a much more precise mystery: how to turn the long planetary periods carved into the instrument into moving indicators, using the pieces that remain.

The four hundred and sixty-two years do not tell us how long the machine ran. They help tell us what the machine was built to calculate. And the sixty-three teeth show why the answer can be tested against something that actually survived.
