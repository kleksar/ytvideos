Eight people moved into a sealed glass world in Arizona. Within sixteen months, almost a third of their breathable oxygen was gone.

In 1991, Biosphere Two was meant to show how humans might live on another planet. Under the glass were a farm, a rainforest and even a miniature ocean. The inhabitants grew food, and the plants were supposed to replace the oxygen they breathed.

But the air was changing. Crew member Jane Poynter later recalled waking at night gasping for breath. By January 1993 the operators had to supply oxygen from outside.

There was a clue in the soil. The builders had added compost and peat to help plants grow. Microbes were breaking down that material, using oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide faster than the plants could keep up.

That explained the oxygen loss, but there was still a problem. Much of the carbon dioxide that should have accumulated in the air was missing too.

Researchers drilled into the concrete. They compared samples from inside the enclosure with concrete of the same age outside. The material inside contained far more of the carbonate formed when carbon dioxide reacts with concrete.

The missing gas had become part of the building itself.

All eight people survived the two-year mission, with outside oxygen added. Their little world hadn't been self-sufficient. But the investigation had revealed an unexpected part of its life-support system: the walls.

The concrete wasn't breathing oxygen. It was absorbing the carbon dioxide released by the living soil. And that's what made the experiment such a strange case of disappearing air.
