How could eight people live for two years inside a glass enclosure? Why did their oxygen disappear even though they were surrounded by plants? And what did the investigators eventually find inside the walls?

On September 26, 1991, the first crew entered Biosphere Two in the Arizona desert. Above them stood a network of glass and steel roofs. Beneath their feet a welded steel liner separated their world from the surrounding ground. There was an ocean with a reef, a tropical forest, and a farm where the crew would have to grow much of its own food.

It wasn't a spacecraft. The doors could be opened in an emergency, and the complex relied on external energy. But the air and the materials circulating inside were intended to function as a largely closed system. If the crew could make that work here, it might teach us something about living in a place where stepping outside was not an option.

For a while, the crew did something rather ordinary under extraordinary conditions. They planted crops, harvested food and took care of the animals. Jane Poynter, one of the inhabitants, later described growing so many sweet potatoes that she ate them constantly. A meal on this farm began long before anyone sat down at the table.

Then the crew ran into a problem they couldn't solve by working harder on the farm.

The oxygen level had started near twenty-one percent, like the air outside. Over sixteen months it dropped to about fourteen percent. Poynter recalled waking up during the night struggling for breath. In January 1993 the operators started feeding pure oxygen into the enclosure.

Where had the original oxygen gone? A leaking building would have been an obvious suspect. But tests indicated that leakage was low. Besides, outside air contained more oxygen than the air inside. Something within the enclosure was changing its atmosphere.

The farm and the forest held the first clue. The builders had made their own topsoil with a generous amount of compost and peat. That was food for microbes. As the microbes broke down organic matter, they consumed oxygen and produced carbon dioxide.

Plants were doing the opposite overall when they grew: taking in carbon dioxide and releasing oxygen through photosynthesis. But the glass reduced the sunlight reaching them. Plant growth wasn't keeping pace with the respiration of the whole ecosystem.

That sounded like a reasonable explanation until you looked at the carbon dioxide readings. There wasn't nearly enough of it to account for the oxygen loss. The researchers knew some had been removed by the equipment used to control carbon dioxide. Even after counting that, a large amount was unaccounted for.

So they began to look at parts of the enclosure that weren't alive.

In June 1993, researchers drilled twelve cores from concrete inside Biosphere Two and four more from outside, using material poured at the same time for comparison. They tested small pieces from different depths. The inside samples showed that carbonation, the reaction that locks carbon dioxide into a mineral, had progressed much further than it had outside.

The investigators estimated that the concrete had captured enough carbon dioxide to explain the discrepancy. Measurements of different forms of carbon backed up the conclusion.

There were two processes at work. The microbes were consuming oxygen. The concrete was taking the resulting carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere. Part of the building had quietly entered a cycle its designers intended to keep going between the soil, plants and air.

And the problem didn't simply vanish when the oxygen was topped up. In August, as the mission approached its end, the operators began adding oxygen again.

All eight inhabitants came out alive in September 1993. Their mission hadn't demonstrated two years of independent life support. But it had identified a real difficulty in making a small, closed ecosystem.

Today Biosphere Two is a University of Arizona research facility. Its value isn't that everything went according to plan. The people who built a tiny world found that the chemistry of its walls mattered just as much as what they planted inside it.
