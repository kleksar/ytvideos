Could you build a world small enough to fit inside a building, but large enough to feed eight people and regenerate the air they breathe? How would you find out what was consuming their oxygen? And why would that investigation end with scientists drilling into the concrete?

On September twenty-sixth, nineteen ninety-one, eight people entered Biosphere Two in the Arizona desert. Under its glass roofs were a small ocean, a rainforest, a desert and a farm. Beneath the structure, a steel barrier separated their soil from the surrounding ground. The machinery still needed outside energy, but the goal was to keep the materials inside circulating for two years.

One of them, Jane Poynter, later described how long a familiar meal could take when she had to raise the ingredients herself. To make a pizza, there was wheat to grow for flour and goats to feed for milk and cheese. The work was measured in months before there was anything to put on the plate.

The farm was also part of the air supply. When the crew breathed out, they gave the plants carbon dioxide. The plants used the carbon to grow, and the crew ate their crops. The cycle had to do more than provide enough food. It had to keep the atmosphere breathable as well.

Initially, oxygen made up about twenty-one percent of their atmosphere. Sixteen months later it was down around fourteen percent. That didn't mean the rooms had suddenly lost their air pressure. The mixture itself had changed, and less of each breath was oxygen.

Poynter later recalled waking at night gasping for breath and struggling to move about the enclosure. She was calling her mother to reassure her while news of the experiment spread outside. The doctor who was monitoring the crew was affected too. According to Poynter, one day he had trouble adding up a column of numbers.

The crew weren't literally trapped: if necessary they could leave the building. But continuing with so little oxygen was not a reasonable test of self-sufficiency. In January nineteen ninety-three, the operators began supplementing the atmosphere with oxygen from outside. The news reports at the time described a recovery in the oxygen concentration and relief for the residents.

The new oxygen helped the people, but it did not explain what had happened to the original supply. The crew had already been trying to change the processes inside their little world. Poynter described growing plants and putting harvested plant material into storage in the basement. They reduced irrigation wherever they could and stopped tilling the soil in an effort to reduce the gases coming out of the ground. The soil that was supposed to feed them had become one of the things they needed to investigate.

One possible explanation for changing air in a sealed building would be leakage. Yet the engineers had tested for leaks, and they were small. With an oxygen-rich atmosphere outside and an oxygen-poor one inside, simply mixing the two would not explain a continuing drop. There had to be an internal process to account for the observations.

The answer began with a decision made before the doors closed. The designers had mixed local soil with compost and peat. Compost and peat together made up about thirty percent of the mixture by volume. As the organic material broke down, it supplied nutrients for the plants. It also supplied food for the microbes living in the ground.

Those organisms need oxygen for much of their activity. They consume it as they break down organic matter, and carbon dioxide is released. The fact that a farm or forest looks green doesn't tell you whether the system as a whole is adding oxygen to the air. Leaves photosynthesize during daylight, while organisms respire during the day and at night.

The roof made it harder for the plants to keep up. Only about forty-five percent of the light outside reached the interior through the glass. The later investigation estimated that respiration was running at roughly twice the rate of photosynthesis. A crew member could look out across a field of green leaves and still be living in a system that was losing oxygen.

There was a second puzzle. As microbes consumed oxygen, they released carbon dioxide. If ordinary respiration accounted for the oxygen loss, the records of the two gases ought to show corresponding changes. They didn't.

The investigation accounted for the air and oxygen that had been added or removed, the carbon dioxide removal equipment, and the carbon held in growing plants. There was still a large discrepancy. Two explanations had to be considered: some other process might be using oxygen without releasing the expected carbon dioxide, or the missing carbon dioxide might have been taken up somewhere else.

One suspect was an ordinary construction material: concrete. It can react with carbon dioxide, but that reaction happens in ordinary buildings too. The team needed to find out how different the concrete inside Biosphere Two had become from concrete exposed to ordinary air.

On June seventh, nineteen ninety-three, researchers drilled cylindrical samples from twelve locations inside the complex. They also took four samples from concrete outside that had been poured at the same time. That gave them a way of comparing concrete exposed to the enclosed atmosphere with concrete exposed to ordinary air.

Each core preserved evidence of the reaction extending from its surface into the concrete. The researchers took small samples at different depths and measured how much carbonate had formed. Inside the artificial world, carbonation had proceeded much further. The later estimate was that the reaction had gone about ten times faster inside than outside.

Concrete contains compounds that can react with carbon dioxide. Calcium hydroxide, for example, can react to form calcium carbonate, a mineral found in limestone. Once carbon dioxide has entered that solid mineral, it is no longer present as a gas in the room.

By comparing the samples and estimating the total exposed area of concrete, the team worked out how much carbon dioxide the building could have taken up. Even allowing for uncertainty, the amount was consistent with the missing gas. That was important: a chemical reaction that merely occurred inside the building wasn't enough. It had to be large enough to account for the discrepancy.

There was another clue in the material. Naturally occurring carbon has different forms with slightly different masses. The proportions found in the concrete were consistent with the carbon missing from the comparison between vegetation and soil. Two different kinds of evidence now pointed to the same place: how much mineral had formed, and what kind of carbon it contained.

Smaller contributions from other chemical processes were still possible. But the main answer was now clear: organic matter in the soil and insufficient light led to a net loss of oxygen, and the concrete captured much of the carbon dioxide released along the way. The walls had concealed the rise in the second gas, even as the first gas was being used up.

The oxygen supplied in January did not make the original imbalance disappear. By August nineteen ninety-three the concentration had fallen again, and the operators started another addition of oxygen before the mission ended.

On September twenty-sixth, nineteen ninety-three, all eight people emerged alive. The definitive published investigation followed in January of the next year. The crew's two years inside had not demonstrated independent life support. But the experiment had supplied an explanation for why an essential cycle had failed, based on samples taken from the actual building.

The story did not end with the first crew. After a change in management, the complex was adapted for other kinds of ecological research. The University of Arizona eventually acquired it and uses it as a laboratory where researchers can study living systems on a much larger scale than a classroom experiment.

The next time someone designs a sealed habitat with plants to provide breathable air, this is one of the questions they have to account for: what will the whole system consume and produce, including the materials used to build it? Eight people had learned to grow their food in an artificial world, but keeping its atmosphere in balance required understanding what happened not just in the leaves and soil, but inside the very concrete around them.
