Could you build a world small enough to fit inside a building, but large enough to feed eight people and regenerate the air they breathe? How would you find out what was consuming their oxygen? And why would that investigation end with scientists drilling into the concrete?

In the late nineteen eighties a complex of glass pyramids and domes began to take shape in the Arizona desert. It was called Biosphere Two because the original biosphere was the planet Earth. The idea was to recreate some of the processes that keep life going on Earth in a space small enough to study.

The inside was designed as a collection of connected environments. A small ocean bordered a tropical forest and mangrove wetlands. There was a grassland and a desert. Elsewhere there were the rooms where people would sleep and work, and a farm where their meals would begin as seeds.

The glass admitted daylight. Underneath the structure, a welded stainless steel barrier kept the ground outside separate from the soil inside. Mechanical systems circulated the air and controlled temperature and humidity. This wasn't a project that promised to run without energy: on Earth the Sun already supplies energy to our biosphere, and the artificial one also needed power for its machinery. What the crew hoped to recycle were materials such as air, water and nutrients.

On September twenty-sixth, nineteen ninety-one, eight people went inside for a planned two-year stay.

One of them, Jane Poynter, later described how long a familiar meal could take when she had to raise the ingredients herself. To make a pizza, there was wheat to grow for flour and goats to feed for milk and cheese. The work was measured in months before there was anything to put on the plate.

The animals and the farm weren't separate from the air supply. Every time the crew breathed out, they returned carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Growing plants used that carbon in their tissues. When the inhabitants ate the crops, some of the carbon passed into their own bodies and some eventually went back into the air.

The hope was that those cycles could keep operating without regular deliveries of food and oxygen. Instead, as the months passed, the very air that the crew had brought inside began to change in a way that made ordinary work increasingly difficult.

Initially, oxygen made up about twenty-one percent of their atmosphere. Sixteen months later it was down around fourteen percent. That didn't mean the rooms had suddenly lost their air pressure. The mixture itself had changed, and less of each breath was oxygen.

Poynter later recalled how exhausting it became to move about the enclosure and how sleep was interrupted by episodes of breathlessness. According to her account, even the doctor who was monitoring the crew had difficulty doing a simple column of arithmetic.

The crew weren't literally trapped: if necessary they could leave the building. But continuing with so little oxygen was not a reasonable test of self-sufficiency. In January nineteen ninety-three, the operators began supplementing the atmosphere with oxygen from outside. The news reports at the time described a recovery in the oxygen concentration and relief for the residents.

This intervention helped the people, but it didn't identify what was taking oxygen out of the atmosphere. And it meant the building was no longer demonstrating independent life support for the full two years.

One possible explanation for changing air in a sealed building would be leakage. Yet the engineers had tested for leaks, and they were small. With an oxygen-rich atmosphere outside and an oxygen-poor one inside, simply mixing the two would not explain a continuing drop. There had to be an internal process to account for the observations.

Consider the soil the designers had brought into the enclosure. Much of it was made from local soil, supplemented with compost and peat to provide organic matter. A plant could use the nutrients released from this material as it decomposed. But decomposing material is also a food supply for microscopic organisms that live in the ground.

Those organisms need oxygen for much of their activity. They consume it as they break down organic matter, and carbon dioxide is released. The fact that a farm or forest looks green doesn't tell you whether the system as a whole is adding oxygen to the air. Leaves photosynthesize during daylight, while organisms respire during the day and at night.

In Biosphere Two, plant growth was also constrained by the roof. Less than half the ambient light penetrated the glass, according to the later scientific investigation. The soil contained so much readily available organic material that the demand for oxygen exceeded what photosynthesis could replace.

That explained a great deal, but the researchers encountered a second and more interesting puzzle.

For a simple case of respiration, when an oxygen molecule is consumed, roughly a molecule of carbon dioxide is released. These aren't exact rules for every biological reaction, but they provide a useful way to check whether the measured change in one gas can account for the change in the other.

There was a carbon dioxide removal system in the enclosure, so its operation had to be included in the calculation. There was also carbon incorporated into growing vegetation. The investigation still found a large discrepancy. The carbon dioxide that went with the loss of oxygen had not all appeared where the researchers expected to find it.

They considered an ordinary construction material: concrete.

Concrete contains compounds that can react with carbon dioxide. Calcium hydroxide, for example, can react to form calcium carbonate, a mineral found in limestone. Once carbon dioxide has entered that solid mineral, it is no longer present as a gas in the room.

The team needed to find out whether enough of this had actually happened. Simply knowing concrete can react with a gas would not establish that it had done so on this scale.

On June seventh, nineteen ninety-three, researchers drilled cylindrical samples from twelve locations inside the complex. They also took four samples from concrete outside that had been poured at the same time. That gave them a way of comparing concrete exposed to the enclosed atmosphere with concrete exposed to ordinary air.

They removed small pieces from the cores at different distances below the surface and chemically measured their carbonate content. In the samples from the artificial world, the reaction had reached deeper and was more extensive. By comparing the profiles and estimating the exposed area, they worked out how much gas the material could have captured since the enclosure was sealed. Even allowing for the uncertainty in the calculation, the result was consistent with the missing carbon dioxide.

There was another way to check the explanation. Carbon occurs in forms with slightly different atomic masses. Measuring their relative proportions in soil, plants and carbonate gave an additional indication of where the carbon had ended up. The researchers found that the concrete was consistent with being the missing destination.

Now the story of the changing atmosphere made sense. An abundance of decomposable organic material, together with insufficient photosynthesis to balance respiration, had caused a net oxygen loss. The concrete had removed much of the carbon dioxide produced along the way. The two processes together accounted for the puzzle far better than treating the building as an inert container.

The oxygen supplied in January did not make the original imbalance disappear. By August nineteen ninety-three the concentration had fallen again, and the operators started another addition of oxygen before the mission ended.

In September, all eight people emerged alive. They had spent two years attempting to maintain a miniature living world, and they had needed outside assistance. Their experiment could not be presented as a successful two-year demonstration of an independent ecosystem. Yet there was now a documented explanation for a failure that had once seemed mysterious.

The story did not end with the first crew. After a change in management, the complex was adapted for other kinds of ecological research. The University of Arizona eventually acquired it and uses it as a laboratory where researchers can study living systems on a much larger scale than a classroom experiment.

The next time someone designs a sealed habitat with plants to provide breathable air, this is one of the questions they have to account for: what will the whole system consume and produce, including the materials used to build it? Eight people had learned to grow their food in an artificial world, but keeping its atmosphere in balance required understanding what happened not just in the leaves and soil, but inside the very concrete around them.
