A London transport inspector walked ahead of a bus with a burning flare. The driver could barely see the street.

On December fifth, nineteen fifty-two, London woke up to fog. That was hardly unusual. But this time, something was stopping the city's smoke from escaping.

It had been bitterly cold. Families were burning coal to heat their homes. Factories and power stations were burning it too. A layer of warmer air had settled over the colder air near the ground, preventing it from rising and mixing.

The smoke stayed down where people were breathing. Inside the fog, sulfur dioxide from burning coal could turn into droplets of sulfuric acid.

Doctor Donald Acheson lost his way on streets he knew. He had to follow the walls until he could read a street sign. At his hospital, the mortuary filled up.

People abandoned their cars. Most buses were eventually taken off the roads. Even staying inside couldn't keep the smog out.

On December ninth, the fog lifted. But the death rate didn't return to normal with it.

The initial estimate was about four thousand deaths. Decades later, a study examining the whole winter put the toll at roughly twelve thousand.

Britain passed a Clean Air Act in nineteen fifty-six. In designated areas, households had to change what they burned. Grants helped pay for new heating equipment. The conversion took years, but London never experienced another smog on that scale.

The city learned that the danger was not just how little people could see. It was what they were breathing.

And what did it take just to guide a bus through that air?
