Why did a bus in London need a man with a burning flare to guide it down the street? And why did people keep dying after the fog had disappeared?

In December nineteen fifty-two, a cold spell hit Britain. In London, millions of people lived in a city accustomed to smoke. Coal heated homes and supplied energy to industry. Power stations stood beside the Thames. An open fire was part of everyday life, especially on a freezing winter evening.

Fog was familiar, too. London had been living with dirty air for generations. But the weather on December fifth produced a much more dangerous combination.

Donald Acheson, a young doctor working in central London, went out on an errand. He knew the neighborhood. Yet he found himself unable to tell where he was. By following the walls of the buildings, he eventually got close enough to a street sign to read it.

A photograph from those days shows a transport inspector walking in front of a bus with a burning flare. The light was there to help guide the vehicle. The air that made the flare necessary was also filling Acheson's hospital.

To understand what had happened, imagine London from the side, as if you could see the layers of air above its rooftops. Normally, warm air near the ground can rise and mix with the air above it. But under the high-pressure system that December, warmer air lay above cold air near the surface. The cold layer was stuck beneath it.

Clear skies allowed the ground to cool. Moisture condensed into fog. There was little wind to clear the city, and smoke from its chimneys remained within the lower layer, which was only about a hundred to two hundred meters deep.

People kept heating their houses. Power stations and factories continued supplying the city. There were pollutants coming in from mainland Europe as well. More material accumulated in air that was hardly being replaced.

And there was another problem. Coal contains sulfur. When it burns, it releases sulfur dioxide. In the moist, polluted air, some of that gas can become sulfuric acid. Now the fog contained more than water and soot.

Measurements at twelve monitoring sites showed the scale of the change. After three to four days, average concentrations of smoke and sulfur dioxide were five to six times their usual levels. Some central London sites reached ten times normal.

Transport broke down. Cars were left in the streets, and at one stage most buses stopped running. The pollution found its way into buildings, too. At Acheson's hospital, patients were dying of heart and breathing problems. The mortuary could no longer accommodate them all.

Then, on December ninth, the fog cleared.

It should have looked like the end of the disaster. But when the daily pollution readings dropped, the number of deaths came down more slowly. The early death toll was around four thousand, already an extraordinary figure. In two thousand and one, researchers looked at the wider winter, comparing hospital and mortality records with other years. They estimated around twelve thousand excess deaths from December through February, including the immediate victims.

How could London stop this from happening again? Nobody could stop another cold spell. But Parliament could change what the city put into the air.

The government appointed an inquiry in nineteen fifty-three. Its recommendations helped shape the Clean Air Act of nineteen fifty-six. Local authorities could establish smoke-control areas where ordinary smoky fuels could no longer be burned. There was financial help for families who needed to alter their fireplaces or heating systems.

The change was slow. Cleaner fuel and new equipment had to be available, and many homes still relied on coal. In nineteen sixty-two, another smog killed an estimated seven hundred and fifty Londoners. Further legislation followed in nineteen sixty-eight.

Over time, gas and modern central heating replaced many of the old coal fires. London still gets fog. It still has air pollution. But there has been no repeat of the Great Smog on the scale of nineteen fifty-two.

The photograph of the man guiding a bus captured the most visible part of the disaster. The hospital records captured the part that lasted long after he could put the flare away.
