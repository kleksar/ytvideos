How did ordinary household fires help bring London to a standstill for five days? Why did the death toll rise even after the sky cleared? And why, six years after Britain passed a law designed to prevent another disaster, were Londoners still dying in the fog?

In the winter of nineteen fifty-two, London depended on coal. A fireplace in a living room was a source of warmth. Across the capital, factories needed fuel, and power stations burned coal to generate electricity. On the road, diesel buses had recently replaced electric trams. Each source of pollution had a different purpose, but the exhaust and smoke all ended up in the air the city shared.

London's reputation for fog was well established. The soot and fumes that mixed with it were equally familiar. A severe fog in nineteen forty-eight had already produced unusually high pollution readings. Yet the city carried on depending on the same fuels.

In late nineteen fifty-two, Britain was experiencing a cold winter, and households needed more heat. There is evidence that some of the coal they burned was particularly dirty; better-quality coal was being exported to earn money for a country whose economy was under strain.

On the fifth of December the weather gave all that pollution nowhere to go.

Doctor Donald Acheson was working at a hospital in central London when he went out on an errand. He knew the area, but the fog was so thick that he lost his bearings. He made his way along the buildings, using their walls to guide him until he could get close enough to read a street sign.

He made it back. The problem was that the hospital could no longer offer much separation between its patients and the air outside. People were arriving with severe respiratory and cardiac problems. The mortuary eventually ran out of room.

Meanwhile, on the streets, the transport system was struggling with the same fog in a completely different way. An extraordinary photograph shows an inspector of London Transport leading a bus with a burning flare. The light was a way to make a route visible to the driver.

What was happening overhead was less obvious. A high-pressure weather system had settled over the area. The air near the surface was cold. Above it was a warmer layer. Meteorologists call that reversal a temperature inversion.

Why should the order of those two layers matter? If a warm parcel of air rises, it can carry pollution away from the ground and mix with a much larger volume of air. But cold air beneath warmer air is relatively stable. The vertical mixing that would normally help disperse smoke was severely limited.

At the same time the clear sky had allowed the ground to lose heat. Moist air touching that cold surface cooled until water began condensing into tiny suspended droplets. The very light winds helped form a fog layer roughly a hundred to two hundred meters thick without clearing the city.

Below the inversion, the sources of smoke were still active. Think of the contrast between those sources and the weather. A household could burn another load of coal, and a power station could continue supplying electricity. But the volume of air available to dilute those emissions was limited.

Not all of the pollution was produced in London; some had come from industrial areas across the Channel. And the mix of pollutants was changing inside the fog itself. Sulfur dioxide emitted when coal burns can be transformed into sulfuric acid in moist air. The droplets were capable of carrying acidic material into the respiratory system.

That explains why visibility alone was an inadequate measure of the danger.

Fortunately, people were not left with photographs and memories as the only record of the event. There were air pollution measurements. A paper published in nineteen fifty-four by E. T. Wilkins of the Fuel Research Station describes what happened at twelve sites where daily samples were taken. Smoke and sulfur dioxide climbed sharply on the first day. Three to four days later, their mean concentrations were about five to six times normal. At individual sites in central London, the increase was as much as tenfold.

The pollution was also unevenly distributed. Some of the highest sulfur dioxide concentrations were recorded in riverside areas. And those readings from the severe fog in nineteen forty-eight? In the limited records available, concentrations had reached only about half to seventy percent of the nineteen fifty-two values. This new event was exceptional even when compared with a previous severe fog.

On the ground, people had to change how they moved. Drivers abandoned vehicles. At one point most bus services were cancelled. The fog was able to enter homes, and people with existing lung conditions were especially vulnerable. Staying warm indoors did not necessarily mean escaping the pollution.

On December ninth the fog dispersed. Roads could be used again. Yet a different set of numbers had begun to tell a more troubling story.

When Wilkins compared pollution records with daily deaths, the two rose together. But they did not fall together at the same speed. Mortality took longer to return to its normal level. Early figures put the death toll at about four thousand.

How many more deaths should be attributed to the episode? A person could suffer an illness during a cold winter for reasons unrelated to the fog. Even an increase in deaths after the fog did not automatically establish how many it had caused.

One possible explanation for the continued increase was influenza. The question mattered because it changed whether the event should be regarded as five days of acute poisoning or a disaster with much longer consequences.

Decades later, epidemiologists Michelle Bell and Devra Davis reexamined the winter of nineteen fifty-two and fifty-three. Rather than counting only the deaths during the fog, they compared several kinds of evidence with earlier years: respiratory and cardiac admissions, insurance claims, mortality records, reports of influenza, weather and pollution. Their two thousand and one study concluded that influenza did not adequately account for the continuing rise and estimated approximately twelve thousand excess deaths from December through February attributable to the smog.

That is an estimate derived from population records, not a list of twelve thousand individually established victims. It includes the deaths in the initial estimate. It reveals what was missed when the visible fog was treated as the boundary of the disaster.

But in the nineteen fifties, even the early figures had serious implications for Britain.

The government initially treated the episode as a natural calamity. Public pressure grew, and in July nineteen fifty-three an inquiry was appointed under Sir Hugh Beaver. Its reports identified a problem far beyond factory chimneys: the low-level emissions from countless domestic fires were also a major contributor.

A proposal to control those fires encountered a basic practical difficulty. What would people use to heat their homes instead? A family could not simply stop heating its house during winter. There had to be a replacement, and somebody had to pay for it.

A private member's bill introduced by Gerald Nabarro, with support from the smoke-abatement movement, helped push the issue forward. The government brought in its own bill. On July fifth, nineteen fifty-six, the Clean Air Act received royal assent.

Under the new framework, local authorities could establish areas where only authorized smokeless fuels could be used. The change didn't require everybody to abandon an open fire immediately. Some could continue using solid fuels such as coke, provided the fuel and equipment met the applicable requirements.

There were also grants. For eligible conversion work, central government covered forty percent, local authorities thirty percent, and the householder thirty percent. Imagine the difference between being told to replace your fireplace at your own expense and having seventy percent of the eligible cost covered.

The problem was no longer simply writing a rule. It was getting equipment, fuel and money into neighborhoods across the country. Progress was uneven. The nineteen fifty-six Act enabled local authorities to create smoke-control areas but did not make that action universally compulsory.

Another serious smog in nineteen sixty-two killed around seven hundred and fifty people in London. In nineteen sixty-eight Parliament passed further clean-air legislation. Greater London's smoke-control program was not fully completed until nineteen eighty.

As heating changed and coal smoke declined, the kind of fog that had stopped that bus became less common. The city still has pollutants that damage health. But it has never experienced a repeat of the Great Smog on the scale of December nineteen fifty-two.

There are two surviving records worth putting beside each other: the photograph of a transport inspector helping a bus find its way, and the mortality figures that continued rising after the streets became visible. One shows the city losing its ability to move. The other explains why making the streets visible again was never enough. London had to change what came out of the chimneys.
