The last known natural case of smallpox began with a driver asking for directions.

On October 12, 1977, two children with smallpox were being taken to an isolation camp near Merca, Somalia. Ali Maow Maalin, a cook at the local hospital, climbed into their vehicle to show the driver the way.

Ali had helped vaccinate other people. But, by his own account, he'd avoided being vaccinated himself because he was afraid of the needle.

The journey put him in contact with the children for less than fifteen minutes. When investigators traced the outbreak, they missed him.

Ten days after the ride, Ali developed a fever. He was treated for malaria. Then a rash appeared, and the diagnosis changed to chickenpox.

Ali suspected it was smallpox. He was afraid to go into isolation, though, and a nurse eventually reported him.

The hospital stopped admitting new patients. Vaccination teams went to the homes around his and then expanded their search. More than fifty thousand people were vaccinated in two weeks. Investigators followed up with his contacts.

Ali recovered. No further cases were linked to him.

That still wasn't enough to declare smallpox gone. Teams kept looking for infections. A laboratory-linked outbreak in Britain in 1978 also had to be distinguished from natural transmission.

In May 1980, the World Health Assembly declared smallpox eradicated.

Ali went on to fight polio, telling reluctant parents about the vaccination he'd once avoided. In 2013, while still working in that campaign, he died of malaria.

But how did the man who later spent years fighting disease become smallpox's last known natural patient in the first place?
