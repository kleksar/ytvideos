How did a man who helped vaccinate people become the last person known to catch smallpox naturally? And how could anyone be sure there wasn't another patient somewhere in the world?

In October 1977, health workers in Somalia were following a smallpox outbreak among families who moved from place to place. By then, almost every country had stopped the disease from spreading. The remaining infections in the Horn of Africa were being hunted down one by one.

Two sick children needed to be taken to an isolation camp near the coastal town of Merca. On October 12, their driver stopped at the local hospital for directions.

A twenty-three-year-old cook named Ali Maow Maalin volunteered to go along. He knew the way. He had even worked in the campaign against smallpox.

But the man sitting in the vehicle with those children hadn't been vaccinated himself.

Years later, Ali explained that he'd been frightened of the needle. He had managed to give the impression that he had already had the vaccination. Now he spent less than fifteen minutes with two people who had the disease.

Health workers investigating the children's outbreak didn't identify him as a contact.

On October 22, Ali became ill while at work. The fever was first treated as malaria. Four days later, a rash appeared. His diagnosis was changed to chickenpox, and he was sent home.

That was the second chance to recognize smallpox, and it had been missed.

Ali knew what smallpox looked like from the campaign's posters. He suspected that he had it, but he was afraid of being isolated. Eventually a nurse from the hospital reported the illness. By the end of October, Ali's case had been recognized for what it was.

The problem was no longer just how to care for one patient. People had visited him while he was sick. He had also been in a hospital, where there were other patients and staff. Investigators had to find out who might have been exposed.

The hospital stopped taking new patients and the people inside were quarantined and vaccinated. Teams began with the houses around Ali's home and widened their campaign. In the following two weeks, more than fifty thousand people were vaccinated.

They were also looking for another rash. A vaccinated contact might be protected, but someone already infected could still develop symptoms. Investigators followed the people who had been exposed.

Ali survived. And the search around him found no new infection linked to his illness.

By January 1978, a report could point to Ali as the last known case. But the word known was doing a lot of work.

Heavy rains had made roads difficult or impossible to use in parts of Somalia. Health workers had continued on foot, and some areas hadn't been fully searched. The campaign intensified its efforts once the dry season returned.

The goal wasn't to finish a vaccination tally. It was to establish that no chain of natural transmission was still running.

There was a further complication. In 1978 a laboratory-linked smallpox outbreak in Birmingham, England, killed a woman named Janet Parker. It was not a continuation of the chain in Somalia, but it meant Ali had not been the last person ever to contract the disease.

Experts kept examining the evidence from affected countries. In December 1979, an international commission certified eradication. On May 8, 1980, the World Health Assembly endorsed that conclusion.

Ali's brief journey had become the last known link in the natural spread of a disease that had afflicted people for centuries.

He lived for another thirty-six years. And when health workers began trying to eliminate polio, Ali joined them.

Somalia stopped transmission, then faced a new outbreak in 2005. Ali was among the people working to reach children again. When parents hesitated over vaccination, he could tell them what had happened to him when he had avoided his own.

Somalia was declared polio-free again in 2008, although the disease returned later. In 2013, Ali was still a district polio officer in the area where he'd once worked as a cook. He developed malaria and died.

The man who had been missed by a vaccination campaign became one of the people carrying the next one from door to door.
