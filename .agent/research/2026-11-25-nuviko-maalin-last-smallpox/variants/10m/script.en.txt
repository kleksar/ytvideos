How did the last known natural infection of smallpox begin with an ordinary request for directions? Why did health workers vaccinate tens of thousands of people after they found one patient? And what happened to that patient once the world's campaign against smallpox was over?

Smallpox had been killing people for centuries. A person who survived it could be left with permanent scars. Long before the last patient was identified, people had learned that vaccination could prevent the disease. Knowing how to protect an individual, however, was very different from actually stopping the virus across the world.

When the World Health Organization began a global eradication effort in 1959, many countries didn't have enough resources to carry it out. There were shortages of money, staff and vaccine. The intensified campaign that began in 1967 made better use of vaccines that were easier to transport and store, and placed much greater emphasis on finding cases.

Suppose a team reached a district and vaccinated thousands of people. That was useful. But the tally alone didn't tell the team where the people with smallpox were. The remaining infection could be in a village that hadn't been reached. Someone who had been exposed could travel to a different district and become ill there.

So teams searched for people with the disease, isolated cases, identified contacts and concentrated vaccination around them. Instead of treating the number of doses as the only measure of progress, they also followed the chains by which the virus was spreading.

In 1975, a young girl named Rahima Banu became the last known person to catch the more severe form of smallpox naturally, in Bangladesh. A different form was still circulating in eastern Africa. By 1977, the remaining known transmission was concentrated in Somalia.

One of the challenges was that the people involved in outbreaks did not necessarily stay in the same settlement. Families moved between settlements. A visit to a place where someone had been sick might not locate everyone who had been there when the illness began.

In the late summer of 1977, a smallpox outbreak developed among a group of nomadic families in southern Somalia. Over the following weeks, several children became ill.

Two of the sick children were discovered in October. Arrangements were made to take them to isolation near the coastal town of Merca.

On October 12 their driver stopped at the hospital to find the way. Ali Maow Maalin, a twenty-three-year-old cook who worked there, offered to accompany him. He had worked as a vaccinator, so he knew about the disease that the health workers were trying to contain.

He hadn't received his own vaccination.

Ali later recalled that he was scared of the needle and had passed himself off as someone who had already been vaccinated. It wasn't a problem that a stranger looking at his job would necessarily have guessed.

He rode with the children for less than fifteen minutes. At the isolation camp, the children could be separated from other people. But one more person had been in contact with them, and that person wasn't on the investigators' list.

Ten days later, on October 22, Ali developed a fever while working. It was initially treated as malaria.

In a place where malaria occurred, a fever wasn't by itself proof of smallpox. But the patient's recent contact with two children would have mattered. The team treating him didn't make that connection.

On October 26, Ali developed a rash. He was thought to have chickenpox and was sent home. That illness is caused by a different virus, despite its English name.

Ali had seen pictures of smallpox through his work with the campaign. He suspected that the rash was something more serious, but he didn't want to go to an isolation camp. In his own later account, he was afraid he might die there.

A nurse eventually reported the illness. By the end of October it had been identified as smallpox.

Now the work of tracing had to begin again, this time with a patient who had spent days in contact with other people. Investigators had to account for visits at home as well as contacts at the hospital. They couldn't simply assume that everyone who knew Ali had been protected against smallpox.

The hospital halted new admissions. People inside were quarantined and vaccinated. Teams began vaccinating around Ali's home and then reached a much larger part of the town.

Within two weeks, they had administered more than fifty thousand vaccinations.

But an injection was only one part of the response. If a person had been exposed before the campaign reached them, the health workers needed to check whether that person subsequently became sick. There was a reason for tracing people by name rather than just counting them in a neighborhood.

The investigators found more than a hundred contacts. They followed up with them, and no subsequent case could be linked to Ali.

Ali recovered. The outbreak had been contained.

Elsewhere, the next question was whether there was still another outbreak that nobody knew about. Ali's exposure had gone unrecognized even though the investigators knew about the original outbreak. If that could happen at a hospital, the absence of new reports from much more remote areas was not enough on its own.

During the rains late in 1977, some roads in Somalia became impassable. Teams continued on foot, but searches in certain areas remained incomplete. Their resources were concentrated where the chance of undetected cases was considered greatest.

When the dry season returned in early 1978, the teams could restore more complete searches. The reports still showed no new naturally acquired smallpox.

The campaign required two years of effective surveillance before confirming that the last endemic area was free of the disease. It was an important distinction: two years in which you were able to look for an infection were worth more than two years in which nobody happened to tell you about one.

Then, in 1978, there was a smallpox outbreak in Birmingham, England, associated with a laboratory. A woman named Janet Parker died. Her illness had to be accounted for, even though it was not connected to the natural chain of infection that had ended in Somalia.

The commission evaluating eradication looked at evidence from countries where smallpox had been endemic and countries where it could have been introduced. Its members signed their conclusion in December 1979. On May 8, 1980, the World Health Assembly formally endorsed the finding.

Ali was the last known naturally infected patient. Janet Parker was the last person known to die from smallpox. Those statements describe two different events, and both belong in the story.

Ali returned to life in the Merca area. Years later, a different eradication effort brought him back to the work of vaccination.

Polio can cause lifelong paralysis. Somalia had interrupted its indigenous transmission by 2002, but in 2005 the virus was introduced again. More cases followed. During years of conflict, families were moving and health services had difficulty reaching them.

For the campaign workers, getting to a household once was often not enough. Children needed repeated vaccinations, and children could be missed when their families moved. In the effort that followed, more than ten thousand Somali workers and volunteers helped repeatedly vaccinate over a million children.

Ali traveled as a health worker and spoke to families who hesitated. He could describe his own decision to avoid vaccination, the disease he subsequently contracted, and what it took to stop it. The campaign's abstract message had a person standing in front of them who could explain it from his life.

In March 2008, Somalia was again declared polio-free. It was not a permanent end to the work: the virus returned in 2013.

Ali was still working as a district polio officer when he became ill with malaria in July of that year. He died in the district where he had helped fight both diseases.

The last natural case of smallpox wasn't the result of a final flawless operation. It was an infection missed in a contact list, a mistaken diagnosis and a patient who delayed reporting his illness. The response found the exposed people and stopped the chain from extending further. Then came the long search to make sure the same disease wasn't still traveling somewhere else.

And the man who once avoided a vaccination spent the later years of his life helping other people get theirs.
