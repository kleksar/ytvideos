How do you lift a ship that is no longer strong enough to hold itself together? And why would you spend the next thirty years spraying it with liquid?

The Mary Rose sank off Portsmouth in 1545. For centuries, mud protected much of its right-hand side while exposed wood deteriorated. By 1982, what remained was more like an open shell than a complete ship.

The engineers could not simply wrap cables around it and pull. They built a steel frame above the wreck and connected it to strong points spread across the surviving timbers. Hydraulic jacks lifted the wood a little at a time until it came free of the mud. A floating crane then carried the wreck, still underwater, into a padded steel cradle.

During the transfer, one leg of the frame bent and had to be removed. The following night, a lifting sling was fitted incorrectly.

On October eleventh, the cradle rose through the water with the frame tilted. Later, one corner suddenly dropped into place with a loud crash.

It looked like the ship might have been crushed. In fact, the misplaced sling had moved into its proper position. Only one timber suffered minor damage. The wreck reached Portsmouth.

But the wood had become saturated with water. If it dried too quickly, it could shrink and split. Conservators kept it wet, then gradually treated it with a waxy substance that strengthened the weakened wood.

In 2016, after years of carefully controlled drying, the major conservation work was complete. The ship that could not safely be lifted by itself was finally on display without its protective drying enclosure. It had survived the sea, the crane, and the return to dry land.
