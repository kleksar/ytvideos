Why did a ship that had spent more than four centuries underwater need a steel bed before it could be lifted? And what made that enormous steel structure suddenly drop during the recovery?

In Portsmouth, England, you can stand in front of the Mary Rose and look into the remains of a warship built for Henry the Eighth. One side of the ship is missing. The other is still tall enough for you to see how the decks were arranged.

That shape tells us almost as much about the recovery as it does about the sinking.

The Mary Rose went down in the Solent on July nineteenth, 1545. A surviving sailor said water rushed in through open gunports when the ship leaned during a turn. The precise combination of causes is still debated. What mattered to the people who would eventually recover her was how she came to rest.

The wreck lay on its right side, which sank into sediment. The parts above the sediment deteriorated; much of the buried wood survived. By the time modern divers found the timbers in 1971, they were looking at a fragile portion of a ship, not a vessel they could pump out and float home.

The archaeologists also had to decide what they were actually trying to save. Pulling the wreck out with all its contents still inside would sacrifice the location of objects that could tell them how people lived on board. From 1979, divers worked through a numbered grid over the site. They recorded what they found and sent objects up separately.

That left the engineers with a huge wooden structure that had lost much of its original strength.

If they ran lifting cables beneath it, the weight could concentrate on a few areas of the hull. And before it could move at all, the wreck had to be pulled free of the sediment that held it down. A powerful crane alone could not solve either problem.

Instead, the team put a steel frame on four legs above the wreck. They fastened lifting wires to bolts installed at carefully chosen points throughout the surviving hull. The bolts and plates also helped hold the old wood together.

Jacks on the frame gradually raised the hull just far enough to break it loose from the seabed. The crane could then take the weight.

But bringing the ship straight up would have left that fragile shell hanging from its attachment points. So the engineers had prepared another structure, a steel cradle shaped to match the wreck. They lowered it beside the ship. The crane moved the Mary Rose into the cradle while both were still beneath the water.

Now the wood had support underneath it.

The operation did not go exactly to plan. On October ninth, one leg of the frame was bent during the transfer and could not fit properly into the cradle. The team removed it. The remaining legs could carry the load.

The following night, the crew changed the lifting slings to take the extra weight of the cradle. One of them was fitted incorrectly. When the structure emerged on October eleventh, part of the frame sat higher than it should.

Then, late that morning, there was a tremendous crash. The raised corner dropped.

What looked like the start of a collapse was actually the sling sliding into its proper position. The steel leg settled farther into its guide. The ship suffered minor damage to one timber, but the lift continued. The Mary Rose reached the dockyard.

And that was not the end of the job.

Inside the old wood, microscopic damage had left spaces filled with water. Letting the water evaporate without treatment could make the structure shrink, distort and crack. The team kept the wreck wet on land and later sprayed it with polyethylene glycol, a waxy material that could stabilize the timber. They changed the treatment as the wood absorbed it.

In 2013, the sprays finally stopped. The conservators spent another three years drying the ship in a controlled environment before the enclosure was removed.

Look again at the Mary Rose in the museum. It is not the complete ship that sailed out in 1545. It is the part that survived, kept in the shape it still had when recovered. The engineers did not need to make it seaworthy again. They needed to get it safely out of one environment and into another, without destroying the very thing they had come to save.
