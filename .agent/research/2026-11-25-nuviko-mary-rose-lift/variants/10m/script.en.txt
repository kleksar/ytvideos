What would happen if you took a crane to a wooden shipwreck that had been buried in mud for four hundred years and simply pulled it out? Why did the engineers who rescued the Mary Rose use two enormous steel structures instead of one? And why did the ship spend decades being sprayed after it was back on land?

At the Mary Rose Museum in Portsmouth, the surviving hull looks almost like a ship that has been sliced open. You can see the ends of the decks and look into the spaces where people once worked. But there is no matching side of the hull to close those spaces up.

This is not how the ship was built. Nor was that side lost in a dramatic accident when the wreck was being raised in 1982. To understand why the recovery was so difficult, we have to go back to the day the ship sank.

On July nineteenth, 1545, Henry the Eighth's fleet was confronting the French in the Solent, the water between the English mainland and the Isle of Wight. The Mary Rose had served for thirty-four years. She was not a new ship going out for the first time.

A sailor who escaped the sinking told a story of a turn, wind in the sails and water pouring through the open gunports. That account does not settle every question about why the ship went down. But there is a useful detail about what followed: she sank close enough to Portsmouth for her masts to remain visible above the surface.

Henry wanted his ship back. The men brought in to recover her had an approach already used for sunken vessels. They would use two larger ships to help lift the Mary Rose from below and bring her into shallower water, where she could be emptied and pumped out.

Preparations were made and the sails were removed. Instead of getting the vessel upright, the salvage crews ended up breaking the masts. The ship stayed where she was. Divers recovered some valuable equipment over the following years, but by 1552 work had ended.

The sea gradually changed the wreck. The hull had come to rest on its right side. Sediment covered much of that side, limiting the oxygen available to organisms that could destroy the wood. The portions that were exposed to the water were less fortunate. The result was a ship with a remarkably recognizable shape on one side and very little of the other side left.

In 1836, fishermen snagged nets near the site. Divers located the wreck and began bringing up objects, including guns. Some recovered wood was even turned into souvenirs.

There was a widespread story after the site was abandoned in 1843 that the remains had been destroyed by the Royal Engineers. The surviving documentation does not establish that this happened. And more than a century later, a team of volunteers set out to find what might still be down there.

Alexander McKee began the search in 1965 with the help of divers and archaeologists, including Margaret Rule. An old chart showed where the wreck had been in the nineteenth century. Acoustic surveys helped narrow the area, but a shape buried in sediment was not the same thing as a confirmed ship.

In 1970 the team recovered an iron gun of the right period. The following year, diver Percy Ackland found the curved timbers that had once formed part of a ship's skeleton. By the end of the day, divers had uncovered a row of frames extending for roughly thirty metres.

There was far more of the Mary Rose left than a scattering of objects. And suddenly the question was no longer just whether they could find it. It was whether they could bring it home.

They first needed to know what they had. Trenches at different parts of the wreck revealed the angle at which the ship was lying and showed that more than one deck survived in places. Eventually the decision was made to excavate the ship and raise the empty hull for a museum.

This was unlike removing a single object. If a chest came up without a record of where it was found, some of its historical value would disappear. If a heavy loose timber fell during the lift, it could damage another part of the ship. Archaeologists and divers worked from a marked grid, recording objects and removing them. The work also included thousands of loose pieces of wood, which were kept wet separately.

The shape of the empty hull created a problem for the engineers. Imagine lifting a complete barrel in a sling. Now imagine that half the barrel is gone and the remaining staves are barely holding together. The same lifting method can squeeze or distort the open structure.

The Mary Rose needed the lifting forces divided between many points. A steel frame was built and set above the wreck on four legs. Workers installed bolts and plates on selected areas of the hull and connected them to the frame with wires. The bolts helped hold the wood together as well as provide lifting points.

Even then, the team could not just pull. The buried wood was held down by sediment. Hydraulic jacks raised the frame in small increments, freeing the wreck gradually. The floating crane took over once the hull was clear.

The second steel structure was waiting nearby on the seabed. It was a cradle made to fit the outline of the wreck, with cushions to distribute the weight of the wood. The crane shifted the ship over to it without first exposing the hull to the air. The frame and cradle were designed to join together.

But one of the frame's legs had bent during the transfer on October ninth, 1982. The crew removed it rather than delay the recovery for months. The other legs could support the load.

There was another difficulty, less obvious. The team changed the slings underwater so that they could lift the cradle as well as the hull. One sling was incorrectly positioned. When the package started to emerge on October eleventh, one corner of the frame stood visibly higher than the rest.

As the operation continued in front of the cameras, that corner suddenly fell. There was a loud crash.

The sound suggested something had failed. But what actually happened was that the sling slipped into its correct position and the frame settled into the cradle. One timber was slightly damaged; the hull was not crushed. The ship was placed on a barge and taken to the dockyard where it had once been built.

The recovery had solved a problem on the scale of a ship. The next one was on the scale of its wood cells.

The sediment had protected the buried side from many larger organisms, but it had not stopped every kind of decay. Some of the material inside the timber was gone and the spaces were filled with water. Drying the ship as if it were ordinary wet wood could cause serious damage.

The conservators covered it and used water sprays to keep it from drying out. They built a shelter around the hull. Fresh water circulated over the timbers, washing out salts while keeping them wet. Some of the loose timbers were returned to the structure, although it could not bear the weight of every piece that had been removed.

The long-term treatment used polyethylene glycol, often called PEG. Think of the old wood as a weakened framework filled with liquid. The aim was to stabilize it before removing that liquid. Different grades of PEG could reach different parts of the timber. The team used a spray system instead of trying to build a tank big enough to immerse the entire wreck.

For years the liquid flowed over the wood, was collected below and pumped round again. Then the treatment shifted to a grade of PEG intended to strengthen the more degraded parts.

In 2013, the spray system was shut down. A controlled drying enclosure around the hull let the conservators regulate the surrounding air. They monitored the timbers while moisture slowly left them. By 2016 the enclosure was no longer needed for the major drying treatment, and visitors could view the ship without it.

Even now, the museum controls the climate and measures movement in the hull. That is part of keeping the surviving material intact, not an attempt to reconstruct a missing half and pretend that nothing happened in the Solent.

When we look through the open side of the Mary Rose today, we can see a ship that Henry's own salvage crews could not recover. The work in 1982 did not reverse four centuries of decay. It gave the surviving wood a new way to support itself. The work that followed gave it a way to remain on land. Both were necessary for the same result: a piece of the Tudor world that people can still stand in front of.
