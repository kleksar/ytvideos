To save the Leaning Tower of Pisa, engineers made the ground beneath its higher side sink.

In 1990, the city closed the tower to visitors. Its weight was pressing heavily on the soil on the south side, and the lean was getting worse. A permanent steel support would have changed the building everyone came to see.

So the engineers placed hundreds of tonnes of lead on the north side of the foundation. It took some of the strain off the south. But when they tried replacing the weights with anchors in the ground, the tower started moving the wrong way. They had to add even more lead.

Then they tried a different idea on a concrete test foundation. A drill took out a little soil beneath the raised edge. The small cavity closed and the edge came down. But when they drilled too far, the opposite edge began sinking. It took six weeks to recover.

In February 1999, they started working beneath the actual tower. They removed tiny amounts of soil and checked the movement before the next extraction.

Soon the tower was moving north. Then one day it moved south again. The measurements showed that the dangerous lower edge wasn't sinking. A sudden cold spell explained the movement. When temperatures rose, the tower resumed moving north.

The team expanded the work, removed the lead and installed drainage to control groundwater. They had shifted the seventh floor back by about forty-four centimetres.

In December 2001 visitors climbed the tower again. It was still leaning, just as the restoration had intended. How had the engineers made it safer without taking away its famous lean?
