Why did engineers remove earth from underneath a tower that was already sinking? Why did they spend eleven years making it safer, yet leave it leaning?

In 1990, the gates of the Leaning Tower of Pisa closed to visitors. The marble bell tower had been standing for centuries, but its angle was still increasing. At the seventh floor, the building hung roughly four and a half metres out from the vertical line through its base.

The trouble lay beneath the marble. The tower stood on soft deposits that compressed unevenly under its weight. As the tower leaned south, more of its weight bore down towards that side. The masonry was under strain too.

The experts appointed to save it faced a peculiar restriction. They couldn't simply build a permanent support beside the tower, or turn it into an upright building. They had to preserve the medieval monument, lean and all.

Their first major intervention gave Pisa something even stranger to look at. In 1993, stacks of lead appeared around the north side of the base. Six hundred tonnes of metal acted as a counterweight. The tower moved back a little.

That bought the engineers some room to work. But two years later, when they tried replacing the lead with anchors buried in the ground, the tower's southward movement accelerated. They abandoned the attempt and increased the load to nine hundred tonnes.

The permanent repair would have to be much gentler. John Burland, a member of the committee, worked on a method of taking small amounts of soil from under the high side of the foundation. If that side came down, the tower could rotate back towards the north without being pushed at the top.

They built a loaded concrete foundation to test the idea. A drill was sent under its raised edge. When the drill was withdrawn, the space left behind gradually closed.

It worked, until the trial crew extracted soil too far under the foundation. The low edge started sinking before the instruments showed the full change in tilt. The test foundation turned the wrong way. Recovery took six weeks.

Now they knew which measurement mattered most. Watching the angle of the real tower wasn't enough. They also had to watch the height of its southern edge.

By February 1999, cables had been installed as a precaution. The crew began with twelve boreholes on the north side. At first, the engineers removed only about twenty litres of earth a day.

Measurements went from the site to the engineers by fax. Instructions for the next day's work came back in writing. After a few days with almost no response, the tower started moving north.

On February twenty-third, the direction suddenly reversed.

The engineers checked the southern edge. It had not settled. A cold northerly wind and a drop in temperature matched movements recorded in earlier winters. When the weather warmed again, the tower resumed its northward journey.

The limited trial gave the committee enough confidence to expand the work. In 2000, forty-one boreholes allowed the crew to work across a much wider part of the foundation. When the tower tended to turn slightly east, more soil was extracted towards the west.

Then the lead began coming off, a few blocks at a time. The repair had to stand on its own once the last counterweight was gone.

By June 2001, even the precautionary cables and drills had been removed. About thirty-eight cubic metres of soil had been taken out. The seventh floor had moved back roughly forty-four centimetres, and a drainage system controlled the changing groundwater level under the north side.

On December fifteenth, people entered the tower again. They climbed a staircase that was still very obviously tilted.

The engineers hadn't erased centuries of history. They had changed the way the ground supported the building. The lean was still there for visitors to experience, and the dangerous movement had been brought under control.
