How do you repair a building when its most famous feature is also the thing threatening to destroy it? Why did removing earth work better than adding supports? And how could the engineers tell whether the tower was in danger, when even a change in the weather could make it move?

In the medieval square at Pisa, the bell tower stands apart from the cathedral. Its curved shape is easier to see if you look at the columns one floor at a time. The builders had adjusted the heights of the stones as they went along. Part of the tower's history is written into the building itself.

The first stones were laid in 1173. Below them were layers of soft silt and clay. The weight of the growing tower compressed those layers, and the ground under one part of the foundation went down farther than under another.

Work stopped about five years after construction began. We don't know exactly why. What we do know is that during the long interruption the soil had time to become stronger under the load already on it.

Work resumed almost a century later. By the late thirteenth century the builders had reached the seventh level, and construction stopped again. The bell chamber was only added in the fourteenth century. Those interruptions had helped the ground carry a building which, according to modern analysis, would not have survived being completed in a single continuous operation.

But the problem didn't end with the last bell.

In 1838, the architect Alessandro della Gherardesca wanted to expose the original stonework at the base of the tower. A walkway was excavated around it. On the low side, the work went beneath the level of the groundwater, and water rushed in. Evidence suggests that the tower leaned farther south by more than a quarter of a degree.

In the twentieth century, efforts to repair the foundation and pumping of water from deeper ground were both followed by further movement in the wrong direction. By 1990, the tower was closed to visitors.

An international committee now had to save the bell tower. The work would reveal something surprising about its movement.

The straightforward explanation was that the southern edge of the building was steadily sinking into soft clay. But records of its position going back decades showed another part of the picture: the northern edge was rising. The foundation was rotating as a whole. The centre wasn't simply sinking faster than the rest of the square.

Imagine a heavy stack of blocks on a soft surface. When it leans a little, the ground resists the extra pressure on one side. But with enough lean, the pressure can become concentrated there. If the ground cannot resist the increase in overturning force, the movement makes itself worse.

The engineers calculated that Pisa was close to that condition. They couldn't safely dig under the low side to build a stronger support. And the risk wasn't just in the ground. Stresses inside the old masonry could damage the tower during an aggressive repair.

In 1993 the committee tried something temporary. Workers installed a removable concrete ring around the base and stacked six hundred tonnes of lead on its northern side. The counterweight reduced the force trying to turn the tower south. Its angle decreased slightly.

It was an effective precaution, but a very conspicuous one. In September 1995 the team attempted to exchange the weights for anchors in the ground. During the work the southward movement accelerated. Instead of getting rid of the lead, they had to increase it to nine hundred tonnes.

The engineers needed a way of moving the tower without putting new permanent structures on it. Among the possibilities they considered were wells to lower groundwater, electrical methods of moving water through the soil, and ways of loading the ground near the high side. None offered a satisfactory solution.

The most promising alternative was to take some of the ground away.

John Burland and the other specialists knew that controlled removal of soil had been used elsewhere to adjust the settlement of buildings. But a small mistake under Pisa could do lasting damage. Before touching it, the engineers set up a seven-metre-wide concrete test foundation and loaded it off centre.

They developed a drill inside a protective steel casing. It could remove a small volume of soil without digging an open tunnel beneath the foundation. As the drill came out, the cavity would gradually close. The high side would settle by a little.

During the trial, the crew extracted soil too far under the concrete. The lower side began settling rapidly. Only then did the foundation start rotating the wrong way.

The test had exposed a crucial delay. By the time the wrong direction of tilt became obvious, the ground supporting the low edge was already moving. It took six weeks for the trial foundation to become stable again. When the method was refined, the team could make it turn in the intended direction.

The real tower needed protection if anything went wrong. In late 1998, steel cables extended from its third storey to frames about a hundred metres away to the north. They were lightly tensioned. Their purpose was to restrain adverse movement if necessary, not to pull the tower upright.

The first extraction beneath the real building took place on February ninth, 1999. The crew used twelve boreholes over a limited width. They removed only around twenty litres of soil a day at the beginning.

Measurements of tilt and settlement were faxed from the square twice a day. The responsible engineer sent signed instructions specifying the objective of the next extraction. They had agreed in advance on thresholds for responding to unfavourable changes.

At first the tower barely responded. Then it started turning north. On February twenty-third the instruments recorded a change back towards the south.

That was precisely the sort of movement the team had been watching for. But the southern edge of the foundation had not sunk. A cold wind and falling temperatures offered an explanation consistent with the tower's past behaviour. It had made small movements like this during earlier cold weather. When the temperature rose, the northward movement resumed.

By early June the first extraction programme had already moved the tower farther than the minimum intended for the trial. Work stopped to observe the response. The movement continued more slowly, and taking away a few lead blocks eventually brought it to a stop.

The southern edge had actually risen during the trial. That was evidence that the foundation was unloading on its most vulnerable side.

In 2000 a wider array of forty-one boreholes went to work. The team could now take out about one hundred and twenty litres of earth per day on average. As the tower showed a tendency to rotate slightly east, the engineers adjusted the distribution of extraction towards the west.

They began removing the lead blocks from the temporary ring in May. As the counterweight diminished, the permanent repair had to carry an increasing share of the job.

The last block came off in January 2001. In May the precautionary cables were taken away. That was followed by another small movement south, which the team corrected with a little more soil extraction. The final extraction took place on June sixth.

The engineers also had to deal with the changing groundwater. The old measurements and new observations showed a connection between heavy rain, rising water levels on the north side and incremental southward rotation. So drainage was installed to limit the fluctuations. It addressed a process that simply correcting the tower's angle would not have dealt with.

On December fifteenth, 2001, tourists were allowed back inside. The seventh floor had moved about forty-four centimetres towards the north, roughly restoring the tilt the tower had before the nineteenth-century excavation. Later measurements showed that its residual northward motion had slowed substantially and the water levels were under control.

Burland returned with his family in 2011. They could see the stonework without the stacks of lead or the precautionary cables that had once surrounded it.

For a person standing in the square, the greatest achievement was almost invisible. The tower still looked wonderfully wrong. Beneath it, the load was being carried in a safer way, and that was exactly the change the restoration had sought.
