Why did engineers remove earth from beneath the Leaning Tower of Pisa, when the ground itself was threatening to give way? Why did they want the tower to keep its lean? And how could a change in the weather help them decide whether their repair was working?

In 1990, the bell tower was closed to visitors. The Italian government appointed fourteen specialists, including soil engineer John Burland. They were dealing with a building weighing fourteen thousand five hundred tonnes. Its top was moving farther south by the equivalent of about one and a half millimetres a year. That tiny movement mattered because the structure was already close to losing stability.

Three years later, visitors to the square could see what the engineers had done. They had put six hundred tonnes of lead around the north side of the base, reducing the force trying to tip the tower over by about a tenth. In 1995, when the committee tried replacing the lead with anchors in the ground, the tower started moving south again. Instead of taking away the lead, they had to bring in three hundred more tonnes.

The lead bought time, but the committee wanted to remove it without losing the character of the building. Look at the rows of columns: the tower itself bends. Medieval builders had used stone blocks of different heights to compensate for the lean. Completely straightening it would have erased part of the history they were trying to preserve.

Construction began in 1173 and took nearly two centuries, with two long interruptions. The cause of the first break is unknown. While the unfinished tower stood, the clay gradually became stronger under its weight. Modern analysis suggests that completing the building without those breaks would have overloaded the ground.

Later alterations gave the committee another reason for caution. In 1838 a walkway was dug around the base to expose the original stonework. Water entered the excavation on the south side, and the evidence suggests that the lean increased by more than a quarter of a degree. In 1934 even injecting grout into the foundation was followed by a sudden movement south.

Old survey records helped explain why. The southern side was sinking, but the northern edge was also rising. Imagine tipping a heavy cupboard: one edge presses harder into the floor while the other starts to lift. A calculation showed that the pressure beneath the tower's southern edge was about twice the average. At the north edge it was close to zero.

If the engineers could lower that raised edge a little, the tower would rotate north and its weight would be distributed more evenly. They wanted to change its angle by just half a degree. A method had already been used to correct uneven settlement at the Metropolitan Cathedral in Mexico City: taking soil away. The challenge was to do this without disturbing the ground carrying the greatest load.

Burland and his colleagues began with models and computer calculations, then built a trial foundation in the square north of the Baptistery. They placed the load off centre to imitate the tower. A screw drill inside a steel tube removed small amounts of earth. An instrument could be left inside the hole to watch the cavity close as the surrounding soil settled.

Then the trial went wrong. The team removed soil beyond the area where the models had predicted a safe response. The lower edge of the foundation began sinking rapidly, before the foundation changed its direction of tilt. It took six weeks to recover stability. The team now knew that the height of the lower edge could warn them of trouble earlier than the angle alone.

The committee agreed to a limited trial beneath the actual tower in April 1996. Administrative delays held up preparation until late 1998. Steel cables were attached to the third storey and led to two frames on the north side of the square. They were kept under light tension, ready to restrain unwanted movement rather than pull the tower upright.

The first extraction took place on February ninth, 1999. Twelve boreholes covered a narrow section of the northern foundation. The drill had to be moved between them, so the crew could manage at most two extractions a day. Initially they removed only about twenty litres of soil each day.

Twice a day measurements were faxed from Pisa to the engineers. The engineer directing extraction reviewed the numbers and sent a signed instruction for the next operation. The team had studied years of records to establish three warning levels. They wanted to detect an unwanted movement without halting the work every time the tower made an ordinary small motion.

For the first few days the instruments showed practically no response. Then the tower started turning north. On February twenty-third it suddenly went south again. In a single day it lost a noticeable part of the small improvement it had made. Was the same thing happening that had gone wrong in the trial?

They checked the lower edge. It wasn't sinking. A northerly wind and a sudden cold spell matched movements recorded in previous winters. As temperatures rose, the tower resumed its northward movement. Burland later recalled the team's relief. They had distinguished a worrying change of angle from the early warning of instability that they had seen in the trial.

By early June the improvement was roughly four times the minimum target set for the trial. The crew stopped removing soil to see what the tower would do. It kept turning north more slowly, and removing three lead weights eventually halted the motion. The southern edge had actually risen a little, showing that the load on that vulnerable side had been reduced.

In February 2000, full extraction began using forty-one boreholes, each with its own drill. The crew could now remove about one hundred and twenty litres a day. When the tower tended slightly east, they adjusted the distribution, taking roughly a fifth more soil from the west side than the east. The team could steer the tower as well as reduce its lean.

In May 2000, the crew began taking away the lead that had surrounded the tower for years. At first they removed two blocks a week, weighing about eighteen tonnes together. By November they were removing four a week. Each time a weight came off, some of the turning force the lead had held back returned. Yet the soil extraction continued to work.

On January sixteenth, 2001, the last lead block came off. The concrete ring was removed next, and in May the precautionary cables were detached. The tower moved south slightly, so the crew removed a little more soil. On June sixth, the last drill was withdrawn.

Work was also needed on the stonework itself. Engineers filled gaps inside the most heavily stressed areas of the masonry and strengthened the outer facing where necessary. The temporary metal bands near the base could be replaced with less intrusive reinforcement. Correcting the foundation's tilt did not mean neglecting the building above it.

The engineers also had to deal with the changing groundwater. The old measurements and new observations showed a connection between heavy rain, rising water levels on the north side and incremental southward rotation. So drainage was installed to limit the fluctuations. It addressed a process that simply correcting the tower's angle would not have dealt with.

On December fifteenth, 2001, visitors could finally climb the tower again. Roughly thirty-eight cubic metres of soil had been removed during the project. At the seventh floor the tower had moved back about forty-four centimetres, a change that a visitor standing in the square would scarcely notice. Its tilt was close to what it had been before the excavation of 1838.

In 2011, Burland returned with his family and showed them the tower he had spent eleven years helping to save. The lead and cables were gone. Monitoring continued; the repair did not promise that the tower would never move again. It meant that visitors could enter a safer building that still looked like the Leaning Tower of Pisa.
