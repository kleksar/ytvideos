How did a doll become so valuable to parents that a department store couldn't keep order? And how could the company making millions of those dolls end up in bankruptcy court?

The story begins with an unusual way of selling a toy. In the 1970s, Xavier Roberts was making cloth figures in Georgia, influenced by the handmade dolls of artist Martha Nelson Thomas. His figures didn't go home with buyers in the usual way. They were adopted. Each had paperwork, including a name and a birth certificate.

Roberts even opened a place where the dolls could be adopted, with staff dressed as medical workers. It was a toy shop that looked like a maternity hospital.

The concept was too successful for a small workshop to keep up with. A licensing deal brought it to the mass market. In 1982, marketer Roger Schlaifer renamed the dolls Cabbage Patch Kids, and a team at Coleco designed a version with a molded plastic head and a soft body. By changing the hair, clothes and other details, factories could turn out large quantities of dolls that still felt personal.

Then came Christmas 1983. At a Zayre store in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, a crowd estimated at a thousand rushed in for two hundred and forty dolls. An assistant manager climbed onto a counter in an attempt to get control of the crowd. The supply was gone within thirty minutes.

Coleco shipped more than three million dolls in its first year. But at the same time that parents were struggling to get their hands on a Cabbage Patch Kid, Coleco had a second product it couldn't sell nearly as successfully.

The company had already entered the home video game market with ColecoVision. In 1983 it launched Adam, a home computer that came as a complete package for about six hundred dollars. The company had planned to make half a million machines that year. It managed less than a fifth of that number. Some buyers returned defective units.

Building a computer business was expensive even when the computers weren't leaving the factory. Coleco had to pay for development, production equipment, advertising and warranty costs. It ended 1983 with a loss.

For a while, the dolls looked like a way out. In September 1984, Coleco was getting about eighty-five cents of every sales dollar from Cabbage Patch products. And at the start of the next quarter, it still had orders worth more than three hundred million dollars waiting to be filled.

Yet Coleco finished 1984 almost eighty million dollars in the red, despite recording five hundred and forty million in sales of the Cabbage Patch line.

At the beginning of 1985, it gave up on Adam. It sold the remaining computer stock and turned its attention back to toys. By autumn it had paid off one hundred and sixty-one million dollars of short-term bank debt over the preceding twelve months. The year ended with a record profit.

That recovery gave Coleco a chance to build a business that didn't depend on a single doll. It took on new toy licenses and bought Selchow and Righter, the company behind games such as Scrabble and Trivial Pursuit in the United States.

But this time the product losing momentum was its biggest success. In 1985, the Cabbage Patch range brought in about six hundred million dollars. A year later, sales had fallen to roughly two hundred and fifty million, according to the company's preliminary figures.

Even at that level the company said Cabbage Patch remained the biggest toy line in North America. It just wasn't enough anymore. A company built to sell enormous quantities of toys had to pay its bills in a much smaller market for its key product. New lines and acquisitions had cost money, and other products didn't fill the gap.

After losing more than a hundred million dollars in 1987, Coleco missed an interest payment. It tried to negotiate with creditors and failed to secure the financing it needed. In July 1988 it filed for bankruptcy protection.

In 1989, Hasbro purchased most of Coleco's remaining non-cash assets for eighty-five million dollars plus stock warrants. It also reached an agreement to manufacture Cabbage Patch Kids. New dolls could still go home with new families.

The toy that parents had fought to buy hadn't disappeared. It was the company on the box that could no longer afford to stay in business.
