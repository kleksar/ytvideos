Why did thousands of parents spend the Christmas season of 1983 searching for a doll that came with a birth certificate? How did the company behind it lose money even as sales climbed into the hundreds of millions? And what happened when the customers finally stopped rushing to toy stores?

The first surprise about Cabbage Patch Kids is how far the original dolls were from an ordinary factory product. In the seventies, an art student in Georgia named Xavier Roberts was working with cloth sculpture. He had encountered the handmade Doll Babies of Kentucky artist Martha Nelson Thomas, who gave her creations names and birth certificates.

Roberts made his own figures, with soft faces and yarn hair, and called them Little People. Instead of just selling them, he offered them for adoption. There was a fee, and the new owner received adoption papers.

In Georgia, the operation grew into BabyLand General Hospital, a place where employees dressed like doctors and nurses. The hospital was part of the make-believe. A child could take home a doll presented as an individual baby rather than one copy of a character on a shelf.

That origin has a dispute of its own. Nelson Thomas sued Roberts, and the case was settled out of court. So it would be misleading to tell the story as if all the ideas behind these dolls appeared from nowhere in one workshop.

The difficulty with the handmade figures was that their success made it harder to supply them. Making another individual cloth doll took work. A national toy business would have to reproduce that sense of individuality without requiring an artist to sculpt every face by hand.

In 1982, marketer Roger Schlaifer acquired licensing rights and developed the Cabbage Patch Kids brand. Coleco's design team, led by Judy Albert, developed the mass-market doll with a molded head. A factory could repeat the same basic processes and change combinations of hair, skin tones, outfits and names. The birth certificate remained. A child didn't have to find a completely handmade original to have a toy that felt like their own.

By the holidays of 1983, the new dolls were an enormous hit. The most vivid proof wasn't on a television commercial. It was happening in the stores.

In Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, an estimated thousand shoppers came for two hundred and forty dolls at a Zayre department store. The crowd pushed inside, and an assistant manager stood on a counter trying to restore order. Within thirty minutes the dolls were gone.

Across the country, shops struggled to meet the demand. Some handed out numbered tickets to control the scramble. For the families who did manage to buy one, the doll's name could become part of the memory of that Christmas.

Years later, a museum staff member named Tara Winner recalled how her father had found a doll at a catalog store in Delaware. It cost fifty dollars, and the store limited him to one. So she and her sister shared a doll named Kendall. She remembered his curly yarn hair and blue outfit decades later.

That helps explain what the factory version had managed to preserve. These dolls could still become particular little characters in particular families, even as the manufacturer was sending millions of them to stores.

Coleco shipped more than three million in 1983. The Cabbage Patch line brought in about sixty-five million dollars. A success like that could change the future of a toy company.

But Coleco had spent the beginning of the decade betting on a very different future.

In 1982, it introduced the ColecoVision home video game console. It was selling cartridges and electronic games. The next step was to turn that console into something a family could also use for work and education.

At a trade show in June 1983, Coleco presented Adam, a home computer. For about six hundred dollars, the family got an entire system rather than having to assemble one from separate purchases. ColecoVision owners could also buy an expansion that converted their console into an Adam.

It was an ambitious offer. Coleco planned to build half a million computers in 1983. But a system that looked attractive on paper still had to be manufactured reliably and delivered in time for the holidays. Production problems cut output to less than one fifth of the plan. Defects led to returns.

For the company, every machine it hadn't managed to sell represented revenue that wasn't arriving. The costs of preparing to make those machines had already been incurred. Development, advertising, manufacturing problems and warranties didn't vanish when production fell behind. Meanwhile, other parts of the electronic game market were slowing down.

Coleco had made a profit of nearly forty-five million dollars in 1982. In 1983, with its overall sales actually higher, it lost more than seven million.

The dolls were no longer simply an unexpected success. They were becoming essential to the whole company.

Coleco didn't immediately abandon the computer. It told shareholders it had fixed early quality problems and extended Adam's warranty. It continued developing equipment and software for the system, hoping those would attract more customers.

And the toy factories worked to meet the demand for Cabbage Patch Kids. The line grew to include smaller babies, pets, clothing and play accessories. By the third quarter of 1984, Cabbage Patch products accounted for about eighty-five percent of Coleco's revenue. Orders worth more than three hundred million dollars remained to be filled at the start of the fourth quarter.

Here is the remarkable part. Coleco recorded five hundred and forty million dollars in sales of those products during 1984, but the company still lost almost eighty million. Half a billion dollars in sales is not half a billion dollars in profit. And a profitable toy business can coexist with another operation that is losing extraordinary amounts of money.

In January 1985, Coleco stopped pursuing Adam and sold off its remaining computer inventory. It began clearing out video game stock as well. This decision finally allowed the company to benefit more fully from the toys.

Its report in October 1985 shows how substantial the turnaround was. Over the preceding twelve months, Coleco had repaid one hundred and sixty-one million dollars of short-term bank debt. Its entire remaining inventory of Adam computers had been sold. Sales of Cabbage Patch products during the first nine months had already passed four hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

By the end of 1985, Coleco had recorded its strongest annual earnings ever: sixty-four million dollars. It wasn't a company doomed the moment the computer failed. It had actually recovered.

The next problem was what to do with that success.

Coleco had begun licensing other characters, including Rambo action figures and another line of soft toys from Roberts, Furskins bears. In 1986, it bought Selchow and Righter, which brought a position in the board-game market. These moves could be understood as attempts to widen the business. They also meant spending more money at a point when Coleco was about to lose much of its main source of revenue.

The Cabbage Patch range had brought in roughly six hundred million dollars in 1985. Preliminary figures for 1986 put it at around two hundred and fifty million. Coleco said the line was still the largest in the North American toy market. But it was selling for less, the company was spending more on promotion and rebates, and its new products weren't earning enough to replace the lost business.

Consider the size of the reversal. A toy company can lead an entire category and still be in trouble if the sales it needs are much higher than the sales it gets. The crowds outside the stores had been a sign to expand production. Now the expenses of running a much bigger operation didn't simply shrink with demand.

Coleco lost more than a hundred million dollars in 1987. In the spring of 1988, it missed a debt interest payment and proposed a deal to restructure hundreds of millions in obligations. The plan needed approval from the holders of that debt. It didn't get enough support.

The company cut jobs and sold product lines. By July, it could no longer arrange the working capital it needed to keep operating as before. It filed for Chapter Eleven protection on July eleventh, 1988.

The story wasn't over for the toys themselves. A year later, Hasbro completed a purchase of most of Coleco's remaining non-cash assets for eighty-five million dollars and the right to buy shares. It negotiated a separate agreement with Original Appalachian Artworks to manufacture and sell Cabbage Patch Kids. The dolls would continue under different manufacturers, long after the original toy-store frenzy.

Think back to the birth certificate. To the child, it was an adoption of a particular little person. To Coleco, it was the product line that rescued the company from one bad bet and then shrank while it was making new ones.

The certificate could stay with the doll for decades. The manufacturer's name on the packaging wasn't nearly as permanent.
