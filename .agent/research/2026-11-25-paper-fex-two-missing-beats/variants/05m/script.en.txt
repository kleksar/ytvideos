How can a song travel all over the internet without anyone knowing who recorded it? And why did the people who finally found the band still have another recording to look for?

In the eighties, a teenager named Darius recorded music off German radio. It was a way of keeping songs that he liked without having to buy every record. One song on his cassette had a deep voice, guitars and a synthesizer. The band's name was a mystery. He had the music, but not the information that would let him find it again.

His sister Lydia started asking for help on music forums in 2007. People could listen to the recording, compare it with their record collections and suggest names. For a while, the question passed from one small corner of the internet to another.

In 2019, it reached a much bigger audience. A Brazilian music fan helped bring the search to Reddit. YouTube videos introduced new listeners to it. People even contacted Paul Baskerville, a presenter on the northern German radio network where Darius had recorded his tape. Baskerville played the song again, hoping someone would recognize it. That still didn't identify the band.

The remarkable thing was that the song itself wasn't missing. Anyone could hear it. The missing piece was the connection between that recording and a person who knew its name.

There were some clues inside the music. A particular synthesizer, a Yamaha DX7, didn't go on sale until 1983. That gave the search a lower limit on the date. And the radio recording had a dip around one high frequency. The same peculiarity turned up on other recordings from the station. Neither clue could tell anyone the singer's name.

In 2024, researchers began looking at documentation from Hörfest, a music competition associated with that radio network. If small local bands sent recordings to the station, a list of those bands was more useful than a catalogue of famous records.

Volunteers divided the work. One of them, Marijn, was investigating a group called Phret. In a regional newspaper archive, he came across an article about FEX, a band from Kiel. One of the names was familiar from his work on Phret.

He contacted Michael Hädrich and asked whether he still had music from those days. Michael sent recordings. One file had the title Subways of Your Mind. The search had led to the people who made the song.

There was a catch. The recording Michael sent was another performance of it. The arrangement differed from the one the community had been circulating. It was enough to identify the song, but not to recover that particular recording.

The discovery was announced on November fourth, 2024. The musicians, who had gone their separate ways decades earlier, got together again and made a new recording in December.

Then Michael and his daughter came across a box of old cassettes while sorting things at home over Christmas. One tape carried the band's name and two of the musicians' addresses.

He compared it with the radio recording, correcting the differences in tape speed. The performances lined up. At the start of Michael's cassette were two drum hits missing from Darius's recording. And the dip at a high frequency that marked the radio copy wasn't present on Michael's tape. His find was an older analogue copy, not a recording downloaded from the internet.

The original performance could now be released without relying on the radio recording. How the music reached the radio station in the first place was still uncertain.

In July 2025, FEX released an album called Skyscraper, assembled from material they had preserved. Its closing track was the version from Darius's own cassette. His artwork was used on an edition of the record.

Decades earlier, he had been trying to put a band's name beside one song on a tape. Now that band had put his tape on their album.
