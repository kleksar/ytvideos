Why did it take seventeen years of internet searches to identify a song that everybody could listen to? How did a newspaper article bring the search to an end? And what could two drum hits tell the musicians themselves about a recording they had lost?

To understand this story, think about the way people collected music before streaming. If a song came on the radio and you liked it, you could put a blank cassette in a recorder and keep it. But the cassette didn't tell you what you were hearing. If you missed the name, all you had was the music.

In the middle of the eighties, a teenager named Darius was doing exactly that in West Germany. Among the music he kept was a song with a low male voice and a distinctive synthesizer sound. He didn't know the name of the group. On his track list, there was a question mark where that name belonged.

He later transferred his tapes to a computer. His sister Lydia began asking other music fans about the unidentified song in 2007. A digital recording could go much farther than a cassette. It could reach a collector in another country, someone who might have bought the record twenty years before. The problem was that none of those early inquiries produced the missing name.

In 2019, a Brazilian listener helped the mystery reach a new audience. It spread through YouTube and Reddit, and a dedicated community began trying to identify the musicians. Lydia eventually joined that community herself. People who had never met the siblings could now work on the same puzzle.

This was a strange sort of missing music. The song could be copied almost endlessly, but there was nothing in those copies that established who owned the original. A person could upload another copy under an invented title, and the invented title could become the thing everybody searched for.

One sensible place to look was the radio station. Darius remembered the northern German network NDR. The researchers contacted Paul Baskerville, a radio presenter associated with the music he listened to. Baskerville couldn't place the song either. He played it on his radio programme in 2019, giving an entirely new set of listeners a chance to identify it. The answer still didn't come.

The community also investigated the sound itself. One of the instruments appeared to be a Yamaha DX7, a synthesizer introduced in 1983. If that identification was correct, recordings made years before its introduction could be excluded. That is a useful clue, even when it can't tell you which of hundreds of bands you are hearing.

Another clue was much less obvious to an ordinary listener. In the frequency spectrum of the recording, there was a dip near ten kilohertz. Other recordings associated with NDR showed a similar feature. It supported the remembered radio station. It didn't point to a particular guitarist, singer or keyboard player.

These were two different kinds of information. One could help narrow the time period; the other could help narrow the place where the song had been heard. Neither supplied the missing label. And a radio station that played music by unsigned bands offered a particularly difficult problem. Some of those bands might have made only a few tapes.

The breakthrough came when the researchers changed the sort of archive they were searching. In 2024, they began investigating Hörfest, an amateur music competition organized by NDR. They obtained material from local archives. Lists of bands and names of musicians became a way of finding people whose work was barely represented online.

The volunteers used shared spreadsheets to organize a large number of leads. Someone could check one group while someone else worked through another. The important result of that work wasn't just a list of possible names. It allowed a name found in one place to be recognized when it appeared somewhere else.

One of the researchers, a Dutch user named Marijn, was looking into the band Phret. He found an old newspaper article about FEX from Kiel. The article described a group that had won a competition in Bremen in September 1984. One of the names was already familiar to him from Phret.

That name led him to Michael Hädrich, a musician who still worked in music. Marijn asked about old recordings from the two groups without initially identifying the particular song he wanted. Michael sent back material from his archives.

One of the tracks was called Subways of Your Mind. After years of going through possible names for the band, the researcher had received a recording directly from one of its members.

FEX had been active in the eighties, when musicians in Germany were experimenting with different kinds of new wave and pop. Their lineup had changed, and its members eventually went their separate ways. In interviews, the musicians explained that they had recorded material for a demo cassette and sold it at their concerts. It wasn't the kind of release that a person could necessarily find in a normal record shop.

Michael also explained why they could have missed the online search. The title by which their music was being discussed wasn't the actual name of their song. None of them had connected that online mystery with their old band.

The researcher waited for the musicians to agree before making the discovery public on November fourth, 2024. The community finally had a title and a group. But anyone who expected a clean copy of the familiar radio recording was in for another surprise. The tape Michael had sent contained a different performance.

A band's song can exist in several recordings. They can share a melody, words and musicians and still contain different solos or endings. Identifying a song doesn't necessarily recover the performance somebody heard on a particular day. The two questions had become separate.

The former band members reunited and made a new recording that December. Meanwhile, another search was happening in a much smaller space than the internet: Michael's own collection of tapes.

Over Christmas, he and his daughter found a box with more than two hundred cassettes. One of them was labelled FEX and carried the addresses of Michael and the singer, Ture Rückwardt. It was a short demo tape, with the whole cassette lasting about ten minutes. Its contents included the old song.

Michael put the recording alongside the one from the radio. Different cassette decks can run at slightly different speeds. A small discrepancy over several minutes can move the notes out of alignment even if two tapes contain the same performance. He corrected the speed differences before making the comparison.

The recordings matched. His tape contained two drum hits at the beginning that Darius's radio recording lacked. They had probably been missed when the radio recording began. The dip around ten kilohertz was absent from Michael's tape, too.

That was important because the band hadn't merely recorded the familiar online file onto a cassette. It was a surviving analogue copy of the performance. The original master tape itself was not what Michael had found.

There was finally material from which the recording that had been broadcast could be released. In early 2025, that version reached streaming platforms and was issued separately from the other demo performance. The label later replaced its digital master with a transfer from another cassette, found by Ture, while the first vinyl edition kept the earlier restoration.

One thing remained unresolved in the reporting: exactly how the tape got to NDR. The station played amateur music, and researchers could find entries for surrounding songs. Michael remembered the instrument and the band, but couldn't establish who had sent the recording to the station. A complete history of the broadcast isn't necessary to know who made the song.

In July 2025, FEX put out an album called Skyscraper. More material from its old demos had been recovered. The people who had once only been able to listen to an anonymous radio recording could now listen to a whole album under the band's own name.

And the last track was taken from the cassette Darius had kept. On an edition of the album, his artwork was used as well.

The teenager who had put a question mark beside a song had inadvertently preserved something the musicians themselves needed to find again. When they finally released their collection, his recording had a place in it.
