Why did it take seventeen years of internet searches to identify a song that everybody could listen to? How did a newspaper article bring the search to an end? And what could two drum hits tell the musicians themselves about a recording they had lost?

In the mid-eighties, a teenager named Darius in West Germany used to tape songs off the radio. He kept a list of the tracks. One of them, with a deep voice and a distinctive synthesizer, had a question mark where the band's name should have been. He could play it whenever he wanted, but he couldn't look it up in a record shop.

He later transferred his tapes to a computer. His sister Lydia began asking other music fans about the unidentified song in 2007. A digital recording could go much farther than a cassette. It could reach a collector in another country, someone who might have bought the record twenty years before. The problem was that none of those early inquiries produced the missing name.

In 2019, a Brazilian listener helped the mystery reach a new audience. It spread through YouTube and Reddit, and a dedicated community began trying to identify the musicians. Lydia eventually joined that community herself. People who had never met the siblings could now work on the same puzzle.

One sensible place to look was the radio station. Darius remembered the northern German network NDR. The researchers contacted Paul Baskerville, a radio presenter associated with the music he listened to. Baskerville couldn't place the song either. He played it on his radio programme in 2019, giving an entirely new set of listeners a chance to identify it. The answer still didn't come.

If even a presenter from the station couldn't recognize the recording, the listeners needed something more than another round of guesses. They started treating the tape itself as evidence. One instrument sounded like a Yamaha DX7, which first went on sale in 1983. If they had identified it correctly, a whole range of earlier records could be ruled out.

They also noticed a narrow dip near ten kilohertz. Other recordings associated with NDR had a similar feature. It supported Darius's recollection of the station. Keep that little dip in mind: years later, its absence would help the musicians recognize what they had found.

The breakthrough came when the researchers changed the sort of archive they were searching. In 2024, they began investigating Hörfest, an amateur music competition organized by NDR. They obtained material from local archives. Lists of bands and names of musicians became a way of finding people whose work was barely represented online.

The volunteers tracked their leads in shared spreadsheets. Instead of asking everyone to recognize the melody, they could follow a particular name through different groups. That was how a Dutch researcher named Marijn got from a band called Phret to a newspaper article about FEX from Kiel. FEX had won a competition in Bremen in September 1984. One of its musicians was a name Marijn had already encountered.

That led him to Michael Hädrich, who was still working in music and had a studio in Munich. Marijn asked for old recordings without telling him which song he was trying to identify. At almost the same time, Michael later recalled, an American researcher contacted another member, singer Ture Rückwardt. The investigation was reaching the actual musicians from two directions.

Michael sent back recordings. One track was called Subways of Your Mind. Marijn recognized the song and told Michael about the years of searching. He waited until the musicians had agreed before announcing the discovery on November fourth, 2024.

FEX had sold demo cassettes at concerts before the members went their separate ways. Michael later explained that the name people used online was not the title he knew. Even though he was still working in music, he had never connected the mystery with FEX. Now the band had an audience that had been listening to their old song for years without knowing who they were.

But there was a problem with the file Marijn had received. It was the right song in another performance. The label later identified that studio version as a November 1984 recording made at Hawkeye Studios near Hamburg. The radio tape that had started everything was still the only familiar copy of a different performance. Finding the band had given the search a new target.

Three days after the announcement, Michael, Ture and bassist Norbert Ziermann performed the song on NDR. There was still someone missing: the drummer. Hans-Reimer Sievers hadn't been in touch with his old bandmates for years. On November eighth he saw FEX mentioned in his local paper and got in contact through the radio station. Within hours, the four musicians were talking again.

His old drums were still stored in the loft at his farm. He had kept photographs and a demo cassette as well. The newspaper search had recovered the band's name; another newspaper had helped reunite its members. In December the four of them recorded the song again in Michael's studio. They could play it now, but a new recording couldn't recover the exact performance Darius had taped decades earlier.

Over Christmas, he and his daughter found a box with more than two hundred cassettes. One of them was labelled FEX and carried the addresses of Michael and the singer, Ture Rückwardt. It was a short demo tape, with the whole cassette lasting about ten minutes. Its contents included the old song.

Michael remembered that he and Ture used to exchange tapes with musical ideas. He played the cassette and compared it with the radio recording. The two versions ran at slightly different speeds, as recordings on different cassette machines often do. He adjusted the speed so he could compare the notes as the song progressed.

The recordings matched. His tape contained two drum hits at the beginning that Darius's radio recording lacked. They had probably been missed when the radio recording began. The dip around ten kilohertz was absent from Michael's tape, too.

The extra beats could be heard before the point at which the familiar radio recording began. The absent dip was another clue that this was not simply a copy of the online file. Michael concluded that he had a surviving analogue copy of the performance. It wasn't the original master itself, but the musicians could finally make a release without depending on Darius's radio tape.

The recovered performance appeared on streaming services in January 2025 and was issued separately from the studio take. Later Ture found another cassette; the label replaced the digital master with a transfer from that tape, while the first vinyl edition kept the earlier restoration. The same old performance could now be heard from more than one surviving source.

There was still a gap in the investigation. In its January report NDR said the band couldn't remember how the recording had reached the station. Researchers had identified entries for songs before and after it in the radio tape, but the mystery song itself was absent from those broadcast records. The recording was identified; its journey to the radio was not yet established.

The recovery went beyond one song. The label assembled ten tracks for FEX's debut album, Skyscraper, released in July 2025. One recovered rehearsal tape even had missing keyboard parts, which Michael reconstructed using his old DX7. The label also made a replica of the yellow-label demo cassette containing six tracks. Recordings that once circulated on a small scale could finally be bought under the band's own name.

The album contains the studio performance that led Marijn to the band. For its closing track, the label went back to the recording from Darius's own cassette. They transferred and restored it for release; Darius also contributed artwork to the record.

The boy who had put a question mark beside a song had preserved a piece of the band's history. Forty years later, the band put his recording alongside their own, on an album bearing their name.
