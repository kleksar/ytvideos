Why would someone remove the most popular free game on the phone in your pocket? Why did people start trying to sell used phones because of it? And how did a new game come back under its name without the man who made the original?

In May 2013, a Vietnamese developer named Dong Nguyen put a little free game on Apple's App Store. It barely attracted attention at first. The whole idea fit on a screen. A bird dropped toward the ground. Tap, and it jumped upward. The pipes kept coming, with a narrow gap between each pair. Touch one and you started again.

You didn't need to buy equipment or learn a complicated set of moves. One more attempt was always just a tap away. A score could also be compared with your friends' scores. Nguyen suggested that this competition might explain the game's popularity.

By early February 2014, Flappy Bird was at the top of the free charts on Apple and Google's stores. A report based on an interview with Nguyen put downloads at fifty million and the advertising income at an average of fifty thousand dollars a day. When Forbes later asked him about the money, he wouldn't confirm the exact figure.

At this point Nguyen could simply have left the game available. Instead, on February eighth he announced that he would remove it in twenty-two hours. He also said he wasn't selling it.

The following day, the two stores stopped offering the download.

Why? Some people pointed to the game's resemblance to Super Mario. Nguyen denied that Nintendo had threatened him or caused the withdrawal. His own explanation was about the game he had built and the life he was leading. He had intended it as a brief diversion. He believed people were spending far too much time with it. He also said that he had been unable to sleep and that the decision had brought relief.

But he hadn't erased the copies on people's phones.

That produced a bizarre little market. A used iPhone could have exactly the same hardware as another iPhone, yet the owner could advertise it as something special because a free game was still installed. Some of the listings drew extravagant bids. Those weren't proof that anybody had actually paid that much.

There was another catch. eBay required sellers to reset smartphones before selling them. That would delete the game. The platform began removing the listings.

And people didn't even have to buy an old phone to play something similar. A game that put a flying superhero between obstacles reached the top of Apple's free chart just after Flappy Bird left. By the beginning of March, a gaming publication counted around sixty new Flappy-style games a day across a four-day sample.

Nguyen himself did make a different version later that year. Flappy Birds Family came out on Amazon's Fire TV with a two-player race and a finish line. He had talked about making the game less addictive. But that wasn't the return of the original phone app.

Something else was happening in the background. The name Flappy Bird was valuable too. Days after the 2014 withdrawal, another business applied to register it in the United States. It obtained a registration in 2018.

In September 2023, Gametech Holdings opposed Nguyen's own application. The record shows a default and then a decision in January 2024 sustaining the opposition. His application ended without registration. That proceeding concerned the trademark; it doesn't establish that the original game's program or artwork was handed over.

In September 2024, a group announced a revival under the Flappy Bird name. Nguyen posted that he had no connection to their game and had sold nothing. He also said he did not support cryptocurrency.

In April 2025, a new Flappy Bird became available on Android through the Epic Games Store. This time it had advertising and purchases that could help the bird keep going after a crash. Its team said this Android release had no Web3 features.

Imagine two phones on a table. One has the little game that its creator stopped offering in 2014. The other has a product released more than a decade later by a different team.

Both can carry the same familiar name. Only one is the game Nguyen made. He could take his own download away, but that didn't make the name disappear with it.
