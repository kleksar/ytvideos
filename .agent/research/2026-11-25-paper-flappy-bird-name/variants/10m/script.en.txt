Why did a developer remove a free game at the peak of its success? How did a copy of that game become the selling point of a used phone? And how could someone put a new Flappy Bird on sale without buying the original game from its creator?

Let's begin with a phone from 2013. On its screen is a small bird, a blue sky and a row of pipes. There are no complicated menus to learn before you start. You tap to make the bird rise. Between taps it falls. To get a point, you have to carry it through the next gap without touching the pipes. Miss, and you are back at the beginning.

Dong Nguyen, a developer in Vietnam, said it took him two or three days to write the game. He released it for free in May 2013. At first, hardly anyone noticed. By February 2014, it was at the top of the free charts in both major phone stores. An interview put downloads at fifty million and average advertising revenue at fifty thousand dollars a day. Nguyen later declined to confirm the amount.

On February eighth, Nguyen announced that he was taking Flappy Bird down in twenty-two hours. He also refused to sell it. Instead of making the most of a game that millions of people wanted, he was about to take away their easiest way to get it. The announcement itself gave anyone who hadn't installed the game a reason to hurry.

Before all this, the game had been easy to fit into an ordinary day. A player didn't have to remember where a story had ended or learn a new set of controls. Every collision brought the bird back to the start. Nguyen thought that the ability to compete with friends over a tiny number on the screen helped explain why people kept trying.

The same short game was changing life outside Nguyen's phone. In Hanoi, his parents learned about his success from the news. Photographers gathered at their house. A few days after the withdrawal he agreed to an interview on the condition that his face would not be published. He had become a public figure through an app he had made in a few days.

There was also a more personal problem. Nguyen described messages from people who blamed the game for trouble in their own lives. He said he had played Counter-Strike so much as a student that it interfered with school, so he took those messages seriously. That is his account of why the complaints mattered to him; it doesn't establish that the game caused every problem described in them.

Some people suspected Nintendo had forced him to remove the game because of its pipes. Nguyen denied that. He said the game had been intended for short breaks, but he believed people were spending too much time with it. He spoke of losing sleep over the situation. On Sunday, February ninth, the download really disappeared from both stores.

A person who had already downloaded Flappy Bird could still open it. The phone did not need to fetch the entire game from the store every time the player tapped its icon. But a person who had never installed it could no longer get it the usual way. That difference created a peculiar opportunity for someone with a secondhand phone.

A London seller, Benio Urbanowicz, put his iPhone 4S with the game installed on eBay. The auction started at eight hundred pounds. Bids reached two thousand before the listing was removed. We know what people offered on the page, not what anyone ultimately paid. What made the listing unusual wasn't a change to the phone's hardware. It was a little app that had cost nothing to download.

The platform had a problem with the offer. Its rules required a factory reset before smartphones and tablets were sold. In a message to another seller, eBay specifically told him to remove the game before trying to list the device again. Follow that instruction and the distinguishing feature of the advertisement would be gone. Ignore it and the listing could disappear instead.

There was a second consequence of the copies staying on phones. The original game had shown ads, and removing the download did not instantly end that business. In March, Rolling Stone reported that the installed game was still generating substantial income for Nguyen. He had quit his job and was working on more games. The precise amount isn't known from public accounts. He had stopped distributing the app, but he had not suddenly lost every source of income from it.

People looking for a similar experience had plenty of alternatives anyway. Just after the removal, a game called Ironpants reached the top of Apple's free chart. It swapped the bird for a superhero and the pipes for different obstacles, but the basic challenge was recognizable.

The scale of that response was striking even in a short sample: a gaming publication counted about sixty new obstacle games a day over four days at the end of February and beginning of March. A fish or a superhero could replace the bird without changing the challenge much. This was a count of similar games, not a finding that each developer had copied the original program.

In his March interview, Nguyen did not rule out bringing Flappy Bird back. If he did, he said, he wanted to include a reminder for players to stop and take a break. That was a different possibility from someone simply putting a similar bird between a new set of obstacles. The person who had made the original was thinking about how he could change the experience himself.

In August 2014 he did release Flappy Birds Family on Amazon Fire TV. This time two people could race each other, and the course had a finish line. He had said he wanted a less addictive game. We don't know whether the new rules made people spend less time playing. But the game itself was an actual release by Nguyen, and it showed that removing Flappy Bird hadn't meant abandoning the character or leaving game development.

There was another decision being made about the bird in 2014, this time on paper. Just days after the removal, a business called Mobile Media Partners filed an application for the US trademark Flappy Bird. Its paperwork referred to a website called FlappyBirdReturns.com. While players were trying to get a copy of the missing game, someone else was pursuing a registration for its name.

In 2018 the company received the registration for game software. In September 2023 a company called Gametech Holdings used that registration in a formal opposition to Nguyen's own application. It was an extraordinary situation for anyone who remembered downloading his game: a different business was now objecting to his attempt to register the very name he had made famous.

Nguyen did not file an answer in that proceeding by the deadline. In January 2024 the opposition succeeded and his application ended without registration. The record doesn't tell us why he failed to answer. It concerns his application for a US trademark, not a ruling that someone bought his program or acquired all rights to his original art and sounds.

In September 2024 a new team calling itself the Flappy Bird Foundation announced a revival. It said it had acquired rights from Gametech Holdings. New characters and new ways to play were part of the announcement. After the Fire TV game in 2014, it might have been reasonable for a player to wonder whether Nguyen was once again trying something different with his bird.

Nguyen answered that question himself. He said publicly that he was not involved with the new team's game and had sold nothing. He also said he did not support cryptocurrency. Early online material for the new project had suggested cryptocurrency features, but the announcement did not establish what every version of the eventual game would contain.

The Android release arrived in April 2025 through the Epic Games Store. Its team said this particular release used advertising and in-app purchases rather than Web3. One of the things players could buy was a helmet to keep going after hitting a pipe. That statement wasn't a guarantee about every other version of the project.

Picture three screens. On the first is the original phone game, where hitting a pipe sends you back to the start. On the second is the game Nguyen later made for Fire TV, where two players could race to a finish. On the third is the 2025 release, where a purchase could help the bird continue after a crash. The second screen actually represents a new decision made by the original creator. The third does not.

When Nguyen removed Flappy Bird, the people who had it could keep playing. He could make other games himself, and other developers could make games with similar rules. But the name that identified his original game became the subject of a separate registration and dispute. That is how a new product with a familiar name could appear even though the person who had created the original had not agreed to bring it back.

In 2014, the thing a seller wanted to preserve was a copy of Nguyen's game on a phone. In 2025, the thing the new team could put on a new phone was a different game under the same name. The bird was back in a store. Its original creator was still out of this particular release.
