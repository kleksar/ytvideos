How could a free game make a used phone seem more valuable than a new one? Why would the developer of that game turn away from a reported fifty thousand dollars a day? And when the game returned more than a decade later, what exactly had returned?

Let's begin with a phone from 2013. On its screen is a small bird, a blue sky and a row of pipes. There are no complicated menus to learn before you start. You tap to make the bird rise. Between taps it falls. To get a point, you have to carry it through the next gap without touching the pipes. Miss, and you are back at the beginning.

The man who put that game together was Dong Nguyen, a Vietnamese developer working under the name .GEARS. By his own account, the game took him two or three days to code. He released it for free on Apple's App Store on May twenty-fourth, 2013.

It wasn't an immediate sensation. Months later, though, the game climbed to the top of the charts on both Apple and Google's app stores. On February fifth, 2014, an interview with Nguyen reported fifty million downloads. In Apple's store alone, the game had accumulated more than forty-seven thousand reviews.

The important number for a player wasn't fifty million. It was the little number that appeared on the screen at the end of a run. Nguyen thought people could compete because the rules were so easy to understand and the game was so difficult to master. Players could compare results through the services built into their phones and share their scores.

For the developer, all those attempts mattered for another reason. Flappy Bird was free, but it contained advertisements. The Verge reported that its advertising revenue was averaging fifty thousand dollars per day. That figure came from its interview, not from published accounts. Nguyen would later say he knew the revenue was substantial without confirming the amount. We can't turn that estimate into a bill for how much money he lost over the next ten years.

At the time, the usual next step for a popular app would have been to keep making it available. The remarkable thing is what Nguyen chose to do instead.

On Saturday, February eighth, he posted a message giving players twenty-two hours before he removed the game. When people asked about selling it, he said he wouldn't. He also made clear that his decision was not about a legal dispute.

By Sunday, the original download was gone from the two major stores. On Apple's store, a direct link could still bring you to a page, but you could no longer obtain the app there. A game that hadn't been popular a few months before now had people searching for it even as its creator was trying to step away.

Two days after the removal, Nguyen met a Forbes journalist in Hanoi. One of the conditions for the interview was that his face wouldn't be revealed. According to Nguyen, his parents had only learned about the game and his role in it through the surge of media coverage.

The game also drew comparisons with Nintendo's Super Mario games because of the pipes. Nguyen said their resemblance was coincidental. He denied receiving a legal threat from Nintendo or taking his game down because of one. There was no need to invent a secret lawsuit to explain the decision.

His explanation was more personal. He'd imagined a game that filled a few spare minutes, but had come to believe that players were using it compulsively. He spoke about guilt and not being able to sleep. After taking it down, he said he had been resting without the internet. Those were his reasons as he described them; they don't constitute a medical finding about the people who played his game.

He hadn't announced that he was giving up game development. Some of his other games were also doing well, and he said he planned to leave them available. He was drawing a line around this particular game.

But what did taking it off the app stores actually mean?

It didn't mean a message appeared on every phone ordering the bird to stop flying. If you had downloaded the original before the withdrawal, you could still launch it. If you hadn't, the obvious way of getting the game no longer worked.

That's why people began putting their iPhones up for auction with Flappy Bird already installed. For a while, a copy of free software could be presented as a selling point for an expensive piece of hardware. Some listings showed huge bids, which is not the same thing as an actual sale.

There was a practical flaw in the offer. The normal procedure on eBay was to wipe smartphones and tablets before selling them. MacRumors obtained a message from the platform to a seller whose listing had been removed. If you followed the rule, the game would be erased. If you didn't, the listing could be taken down. The device itself wasn't rare.

People looking for a similar experience had plenty of alternatives anyway. Just after the removal, a game called Ironpants reached the top of Apple's free chart. It swapped the bird for a superhero and the pipes for different obstacles, but the basic challenge was recognizable.

In early March, Pocket Gamer counted games using a similar obstacle-course mechanic. On February twenty-eighth, it found about sixty-nine new ones. The next three days brought roughly forty-eight, seventy-five and forty-nine. That's about sixty a day in that particular sample. It wasn't a count of every clone everywhere, or a test of whether all those games had copied Nguyen's code.

Some didn't even use birds. Developers could put a fish, a different animal or a person in the same kind of obstacle course. Taking the original off the shelf hadn't taken the idea out of circulation.

Nguyen had another opportunity to approach the problem himself. In August 2014, he released Flappy Birds Family for Amazon Fire TV. It offered a race between two players with a fixed finish and additional enemies. He had said he wanted a less addictive game. Whether the changes actually achieved that goal is unknown. But the release shows he hadn't abandoned the character or the idea completely.

The original's title, meanwhile, had a life of its own.

A business called Mobile Media Partners had filed an application to register the name in the United States just days after the February 2014 removal. It received a registration in 2018. Years later, in September 2023, Gametech Holdings filed a formal opposition to Nguyen's own US application.

An application isn't the same thing as a registration. The public record identifies the mark Nguyen sought as one word, FLAPPYBIRD, and the registration cited by Gametech as two words, FLAPPY BIRD. The record set an answer deadline in November. A notice of default followed. In January 2024 the opposition was sustained, and Nguyen's application was recorded as abandoned after that proceeding.

That is a narrow finding about a US trademark application. It doesn't say why Nguyen didn't respond. It doesn't say the original source code, sounds or artwork were purchased. Losing an application for a name and transferring the underlying creative work are different events.

Then, in September 2024, a group announced a new Flappy Bird with new characters and ways to play. To someone who remembered the name from the phone in their pocket ten years earlier, it might have looked like Nguyen had changed his mind again.

He said publicly that he had no involvement with their game, had sold nothing, and didn't support crypto. Early material about the new project had suggested possible connections with crypto features.

The Android release arrived in April 2025 through the Epic Games Store. Its team said this particular release used advertising and in-app purchases rather than Web3. One of the things players could buy was a helmet to keep going after hitting a pipe. That statement wasn't a guarantee about every other version of the project.

Look again at that number on the old phone after the bird hits a pipe. In Nguyen's original game, the crash means starting again. A decade later, a game with the same name could offer a paid way to continue. The new team's decisions are not decisions by Nguyen.

That's why the two games can share a name without sharing a history. The developer removed the product he could distribute. People kept the copies they already had; others made alternatives; a different company secured a registration for the name. The release that arrived in 2025 was not evidence that Nguyen had decided to put his old game back on your phone.

The bird had found its way into a new store. The man who made it famous wasn't the one who put it there.
