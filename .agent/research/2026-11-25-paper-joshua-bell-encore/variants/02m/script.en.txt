One of the world's finest violinists earned just thirty-two dollars and seventeen cents from strangers who didn't recognize him. Seven years later, hundreds of people came to a railway station to hear him play.

What changed?

In January 2007, Joshua Bell had just performed to a sold-out audience in Boston. A good seat there could cost a hundred dollars.

Then a newspaper asked him to try something very different.

Bell put on a baseball cap, took his violin to a passageway by L'Enfant Plaza station in Washington, and opened his case for tips. There was no concert announcement.

Over forty-three minutes, 1,097 people went past. Only seven stopped to listen.

But look at two of those seven. One man, John Mortensen, had a few minutes to spare before work. He used them to listen. Another, John Picarello, had studied the violin himself. He stayed for nine minutes.

A woman who had seen Bell at another concert recognized him near the end. She gave him twenty dollars. That donation wasn't included in the thirty-two dollars.

The story spread online. People kept telling Bell they wished they'd been there.

So in 2014, he returned to Washington's public transport system. This time, Union Station had an announced concert, with young musicians Bell had mentored. Hundreds gathered. Some arrived two hours early.

The two performances weren't a controlled experiment. The hour, the audience and the setting had all changed. But one thing is clear. In 2014, people could arrange their day around hearing Bell. In 2007, they had to fit him into a day that was already underway.

And how much did that unannounced performance earn from people who didn't recognize him?
