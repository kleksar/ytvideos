How could a violinist who filled concert halls stand almost alone in a railway passage? And why did people later turn up two hours early to hear him in another station in the very same city?

To understand the difference, we need to follow the listeners as well as the musician.

In January 2007, Joshua Bell performed in Boston to a sold-out audience. A good seat could cost a hundred dollars. At a concert like that, people have made their decision to listen before Bell even walks onstage.

Three days later, he was in Washington, about to perform for an audience that hadn't made any such decision.

A Washington Post journalist, Gene Weingarten, had asked Bell to play anonymously near a metro station. The paper even wondered whether a crowd might form and obstruct the passage. Conductor Leonard Slatkin, asked to predict the outcome, expected dozens of listeners and considerably more money than Bell would actually receive.

On January twelfth, Bell stood in the covered pedestrian arcade outside L'Enfant Plaza station. He wore a cap and ordinary clothes. His open violin case was the only obvious invitation to stay. He had brought his own valuable violin, not an instrument chosen to make his performance sound deliberately ordinary.

The music began at seven fifty-one in the morning. The commuters continued towards work.

The paper counted 1,097 passersby over forty-three minutes. Seven stopped to listen. That sounds like the entire story right there. But the people who stopped are the reason it deserves a closer look.

John David Mortensen was on his way to work. He had enough time to spare a few minutes, and he did. He wasn't an expert on classical music. He listened, left a tip and still made it to work on time.

John Picarello had a different reason to linger. He had once seriously studied the violin. He stayed nine minutes. He appreciated the performance without knowing that he was listening to one of the country's leading soloists.

And near the end, Stacy Furukawa recognized Bell. She had seen him play at a free concert at the Library of Congress a few weeks before. She stopped and gave him a twenty-dollar bill.

The money from people who hadn't recognized the musician came to thirty-two dollars and seventeen cents. The newspaper deliberately left her twenty dollars out of that figure.

In 2008, Weingarten's article won a Pulitzer Prize. The event became famous far beyond the people who had actually passed through that arcade. An anonymous online retelling spread internationally, flattening the individual responses into a story about people who failed to notice beauty. Weingarten would later point out that even some basic details of that retelling were wrong.

Meanwhile, Bell kept encountering people who told him they wished they had been there.

On September thirtieth, 2014, he gave them another chance.

The venue was Union Station, a different transport hub in Washington. His appearance was announced in advance. People gathered in the main hall, some two hours before the concert. Bell performed with young musicians he had mentored. The programme also promoted music education.

Now there were hundreds of listeners, and the music was amplified. It still wasn't a perfect concert hall. There was noise from the station and not everybody had a good place to hear or see the performance.

But the public response was unmistakably different.

It would be tempting to say the first crowd failed and the second crowd passed. The trouble is, they weren't the same test. At Union Station, listeners could choose to come, plan their schedules and find out what was being performed. The earlier audience was selected by the accident of a morning commute.

And the three listeners from 2007 give us a more useful way to understand the difference. One had time. One had relevant musical experience. One recognized a performer she'd heard before.

Each had a different reason to interrupt an ordinary journey.

The second concert doesn't prove which of those factors mattered most. It shows something simpler. Bell's music could reach a large audience inside a busy transport building when people had an opportunity to make listening their purpose.

In 2007, a man had three minutes before work and chose to spend them with a stranger playing the violin. In 2014, others set aside hours to hear that same musician. That's the part of the story that gets lost when we count only the people who walked away.
