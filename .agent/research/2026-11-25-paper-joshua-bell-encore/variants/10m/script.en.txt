If one of the world's great violinists played without introducing himself, would you recognize the quality of the music? Would you stop if you did? And what would happen if the violinist came back to the same city and gave people a chance to plan for his performance?

In 2007, Joshua Bell put those first two questions in front of Washington commuters. Seven years later, he was back. The familiar story about his first appearance has a much less familiar second act. And the people who actually stopped to listen help explain why the two audiences behaved so differently.

A few days before the first experiment, Bell had played a sold-out concert in Boston. A good ticket could cost a hundred dollars. Think about everything that happens before the first note at a concert like that. Somebody finds out who is performing, buys a ticket and puts the event in a calendar. They travel to a place designed for music. Even if they have never heard that particular piece before, they know why they are there.

Then came an invitation that stripped almost all of that away.

Gene Weingarten, a journalist at The Washington Post, wanted to see what would happen if someone who normally enjoyed the attention of a concert audience became an anonymous performer on a busy weekday morning. Bell agreed.

Beforehand, the prospect of success seemed quite real. The newspaper considered whether a gathering crowd might interfere with pedestrian traffic. Leonard Slatkin, a prominent conductor, was asked what he thought would happen. His prediction was that dozens of people would stop and the performer would collect a respectable sum of money.

On January twelfth, 2007, Bell brought his violin to L'Enfant Plaza in Washington. The spot was a covered pedestrian arcade outside the metro station, not a stage and not the inside of a moving train. He stood in ordinary clothes and a baseball cap. The open case in front of him was there to receive tips.

At seven fifty-one, he began.

There were no tickets to inspect, no announced programme and no introduction that would tell anyone they were looking at Joshua Bell.

The result was almost the reverse of the organisers' expectation. In forty-three minutes, 1,097 people passed the spot. Only seven stopped to listen. The crowd that the paper had considered planning for never materialized.

This is where many versions of the story end. But if the question is why people didn't listen, then the exceptions matter just as much as the overall number.

One of them was John David Mortensen. He was heading towards his workplace and discovered that he had a little time in hand. He used about three minutes of it to listen. He wasn't someone who could necessarily identify the composition or the musician. He gave Bell a tip and went on to work.

That small detail changes the way we can look at the whole corridor. Mortensen did not need to become a concertgoer for the morning. He did not need a ticket or knowledge of the performer's name. He had just enough freedom in his schedule for the music to become an activity, rather than another sound on his route.

Another listener, John Picarello, had a more personal connection to the instrument. He had once pursued the violin seriously, then chosen another career. He was working for the postal service by 2007. When he heard Bell, he stopped for nine minutes and left five dollars.

Picarello did not have to recognize a celebrity to recognize an exceptional musician. His own experience gave him a way to make sense of what he was hearing.

Then there was Janice Olu. She too had played the violin as a child. She was on a break from work and stayed for a while, although she eventually had to return.

A fourth listener brings yet another kind of information into the story.

Stacy Furukawa had been to a free Bell concert at the Library of Congress a few weeks earlier. When she saw the musician in the arcade, she knew who he was. This was near the end of the performance. She watched until it finished, spoke to him and left twenty dollars.

The newspaper treated that money separately. Its reported total for donations unconnected to recognition was thirty-two dollars and seventeen cents. Adding Furukawa's twenty would produce a different figure, because one contribution came with information the other donors did not have.

The numbers tell us what happened at one place during one interval. The individual accounts tell us that the people in the same passage were not all experiencing the same situation. They had different schedules, memories and musical backgrounds.

The article appeared in April 2007 and won the Pulitzer Prize for feature writing the following year. But a second version of the story began circulating too. An anonymous summary travelled across the internet. It turned a complex set of observations into a much simpler message about modern life. Years later, the journalist wrote a correction because the retelling had changed the number of listeners, described the location inaccurately and left out an actual recognition.

For Bell himself, the incident began to follow him around. People would hear about the underground performance and tell him they wished they could have been there. He had a career that included hundreds of engagements, yet this unusual morning was becoming one of the things strangers remembered about him.

In September 2014, he was scheduled to play in Washington's Union Station.

This was not another surprise performance. The date and venue were public. The free concert would feature young musicians Bell had mentored, and it was also an opportunity to promote music education. Instead of trying to catch commuters with an unexpected performance, the organizers were inviting listeners to come.

On September thirtieth, people started gathering well before the concert. Some waited for two hours. Hundreds filled the station's main hall.

Bell and the young musicians performed Bach and Mendelssohn. There was an amplified sound system. Children sat close to the musicians. Parents had brought them specifically to hear the concert. Other listeners had made use of their lunch breaks.

There were also real disadvantages. The hall was a railway station, after all. Sound bounced around the room. People at the back didn't necessarily have a good view. Some had difficulty hearing. It was not a complete recreation of the conditions of a concert hall.

Yet hundreds of people had made listening the reason for being there.

If we wanted to isolate the effect of Bell's name, we would need a different comparison. We might arrange two otherwise comparable performances, with similar audiences, times and acoustic conditions, and vary whether the performer was identified. Even then, we'd need to decide whether our outcome was recognition, money, time spent listening or something else.

That didn't happen here. We cannot assign the difference between seven and hundreds to publicity alone. The time of day, venue, audience and format had all changed. Nor can we infer that somebody who walked past in 2007 was incapable of appreciating music. They might have been perfectly willing to attend the concert in 2014.

What we do have is a remarkably concrete demonstration of the difference between encountering a performance and choosing to attend one.

Remember Mortensen, the commuter with three minutes to spare. He found a way to listen within the day he already had. Seven years later, people at Union Station created time in their day specifically for the music.

The first morning produced a famous story about the people who didn't stop. The second produced a crowd of people who had chosen to be there. Put the two together, and the most interesting part isn't a verdict on either audience. It's the opportunity to listen, something that the concertgoers in Boston had already arranged before Joshua Bell played a single note.
