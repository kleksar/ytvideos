The company behind Monopoly tried to stop an economics professor from selling a board game called Anti-Monopoly. To save his game, he started looking for the origins of theirs.

In 1904, a woman named Elizabeth Magie patented The Landlord's Game. You bought property, collected rent and went around a board. But she wanted players to see what happened when landowners gained an advantage over everybody else. She even patented an updated version twenty years later.

People made their own boards and taught their friends to play. In Atlantic City, they put local street names on the squares. One of those homemade versions reached Charles Darrow.

Darrow started selling sets and sold his game to Parker Brothers in 1935. The company also bought Magie's patent for five hundred dollars. She publicly challenged the story that Darrow had invented the game.

Decades later, professor Ralph Anspach launched Anti-Monopoly. After the trademark dispute began, he tracked down people who had played the older game before Darrow. Their testimony reached federal court.

The company won at first. It won again after an appeal. But in 1982, an appeals court ruled that the name Monopoly had become a generic name for the game. The Supreme Court declined to revisit the case.

Congress changed the trademark test in 1984. The Monopoly brand survived. So did Anti-Monopoly, under a later settlement.

And the court record established something the original sales story had left out: Darrow had been taught the game before he sold it. Magie's patents were there to see.

That history might have stayed a footnote, if not for what happened in 1973.
