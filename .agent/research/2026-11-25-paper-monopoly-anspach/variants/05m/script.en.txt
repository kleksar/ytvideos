Why did a board game about breaking up monopolies end up in a fight over its own name? And what could a patent from 1904 tell a court about a game sold in the 1970s?

Imagine a board before the familiar little metal pieces and the red hotels. Elizabeth Magie drew up a game in which players traveled around a track of properties. They could own land, take in rent and have to pay when they landed on somebody else's square. Some spaces represented the things people needed to live.

In January 1904, Magie received a patent for her invention, The Landlord's Game. Two decades later she received another. That second patent explained what she was trying to teach: the financial advantage enjoyed by owners of land, and the alternative she favored, a tax on land values.

The game did not need a factory to travel. Players could reproduce a board and teach a new group. Copies circulated among students, and variations took on different names. Some players in Indianapolis marketed a related game as Finance. In Atlantic City, the names on the board became names of real local streets.

Then one of those players introduced Charles Darrow to the game. Darrow began producing sets for sale, and in 1935 made a deal with Parker Brothers. It was the Depression. The story of a man who had made a fortune with a homemade game was almost as marketable as the game itself.

There was a problem with that story. Parker Brothers bought rights to Magie's invention too, for five hundred dollars. In 1936, she showed her own board to reporters and disputed the story of Darrow's invention. The company continued promoting Monopoly under Darrow's name. Magie died in 1948.

Now move to 1973. Ralph Anspach, an economics professor, designed a game that reversed the usual goal. Instead of building monopolies, players worked to dismantle them. He sold it as Anti-Monopoly.

The company that then owned Parker Brothers said the title infringed its trademark. Anspach challenged the trademark in court. And a dispute over two words turned into an investigation of what people had been playing forty years earlier.

Anspach found former players. Some described copying their boards from earlier handmade boards. Others remembered the name Monopoly being used before Darrow began selling it. The case even produced testimony from a former Parker Brothers president who had doubted Darrow's claim to have invented the game.

The first court still ruled for the established company. The appeals court sent the case back, and the lower court ruled for the company again.

The second appeal brought the focus to the people buying the game. In one telephone survey introduced in the case, eligible buyers were asked whether they wanted Monopoly mainly to play the game, regardless of the manufacturer, or mainly because they liked the manufacturer's products. Sixty-five percent chose the first explanation. Thirty-two percent chose the second.

In August 1982, the appeals court held that Monopoly had become a generic name for that game. That did not mean the judges thought the trademark had been invalid from the very beginning. It was the meaning of the name after decades of sales that decided the case.

The Supreme Court refused to review the decision in 1983. But there was one more turn. In 1984 Congress amended trademark law, directing courts to focus on what a name primarily means to the relevant public, rather than on why people decide to buy the product.

Monopoly remained a protected brand. Anti-Monopoly survived under a subsequent agreement.

Anspach had not set out to recover Magie's reputation when he designed his game. Yet his fight put the earlier players, the copying of boards and the two patents on the judicial record. The famous commercial game was not the creation of a single evening by one inventor. Its history had passed through dozens of hands.

There are two things the trademark dispute never settled into one answer: who helped create a game, and who owns the brand on the box. Those are different questions. And Elizabeth Magie's name was on the evidence for the first one, decades before the second one went to court.
