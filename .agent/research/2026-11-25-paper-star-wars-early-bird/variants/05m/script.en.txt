What do you sell when everyone wants your toy and the toy isn't finished? And how did the thing Kenner put on a shop counter to solve that problem end up selling for more than a set of the actual toys?

It's May 1977. Star Wars arrives in cinemas. Kenner has the rights to make toys based on the film. It can already turn pictures of spaceships and characters into things like puzzles, games and posters. But the products people want to hold in their hands are miniature versions of the characters.

Those are more difficult to make. A designer has to turn the image of an actor or a costume into a little solid object. Then the company needs molds, plastic parts, paint, packaging and time to get everything into stores. Kenner is working on twelve characters. The calendar doesn't give the company enough time to get the figures on shelves for Christmas.

So instead of waiting until 1978 to sell the characters, Kenner starts selling a package in 1977 that doesn't contain any of them.

It's called the Early Bird Certificate Package. It looks like something you could give to a Star Wars fan. Open it and you find stickers, a club membership card and a cardboard display. It shows a whole cast of characters, ready for a collection that doesn't exist in stores yet.

There's also a certificate. Fill it out and send it in, and Kenner will send four figures when they're available. The package is essentially a gift you open twice: once at Christmas, and again when a parcel arrives.

For a toy shop, that is an awkward proposition. A parent can normally look at the object, pay for it and take it home. Here the store is taking money for something it cannot hand over. Some shops decide they won't carry it. They don't want to deal with angry customers if Kenner fails to deliver.

Kenner has tested the idea with potential customers, though. And the demand turns out to be real. By December, newspaper reports describe shops in New York and Chicago running out of packages. Kenner's John Beck reports that certificates are already coming back before Christmas. Some customers are adults buying for themselves. The packaging has even imposed an end-of-year deadline on retail sales.

Then the second part of the gift has to happen. In 1978, the company begins sending out white cardboard boxes. Inside, a plastic tray holds four small figures: Luke Skywalker, Princess Leia, Chewbacca and R2-D2. Plastic pegs let the owner put them on the display from the Christmas package.

The remaining eight characters follow. The National Museum of Play estimates that Kenner sold about forty million Star Wars toys by the end of 1978. That figure is for the toy line, not the number of certificates.

The company has made a product that people can play with. But to a collector decades later, each object tells a different part of the story.

In July 2025, Hake's auctions three remarkably well-preserved examples. One is the retail envelope, with the certificate still inside. It sells for eighteen thousand, five hundred and sixty-one dollars.

Another is the mail-away kit: the white box, the tray and the four figures, still sealed in their bags, along with other original contents. It brings forty-five thousand, four hundred and thirty dollars.

The third is something different. It's a cardboard shop fixture made to hold the envelopes in front of customers. Photos of the toys appear on the header, and an example of the certificate package sits in its intended place. The sale price is one hundred and twelve thousand, one hundred dollars, including the buyer's premium.

These are three specific auction lots, not prices for every old Star Wars toy. The store fixture is not the little stand a child would have found at Christmas. But that distinction is exactly what makes the result interesting.

In 1977 the shop display was a means to an end. In 2025, at that auction, it was the main attraction. The material that helped sell toys before they existed on store shelves had become a collectible with a story of its own.
