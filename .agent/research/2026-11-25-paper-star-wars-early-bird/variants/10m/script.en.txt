If a child wanted a Star Wars action figure for Christmas in 1977, what could a parent actually buy? How did the manufacturer get people to pay months before the figures arrived? And why did an object designed to advertise those toys eventually sell for more than a set of the toys themselves?

The answers start with a distinction that was easy to overlook when the first Star Wars film came out. Watching a film and having a toy based on that film are two different experiences. A film has an ending. With a set of figures, the story can go on as long as you like. You can put two characters together who never meet on screen. You can take apart the scene you've made and build a different one tomorrow.

In May 1977, the film opens. Kenner, the company licensed to make Star Wars toys, has products it can develop from flat artwork, including puzzles, posters and games. Making a plastic figure is a different problem. It has to turn a character on screen into something that can be produced in quantity, sold at a price a family can afford and recognized from a few feet away in a toy store.

The size of the figure matters. Kenner's Star Wars characters are around three and three-quarter inches tall. At the time, with the cost of plastic affected by the oil shortage, making them smaller than competing figures such as G.I. Joe reduces the material needed. It also allows children to put several characters in the same little world. In the years that follow, that world will grow to include vehicles and play sets.

But a smaller toy isn't automatically a faster toy to develop. Designers still have to work out how the characters look in three dimensions. Molds have to be made and tested. Parts have to be produced, assembled, decorated and packaged. Kenner speeds up the development process, but there still won't be a line of finished figures on store shelves in time for Christmas.

Now the toy company has a curious problem. The film has made its characters valuable to customers, while the physical products are still being made. It can sell other merchandise. But it can't offer the main attraction.

Kenner's solution is to put something on sale before the thing it's selling is ready.

Consider the package itself. The company calls it the Early Bird Certificate Package. It is made of illustrated cardboard, so it has a recognizable presence in a store and can be wrapped as a present. Inside are stickers, a club card and a display with pictures of twelve characters. The display is not simply a piece of advertising. It will become part of the toy once the figures arrive.

At the center of the transaction is a certificate. The customer fills it in, mails it to the company and waits. Kenner is promising four specific characters in 1978, rather than some vague selection of future merchandise. The buyer is reserving a place in the first delivery of a line of toys.

Notice what that means for a store. Under normal circumstances, a shopper and a retailer finish their transaction at the counter. The shopper has the object, the retailer has the money. If the toy is a gift, the child can start playing with it immediately. With the Early Bird package, the store hands over a promise that a different company will have to fulfill much later.

Some shops simply refuse to carry it. From their perspective, even if Kenner intends to deliver, there's an obvious risk. The parent who's paid has nowhere else to go if the product doesn't show up. There is no substitute figure available from the package itself.

Yet Kenner has done research suggesting that customers are prepared to wait. And as the holiday approaches, the reports coming from stores are striking. A December newspaper story describes the packages selling out in New York and Chicago. The company has made them a limited-time offer; the retail package sets the end of December as the last date for sale.

The mail is already coming back to Kenner before Christmas. John Beck, a representative of the company, tells the press that some of the people asking about the toys are adults. One caller he mentions is a twenty-six-year-old engineering student. Kenner had prepared a present that children could receive under the tree. It is also selling directly into an adult audience that wants a little piece of the film.

The deal now has to be completed. In 1978, Kenner mails the first four characters. There is a new physical object to distinguish from the original purchase. Instead of the colorful retail package, the toys arrive in a plain white mailer. A tray inside holds Luke Skywalker, Princess Leia, Chewbacca and R2-D2, each in a bag. The kit also supplies pegs that fit the figures' feet and the display. A child can finally populate the scene the company sold the previous winter.

The other eight characters come out soon afterward. By the end of 1978 the National Museum of Play puts the sales of the Star Wars toy line at roughly forty million items. That is not the number of Early Bird certificates. It is a measure of the much bigger business that followed their delivery.

And Kenner keeps changing the toys. Some early lightsabers have two sliding sections that extend out from the figure. A simpler version reduces manufacturing costs. A figure that looks almost identical at first glance can therefore represent a different period in production.

For children playing with the toys, the change in a lightsaber is a small detail. For later collectors, it can be a way to distinguish different releases. The original packaging has a similar role. It can tell you whether you are looking at the Christmas offer or the parcel that completed it.

That distinction is particularly important because the Early Bird package itself returns in 2005. Hasbro issues a new edition through Walmart. Once again, customers can mail in a certificate and get four figures. The figures are new versions of the characters, not the old Kenner stock. What was originally a response to a missed deadline has become a format that can be revived for nostalgia. An Early Bird package is no longer enough on its own to identify the year.

In May 2024, Heritage Auctions sells an unused original 1977 retail envelope for one thousand, three hundred and twelve dollars and fifty cents. The description notes its wear and even a price sticker from Target. The object has a history that is visible on the cardboard. The result is a recorded sale, not a universal value.

A year later, another auction gives us an unusually clear way to separate the three parts of the original sales process.

Hake's July 2025 auction includes a very highly graded, sealed original certificate package. It's the first thing a customer could buy. It sells for eighteen thousand, five hundred and sixty-one dollars.

The second lot is a highly graded original mail-away kit with the figures, the white box and its accompanying parts and paperwork. That is the second half of the purchase, the promise fulfilled. The price is forty-five thousand, four hundred and thirty dollars.

The third object never belonged inside either of those packages. It's the shop's own cardboard display with a compartment for the envelopes. The header shows photographs of the figures at their actual size. A sample retail envelope is included, positioned as it would have been for a customer to see. This particular fixture sets an auction record for a display associated with an action figure line. Including the buyer's premium, it brings one hundred and twelve thousand, one hundred dollars.

Those results do not make every cardboard stand more valuable than every toy. The condition, rarity and history of each object matter. Nor does a photograph of an opened Early Bird package establish that it is the item sold at Hake's. These are distinct lots.

But look at what changed. In 1977 the colorful envelope existed because there wasn't a finished toy to buy. The white mailer arrived later to make good on that promise. The store display had a job only as long as there were people looking to buy the envelopes. Once that job was over, it wasn't even the thing customers had paid for.

Nearly half a century later, someone paid six figures for an example of that store display. It is a record of the moment when a toy company found a way to make waiting itself part of a Christmas gift.
