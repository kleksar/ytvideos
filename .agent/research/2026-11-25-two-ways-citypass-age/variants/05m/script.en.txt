Can a birthday make a sightseeing pass more expensive than separate tickets? And what happens to my discount if I can enter one of the museums for free?

I'm putting together a New York trip with five places I actually want to see. I'm using published admission prices in a model, not reporting a real booking. I have nine days and assume I can get the necessary reservations. I'm comparing the cost of admission to the same experiences.

I can buy a New York CityPASS for $151 including its fee, or I can book each attraction separately. I want to know which way costs less before I commit to anything.

The first place is the American Museum of Natural History. I don't just want to walk through the general galleries. I want to see one ticketed exhibition too. For an adult visitor who lives outside New York State, that combination is $43.

Next is the Intrepid museum, on an aircraft carrier. Its adult ticket is $38. At the Guggenheim, I add $30 for the regular adult ticket. I also want to take the authorized ferry to Liberty and Ellis islands. The basic ticket starts at $26 and doesn't give me access to the crown of the statue. That's the experience I want in both versions.

Those four stops cost $137. If I were buying individual tickets, I could already see that number on my list.

But there is still one museum I want to visit: the Whitney. Let's say I'm 26, I have no special discounts, and I'm going during regular paid admission hours. That's $30 more.

The final bill is $167. With the CityPASS, I can choose all five of those places for $151. The difference is $16. I have that much left in my hypothetical budget without changing where I go.

Now I imagine the identical trip a year earlier in my life. I'm 25. I still want to see the same exhibition, museums and islands. Nothing about the attractions or the schedule needs to change.

At the Whitney, the price of my admission goes to zero. Visitors aged 25 and under enter free. My separate tickets now total $137.

The pass doesn't become cheaper because I've chosen four paid visits instead of five. I could use four of its admissions and get my free ticket to the Whitney separately, but I would still have spent $151. The winner changes: individual tickets save $14.

I can make the same decision without adding up all five prices again. My four other visits cost $137. The pass is $14 more than that. If the Whitney would cost me more than $14, the pass wins. If my Whitney admission costs less than $14, separate tickets win. At exactly $14, the two bills match.

That explains why my age isn't the only thing that matters. Suppose I'm 26 again, but I can fit the Whitney's free Friday evening hours into my schedule and secure a ticket. I now have the same $137 admission bill with separate tickets. I haven't dropped the museum to save money. I've changed the time of the visit in both comparisons.

And what if I decide before paying that I don't really want to visit the islands? At 26, the four remaining visits would cost $141 separately. Buying the full pass just for those four would cost $10 more. If I've already paid for my pass, that's a different decision; I can't pretend the original purchase never happened.

For this trip, I'd write down every visit I truly want, price the admission I'm eligible for, and only then compare the total with the pass. I'd check the reservations too, because a ticket I can't use isn't equivalent to one I can.

The pass saves me $16 on the original trip at 26. At 25, or with an available free Whitney slot at 26, the separate tickets save me $14. The same five sights can give me opposite answers. I don't need a universal winner. I need the cheaper way to take my particular trip.
