If I'm going to New York, is it cheaper to buy a sightseeing pass or pay at every attraction? Can my age reverse the answer? And what happens if I add one more place to a trip after I've already found the cheaper option?

I'm going to work through a trip that I can plan on paper. These are published admission prices and hypothetical choices, not receipts from a trip I've taken. I'll compare two methods of paying for the same experiences. I'll keep admission prices separate from travel costs, time in lines and anything I buy inside a museum.

My first decision is not which ticket to buy. It's where I want to go.

I've set aside nine days, and there are five visits on my list. I want the natural history museum, the aircraft carrier museum, the Guggenheim, the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island ferry, and the Whitney Museum. I assume I can find suitable reservation slots. If I can't, I'll have to change the itinerary for both methods rather than quietly give one method a different trip.

One option is to pay $151 for a New York CityPASS, including its fee. The current ticket lets me choose five attractions from a list of ten. That matters because older versions of this ticket had a different set of rules. I need today's offer, not a price or a selection rule from an old travel video.

The other option is to buy each admission separately. I want to check what those tickets actually include, because the cheapest ticket with the same museum name might not give me the same experience.

At the American Museum of Natural History, for instance, general admission for an adult from outside New York State is listed at $37. But my plan includes one ticketed exhibition as well. For that I use the $43 ticket. The pass includes the relevant exhibition admission, so I'm not comparing an exhibition visit on one side with a general admission visit on the other.

The Intrepid costs $38. I add the Guggenheim's regular adult price of $30. Then I add $26, the starting fare for the authorized ferry to the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island. I am not buying access to the statue's crown. The ferry experience must be the same in both columns.

At this point I have four paid visits and a bill of $137 if I buy them separately.

The fifth visit is the Whitney. I'm 26, I don't have a student discount or another special entitlement, and I'm visiting during regular paid hours. That means another $30.

Now I can compare the two bills. The five individual admissions come to $167. I can choose those same five with the $151 pass, so option A saves $16. I have my answer for the original conditions.

But I haven't bought anything yet. I want to see how sensitive that answer is to one change.

In the second version of the trip, I am 25. I keep all five experiences, the same exhibition, the same schedule, and the same nine-day window. The Whitney admits people aged 25 and under for free. My fifth ticket now costs nothing. My four paid admissions are unchanged, so my total with option B falls to $137.

What happens to option A? I can choose just the four paid visits from the pass and enter the Whitney with a free ticket. In this comparison the price of the pass hasn't changed. I still pay $151.

The birthday has reversed the result. At 26 the pass saves $16, and at 25 the individual tickets save $14. That's a $30 change in the gap between the two methods, exactly the change in the Whitney ticket price.

I can turn that into a useful number for my own plan. The other four visits cost $137. The pass costs $151. The difference is $14. If the Whitney costs me more than $14, the pass is cheaper. At $14 the bills are equal. Below $14, the individual tickets win.

This is why I shouldn't assume that my friend's saving will be my saving, even if we visit all the same places.

Let's go back to age 26 and look at the schedule. The Whitney also offers free Friday evening admission, with limited tickets. If I can secure one of those tickets and the time suits my trip, I'll reset both options to that visit. My bill for separate admissions is $137 again. The pass still costs $151. I don't need to be 25 to make the separate tickets cheaper.

Of course, if the free time doesn't work or the reservations are gone, I can't put zero on my spreadsheet. I go back to the regular $30 price. That's the difference between a discount I can use and a discount that just exists.

I also want to test whether I've accidentally priced more museum than I need. Suppose I change the original itinerary and decide I only want general admission at the natural history museum. I take the ticketed exhibition off both versions. My separate bill at age 26 falls by $6 to $161. The pass still wins, but now by $10 instead of $16. At 25, the separate bill becomes $131. The exhibition included in the pass doesn't save me six dollars if I wouldn't buy it anyway.

Next I reset to the original trip with the exhibition, and change one more thing. Before purchasing anything, I decide I don't want to take the ferry after all. I'm now comparing four visits, not the original five. At 26 they cost $141 separately, less than the pass by $10. If I've already bought the pass, I need to consider the actual refund terms and which tickets I've used. This calculation is only for the decision before purchase.

So far, taking things off the list or getting discounts has made separate tickets more attractive. But I also want to see what happens when there is something I genuinely want to add.

I restore the original five visits, and add a Circle Line sightseeing cruise listed at a $55 reference price. That's a sixth experience. It isn't the same as my ferry: the sightseeing boat goes around the harbor, while the ferry lets me visit the islands. I want both in this version of the trip, and assume the listed cruise is available at that price.

At 25, I have five paid experiences plus the free Whitney visit. The separate prices add up to $192. The pass can cover the five paid experiences, with the Whitney free outside the pass. The admission bill is $151. This time the pass wins at 25, by $41.

At 26, all six places have a paid admission in regular hours. Separately they cost $222. I only have five selections on my pass. Which ticket do I leave outside it?

In this model, the cheapest is the $26 ferry. I pay for that separately and cover the other five with the pass. That brings option A to $177. The saving is $45 compared with $222. Leaving the $55 cruise outside instead would cost me $29 more.

That's not a reason to add a cruise I don't want. At 25, my original five visits with separate tickets cost $137, less than the $151 I'd spend on the larger trip with a pass. A bigger saving against a different itinerary is still a bigger trip. If I didn't want the cruise, I haven't saved money by adding it.

Before buying, I'd also check the nine-day validity window and the reservation instructions. Some pass reservations and individual ticket reservations work differently. My model doesn't measure waiting times, and I won't put a dollar value on skipping a line that I don't know I can skip.

My final decision is a short list of prices for experiences I actually want, at the times I can go, with my own eligibility. For five visits at 26, I choose the pass. For the identical five at 25, I buy individual tickets. If I truly want six experiences including the cruise, the pass can win again. That's how I can use the discount without letting the discount choose my vacation for me.
