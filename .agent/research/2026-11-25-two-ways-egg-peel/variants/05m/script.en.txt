I've got ten eggs and a serving dish with room for twelve deviled egg halves. I need six whole eggs that will look good after I cut them. Do I put the eggs into cold water and heat everything together, or wait until the water is already boiling?

There are plenty of ways to rescue an egg with a chunk missing. I can chop it up for a sandwich, for instance. But I can't put that chunk back into the white. For this dish, the number of smooth eggs is what matters.

I have two methods to choose from. I'm going to compare them by reconstructing the results of an actual published kitchen test. These aren't eggs I've cooked or measured myself. Each side gets ten eggs, and each is aiming for hard-cooked eggs. The cooling time before peeling is identical. The published comparison doesn't tell me whether the eggs in each group were matched by age or give all the details of the boiling schedule. I'll keep those limits in mind.

On side A, I place the eggs in a pot and cover them with cold water. I bring the water to a boil, cover the pot, take it off the heat and let the eggs sit for ten minutes. This method was valued for producing nicely cooked whites and yolks.

On side B, I bring the water to a boil before adding the eggs. The important difference is that the outside of the egg heats up quickly instead of gradually warming with the pot.

Both groups spend five minutes in an ice bath before they're peeled.

Now I can look at the whites. I'll use three different standards, starting with the strictest. I want a smooth egg with no visible damage.

The ten cold-start eggs give me none. Three have slight imperfections, two are pitted, two have more serious damage, and three are torn down to the center. I can use some of them for other dishes, but I don't have six pristine eggs.

The boiling-water batch gives me nine smooth eggs and one with a minor imperfection. My serving dish is full, and I can put three more smooth eggs aside.

What if I'm not so fussy? If slight imperfections are acceptable, I get three eggs from side A and all ten from side B. If I also accept pitted eggs, side A goes up to five. The difference gets smaller, but the hot-start batch still supplies more eggs under these standards.

The interesting part is underneath the shell. There's a thin membrane between the shell and the white. It's that layer that can remain attached when I try to peel a cooked egg.

One proposed explanation is that slow warming gives proteins in the white more opportunity to attach to the membrane. With a fast hot start, they bind to one another sooner and contract, making separation easier.

That doesn't mean I should keep the pot at a violent boil. A hot start and a hard boil for the entire cooking time are different things. In another set of kitchen tests, a brief initial boil followed by a gentle simmer produced eggs that were easier to peel without sacrificing as much tenderness.

And there's a useful check on that dramatic result of zero against nine. Another cook who compared methods reported that even two-week-old eggs started in cold water peeled cleanly just over half the time, whereas eggs started in boiling water or steam were above ninety percent. The exact definitions and methods differ, so I can't combine those figures into a prediction for my next carton. They do point in the same direction.

My decision depends on what I'm making. For twelve neat halves on a serving dish, I'll start hot and use a recipe suited to my egg size and batch. If a few pieces come off during peeling, I can still use those eggs elsewhere.

For a bowl of chopped egg salad, the same appearance score is much less important. I might prefer the texture or convenience of the cold-start method instead.

The useful trick happens before peeling. I don't have to wait until the white tears to improve my odds. I can choose how I heat the eggs in the first place.
