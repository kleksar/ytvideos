I have ten eggs in front of me and a serving dish with twelve spaces for deviled egg halves. Six good eggs will fill it. The other four are insurance. My decision is whether to put all the eggs in cold water and heat them with the pot, or to bring the water to a boil before they go in.

Which method would you choose? And what would make you change your mind? If I get six beautiful eggs but the whites are unpleasantly tough, I haven't really made the dish I wanted. If all ten taste fine but the surfaces have holes in them, I can make egg salad instead, but I still haven't filled that serving dish.

I'll start with the appearance problem, then consider the cooking problem separately. This is a reconstruction of published kitchen tests, not a new experiment in my own kitchen. I'll use the results that were actually reported, and I won't invent a cooking time or a score that wasn't recorded.

For the main comparison, each side gets ten eggs and aims for hard-cooked eggs. Both batches are chilled for five minutes in ice water, then peeled. I'm counting the eggs with completely smooth whites. The original account didn't give enough detail to establish whether every egg was matched by age and size, so this comparison is evidence from those particular batches, not a promise about the next carton I buy.

Side A is a method that starts with a cold pot. I cover the eggs with about an inch of cold water. The eggs get warmer as the water warms. Once the water boils, I cover the pot, take it off the heat and wait ten minutes. The method was used because it produced appealingly cooked whites and yolks. The trouble came when it was time to take the shells off.

For side B, I get the water boiling before the eggs go in. The published test calls this the boiling method, in contrast to the cold-water method. It doesn't describe every step of the hot-water schedule for this particular batch, so I can't give an exact minute count for it. What I can compare is the finished result of both methods under the reported five-minute cooling procedure.

As I start peeling, I don't just want to count how many shells break. Every shell has to break at some point. I want to know how much of the cooked white comes away with it.

Here are the recorded results on side A. Three eggs have only small imperfections. Two are pitted. Two are badly damaged, and three are torn as far as the center. That accounts for all ten eggs. There isn't a single perfectly smooth white in this batch.

Now I have to decide whether to change my standard. I could accept the three lightly marked eggs, but that's only enough for six halves. Even if I accept the two pitted eggs as well, I have five eggs for a dish that needs six. I would have to use something more heavily damaged, make fewer halves, or cook another egg.

On side B, nine out of ten eggs have flawlessly smooth whites. The remaining one has only a small imperfection. The six I need fit the dish, and three smooth eggs remain. I've got a clear answer on the appearance metric without pretending the cooking method was perfect.

I can make a little decision table out of these results. If I require six completely smooth eggs, the recorded batches give me zero and nine. If I need six eggs with no more than minor imperfections, they give me three and ten. If I accept pitting, they give me five and ten. In every one of these three versions of my party problem, the second batch meets the target and the first doesn't.

But notice what happens if the job changes completely. Say I'm making chopped egg salad. Now that beautiful white is about to be cut into pieces. The peeling damage isn't necessarily important anymore. I can't say the first method produced zero useful eggs, because I didn't count edible eggs or measure how much white was lost. I only counted appearances. I would need to weigh the lost white or taste the cooked eggs to settle that different question.

The result also doesn't mean all eggs started cold are impossible to peel. In a separate comparison, a different cook reported a clean-peeling success rate a little above half for two-week-old eggs started in cold water. For eggs started in boiling water or steam, the rate was above ninety percent. These aren't the same batches or necessarily the same grading rules. Still, both comparisons support the idea that the way I start heating an egg can influence how it peels.

So what is happening inside? I can think of an egg as having three layers here: the hard shell, a thin membrane underneath, and the white. When I peel a cooked egg and pieces of white come away, the membrane is often the troublesome layer.

The white contains proteins that change their shape and bind together when heated. One explanation for the difference between the methods is that when the outside heats quickly, those proteins set and pull away before they can attach as firmly to the membrane. When the heating is gradual, they may have more opportunity to bind to it. I can draw that mechanism, but I can't say the peelability scores themselves measured protein bonds. They measured the appearance after peeling.

What about the age of my eggs? The same kitchen that compared cooking methods also tried eighteen fresh eggs and eighteen eggs that were a month old. Some of the older eggs were easier to peel, but others still caused trouble. As an egg ages, its internal chemistry changes, and that may reduce the tendency to stick to the membrane. It doesn't remove the need to choose a good cooking method. I won't deliberately keep a carton beyond safe storage guidance just to chase a prettier surface.

There is one more decision, because putting eggs into boiling water doesn't mean keeping them at a fierce boil from start to finish. The surface heats before the center, and prolonged strong heating can make the outer white rubbery before the yolk has the texture I want. Another tested recipe deals with that by starting in boiling water, then lowering the heat to a very gentle simmer after about thirty seconds. Its directions are for a particular batch and amount of water. I'd follow that recipe's conditions rather than borrowing the ten-minute resting time from the cold-start method.

Even with a hot start, the amount of water and the number of cold eggs matter. The test kitchen ran a separate comparison with six eggs in boiling water and six above boiling water in a steamer basket. Both groups cooked for thirteen minutes and were chilled for fifteen. The steamed eggs had more evenly set yolks, while the boiled ones were slightly translucent in the middle. The authors suggested that the cold eggs temporarily lowered the temperature of the water. This separate test doesn't change the nine-against-zero appearance score; it explains why I should not copy a timer from a different pot and expect identical yolks.

If I wanted to find the best method for my own stove, I would split eggs from the same carton into two groups, match the size and storage conditions, use tested hard-cooking schedules for each method, and chill them in the same way. I'd photograph the whites before tasting, and score each one without looking at which pot it came from. Then I'd cut them open and record the yolks separately. Repeating this with more than one carton would tell me more than treating ten eggs as a universal law. I haven't performed that test.

For tonight, I know enough to make a practical choice. I will use a hot start with gentle finishing heat for my party eggs, cool them properly and keep a few extras in case peeling goes wrong. Nine smooth eggs in a reported batch of ten is a useful result, not a guarantee. If appearance is the priority, a hot start gives me a better-supported option. If I'm chopping everything up, I can decide on texture, convenience and how much egg I actually lose.

When I'm looking at an unbroken shell in the kitchen, the first useful decision is still the one I make at the stove.
