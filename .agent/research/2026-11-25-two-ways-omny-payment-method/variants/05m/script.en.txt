I'm planning a week in New York, and I want to use the subway instead of paying for taxis. I have a bank card in my pocket and the same card in my phone. Can I swap between them without losing the weekly transit discount? And what happens if I pay for a friend as well?

I'll compare the total fare for an identical set of journeys. This is a calculation, not a test at an actual turnstile. I'm using the published full adult fare of three dollars and the thirty-five-dollar seven-day cap. Every trip in the main example is an eligible subway ride, with no free transfers. There is no earlier spending on either payment method.

Here's my itinerary. For the first three days, I'll take four rides per day. For the next four days, I'll take two per day. That's twenty rides, all inside seven days.

In A, I stick with the plastic card. In B, I alternate between that card and my phone. The phone has the same bank card loaded in its wallet. Before I start, which do you think will cost less? Or will both come to the same amount?

On day one, I make four trips. Both versions cost twelve dollars. But the fare system is counting something different in the background. In A, all twelve dollars build toward one discount. In B, six dollars build toward the card's discount, and six toward the phone's.

Day two ends at twenty-four dollars in both versions. The difference is still invisible in the total. If I only look at that total, there's no reason yet to think I've made an expensive choice.

By ride eleven, both versions are at thirty-three dollars. Then I go through the gate for the fourth time on day three.

In A, I hit the thirty-five-dollar ceiling. I can take the remaining eight rides without adding to that total during this cap period.

In B, I've now used the card six times and the phone six times. Each method has eighteen dollars of eligible fares. Together they add up to thirty-six dollars, but they do not form a single thirty-six-dollar balance for the discount.

This is where the two versions split. On day four, I take two more rides. A stays at thirty-five. B goes up to forty-two. By the end of day seven, A is still thirty-five and B is sixty.

The difference is twenty-five dollars. There isn't a more expensive fare for using my phone. I could have used the phone alone from the beginning and got the same result as A. The mistake in B was switching between two separately counted payment methods.

Now I want to travel with a friend. Both of us need the same twenty rides. I can tap my card twice at each station, or we can each use our own consistent method. This time I'm comparing the combined cost for two adults, not just my bill.

If I tap twice, only my first tap builds toward my cap. Even after that tap becomes free, the second one is still a paid fare. My twenty rides cost thirty-five dollars; my friend's twenty rides cost sixty. Together, that's ninety-five.

If my friend pays separately and keeps using their own method, we can each reach a thirty-five-dollar cap. The combined bill is seventy dollars.

So the useful rule changes with the number of travelers. On my own, I keep one method. With another adult taking the same trips, I don't pool our rides on one method expecting two free passes.

For a short visit with fewer than twelve separate paid rides and no previous spending, the cap doesn't save me any money. Express buses and reduced fares have different rules, and free transfers aren't extra paid rides. I'd check my exact trip history before concluding that I was charged incorrectly.

But for this week of twenty subway rides, I know which way I'll pay. I can keep twenty-five dollars just by leaving the other payment method in my pocket.
