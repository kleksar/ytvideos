I have two ways to open a New York subway gate. I can tap the bank card in my pocket, or I can tap my phone, which has that same card in its wallet. Both payments go to my bank account. What I want to find out is whether they also go toward the same weekly transit discount.

There are three things I want to know before I travel: how much the choice matters over twenty rides, whether changing methods just once can make a difference, and what I should do if a friend comes along for the whole week.

I'll use a model based on New York's published OMNY fare rules. OMNY is the system that accepts these taps for travel. For the main comparison, I have no existing fares on either method, I'm paying the full adult subway fare of three dollars, and all my rides qualify. I'm not counting free transfers or express buses. I'll keep the itinerary identical and compare only the money spent on fares. These are calculated totals, not receipts from an actual trip.

I've planned three busy days and four quieter ones. On each of the first three days I'll go out in the morning, come back, go out again, and come back once more. That's twelve paid subway rides. On each of the next four days, I only need a return trip. That brings me to twenty.

In A, I use the same physical card every time. In B, I alternate between the card and the phone, beginning with the card. Both are available to me in both versions. Which way would you choose?

After my first four rides, the cost is twelve dollars either way. After eight, it's twenty-four. After eleven, it's thirty-three. At this point, my total spending is the same in both models. If I compare only that number, the two methods appear to be interchangeable.

But the next ride is different. The subway has a seven-day fare cap of thirty-five dollars. Once I've accumulated that much eligible spending on the same payment method during its cap period, the remaining eligible rides are free for the rest of that period.

In A, ride twelve takes my fare total to thirty-five dollars. In B, the total reaches thirty-six, but it's divided into two parts: eighteen dollars toward the cap on the card, eighteen toward the cap on the phone. The published rules treat the physical card and the phone as separate payment methods, even when the phone is using the same underlying bank card.

On day four I have a simple return journey. A stays at thirty-five; B rises to forty-two. Every subsequent pair of rides adds another six dollars in B. By day seven, I've reached twenty rides.

The result is thirty-five dollars in A, and sixty in B. That's twenty-five dollars more without taking a different train, traveling farther, or changing my plans.

Notice what happens if I choose the phone alone instead of the physical card. All my eligible spending would then build toward one cap on that phone. It isn't a contest between the technology in a plastic card and the technology in a phone. It's a contest between keeping one payment method and splitting my trips across two.

How much does it matter if my itinerary changes? I'll keep the same rules and shorten the trip. With eight separate paid rides, I pay twenty-four dollars either way. Neither cap matters. At twelve rides, the consistent method costs thirty-five dollars, while alternating costs thirty-six. I only save one dollar.

At sixteen rides, the difference has grown to thirteen dollars: thirty-five versus forty-eight. At twenty-four rides, I can reach both caps in the alternating version, because each method now has twelve paid rides of its own. I end up paying seventy dollars instead of thirty-five. The savings depend on how many rides I actually need. It makes no sense to take unnecessary trips just to reach a cap.

What if I don't alternate all week? Suppose I follow A through ride twelve and reach thirty-five dollars. On ride thirteen, I use my phone instead, perhaps because it's the payment method I have in my hand. Under the same assumptions, the phone starts with no accumulated fares and that ride adds three dollars. If I return to the original card for the remaining rides inside its cap period, my modeled total is thirty-eight, not sixty. One switch is not equivalent to starting over on every ride, and it doesn't erase the progress on the original card.

The date matters too. I need to distinguish twenty rides inside one cap period from twenty rides across two periods. The first tap starts the seven-day cap period. Imagine ten paid rides in the first period, followed by ten in a new period. Even using one card, I haven't spent enough in either period to get a free ride. That's sixty dollars. Merely counting the last twenty rides in my bank statement would hide that boundary.

There's another place where the exact payment method matters. For an eligible free transfer within two hours, I need to use the same method for both parts of the journey. If I take a local bus and transfer to the subway, I shouldn't treat a change from card to phone as automatically preserving that transfer. For a new payment method with no cap or transfer credit in this example, two separate full fares would be six dollars rather than three. That is a different source of extra cost, not something I've included in the original twenty-ride calculation. A free transfer doesn't add a paid ride toward the cap.

Now my friend joins me for the entire original week. We need forty passenger rides between us, twenty each. I want to know whether sharing one payment method helps us reach the discount sooner. I'll compare our combined cost, with the same full fare and no earlier spending.

In the first version, I tap the same card twice at every entry. The first tap pays for me. The second pays for my friend. The crucial rule is that the additional passenger's fare doesn't build toward my cap. After twelve entries, my first taps have taken me to thirty-five dollars, and the second taps have cost another thirty-six. At each of the last eight entries, only the first tap is free.

The combined total is ninety-five dollars: thirty-five for me and sixty for my friend.

In the other version, my friend has their own valid payment method. We both use our respective methods consistently. Each of us reaches a thirty-five-dollar cap and travels free for the rest of the same respective period. Together, we pay seventy dollars.

That's another twenty-five-dollar difference, but this time keeping all the fares on a single payment method would be the more expensive choice. I want to keep one payment method per rider, not one for every person in the group. Both travelers in this calculation are adults paying the regular fare. I'm not assuming either qualifies for a special discount.

Before I travel, I can decide which method I'll keep using. If I notice an unexpected charge, the OMNY account can show the trip and charge history for a linked method. I'd check the correct card or device, the cap period, and whether the journey was eligible. If I believe there was an overcharge, I can contact OMNY rather than assume the bank total proves one. This model doesn't diagnose a real disputed payment, and the prices can change.

My original twenty rides have a clear winner: thirty-five dollars instead of sixty, whether I choose the card alone or the phone alone. When I'm traveling with another adult, we can each earn our own discount. The question I need to remember at every gate is not which of my two objects looks more convenient, but which payment method I have already been using for my rides.
