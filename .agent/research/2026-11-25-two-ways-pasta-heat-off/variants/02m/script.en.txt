Can I cook spaghetti with the stove switched off?

I want dinner, but I don't want to keep paying to heat water if it's already hot. I have two ways to make the same portion. A: keep the heat on. B: switch it off and cover the pot. Which would you pick?

I'm recreating a published laboratory comparison, not cooking two real dinners on camera. Both sides start with 150 grams of dry spaghetti and one and a half liters of water. I count energy from heating the water through cooking, and I also check the time and texture.

On side A, the water stays near 95 degrees Celsius. The pasta reaches the study's chosen stopping point in thirteen and a half minutes.

On side B, the lid goes on and the heater stops. The water gradually cools to 80 degrees, but it's still hot enough for the spaghetti to keep cooking. This portion needs fifteen minutes. That's only another minute and a half.

The energy estimate is the surprise. If I give A a score of 100 energy units, B needs about 40. But I haven't checked the pasta itself.

In the real study, researchers pressed and stretched the cooked strands. The pasta from the switched-off stove had a softer outer layer and a thinner firm center. It didn't resist stretching as well.

So B wins on estimated energy, while A finishes sooner and gives the firmer texture in this particular comparison. I'd choose B if saving energy matters most, and A if I want that firmer bite. The percentages aren't a guarantee for my own stove.
