I can make a parcel cheaper to send without making its contents any lighter. I've got a gift to mail across the United States, and two cardboard boxes that differ by just one inch.

I'm comparing postage, not the cost of the cardboard. This is a model using published USPS retail Ground Advantage prices from October 2026, not a purchase. In both cases the gift fits safely, the packed weight is three pounds, and the destination is in Zone Eight.

Box A measures twelve by twelve by thirteen inches. Box B measures twelve by twelve by twelve. Which would you pick?

Both give me the same reading on the scale. But when I multiply the outside dimensions, A takes up eighteen hundred and seventy-two cubic inches. B takes up exactly seventeen hundred and twenty-eight.

That's one cubic foot. At that size, this service charges me for the actual weight. Go above it and another rule kicks in: I divide the volume by a hundred and thirty-nine and round the result up to the next whole pound.

Box A now has a billable weight of fourteen pounds. Nothing was added to my gift. The price for A is fifty-seven dollars and sixty-five cents. B costs twenty-three dollars and fifteen cents.

That's thirty-four dollars and fifty cents for a difference of one inch.

Even a bulging lid can matter: under these rules, a fractional inch rounds up. So I'd measure the closed box, and never sacrifice the padding just to hit a price. If the gift fits safely in both, there's a reason I check the box before I buy the label.
