I'm getting ready to mail a gift to someone across the United States. On the table are two ordinary cardboard boxes. The gift fits safely in either of them, and the packed weight will be three pounds in both. The only difference is an inch of height. If I pay for three pounds either way, why should I care? And if the smaller box is cheaper, does that mean I should always buy the smallest possible box?

I want to answer both questions before I buy a shipping label. I'm using a model with published USPS Ground Advantage retail prices effective October fourth, twenty twenty-six. I'm not reporting a shipment or a price I personally paid. The initial route is Zone Eight, the service is the same, and the contents and safe packing are identical in both alternatives. The price table may change, and other services or discounted labels can work differently.

My first measure is the amount of postage I need to buy. I'll consider the extra cost of obtaining a different box separately. That distinction matters because sometimes I've already got both boxes and sometimes I've only got one.

Box A is twelve by twelve by thirteen inches on the outside. Box B is twelve by twelve by twelve. Put them next to each other and they hardly look different. Which one would you send? A or B?

The scale reads three pounds in either case. If I enter only that number, I've missed the part of the calculation that can make the two labels completely different.

I need the space each box occupies. Twelve times twelve gives me a bottom area of a hundred and forty-four square inches. Box B is twelve inches tall, so its volume is seventeen hundred and twenty-eight cubic inches. Box A is thirteen inches tall, so it's eighteen hundred and seventy-two cubic inches. The extra space is a hundred and forty-four cubic inches, or a single layer one inch high across the top of the box.

Now I know the volume. But I haven't priced either parcel yet.

Seventeen hundred and twenty-eight cubic inches is exactly one cubic foot. Under the rules I'm using, qualifying parcels larger than one cubic foot are subject to dimensional-weight pricing. It's a way of turning the outside volume into a weight used for the price comparison. The divisor for this service and date is a hundred and thirty-nine. If the result isn't a whole number of pounds, I round it up.

The rule doesn't mean the parcel has actually gained weight. The scale still measures its physical weight. Dimensional weight is a separate number for billing, and the greater of the actual and dimensional weights determines the applicable rate when this rule applies.

So I handle the two boxes differently. B is exactly one cubic foot. It hasn't exceeded the threshold, so its three-pound weight is the figure I need for the table. A is above the threshold. I take eighteen hundred and seventy-two, divide by a hundred and thirty-nine, and get about thirteen point four seven. Rounded up, that's fourteen pounds.

I look at the Zone Eight column of the retail table. The three-pound price is twenty-three dollars and fifteen cents. The fourteen-pound price is fifty-seven dollars and sixty-five cents.

A difference of thirty-four dollars and fifty cents, without adding anything to the gift.

That's the answer to my original question. But it would be easy to draw the wrong conclusion from it. I haven't shown that a twelve-inch box is always cheaper than a thirteen-inch box. I've shown what happens at a particular boundary under a particular pricing rule.

To see why the boundary matters, I can imagine another pair of boxes. Both are twelve inches wide and twelve inches long, but one is eleven inches tall and the other is twelve. Assume both are safely packed and weigh the same three pounds. The eleven-inch box has a volume of fifteen hundred and eighty-four cubic inches. The twelve-inch box has a volume of seventeen hundred and twenty-eight. Neither exceeds a cubic foot, so both are priced at three pounds in this comparison. At Zone Eight, both cost twenty-three dollars and fifteen cents.

Now imagine that I still have the twelve-inch cube, but after I pack and close it, the height measures twelve point one inches instead of exactly twelve. I'm not talking about a tenth of a pound here. I'm talking about a fraction of an inch. Under the rules in effect for this example, fractional outside dimensions round up to whole inches. With the other two dimensions unchanged, the measured height becomes thirteen for the calculation. I've landed right back at the eighteen hundred and seventy-two figure and its fourteen-pound billing weight.

The box didn't become substantially heavier or occupy thirteen full inches in reality. That's the consequence of the measurement rule combined with the volume threshold. If I measured the empty box instead, or relied on the label printed on the cardboard, I could miss that difference. So I measure the closed, packed parcel at its widest outside points.

Next, I'll keep the same two original boxes and change the destination for both. Suppose the parcel goes to Zone One instead of Zone Eight. I'm not changing how much the gift weighs or how much space it occupies. I only change the rate-table column.

The fourteen-pound entry is twenty dollars and twenty cents, and the three-pound entry is eleven dollars and eighty cents. My postage saving is now eight dollars and forty cents. The same rule gave me the same two billable weights, but not the same money saved.

That changes a real decision about the packaging. If I already own both boxes and they're equally suitable, B saves postage in both routes. If I only own A, the additional price of acquiring B has to be less than the postage savings for the switch to reduce my combined cost. On the longer route in this model, the break-even is thirty-four dollars and fifty cents. On the shorter one, it's eight dollars and forty cents. At those exact amounts the combined costs tie, before I include my time or other expenses. I'm not claiming that a replacement box actually costs either amount.

There is another way the result can change. I return to the original Zone Eight destination. Now my hypothetical item is heavier, and with safe packing both packages weigh sixteen pounds. The outside dimensions remain twelve by twelve by thirteen for A and twelve by twelve by twelve for B.

A still produces a dimensional weight of fourteen pounds, but the actual weight of sixteen is greater. The actual weight is now the one used for pricing. B is below or at the volume threshold and also has an actual weight of sixteen. At this rate and zone, both labels cost sixty-two dollars and sixty-five cents. The inch doesn't save a cent of postage this time.

I could even buy a new, smaller box for that heavier item and end up paying more overall, because the postage is unchanged. There's no reason in this calculation to do that unless the smaller box offers some other benefit. And this comparison hasn't measured benefits such as handling, storage, or damage rates.

Here's how I'd turn the model into a decision about a real parcel. First I'd choose packaging that protects what I'm sending. I wouldn't crush a fragile item into a cube to make a price table happy. If only the larger box provides the necessary protection, it's the only valid choice in my comparison.

Then I'd weigh the packed box and measure the longest external dimensions, including any bulge. I'd use the current calculator or quote for my actual origin, destination, service and package type. I'd check any other charges that may apply to an unusual or oversized parcel, rather than assuming the dimensional-weight calculation is the entire price. These two ordinary boxes aren't big enough to trigger the length or volume surcharges in this example.

If I have a second safe packaging option, I'd repeat the quote with its actual weight and dimensions. Finally I'd compare the postage difference with the extra cost of changing the packaging. That is a decision I can make before paying, instead of discovering the consequence after I've sent the gift.

I started with two boxes and the same three-pound weight. At the modeled Zone Eight retail rate, the smaller box saves thirty-four dollars and fifty cents. To Zone One it saves eight dollars and forty cents. With sixteen pounds inside, the postage is equal. My conclusion isn't to buy the smallest box every time. It's to compare two genuinely suitable packages before buying a label, because the space on the outside can change the price just as much as the weight on the scale.
