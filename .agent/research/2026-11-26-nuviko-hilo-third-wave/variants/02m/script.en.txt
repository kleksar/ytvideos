The radio said the tsunami had already reached Hilo. The waves were only seven feet high. Sixteen-year-old Carol Brown and her brother Ernest headed back into town.

Earlier that evening, May 22, 1960, sirens had sounded across the waterfront in Hawaii. An earthquake of magnitude 9.5 had struck Chile. Its waves had spent about fifteen hours crossing the Pacific.

Carol and Ernest had gone outside town to check on a niece. On their way back, Carol later recalled, a police officer told them the danger was over. They went to their sister's house on low ground.

Meanwhile, five men were measuring the water beneath a bridge. Around midnight it had risen four feet above normal. The next wave reached nine feet. Around one in the morning, the water dropped seven feet below normal.

Then a roar came from the bay. The men ran uphill. Behind them, a nearly vertical front of water struck the bridge.

At the sister's house, Carol heard a rumble, then the sound of things breaking. Water lifted the entire house off its foundation. When it stopped moving, the town was dark. The power plant had been hit.

Carol's family survived without serious injury. Sixty-one people in Hilo died that night.

A clock recovered from the wreckage still shows 1:04. It marks the largest wave, not the first. During a tsunami, stay outside the danger zone until officials confirm it's safe to return.
