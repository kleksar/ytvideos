Why did people return to a Hawaiian town after a tsunami warning? How could the scientists watching the ocean know that the first wave had arrived, and still have to run for their lives? And why does a clock in Hilo show four minutes past one?

On May 22, 1960, the ground off southern Chile shifted during the largest earthquake ever measured, magnitude 9.5. Water displaced by the movement of the seabed began traveling across the Pacific. Hilo, on the island of Hawaii, was about fifteen hours away from the approaching waves.

That gave the town time to prepare. It had been devastated by a tsunami in 1946. There were now coastal warning sirens. At 6:47 in the evening an official warning was issued, and around half past eight the sirens began sounding.

Sixteen-year-old Carol Brown was at home with her family. Their house stood on low ground. Her parents took valuables to a relative's place outside Hilo. Carol and her brother Ernest went to check on a niece who was babysitting outside town.

By midnight, the danger they had been warned about seemed to have arrived. But the report on the radio described waves only seven feet high. The two headed back into Hilo. In Carol's later account, they even encountered a police officer who said the danger had passed.

They went to their sister's house. It was in the low part of town.

On the other side of this story, a seismologist named Jerry Eaton and four companions were standing on the Wailuku River Bridge. They had driven down from the volcano observatory with measuring tapes, flashlights, notebooks and cameras. The old bridge had been destroyed in 1946. They chose an escape route uphill before starting work.

Around midnight, their measurements showed the first rise in the river, four feet above normal. Then it fell. At 12:46, the next wave raised the water nine feet above normal. As one o'clock approached, the surface sank seven feet below its usual level.

From out in the bay they heard a sound that Eaton would later compare to a distant train. A pale line became visible in the darkness. As it drew nearer, it became a steep wall of turbulent water.

The five men abandoned their position and ran toward higher ground. Looking back, they saw the front of the wave, about twenty feet high, collide with the bridge. Elsewhere in the bay the water would reach about thirty-five feet above sea level.

At about the same time, Carol heard the noise from inside her sister's house. The water hit and lifted the building off its foundation. When the house finally stopped moving, the lights of Hilo were out. The wave had disabled the power plant.

Carol and her family survived without serious injury. Other families were not so fortunate. Sixty-one people died in Hilo, and hundreds of homes and other buildings were destroyed.

Eaton's team recorded more waves after the largest one. Their measurements showed exactly why waiting for a single wave to pass was not enough. A tsunami is a succession of waves. A drop in water level can precede another surge, and the size of a wave at one stretch of coast does not tell you its size everywhere else.

The warning sirens in 1960 had also meant different things to different residents. Only about a third understood them as a signal to evacuate immediately. Previous alerts that had ended with little damage had made the danger harder to judge.

Hilo changed both its waterfront and the way it prepared residents for tsunamis. The damaged town clock was recovered and restored as a memorial, with its hands still pointing to 1:04.

The time on that clock is not the arrival time of the tsunami. It is the time of the wave that came after people had begun returning. That's why a tsunami warning isn't over when the ocean looks normal again. Stay outside the hazard zone until authorities officially say that returning is safe.
