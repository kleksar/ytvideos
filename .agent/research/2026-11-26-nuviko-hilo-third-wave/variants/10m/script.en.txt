How did an earthquake in Chile destroy part of a town in Hawaii fifteen hours later? Why did residents who had already left turn around before the worst wave arrived? And how did a group of scientists on a bridge capture the evidence that explains what went wrong?

On the afternoon of May 22, 1960, the boundary between two enormous plates of the Earth's crust gave way off the coast of southern Chile. The Nazca Plate moves beneath South America. Stress had accumulated along their boundary, and the sudden movement during the earthquake displaced a vast area of the seabed.

Its magnitude was 9.5, the largest earthquake ever recorded by instruments. The movement of the seabed disturbed the ocean above it. The resulting waves spread across the Pacific, far beyond the region where anyone could feel the shaking. They would reach Hawaii after about fifteen hours, and Japan after about twenty-two.

The people of Hilo, on the island of Hawaii, had experienced something like this before. In 1946, another earthquake, this time in the Aleutian Islands, sent a tsunami into their bay. The bridge across the Wailuku River was destroyed. Parts of the town were swept away. A coastal warning system was installed, the waterfront road was raised and some of the shore was set aside as a buffer.

In 1960 there was time to use those preparations. At 6:47 in the evening an official warning was issued. The forecast was that the tsunami would arrive around midnight. The sirens in Hilo began sounding at about half past eight, and continued intermittently for roughly twenty minutes.

At her family's home on low ground, sixteen-year-old Carol Brown heard the sirens. Her parents took valuable possessions to a relative's house in Papaikou, a few miles outside Hilo. Carol and her brother Ernest left to check on a niece who was babysitting outside town.

That evening, people in Hilo were making decisions based on different interpretations of the same alarm. A later study found that only about a third of residents understood the sirens as an instruction to evacuate without waiting for another signal. Many expected a second warning. Some earlier tsunami alerts had ended without much damage, and residents had to decide how seriously to take this one.

Now another group was driving toward town. Jerry Eaton, a seismologist from the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, and four companions had packed notebooks, measuring tapes, flashlights and cameras into a station wagon. They were going to measure the water, because the size of earlier tsunamis from the Aleutians was much better documented in Hawaii than the size of waves arriving from Chile.

Their destination was the new Wailuku River Bridge, built where the earlier bridge had been destroyed. They checked their plans with police and chose a route from the bridge to higher ground. Then they began recording the level of the water below them.

Around midnight the first rise came. The river was about four feet above its normal level.

For someone waiting away from the waterfront, news that the waves had arrived could sound like the end of the emergency. In Carol's later account, the radio reported waves of only seven feet. She and Ernest headed back to town. On the way they met a police officer who, she recalled, told them it was safe. They stopped at the house of another sister, in the low part of Hilo.

That report and the reading from the bridge were not necessarily measuring the same thing in the same place. But both described water much lower than the height that the largest wave would eventually reach. And while people were coming back, the scientists were seeing the next part of the tsunami.

At 12:46 in the morning, water rose under the bridge again. This time it was nine feet above normal. Then the level began to fall. Near one o'clock it was seven feet below normal.

To understand why that matters, imagine a wave passing through a bay. The high part raises the sea and the low part draws it down. In an ordinary day at the beach, those changes happen in seconds. In a tsunami, the movement can persist for much longer and carry water far beyond the usual shoreline. Water can be moving back toward the ocean even while another surge is approaching.

Eaton remembered hearing a rumble from the dark bay, like a train somewhere in the distance. Then the men could see a pale line. The front of the wave steepened as it approached.

They left the bridge and ran for the high ground they had picked out earlier. When they turned around, they saw a face of water roughly twenty feet high strike the bridge. The first wave had not even come close to preparing them for what they were now looking at.

At the sister's house, Carol and her family heard the approaching noise, followed by the sound of things breaking. The wave reached the house and lifted it away from its foundation. A building that had been standing on solid ground was now moving with the flood.

When the house came to rest, the city lights had gone out. The wave had struck the power plant. Carol and her family escaped without serious injury.

Elsewhere in the Waiakea district, Fusayo Ito was knocked unconscious when the tsunami reached her home. When she woke up, she was in the dark, out in the water. A screen door torn from its frame was keeping her afloat. Hours went by before the Coast Guard rescued her. Decades later, Ito was one of the survivors honored at an anniversary event in Hilo.

The city lost sixty-one people. The state records the destruction of 229 homes and 308 businesses or public buildings. But Hilo's destruction was striking even compared with other shores on the same island. There, observed heights were generally between three and seventeen feet. In Hilo Bay the third wave rose about thirty-five feet above sea level.

Even inside Hilo, the height depended on where it was observed. The twenty-foot front seen at the bridge, the measurements of the water level there and the maximum elevation reached elsewhere in the bay describe different parts of the same event. Combining them into one exact number would conceal what made this tsunami difficult to predict.

Eaton and his companions went back to collect further readings after the largest wave. The recorded series extended for more than two hours. It preserved the rise, the fall and the much greater surge that followed. Anyone reading that record afterward could see that midnight was the beginning of the danger, not its end.

The same waves continued on toward Japan. In Onagawa, a fireman on watch at the harbor noticed unusual movement of the water and raised the alarm. People gathered on a hillside. Photographs show them watching a large wave enter the town, and still gathered above the water almost three hours later when another wave arrived. Buildings flooded, but no one in the town died. The accounts suggest that remaining on high ground helped make the difference.

Hilo faced another decision after the water receded: what to put back on the shore. The city expanded its coastal buffer through Project Kaikoo. Instead of filling the whole low-lying area with buildings again, planners created a wider greenbelt with gardens, lagoons and recreation space. The Wailoa River State Recreation Area was completed in 1965. Local officials revised the warning and evacuation system and renewed public education. Across the Pacific, the scale of the 1960 event helped lead to an international tsunami warning system in 1965.

Among Hilo's wreckage was the town clock. It was restored, but its hands were left at four minutes past one. The time of the largest wave was kept visible for the people who would return to the waterfront in the years ahead.

Today, official safety guidance still makes the distinction that mattered that night: a tsunami can keep coming after the first wave, and the intervals between waves do not mean it has ended. Leave the hazard zone when warned and remain outside it until authorities officially say it is safe.

Hilo had fifteen hours of warning. The clock shows why the decision about when to come back was just as important as the decision to leave.
