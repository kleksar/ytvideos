A laboratory slide held a clue to the Philadelphia outbreak of 1976. The scientist who had examined the material had dismissed the bacteria as contamination.

That summer, American military veterans gathered at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel. After the convention, people began falling ill with pneumonia. Twenty-nine of the 182 people identified in the main outbreak died.

Investigators questioned the guests about everything from their meals to the cigarettes they smoked. One pattern stood out: people who had spent longer in the hotel lobby were more likely to become ill. Yet some patients said they had never entered the hotel at all.

The laboratory tests failed to identify a familiar cause. The hotel closed in November, with the mystery still unsolved.

Near the end of the year, microbiologist Joseph McDade returned to material he had examined months earlier. Under the microscope, he found cells filled with bacteria.

This time he took them seriously. His colleagues compared the bacterium with blood samples from patients. One colleague even supplied samples without telling him which belonged to the outbreak.

The patients' immune responses pointed to the same organism. In January, the CDC announced the discovery. The bacterium would be named Legionella pneumophila.

Stored blood samples from an earlier pneumonia outbreak also reacted to it. The disease had existed long before it acquired its name.

Investigators never established the exact environmental source of the Philadelphia outbreak, but they had finally identified the organism responsible. Other outbreaks could now be recognized and investigated.

Where had the breakthrough been hiding while the investigators were searching for a cause?
