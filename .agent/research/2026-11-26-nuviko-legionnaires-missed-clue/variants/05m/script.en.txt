How could a hotel convention end in a wave of pneumonia that baffled America's disease detectives? Why did the first promising clue go unrecognized? And what did the investigators still not know when they finally announced the discovery?

In July 1976, members of the American Legion, an organization of military veterans, gathered in Philadelphia. The Bellevue-Stratford Hotel was the center of their convention. People came to attend meetings and see friends. When the gathering ended, they traveled home.

Then doctors in different places began treating veterans with fever and serious breathing problems. The cases were linked to the convention. Eventually, investigators counted 182 people in the main outbreak, 29 of whom died.

The obvious question was what these people had shared. Investigators asked about meals, drinks, cigarettes and even the souvenirs handed out at the convention. If everyone who fell ill had eaten one particular dish, for instance, the search could concentrate on that meal.

But the interviews pointed in a different direction. The longer delegates had spent in the hotel's lobby, the more likely they were to become ill. Meanwhile, other people developed a similar pneumonia even though they said they had not entered the building. They had been near it on Broad Street.

The investigators could narrow down the area where the illness might have been acquired, but they couldn't explain what had made people sick. The usual laboratory investigations failed to give a clear answer. No known virus or ordinary bacterial explanation accounted for the outbreak.

The consequences continued long after the convention. Visitors stayed away from the Bellevue-Stratford. By November the hotel had closed, leaving hundreds of workers without their jobs.

In Atlanta, microbiologist Joseph McDade had been examining material from the investigation while looking for an organism associated with another disease. He had encountered a small amount of bacteria, but treated it as contamination. It seemed to be something that had found its way into the sample rather than the explanation for the epidemic.

Months later, near the end of the year, McDade went back over his earlier work. He inspected the old microscope material again and found cells packed with bacteria. This was something he had to investigate rather than dismiss.

Finding bacteria was only the beginning. What if they really were an accidental contaminant, as he had originally thought? Their presence in a laboratory sample did not, by itself, prove they had made the veterans ill.

The researchers needed another line of evidence. They looked at the patients' immune responses. When the body encounters an infectious organism, it can produce antibodies that recognize it. If this particular bacterium had been responsible for the outbreak, samples from the patients might show that their immune systems had encountered it.

McDade's colleague Gary Noble sent him a group of blood samples without revealing which came from the Philadelphia patients. McDade later recalled that the samples belonging to the outbreak were the ones that reacted. This was a connection to the actual patients, not just another observation under a microscope.

The public announcement came on January 18, 1977. Among 33 convention delegates selected for testing because they had experienced a typical severe illness and survived, 29 showed evidence consistent with infection by the newly identified bacterium.

It was eventually called Legionella pneumophila. Its discovery also allowed the researchers to revisit stored blood from an unexplained outbreak of pneumonia at a psychiatric hospital in Washington, D.C., years earlier. Many of those samples showed evidence of the same infection. The Philadelphia convention had revealed an illness that was not actually new.

But one question remained. Although the pattern of cases suggested that the organism had been carried through the air, the investigators could not identify its exact environmental source. Later outbreaks established the importance of contaminated water systems, including cooling towers. That knowledge did not retrospectively prove which part of the Philadelphia hotel, or its surroundings, had released the bacteria.

The distinction mattered. The researchers had solved the mystery of what had caused the disease, not every detail of how the convention guests had encountered it.

The Bellevue-Stratford had lost its guests and its workers had lost their jobs. No discovery could undo the deaths. What it could do was change the way the next outbreak was investigated. Doctors and public health officials now knew there was another bacterium to look for, and that an apparent mystery in an old medical record might have an explanation.
