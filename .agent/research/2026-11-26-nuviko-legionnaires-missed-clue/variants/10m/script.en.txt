What happened to the military veterans who went home from a Philadelphia convention in the summer of 1976? How did a bacterium that had been dismissed in a laboratory become the answer to a national medical mystery? And why, even after the discovery, was it impossible to say exactly where the people in the convention had acquired their infection?

Philadelphia was hosting events marking America's bicentennial. In July, members of the American Legion, an organization for military veterans, arrived for their state convention. The Bellevue-Stratford Hotel on Broad Street was a focus of the gathering. Over several days, participants attended meetings, spent time at the hotel and then returned home.

In the days that followed, physicians began dealing with a disturbing pattern. People who had been at the same convention were now being treated for a serious respiratory illness in different communities. Some had pneumonia severe enough to require hospitalization. The investigation eventually identified 182 cases in the main outbreak and 29 deaths.

It was a puzzle that could not be solved by looking at one patient in isolation. What distinguished these patients from other people with pneumonia was the link to the same gathering. Investigators had to work backwards from that connection to find the exposure that the sick people had in common.

They began with interviews. Think about the sheer variety of things a visitor might have done during a convention. A delegate could have stayed at the central hotel or somewhere else. He might have gone to a restaurant with friends, accepted a souvenir, stopped in the lobby, smoked a cigarette or spent time outside. Any one of those activities could have been more common among the sick delegates than among those who remained well.

Investigators did not simply collect accounts from the sick. They compared them with delegates who had attended but had not fallen ill. Without that comparison, it would be easy to mistake a normal feature of the convention for the cause of the disease. If everyone was given the same souvenir, the fact that sick people also received it would tell the investigators very little.

One association was particularly useful. The risk of illness rose with the amount of time a delegate had spent in the Bellevue-Stratford lobby. That made something about the shared environment more plausible than a hazard restricted to one bedroom. Investigators considered the possibility that the agent had traveled through the air.

Yet the hotel doors complicated the story. There were also cases of similar pneumonia among people who had been near the hotel on Broad Street but said they had not gone inside. The investigators treated these as a separate group of cases. It was possible that the two groups had encountered the same thing, but the location alone could not establish that.

There were other associations, too. Later interviews found that cigarette smoking was more common among the hospitalized delegates than among the matched delegates who had remained well. But investigators had not found a single cigarette brand that accounted for the cases. The finding was not sufficient to turn cigarettes into the explanation. It might instead reflect a difference in susceptibility to the illness.

The interviews could suggest where to look. The laboratory had to identify what to look for.

That was proving unusually difficult. The appearance of the patients' lungs had suggested the possibility of a virus. Yet searches for viruses did not uncover a convincing cause. Nor did the conventional bacterial examinations consistently reveal an organism responsible for the disease.

This left investigators with an epidemic that was unmistakably real but for which they could not produce the kind of laboratory answer they normally expected.

It also left the hotel's employees in an impossible situation. The business depended on visitors feeling safe enough to stay there. In September, employees marched in support of the hotel. The attendance at the convention had long since ended, but the consequences for the hotel continued. With business suffering, it closed on November 18. Hundreds of jobs disappeared.

Meanwhile in Atlanta, a microbiologist named Joseph McDade had been looking at specimens associated with the outbreak. His expertise was in a group of infectious organisms that included the cause of Q fever. He was searching for an explanation that fitted that field of work.

He had found a few bacteria in the course of examining the material, but they were not what he had been looking for. He treated them as a contaminant. It was a judgment about whether what he saw belonged to the disease or had entered the material accidentally.

In a later account of his life, McDade recalled that he went over his work again near the end of 1976. This time, when he examined the earlier material, he saw cells containing large numbers of bacteria.

He took the observation seriously. But now there was a second puzzle. If an unusual bacterium appeared in material from a fatal case, it did not necessarily follow that it had been the primary cause of the pneumonia. An unrelated organism could also be present. A persuasive answer had to connect it with many patients, not just one sample.

Fortunately, the outbreak investigators had already assembled material that could help establish that connection. The patients had been through the illness, and their immune systems could have left evidence of the organism they had encountered.

A person's blood can contain antibodies that recognize a specific infectious agent. A rising antibody response during recovery can be evidence that the person has recently encountered it. Researchers could look for that relationship without relying entirely on what one microscope slide appeared to show.

McDade tested blood samples from people affected by the outbreak. A colleague, Gary Noble, then supplied a set of samples without revealing which ones came from the Philadelphia patients. According to McDade's later recollection, the Philadelphia samples reacted with the bacterium. The result was important because he had not been told which answers to expect.

The evidence accumulated. On January 18, 1977, the CDC published the first public account of the finding. The report described results from 33 selected convention delegates who had survived a typical severe illness. In 29 of those cases, the immune response was consistent with infection by the bacterium. In many, the antibody response had increased as the patient recovered.

This was not a survey of every hotel guest, and it did not mean that 29 out of every 33 visitors had become infected. The group was deliberately composed of patients who had already been ill. The question being tested was whether their illness had a common microbial cause.

The findings were also compared with samples from people unrelated to the outbreak. Those results helped distinguish the strong responses in the affected group from background reactions that could otherwise have muddled the conclusion.

And the scientists had a chance to revisit the cases from outside the hotel. Blood from four of those patients had been examined by the time of the announcement. Two showed a substantial change in their antibody response to the organism. The hotel visitors and at least some of the people nearby appeared to have experienced the same infection.

The answer was becoming clear. An unfamiliar bacterium, later named Legionella pneumophila, was responsible for the outbreak. Ordinary laboratory tests had failed to reveal it reliably. The original failure was not evidence that the illness had no bacterial cause.

Then the discovery reached backwards in time.

Years before the Philadelphia convention, a psychiatric hospital in Washington, D.C., had experienced a mysterious outbreak of pneumonia. Blood samples from that investigation had been preserved. Researchers were able to examine them using the evidence obtained in 1977. Thirteen of the fourteen patients whose earlier and later samples were available showed clear rises in antibodies.

What had looked like two separate medical mysteries, separated by years, could now be understood as outbreaks caused by the same kind of organism. The bacterium had not suddenly come into existence at the veterans' convention.

Subsequent investigations established that Legionella can inhabit water systems and that people can acquire the infection by inhaling contaminated droplets. Other outbreaks have been linked to cooling towers and other water-containing equipment. Public health investigators gained a new way of identifying and preventing infections.

But the first Philadelphia investigation did not establish exactly which environmental source had exposed the patients. Its January announcement explicitly left that question open. The association with the hotel lobby and the cases on the surrounding street narrowed down the possible exposure. They did not constitute a recovered sample from a particular piece of equipment that could be matched to the patients.

That distinction survives even now. Identifying the bacterium explains why people fell ill. Identifying a source would explain exactly how the bacteria reached those people. One result does not automatically provide the other.

The hotel had already closed by the time the first result was announced. The workers had already lost jobs, and the patients who had died could not be brought back. But future investigators would not have to begin with the same blind spot.

A patient with unexplained pneumonia could be tested for a disease that had previously gone unrecognized. A cluster of cases could be investigated with knowledge of an additional organism and its possible environmental origins. Even stored medical samples, like those from Washington, could answer questions that had been impossible to settle at the time.

What changed the direction of the Philadelphia investigation was the decision to look again at evidence that had once seemed irrelevant. The finding was then tested against evidence from the patients themselves. The researchers identified the cause of a new recognized disease, while remaining unable to name the exact source of that particular outbreak. Both parts of that answer belong to the history of the discovery.
