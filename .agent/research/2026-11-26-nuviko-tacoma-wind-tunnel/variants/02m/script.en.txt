Five days before the Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell, an engineer finished testing it in miniature.

It was 1940, in Washington State. Even before the new bridge opened, its roadway moved in waves. Workers nicknamed it Galloping Gertie.

The state tried shock absorbers and cables tied to the ground. The cables helped the outer sections, but the middle of the bridge kept moving.

Professor Bert Farquharson took the problem into a wind tunnel. On his model, he saw something worse than the familiar bounce. One edge of the road rose while the other fell. Then they switched.

He proposed changing the shape of the solid steel sides of the roadway, so the wind wouldn't hit them like a wall. On November seventh, engineers were preparing to order the work.

That same morning, the real bridge began to twist. A driver abandoned his car and crawled back toward land. Farquharson was there, photographing the movement.

The wind was feeding energy into each twist. As the road changed its angle, it changed the way the air pushed against it. The next twist grew larger. Sections of the roadway tore away and fell into the water.

Farquharson went back to his models. This time, he tested the replacement before it was built. Deep steel trusses made it harder to twist, and open grates let air pass through the roadway.

In 1950, a new bridge opened on the old concrete piers. The model had become a test of the design, not a way of trying to repair a finished bridge.

How late had that first study arrived?
