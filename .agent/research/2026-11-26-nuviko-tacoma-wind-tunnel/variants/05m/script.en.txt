Why did a bridge that had survived stronger winds collapse in a relatively ordinary windstorm? And why did the man who had seen the danger on a tiny model have to watch it happen on the real bridge?

In the late nineteen thirties, Washington State was building a new crossing of the Tacoma Narrows. The road would hang from two huge cables, with hundreds of smaller cables holding it up.

Engineer Clark Eldridge had proposed supporting the road with deep steel trusses. But another design, by the respected bridge engineer Leon Moisseiff, used much shallower solid steel girders. That would make the bridge slimmer and cheaper. It was the design that won.

Even before the bridge opened in July 1940, the deck was moving up and down. Workers gave it a nickname: Galloping Gertie. Some of them became seasick on the job.

The state installed hydraulic buffers at the towers. They made little difference. Later, cables were added to hold parts of the roadway down. That helped the side spans, but the central span was still free to move.

Meanwhile, engineering professor Bert Farquharson was building models at the University of Washington. He could put a miniature section of roadway in a wind tunnel and change the wind without having to wait for a storm at Tacoma.

In some tests, the model did something the full-size bridge wasn't known for. Instead of both edges of the road rising together, one edge rose and the other dropped. The whole road twisted along its length.

Farquharson knew that such movement, if it developed on the actual bridge, could destroy it.

He finished his studies on November second. The solid steel sides of the roadway were a likely part of the problem. He proposed either opening holes in them to let air pass or adding curved metal surfaces to redirect it.

On November sixth, Eldridge and Farquharson discussed the second option. The following morning, Eldridge was working on drawings and getting prices for steel. There was a plan to fit the first wind deflectors in about ten days.

On that morning, November seventh, the bridge began doing its usual bounce in the wind. Farquharson arrived with his camera. Then the movement changed.

The road was tilting sideways. Leonard Coatsworth, a newspaper editor driving across with his daughter's dog, had his car thrown against the curb. He climbed out and crawled back toward a tower. Officials stopped traffic.

Farquharson was on the bridge. He photographed the twisting and later tried to get the dog out of the abandoned car. The dog bit his finger, and he had to retreat without it.

Concrete and steel started coming apart. Farquharson and the photographers got off the bridge. The central span fell into the water. The dog did not survive.

Why couldn't the bridge shake itself back to normal?

Imagine the road as a long, flexible strip facing a steady wind. When it tilts, the air moves differently around it, changing the forces on it. At Tacoma, that interaction could put more energy into the movement than the bridge could dissipate. Each cycle could make the next one bigger.

That is torsional flutter. It wasn't simply a storm pushing a bridge past the strength of its steel, or a series of gusts hitting it at the right rhythm.

Farquharson went back to work. This time, he and the design team investigated the new bridge before construction. They made the structure underneath the road deeper and more resistant to twisting. They also tested open grates through which air could pass. With the grates in place, their model showed almost no twisting in the tests.

The team checked the old concrete piers and found they could support a much heavier replacement.

In October 1950, traffic returned to Tacoma Narrows. From the water, the crossing had a familiar outline. Look beneath the road, though, and the difference was unmistakable. The thin solid wall had been replaced by a deep, open steel framework.

The second bridge stood on the same foundations. What had changed was the order of the work. Before the steel roadway went over the water, its design had already faced the wind.
