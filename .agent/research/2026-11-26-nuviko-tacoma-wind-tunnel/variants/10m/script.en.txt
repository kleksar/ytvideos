What did an engineer discover in a wind tunnel just days before the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed? Why did the bridge twist itself apart when it had already survived stronger winds? And how did the same engineer help build another crossing on the foundations of the one that had fallen?

If you look at a photograph of the first Tacoma Narrows Bridge, taken from the water in 1940, its roadway appears remarkably thin. It hangs between two towers across a channel of Puget Sound in Washington State. Above the road are the great curved suspension cables. Below it there is hardly anything compared with the deep steel framework on many other bridges of the period.

That appearance was the result of a choice made before construction began.

Clark Eldridge, an engineer working for the state, had designed a crossing with steel trusses twenty-five feet deep beneath the roadway. Think of a row of connected steel triangles running under the road. The structure takes up space, but it helps resist bending and twisting.

Leon Moisseiff, a prominent engineer who had worked on major suspension bridges, offered another design. He used solid steel girders only eight feet deep. He and his colleague Frederick Lienhard argued that the main cables could provide much of the stability the structure needed against wind. The thinner design was less expensive, and it was selected.

Construction started in 1938. In May 1940, while the roadway was still being completed, workers could already feel it moving vertically. Waves traveled along the deck. Some workers chewed lemons because the motion made them seasick. It became known as Galloping Gertie.

When the bridge opened that July, the public could experience that movement too. But the state was already trying to reduce it. Four hydraulic buffers had been installed at the towers in May, with little noticeable improvement.

The state asked Professor Bert Farquharson at the University of Washington to investigate. His team built models, including an approximately fifty-four-foot-long model of the whole bridge and a smaller model representing just a section of the road. Instead of having to wait for wind to blow across the Narrows, he could study different conditions repeatedly.

The professor also watched the actual bridge and recorded how it moved at different wind speeds. Engineers added cables to restrain the outer spans and more cables to connect the middle of the roadway to the main suspension cables.

For a while, there were encouraging signs. During autumn storms the wind exceeded fifty miles per hour, and the bridge stayed standing. The outer spans moved less with the added restraints. But the long middle section was still moving, and on November first one of the restraint cables broke.

Then there was the movement Farquharson had observed in the wind tunnel.

Take a ruler in both hands. If you move both hands up and down together, that's one kind of motion. If you turn one edge up and the other down, you're twisting the ruler. The bridge model could do the second thing. Farquharson recognized that if the full-size road began moving like that, the consequences could be very different from its normal bounce.

It was an observation about a possible failure, not a prediction of the date it would happen.

On November second, he completed the studies. The solid faces of the roadway's girders were an important suspect. He suggested making openings through them, or fitting curved metal surfaces to guide the wind around them instead.

On November sixth, he met Eldridge and another engineer to discuss fitting deflectors to the south side of the central span. The next morning Eldridge was preparing sketches and checking prices. If the work proceeded as planned, the first part would be in place in about ten days. Completing the protection on both sides would take longer.

Nobody had proved that this retrofit would save the bridge in every possible wind. But the state was preparing a real repair, and its central span was still there.

On November seventh, a cold wind was blowing across the Narrows. Early that morning the deck was producing its familiar vertical waves. Eldridge himself crossed the bridge and returned to his office. Farquharson came from Seattle to photograph the movement.

Then the road started twisting. A cable fitting near the center had slipped, an event later associated with the change in motion. The problem was no longer simply the roadway traveling up and down.

Leonard Coatsworth, a newspaper editor, was driving across to the family's summer cottage. His daughter's dog, Tubby, was in the car. The tilting road threw the vehicle against the curb. Coatsworth got out through a window and made his way back toward the nearest tower on his hands and knees. He eventually reached the toll plaza and called his office.

The bridge was closed to traffic. Photographers came to record the extraordinary movement. Farquharson stayed near a tower with his camera.

One photographer tried to get to the abandoned car but had to turn back. Farquharson also made an attempt to save Tubby. When he reached into the car, the dog bit him. He returned to the tower without it.

By then sections of concrete were breaking off. The professor and the photographers retreated. The steel connections holding the road began failing, and large sections fell into the channel. By the end, the middle span was gone. The people on the bridge got out alive. Tubby did not.

The striking thing about that footage is how much steel can move before it actually breaks. So what was the wind doing to the road?

On a playground swing, a person can push at just the right moment on every cycle, gradually increasing the height of the swing. For decades, many students were taught a similar explanation for Tacoma: wind repeatedly pushing the bridge at its natural frequency. But that picture leaves out something essential.

At Tacoma, the road itself changed the airflow as it moved. When one side tipped upward, the wind had a different path around the steel and the shape presented to the air changed. The resulting forces could reinforce the twisting, rather than bring it to a stop.

The energy came from the wind, but the timing of the forces was coupled to the motion of the bridge. In a structure flexible enough to respond this way, the movement could keep growing. Engineers call that kind of instability torsional flutter.

The bridge's narrow road and shallow girders made it particularly susceptible. Wind tunnel testing later helped explain why. This was not a case of steady wind being harmless until it reached some simple breaking strength printed on a piece of steel.

The state still needed the crossing. The first bridge had not vanished altogether: the damaged towers, main cables and side spans had to be dismantled. The workers removed thousands of tons of steel. Selling that scrap did not even cover the cost of removing it.

But the concrete piers had survived, as had the cable anchorages. If those could be reused, the new crossing did not have to start completely from scratch. The new design was going to be considerably heavier, so the engineers checked that the piers could support it.

Farquharson and the replacement bridge team now had the opportunity to test the design before it was built. Working with chief design engineer Dexter Smith, they studied the old and proposed structures in a specially built wind tunnel laboratory.

They made a full model of the replacement at one-fiftieth scale and tested sections of the proposed roadway. They turned the airflow relative to the model, examining wind arriving from different directions. A deep open framework of steel under the road gave the deck much greater resistance to twisting without presenting the same broad solid wall to the wind.

Then they tested a further change. Instead of making the whole deck a solid surface, they added strips of open grating. Air could travel through them. With the grates in place, the model showed virtually no twisting under the test conditions.

They added bracing and hydraulic dampers at several points too. This time, each of those measures could be studied as part of the entire design rather than applied only after a completed road began to move.

The concrete piers proved suitable for the heavier bridge, and the old anchorages were adapted for the new cables. Construction of the replacement began in 1948. On October fourteenth, 1950, the new four-lane crossing opened.

Look at the old and new bridges from the same place. The middle span is the same length. The piers are in the same positions. But the slender solid steel walls of the first bridge have become a steel framework more than thirty feet deep, and the new road has openings for the air.

The professor had watched a small model display the dangerous motion before he watched the real road tear apart. Ten years later, there was a new road over the same water. This time, it had met the wind in a laboratory before the construction crews ever suspended it between the towers.
