The window that cracked on the first crewed dive to the Challenger Deep was in the only passage out of the bathyscaphe.

On January 23, 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh climbed into the steel cabin of the Trieste. They were heading for the deepest part of the ocean.

Above their heads was a huge tank of gasoline. It was lighter than seawater, so it could lift the cabin back to the surface. To go down, the men carried iron weights.

The entrance tunnel outside their cabin filled with seawater. The steel hatch kept that water away from them.

After hours of descending, there was a loud bang. The whole craft shook.

The instruments showed that they were still descending normally. They couldn't detect a loss of gasoline. So they continued.

At the bottom, almost eleven kilometres down, they eventually turned on a light behind their cabin. Through a small window in the hatch, they saw a much larger window in the entrance tunnel. It had cracked.

Water was already on both sides of that window. It wasn't the glass keeping the ocean out of their cabin.

The problem would come at the surface. To open the hatch, they would have to push the seawater out of the tunnel with compressed air. A damaged window might not hold that air.

They shortened their stay and released some of the iron. The Trieste rose for hours.

In daylight, they slowly fed air into the tunnel. Instead of a few minutes, it took about fifteen. The water level finally dropped, they opened the hatch, and both climbed out.

The deepest part of the journey was over. But to get home, they had to get through the part of their craft they couldn't leave behind. Which window had cracked?
