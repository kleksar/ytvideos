How could two men continue a dive after hearing a window crack? And why did that window become a problem when they were almost home?

In January 1960, a US Navy support crew towed a strange craft out from Guam. From a distance it looked like a small submarine with an enormous tank on top. Hanging underneath the tank was a steel sphere, just big enough for two men and their instruments.

The tank was filled with gasoline. Underwater, gasoline gives the craft lift, much as helium lifts a balloon in the air. The sphere underneath protects the people from pressure. The two parts have very different jobs.

The Trieste was bound for the Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench. Its passengers were Swiss explorer Jacques Piccard and US Navy lieutenant Don Walsh.

Getting there had already cost them equipment. Rough seas damaged instruments and ripped away a telephone they could normally use to speak to the support crew from the craft. They worked out another way to signal before the dive, checked their ballast controls, and prepared to descend.

To reach the cabin, they climbed down a passage through the top of the craft. Once they were inside the sphere, they shut the hatch. The entrance passage flooded. That was normal. Only the cabin had to stay dry.

For the journey down they carried iron ballast. When they wanted to rise, they could release some of it and let the gasoline tank lift them. No engine was going to pull the two men back up eleven kilometres.

The Trieste descended past the depths it had reached in its earlier tests. Then, somewhere around ten kilometres down, there was a bang. The craft shook.

They had no clear view of the entire outside of the vehicle. They didn't yet know what had broken. But they could watch what the craft was doing. Piccard recalled that its descent remained steady and they could detect no loss of gasoline. They listened. They decided to continue.

As the bottom came into range, they eased the descent. Soon the craft settled in sediment that rose around it, obscuring much of the view.

They had made it. The number indicated by their instruments was too high; the corrected depth was close to eleven kilometres. They shook hands, took observations and, remarkably, made contact with the support ship by sound travelling through the water.

Then they turned their attention to the bang.

A light behind the cabin illuminated the entrance tunnel. Looking through a small viewing port in the hatch, they saw the much larger transparent window in the tunnel. It had cracked.

It was a crucial distinction. The window was not part of the pressure boundary of their cabin. Both sides of it were underwater. Its damage did not mean that the cabin was about to flood.

But it was part of the route out.

The men could not simply open the hatch the moment the Trieste reached the surface. The tunnel was still full of seawater. Normally they would feed compressed air into it, pushing the water down until they could safely climb out. Now they had a cracked window in that tunnel.

Could it hold enough air to empty the passage? If not, they would need help from outside. They decided to give themselves more daylight for the recovery and cut their planned half-hour on the bottom to about twenty minutes.

They released ballast. The Trieste left the seabed and began its long ascent. As it rose, its gasoline passed through warmer water and expanded. The craft gained lift. Hours after leaving the seabed, they were back in daylight.

There was still the matter of the entrance.

Piccard directed the clearing of the tunnel while Walsh watched the pressures. They fed in air slowly. The operation usually took only a few minutes. This time it took about fifteen. At last the water level dropped below the viewing port in the hatch. They opened the way out and climbed onto the deck.

Both men returned alive. The crack had not opened a hole into their cabin, and they had not needed to replace the window at the surface.

It was another fifty-two years before a person visited the Challenger Deep again. The first two had reached a place nearly eleven kilometres beneath the ocean, and the final passage they had to clear to finish their journey was the short entrance tunnel itself.
