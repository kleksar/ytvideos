What would it take to send two people to the deepest part of the ocean with no engine to bring them back? What happened when a loud bang shook their craft close to the bottom? And why did they have to deal with the damage after they had already reached the surface?

In January 1960, the Trieste looked nothing like a conventional submarine. A long, bulky tank sits above a much smaller sphere. The two men going down to the Mariana Trench would spend almost the entire day inside that sphere.

Jacques Piccard was the son of Auguste Piccard, who had developed the bathyscaphe: a craft built for going deep rather than travelling far underwater. The US Navy bought the Trieste in 1958. A young naval officer named Don Walsh joined the project. The objective was eventually to make a dive in the Challenger Deep.

First, consider the problem the engineers had to solve. Every metre farther down puts more water above the vehicle. A large conventional submarine would need an immense, very heavy pressure hull to withstand the conditions at the bottom of the trench. A sphere can distribute the pressure around its curved surface. Make the sphere small enough to accommodate only the crew and instruments, and the amount of heavy steel needed becomes more manageable.

The rest of the craft did not need to be a habitable pressure chamber. The buoyancy tank above it held gasoline, which is less dense than seawater. The liquid in that tank could remain close to the surrounding water pressure. There was no need to maintain a large space full of air at ordinary atmospheric pressure.

But the craft still had to go down before it could come back up. It carried a load of iron ballast. Iron pellets could be released when the men needed to reduce the weight of the craft. Electromagnets held the ballast in place. That provided a means of letting it go if the electrical supply failed.

This was a machine that made the weight of the ocean part of its operating principle. It could descend slowly, take observations, then become light enough to rise.

By the time of the expedition, the original sphere had been replaced with one made for the extreme depths of the Pacific. The team had already made a succession of test dives. Those dives were necessary not just to test the strength of the craft, but to learn how it behaved as the properties of the water changed.

The deep site was hundreds of kilometres from Guam. A vessel had to tow the Trieste across the open ocean. The sea was rough. Piccard later recalled that the surface telephone and instruments for measuring movement had suffered damage in the waves.

The crew could not simply assume every system would behave as it had on previous dives. They devised a way to signal before submerging and checked the circuits that controlled the release of ballast.

On January twenty-third, Piccard and Walsh went down the entrance passage into the sphere. They shut the hatch above them. Water flooded the passage through which they had just climbed. It was supposed to. The men were now enclosed in a much smaller dry space, separated from the sea by the steel sphere, its ports and its hatch.

What they were about to discover was that the entrance passage, which mattered very little to their survival at depth as long as the cabin was sealed, could become quite important again at the very end of the dive.

The craft began to sink. For a while, they could exchange messages with their support ship using sound transmitted through the water. But as they went deeper, communications became unreliable.

The descent was not simply a matter of dropping a weight into the ocean. The conditions changed as the Trieste moved from one layer of water into another. According to Walsh, temperature changes slowed its descent considerably at points. They had to manage buoyancy to keep going.

For hours the two men watched their instruments. They eventually passed the depths they had previously reached. By then, if they wanted to return to the surface, it would be a long journey even if everything worked as intended.

Somewhere around ten kilometres below the surface, the Trieste shook with a loud bang.

Piccard and Walsh later gave somewhat different readings for the depth at that moment. They agreed on the important part: it was a very deep stage of the dive, and neither knew immediately what had happened.

They had instruments to show whether the craft was still behaving normally. They could check whether they were continuing to descend. They could look for a change that might indicate a loss of buoyancy. Piccard recalled that they detected no loss of gasoline and that they continued descending steadily. They stopped some of the machinery to listen.

They did not know that nothing had been damaged. They knew that they had not detected a serious change in the behaviour of the craft. On that evidence, they decided to proceed.

Eventually their instruments showed the bottom drawing nearer. They could now slow the descent by letting go of iron ballast. The Trieste touched the bottom and disturbed fine sediment. A cloud rose around it.

Walsh later recalled that the depth reading at first suggested an even greater depth than the one ultimately calculated. After correction, it was close to eleven kilometres. The reading alone was not the final measurement.

They had achieved something no other people had done. And their surroundings were not particularly easy to see. The disturbed sediment made it difficult to obtain an exterior view or photographs of the bottom.

They shook hands. They took scientific observations, including the temperature. They also discovered that the acoustic connection to the surface could work again. A message reached the support ship through nearly eleven kilometres of seawater.

Then came a task that was not part of the original plan. They needed to see what might have broken.

They turned on a light at the rear of the cabin. There was a small viewing port in the hatch through which they could look back into the passage. Outside it was a much larger clear window in the entrance tunnel. That window had a crack.

They had not been separated from the sea by that larger piece of transparent material. The tunnel had flooded before the dive began. Water was pressing on both sides of the damaged window. The small hatch port and the pressure sphere were what mattered to the dry cabin.

Piccard thought that the difference in the contraction of the materials in the cold might have contributed to the crack. That was his explanation, not a confirmed forensic result. The more immediate question was what to do about the damaged window at the end of the dive.

To understand that problem, picture the Trieste floating on the surface again. Its sphere would still be underwater. The men would need to come out through the same tunnel they had used to enter. That meant clearing the seawater first. The normal operation involved admitting compressed air and pushing the water down out of the passage.

If the cracked window leaked too much air or failed, the men could remain inside a perfectly intact cabin even with the craft floating at the surface. They might need divers or other assistance to make a usable exit.

There was another practical concern. Doing that work would be easier in daylight. Piccard and Walsh reduced the planned thirty minutes on the bottom to approximately twenty.

They released ballast and began to rise. The sediment cloud was left behind. As the craft passed into warmer water, the gasoline expanded, increasing its buoyancy. Later the conditions changed again. The ascent took hours.

When they surfaced, daylight was still available. The crack was visible. They did not have to replace the damaged window, but they proceeded carefully with the air. Piccard directed the operation; Walsh read the pressure indications. Normally the tunnel could be cleared in two or three minutes. This time it took about fifteen.

Finally the water dropped below the small window in the hatch. The men opened the passage and came out onto the deck. There were aircraft and a support crew outside. Nearly nine hours after entering the water, they were back.

The Trieste's dive did not turn the deepest part of the ocean into an easy destination. The next person to visit the Challenger Deep, James Cameron, did so in 2012, fifty-two years later. He was able to spend far longer on the bottom.

For the first two visitors, the cracked window illustrated the difference between a craft that can survive at the bottom of the ocean and one that can also bring its passengers all the way home. They had reached the bottom in a steel sphere beneath a tank of gasoline. They completed the return with compressed air, clearing the last short passage between their cabin and the open sky.
