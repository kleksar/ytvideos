How did two spacecraft survive the surface of Venus without taking a single picture? And why did solving that problem ruin a different experiment?

In December 1978, the Soviet landers Venera 11 and 12 reached the ground. Down there, the temperature is hot enough to melt lead, and the atmospheric pressure is around ninety times Earth's. Yet both spacecraft kept sending scientific readings for more than an hour.

The protective covers over their cameras wouldn't come off. The instruments that were supposed to analyse the ground also failed.

Four years later, Venera 13 landed with a revised design. This time the covers came off. The lander sent back colour panoramas of slabs of rock surrounded by dark soil.

Its twin, Venera 14, landed on March fifth, 1982. Its cameras worked too. But one discarded cover had landed in the path of an instrument designed to test the ground.

The arm struck the cover. Scientists could no longer separate the resistance of the rock from that of the spacecraft part.

Fortunately, a different instrument was taking a sample. The drill reached its planned depth of three centimetres. Analysis of that sample revealed rock chemically similar to basalt on Earth's ocean floor. Data from the landing itself also suggested that the rock was porous.

The cover had spoiled one experiment, not the mission. The pictures showed the object that interfered with the test, and the other instruments still told scientists something about the planet beneath it.

The spacecraft had overcome Venus's heat and pressure. One of the things it couldn't overcome was the position of its own discarded camera cover.
