Why did two spacecraft land on Venus and send back no photographs? How could a camera cover ruin a test even after the camera was working? And what did the spacecraft discover despite the mistake?

The Soviet Venera programme had already photographed Venus in 1975. Venera 9 transmitted a black-and-white image of angular stones lying in dusty ground. The next challenge was to get a much better look at that landscape and find out what the rocks were made of.

Three years later, two new landers approached Venus. They separated from their carrier spacecraft and descended through the atmosphere, while the carriers passed the planet and relayed their radio signals to Earth.

Venera 12 touched down on December twenty-first, 1978. Venera 11 followed four days later. The first sent readings from the surface for a hundred and ten minutes, the second for ninety-five.

Neither sent a photograph.

The camera covers had failed to release on both landers. The equipment for analysing the ground didn't work either. The two missions still returned useful atmospheric data, but a camera that had survived the journey was looking at the inside of its own cover.

It was a particularly frustrating kind of failure to solve. Venus is surrounded by a dense atmosphere. At the surface, the pressure is comparable to that found nearly a kilometre underwater on Earth. The temperature is around four hundred and sixty degrees Celsius. A window has to withstand that environment and the camera must be able to see through it once the spacecraft lands.

The next pair of landers, Venera 13 and 14, carried revised equipment. Their designers tested the new cover-release system on Earth under heat and simulated landing conditions. Then the two spacecraft flew to Venus.

On March first, 1982, Venera 13 reached the ground. The covers released. Cameras scanned the landscape, using different colour filters to build up panoramas.

There was no need to imagine what the terrain looked like any more. Pale slabs of rock lay among patches of dark material. Part of the spacecraft was visible at the bottom of the image. So was one of the covers, lying on the ground where it could no longer block the camera.

Venera 13 transmitted for two hours and seven minutes.

Four days later, Venera 14 landed at another site. Its photographs showed a flatter expanse of fractured rock, with much less loose material between the slabs.

But there was a problem with the experiment taking place just outside its landing ring.

A mechanical arm carried a device called a penetrometer. It was meant to press into the surface so that scientists could work out how much force the ground could bear. Venera 13 had already made this kind of measurement successfully.

On Venera 14, the device struck a discarded camera cover.

That didn't necessarily mean the instrument had touched nothing but the cover. The object was resting on the rock. The trouble was that the measured response could include both materials, and the scientists couldn't reliably separate them. A valuable result about the strength of the ground had been lost.

But the penetrometer wasn't the drill.

The sampling equipment reached three centimetres into the surface. The sample was examined inside the lander, and the chemical results went back to Earth. Venera 14 had encountered a basalt-like material with a composition similar to rocks found on Earth's ocean floor. Its twin had encountered another kind of basalt, richer in potassium.

There was also evidence from the way the landers slowed when they struck the ground, and from the resistance encountered by the drills. Those readings pointed towards relatively weak, porous rock.

Venera 14 sent signals from the surface for roughly an hour. It had photographed the planet, collected a sample and measured its composition.

A piece of discarded hardware had made one experiment impossible to interpret. But the photographs and the independent measurements survived. And that is why the famous accident with the camera cover is only half the story of Venera 14.
