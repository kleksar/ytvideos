How did the Soviet Union get colour pictures from a planet where lead can melt? Why did two earlier spacecraft survive the landing but fail to take a single picture? And what could scientists actually learn when a later spacecraft's instrument hit its own discarded camera cover?

Venus is often compared with Earth because the two planets are similar in size. For anyone trying to land there, the resemblance ends well above the surface.

Clouds hide the landscape from ordinary visible-light photographs taken from space. Beneath them is a thick atmosphere, mostly carbon dioxide. At ground level, the atmospheric pressure is around ninety times the pressure at sea level on Earth. The temperature is hotter than the melting point of lead.

A spacecraft has to arrive at the surface with functioning instruments. Then it has to use them before the conditions outside overcome its protection, or it loses the radio connection to the spacecraft carrying its signal back to Earth.

In October 1975, Venera 9 reached Venus and worked on the surface for fifty-three minutes. One of its pictures showed angular stones, many partly buried in soil. The horizon appeared near the corners of the image, with part of the lander in the foreground.

This was a major change. A scientist on Earth could look at the size and arrangement of stones on another planet rather than just at an artist's idea of what lay beneath the clouds. But a photograph alone could not tell whether those rocks were strong or fragile, or what elements they contained.

The next two Soviet landers, Venera 11 and 12, were intended to investigate the surface further. Each travelled to Venus attached to a spacecraft that would fly past the planet. The landing craft separated, entered the atmosphere and descended, while the carrier continued along its own trajectory and relayed data to Earth.

In December 1978, both landers arrived intact. Venera 12 sent measurements from the surface for a hundred and ten minutes; Venera 11 for ninety-five.

And then there was a problem that had nothing to do with the radio or the survival of the entire spacecraft. The covers over their camera windows would not release. No pictures came from either lander. The instruments for determining the chemistry of the ground also failed.

The two spacecraft had reached the place where they were supposed to perform their experiments. They could transmit information about their surroundings. Yet for some of the most important things they had gone there to do, they could not get access to the planet immediately outside their walls.

The next missions, Venera 13 and 14, carried redesigned systems. The spacecraft builder's account describes a test programme in which descent equipment was heated to four hundred and fifty degrees Celsius, subjected to a simulated landing, and only then tested for cover release. The programme carried out seventy-eight tests of the camera-cover system.

It wasn't enough to check that the cover could be removed from a cold spacecraft standing on a workbench. It needed to come off after the equipment had endured the conditions of descent and touchdown.

The new landers launched in October and November 1981.

The descent made use of something that made the surface so difficult to explore: the density of the atmosphere. After the entry shell had slowed the craft, a parachute opened. But lower down, the parachute was discarded. The lander continued down with a rigid braking disc above the instrument body. The thick air supplied enough resistance to slow the fall. A deformable ring below the spacecraft absorbed part of the landing impact.

Venera 13 reached the surface on March first, 1982.

This time the camera covers came away. The cameras were designed to scan across the view, building up a panorama. Different colour filters provided the information needed to construct a colour image. This did not mean that every hue in the resulting picture was certain. The atmosphere filters the sunlight, particularly the blue component, and reconstructing the colour balance accurately is difficult.

Still, the shape of the terrain was there. Slabs of rock lay among darker loose material. The landing ring was visible, as well as a curved object that did not belong to the landscape. It was one of the discarded camera covers.

The spacecraft kept transmitting for a hundred and twenty-seven minutes. The colour images were an extraordinary achievement, but the camera was just one part of the scientific programme. Venera 13 also tested the strength of the ground with a device that drove a small punch into it.

The fourth of March passed on Earth. On March fifth, Venera 14 reached a different part of Venus. The twin lander used the same general approach, with instruments that could be compared against those of its predecessor.

The landscape was different. In its pictures, large, flat pieces of fractured rock occupied much of the view. There was little loose soil between them.

One of the camera covers had fallen where the arm for the ground-strength experiment would operate. The instrument, known as a penetrometer, struck the cover.

To understand why this mattered, imagine testing the hardness of a floor with a small punch. If the punch hits an object lying on the floor, the force tells you something about the object and its support together. Even if the punch ultimately touches the floor, you cannot simply use the reading as if nothing had been in the way.

That is the difficulty with Venera 14's result. It wasn't a clean measurement of the ground. A later scientific review lists the experiment as failed because the contribution of the cover and the underlying rock could not be disentangled.

However, the spacecraft had more than one way to investigate the surface.

The landing impact had itself produced useful measurements. Sensors recorded the change in the spacecraft's motion as it came to rest. On both Venera 13 and 14, the recorded forces suggested that the ground was weaker and more porous than a block of solid crystalline rock.

And there was a completely separate device that drilled into the surface to collect a sample. It was positioned inside the supporting ring, outside the camera's view.

On Venera 14 it drilled into rock, reaching its planned depth of three centimetres. The resistance during drilling provided another clue to the mechanical properties of the rock. The material behaved more like porous basalt or consolidated volcanic ash than a dense slab of solid rock.

The sample was then examined by an X-ray fluorescence instrument. Different elements emit characteristic X-rays when excited. The measured signals allow scientists to estimate which elements a sample contains and in what proportions, without bringing the rock back to a laboratory on Earth.

Both landers obtained chemical results. Venera 13 found a potassium-rich basaltic composition. Venera 14 found a different basaltic composition, closer to that of volcanic rocks that form much of Earth's oceanic crust.

That resemblance doesn't establish that Venus once had oceans or Earth's style of plate tectonics. It is a comparison of measured chemistry, not a reconstruction of the entire history of the planet.

The pictures and measurements also left a question about how the layered rocks themselves formed. They looked like slabs, but the mechanical evidence suggested that they were unusually porous. Decades later, scientists used the landing data alongside radar observations to investigate whether some of those layers could include deposits thrown out by impacts and later modified. That remains a geological interpretation, not something that the photographs alone proved.

Venera 14's transmission ended roughly an hour after landing. There was no way to move the camera cover and repeat the test. That single reading was lost.

But by the time contact ended, the lander had taken pictures, sampled rock and sent its chemical analysis back to Earth. The scientists could compare the results with those from Venera 13 and with the data recorded at touchdown.

In one sense, the new missions had solved the problem of 1978 completely: the cameras could finally see. In another, the detached part created a new problem in a place that could not be reached from Earth. The failure mattered because it deprived scientists of a particular measurement they had travelled millions of kilometres to make.

The rest of the mission mattered even more. Instead of treating one instrument as if it could answer every question about the ground, the spacecraft had arrived with several ways of examining it. So even after one struck the wrong object, the others could still tell us about the rock on Venus.
