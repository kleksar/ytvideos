A computer gave away a knight to get at the world chess champion's king.

In 1996, IBM's Deep Blue had won its very first game against Garry Kasparov. But he recovered and won the six-game match. The following year, IBM came back with a stronger machine.

This time, with one game left, the score was tied. Kasparov had the black pieces. He moved a pawn to attack a white knight. A retreat would have been perfectly ordinary. Years later, Kasparov recalled that he simply didn't believe Deep Blue would sacrifice that knight.

Instead, it jumped right into the center of his position and captured a pawn.

Kasparov moved his queen, then took the knight with another pawn. That pulled a defender away from his king. A white bishop gave check, and the king had to move. It could no longer castle to safety.

Deep Blue brought a rook into the attack. A second sacrifice opened the line to Kasparov's queen. The rook took it, and on move nineteen, Kasparov resigned.

IBM won the match by three and a half points to two and a half.

But there was a human contribution behind that first sacrifice. Deep Blue had an opening book, a collection of prepared moves. Members of the IBM team said that the knight sacrifice had been entered weeks before the game.

The machine still had to play the rest of the position. The grandmaster still had to defend it. And the difference between keeping an extra piece and keeping a king safe had decided a historic match. What had Deep Blue been willing to lose in order to open that attack?
