How did a computer beat the reigning world chess champion? Why did Garry Kasparov offer it a chance to sacrifice a valuable piece in the deciding game? And what had the people building the machine contributed to that move?

A year before the famous defeat, the champion had already faced IBM's Deep Blue. In February 1996, they met in Philadelphia for a match of six games. Deep Blue won the first. For a machine playing under ordinary tournament time limits, that was an extraordinary result. Yet the match wasn't over. Kasparov adjusted his play, won three games and drew two. He left with a four-to-two victory.

IBM had a year to improve its machine. The team returned with faster equipment and a stronger chess program. It also had help from people who understood chess at the highest level. Grandmaster Joel Benjamin was part of that effort.

In May 1997, Kasparov won the opening game of their rematch. Then came a disturbing reversal. In the second game, Deep Blue put him under pressure without rushing into a simple material gain. He resigned. Afterward, analysts found that he might have been able to save the game by giving repeated checks with his queen. The computer had made an error, but the man who expected it to see everything had given up before trying the defense.

The next three games were drawn. After five games, each side had two and a half points. One victory would decide the entire match.

Kasparov played black in the last game. He chose the Caro-Kann Defense, an opening in which black tries to build a solid position with pawns. By the seventh move, Deep Blue had a knight advanced on the right side of the board. Kasparov pushed a pawn toward it.

The ordinary response was to move away. But the computer's knight went the other way. It took a pawn near the middle of the black position, where another pawn could capture it.

There is a reason this matters even if you have never played chess. A knight is normally worth much more than a pawn. A player who gives up a knight needs something in return, and here that something was access to the king.

Kasparov later said that he had not expected the computer to make the sacrifice. He first moved his queen. Deep Blue castled, bringing its own king to safety, and only then did Kasparov take the knight.

Look at the two kings now. The white king has protection on the edge of the board. The black king has lost part of its pawn cover. A white bishop gives check, and the black king has to step away from its original square. That means it cannot castle anymore.

Kasparov tried to move his king to the other side, but Deep Blue brought its pieces into the open lines. On move seventeen, it offered a bishop as well. When a pawn captured that bishop, a white rook had a clear route to the black queen.

The rook took the queen. Kasparov captured the rook in return, but his remaining pieces were poorly placed. Deep Blue pushed one more pawn. Kasparov resigned on move nineteen.

A machine had won a match against the reigning world champion, three and a half to two and a half.

Yet the most surprising move was not necessarily a discovery made in front of Kasparov that afternoon. Deep Blue used a store of prepared opening moves. Two members of its team later said that the knight sacrifice had been in that store weeks in advance. That was legal preparation, not evidence that somebody had secretly played the move for it.

Kasparov questioned the machine's conduct during the match and wanted another chance. IBM declined. There would be no third Deep Blue match.

Much later, Kasparov described his defeat as an experience that helped him see the value of people working with computers. Look back at that board. Before the computer could calculate an attack on the king, people had already helped it find a move the champion thought it would never play.
