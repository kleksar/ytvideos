Why did the greatest chess player of his generation expect a computer to move a knight out of danger? How did that piece become the beginning of an attack that ended an entire match? And why did a victory celebrated as a triumph of machines over people depend so heavily on people?

In 1989, Garry Kasparov played two games in New York against a computer called Deep Thought. He won both. The computer's developers were working on a problem that went well beyond the game itself. Could machines examine enough possibilities and judge them well enough to compete with the best decision makers in a field where the rules were clear?

Deep Thought was the predecessor of IBM's Deep Blue. The two opponents who would meet in 1996 were very different kinds of chess players. Kasparov had years of experience facing the world's strongest grandmasters. Deep Blue was designed to look ahead through vast numbers of possible positions.

Imagine three legal moves from the same position. Each allows several replies, and those replies allow further moves. Follow even a few turns down each path and you already have a tree of possibilities. But looking further is not enough. A chess player must decide whether a position at the end of one of those paths is better than the position at the end of another.

Deep Blue combined special chess hardware with a program that evaluated positions. It could assign value to material, examine tactical sequences, and give weight to positional features. Its processors divided the search work. The people designing it could make adjustments to what the machine looked for and how it judged the board.

There was another kind of knowledge available to it. Chess players have spent generations studying openings, the early stages of a game. Instead of calculating every familiar opening from scratch, a program can consult prepared moves. That was going to matter in a way even the champion did not expect.

In February 1996, Kasparov sat down to face Deep Blue in Philadelphia. Six games were scheduled. He lost the first one, a historic result for a computer playing a reigning world champion under tournament conditions.

But a match is very different from a single game. Kasparov won the second, drew the third and fourth, and won the fifth and sixth. The computer that had made headlines by winning once had lost the match four to two.

The fifth game illustrated one of the awkward differences between playing a computer and playing a human. Kasparov offered a draw. The people managing Deep Blue turned it down. The computer could not negotiate an offer on its own in the ordinary human manner. Kasparov went on to win. The machine's search could evaluate moves, but decisions about how a match was run still involved people.

Kasparov was willing to play again. In the intervening year, the IBM team improved the hardware and the software. Grandmaster Joel Benjamin helped with chess expertise. The new system was able to examine far more positions in a given time. It was not just the old opponent returning for another six games.

In May 1997, the rematch began in New York. Kasparov won game one.

Then Deep Blue played a second game that troubled him. He was accustomed to using an opponent's tendencies in his own planning. A machine might be expected to grab material if its calculations showed that doing so was profitable. Instead, Deep Blue maintained pressure with positional play. Kasparov began to question how the machine was reaching its decisions.

Near the end, he resigned. But there was a possibility that he could have kept the game going by repeatedly checking the white king with his queen. A repeated series of checks can sometimes force a draw, even when one player looks much worse. Kasparov later explained that he had assumed the computer could not have overlooked this possibility. He did not try it.

That is an important distinction. Being confident that an opponent has calculated an answer is not the same thing as knowing that answer. A strong computer can still miss a line, and its reputation can influence the choices made by the person across the board.

Kasparov and Deep Blue drew games three, four and five. After five games, both had exactly two and a half points. The final game would settle the match if either player won.

Kasparov took the black pieces on May eleventh. He selected the Caro-Kann, a defense that was not one of his usual choices. The early pawn exchanges gave Deep Blue's white knight room to advance. By move seven, that knight stood on g5, near the black king's side of the board.

Kasparov pushed the pawn on the edge of the board one square, attacking the knight. It could retreat toward the middle of the white position. Instead, it leapt to e6 and took a pawn.

To see why that was so surprising, look at what the black pieces could do. Black had a pawn on f7 that could take the knight. The knight was worth considerably more than the pawn it had captured. White was deliberately making a valuable piece available to the opponent.

But that black pawn had another job. It was part of the barrier in front of the king. If it moved away, lines and diagonals that had been obstructed could become available to white's attacking pieces.

Kasparov later recalled that he had expected a retreat rather than this sacrifice. The opening move he had relied upon had produced precisely the situation he had not expected to defend.

It would be misleading to say that the pawn push alone made defeat unavoidable. Black still had defensive options. Taking the knight at once would have led to a different game. Instead, Kasparov moved his queen to e7.

White now made a quiet but crucial move. Deep Blue castled. That moved the king toward the corner and brought a rook closer to the center. Kasparov then captured the knight with the pawn from f7.

A white bishop arrived on g6 and gave check. Black's king moved to d8. A king that has already moved cannot castle, so the convenient route to safety was gone. Notice how a sacrifice that had apparently cost the computer material had altered the geography of the whole board.

Deep Blue developed its other bishop. Kasparov advanced pawns on the queen's side and tried to organize his remaining pieces. A white rook occupied the e-file, facing the black pawn. Kasparov's king eventually moved to c8, but its new position did not end the attack.

White opened another line by exchanging pawns on the left. Its queen came toward the center. Black's bishop moved to c6. Both sides had moved several pieces since the original sacrifice; the computer could not win simply by repeating a line from the opening.

Then came the decisive tactical sequence. Deep Blue moved its bishop to f5. Kasparov took it with a pawn. That pawn had been blocking the white rook's route up the e-file. Now the rook could travel straight to e7 and take Kasparov's queen.

Kasparov captured the rook with a bishop, but white had traded a rook for the queen after clearing the line. Deep Blue made one final pawn move, c4. Black's position was badly compromised. The champion resigned without waiting for checkmate.

The score was three and a half points to two and a half. A machine had beaten the reigning world chess champion in a match.

What about that first knight sacrifice? Years later, the accounts of two members of the IBM team placed the move in Deep Blue's opening book weeks before the game. A book move is part of ordinary chess preparation. It need not be invented by the computer at the board. It also is not evidence of an operator secretly choosing a move during a game.

Kasparov had concerns about the conduct of the match, and IBM denied improper human intervention. His request for another match was declined. There is no third encounter to settle the arguments by a further contest.

Decades later, Kasparov spoke about the defeat in a different context: what humans could do in collaboration with computers rather than in competition with them. The chessboard already contained an example. The engineers built a machine that could examine and judge possibilities. Chess experts gave it prepared knowledge. And an outstanding human opponent found himself defending against the combined result.

In the decisive game, the knight disappeared early. The position it left behind was what won the match.
