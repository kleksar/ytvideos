How can a contestant win a million pounds, hold the cheque in his hands, and never receive the money? And why did a television quiz end up in a courtroom where even the jury's coughing became a problem?

In September 2001, Charles Ingram appeared on the British version of Who Wants to Be a Millionaire. The host, Chris Tarrant, sat across from him. Each question offered four answers. Ingram could take the money he had earned and leave, or risk playing for the next amount.

His first night didn't look like the beginning of a jackpot. He used two of his three lifelines, and when filming ended he had reached four thousand pounds. He still had a long way to go.

When he returned, Ingram made some unusual choices. One question asked who had recorded an album called Born to Do It. He talked about the possibilities and said he didn't know the singer Craig David. Then he picked Craig David. He was right.

At thirty-two thousand pounds, he had reached a guaranteed prize level. He kept going.

While Tarrant listened to the man in front of him, members of the production team noticed something happening elsewhere in the studio. There was coughing, and it seemed to line up with correct answers.

One of the people waiting to compete was a college lecturer named Tecwen Whittock. He would become central to the investigation.

Ingram reached the last question. What name is given to the number written as a one with a hundred zeroes after it? The possibilities included a nanomole, a gigabit and a googol. Ingram eventually settled on googol, even though he had said he didn't know the term.

Correct again. Tarrant presented him with a cheque for a million pounds.

That piece of paper mattered. The game had been filmed on September tenth, but the cheque was dated September eighteenth, when the episode was scheduled to go out. The production company had time to investigate before the payment was due.

Its managing director, Paul Smith, came to the studio after hearing about the suspicions. The next day, executives decided to go to the police. The prize payment was stopped. The episode was removed from the schedule.

Now the recording had a different audience. A jury watched the footage in 2003. Prosecutors said the coughing was part of a conspiracy involving Ingram, his wife Diana and Whittock. They singled out nineteen coughs as deliberate signals. An audio expert counted a much larger number of coughs in the studio: a hundred and ninety-two. That total was not a hundred and ninety-two correct clues.

Whittock said the timing was coincidental and explained his coughing by pointing to his allergies. Ingram told police that he had deliberately considered alternative answers while making up his mind. Tarrant testified that he had not noticed the coughing while hosting. When he watched the recording afterwards, he said he could hear a pattern.

At one point, coughing among members of the jury actually interrupted the trial.

On April seventh, 2003, all three defendants were found guilty. The Ingrams received suspended prison sentences of eighteen months; Whittock received a suspended sentence of twelve months. They also faced financial penalties.

The story did not stop there. Charles and Diana maintained that they had not cheated. In 2020, their lawyer announced plans to challenge the convictions and raised questions about the handling of the audio recordings. Those objections are not the same as a court overturning the verdict.

Meanwhile, the footage that had been kept off television in 2001 had found a huge audience after the trial. A documentary about the case was shown in April 2003. Millions watched the very game that its producers had stopped from being broadcast as an ordinary episode.

For the audience, Ingram's answers were the spectacle. For the court, the question was how he had arrived at them. The jury accepted the prosecution's case, the payment remained stopped, and the contestant who had held a million-pound cheque never received the jackpot.
