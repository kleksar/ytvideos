What happens when a game show gives someone a million pounds, then decides that the contestant never earned it? How do you prove that a noise in the background was a deliberate signal? And why would millions of viewers eventually tune in to watch a game that its makers had taken off the air?

The British version of Who Wants to Be a Millionaire began in 1998. Its central idea was wonderfully simple. One person sits opposite the host. There are four possible answers to each question, and the prizes increase as the contestant gets them right.

The contestant has something to lose. Beyond certain guaranteed amounts, an incorrect answer can send the prize tumbling. At any question, there is also the option of stopping and keeping the money already earned. The host is there to ask questions and confirm the answer, not to tell the player which one to choose.

By September 2001, a British army major named Charles Ingram had made it into that chair. The game was already familiar to his family. His wife, Diana, had previously won thirty-two thousand pounds on the show.

Ingram's own appearance started on September ninth. He used two of his three lifelines and had reached only four thousand pounds by the end of the session. The game would resume the next day, with one lifeline left.

This is where the familiar television game started turning into something different. On his return, Ingram often discussed more than one possible answer at length. Sometimes, having appeared to favour one answer, he chose another.

One question concerned the album Born to Do It. Ingram said he hadn't heard of the singer Craig David. He eventually chose Craig David, and the answer took him to thirty-two thousand pounds.

You can see why that particular moment later attracted so much attention. A person can get an answer right through elimination or a lucky guess. A person can also know something without initially recognising that they know it. But when a player describes an answer as unfamiliar and then suddenly picks it for a large sum of money, the change itself becomes interesting to watch.

At thirty-two thousand pounds, Ingram had a guaranteed amount. He continued towards the top of the money ladder.

There were other contestants in the studio waiting for their chance to play. Among them was Tecwen Whittock, a lecturer from Wales. Diana Ingram was there as well.

During the recording, members of the production team became concerned about the amount of coughing and its apparent connection to the answers. A person watching a quiz normally listens to the question, the contestant and the host. But these staff members had a reason to pay attention to noises that had nothing to do with the words on the screen.

Ingram continued. Tarrant later described in court how difficult he had found it to follow the contestant's way of making decisions.

Eventually there was only one question left. The host asked for the name of an enormous number: a one followed by a hundred zeroes. The answers included googol, megatron, gigabit and nanomole.

For anyone playing along at home, the correct choice is googol. The other familiar-looking words don't name that number. But someone sitting in the studio with a million pounds at stake has to decide whether to trust what they think they know.

Ingram considered the alternatives. He had said he was unfamiliar with googol, but it became his final choice. He got the answer right, and Tarrant presented him with a million-pound cheque.

The important part of that moment is what happened to the cheque afterwards. The broadcast was scheduled for September eighteenth. The recording had taken place on the tenth. The cheque was postdated for the broadcast date.

Paul Smith, the managing director of Celador, the company behind the programme, had been working on another show when the suspicions reached him. He went to the Millionaire studio to look at the recordings. Executives met the following day and decided to contact the police.

The company stopped the cheque and took the episode off the schedule. The audience that normally would have watched the game at home did not get to see it that week.

The matter eventually reached Southwark Crown Court. It was no longer enough to say that Ingram's choices looked strange. The prosecution had to make a case that the cheque had been obtained through deception.

The recording became central evidence. Prosecutors alleged that Whittock's coughing was intentional and that it was being used to give Ingram information about correct answers. They also alleged that Diana was part of the arrangement.

The prosecution pointed to nineteen coughs as significant, but an audio expert told the court that there were a hundred and ninety-two coughs on the recording overall. Those are two very different numbers. One is the set that the prosecution considered deliberate clues. The other is a count of coughing in the studio, not a count of proven signals.

Phone conversations between Diana and Whittock before the recording also formed part of the prosecution case. They argued that the contacts supported the existence of a conspiracy.

The defence offered a different explanation. Whittock said he suffered from hay fever and a dust allergy and that the timing of his coughing was accidental. Ingram had told police that he used discussion of the alternatives to give himself time and that he had known almost all the answers.

Tarrant provided another interesting piece of evidence. He had not noticed the coughing during the game itself, but he later heard what he regarded as a pattern on the recording. That distinction matters. What a person hears on a recording is not automatically identical to what somebody in a busy studio notices while concentrating on a question.

By now, coughing had become such a central subject that even an outbreak of it among the jurors caused the judge to interrupt the proceedings.

On April seventh, 2003, the jury returned guilty verdicts for Charles Ingram, Diana Ingram and Tecwen Whittock. The charge concerned obtaining a valuable security by deception: the cheque, even though the prize had not actually been paid out.

The Ingrams were each given an eighteen-month suspended prison sentence, and Whittock a twelve-month suspended sentence. The sentences were accompanied by financial penalties. In 2004, Charles's attempt to challenge his conviction was refused. Diana succeeded in having her fine and prosecution costs set aside on financial grounds, which was not an acquittal.

Meanwhile, something unusual happened to the missing television episode. After the trial, ITV showed a documentary about the scandal. In April 2003, it attracted an average audience of more than fifteen million people. The full game was shown afterwards on the broadcaster's digital channel, ITV2.

A quiz normally makes you wonder whether someone will reach the top of the ladder. In this broadcast the audience already knew the result, and had a different question: could they recognise the alleged signals and understand why the jury had convicted the contestants?

The Ingrams continued to deny the allegations. The public story was adapted into a stage play and then the 2020 television drama Quiz. That same year, their lawyer announced plans to challenge the convictions, raising concerns about whether the audio had been altered and how different microphones had captured sounds from the studio.

Those were challenges to the evidence, not proof that the convictions were wrong. Nor does seeing a television dramatization, or hearing a particularly clear cough in an edited clip, settle what happened in the room. The jury's verdict and the later objections are distinct events, and both matter when telling this story.

Ingram had achieved a result that had made only two earlier contestants on the British show millionaires. He got through the final question and held the cheque. But unlike the earlier winners, he had to defend how he got there in court. He never received the million pounds.
