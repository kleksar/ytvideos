A man paid seventy-five dollars for a lot of office goods and came home with a game console Nintendo never sold.

It had a slot for Nintendo cartridges, a disc drive and a name that made no sense if you grew up with the console wars: Sony Play Station.

In the early nineties, Sony and Nintendo had worked on combining the Super Nintendo with CD technology. At a trade show in 1991, Sony announced its plans. Nintendo announced a competing partnership with Philips. The combined console never reached stores, and Sony eventually released its own PlayStation.

Years later, Terry Diebold bought boxes at an auction connected with his employer's bankruptcy. Among the contents was a machine associated with a former Sony executive who had worked at the same company.

Terry's son Dan posted pictures in 2015. People wanted to know if it was real. That November, a demonstration in Hong Kong gave them part of the answer. A Super Nintendo cartridge worked. But the disc drive did not.

Repair specialist Ben Heckendorn took on the machine. His team restored the sound and got the drive working. In 2017 the console loaded a game from a disc. It was a modern homebrew game made for the old hardware, not a recovered commercial title.

There was finally something more convincing than a picture of a strange plastic case. A version of gaming history that had never reached the shops could actually play games.

In March 2020, the prototype sold at auction for three hundred and sixty thousand dollars, including the buyer's premium.

Before anybody knew what it was worth, how had a piece of that abandoned project ended up in a family's attic?
