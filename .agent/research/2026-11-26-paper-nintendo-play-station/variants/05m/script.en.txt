Why did a box of secondhand office goods contain a Nintendo console with Sony's name on it? Could that machine actually run games from a disc? And why would somebody eventually pay hundreds of thousands of dollars for it?

Terry Diebold worked for an American financial company called Advanta. The company went bankrupt in 2009, and Terry had a chance to bid on some of its property. He says he paid seventy-five dollars for a lot of household and office items. There were more boxes than he had expected. He brought them home.

One of the things inside looked vaguely like a Super Nintendo. There was a cartridge slot on the top, and the controller had the familiar arrangement of colorful buttons. But below the cartridge slot was a CD drive. And the machine carried the name Play Station.

Terry had acquired property from the same company where Olaf Olafsson, a former Sony executive, had worked. That connection explained how a piece of Sony hardware could have ended up among the possessions of a financial company.

But what was Nintendo doing on it?

In 1991, Sony was planning a system that would combine Nintendo cartridge games with disc technology. The attraction was obvious: a CD could carry far more data than a conventional game cartridge. Nintendo and Sony had been working together, but the partners disagreed about licensing. At the summer electronics show in Chicago, Sony revealed its plans. Nintendo announced that it was working with Philips on a CD attachment instead.

The hybrid never became a consumer product. Sony's own PlayStation arrived in Japan in December 1994. It was a different machine, built around disc games rather than compatibility with Super Nintendo cartridges.

That left Terry's console looking like something that should not exist.

It sat among the family's old electronics. In 2015, his son Dan found an online discussion of the canceled system. He told people his father had one. Pictures followed, and then a video. A name stamped into plastic was not enough to establish that the electronics inside really worked.

Later that year, the family brought the machine to Hong Kong. There, the question became a very practical one: what would appear on the television if they turned it on?

A Super Nintendo cartridge ran. The machine was not just an empty shell. But it had a sound problem and could not read discs. Inspection of the hardware revealed components from the two companies. The CD side of the system remained an unfinished test.

That's where a repair specialist named Ben Heckendorn entered the story. He took on the prototype in 2016. This was not a machine for which he could simply order a replacement motherboard. His team had to understand what the individual parts were doing. They traced the audio circuitry, worked on the electronics and replaced capacitors. Eventually the disc mechanism began operating. Heckendorn could not reduce the recovery to one certain faulty component.

An audio CD was one test. A game was another. In 2017 the team ran a small puzzle game called Magic Floor from a disc. It had been written long after the original project ended, by enthusiasts working with the old hardware. The result was initially imperfect. Its developer adjusted the code, and the game ran properly.

Now you could see a Nintendo cartridge and a disc work in the same machine. The canceled partnership had become something you could demonstrate, rather than just a story about two companies falling out.

The Diebolds took the console to gaming events. But traveling with it was expensive. Terry later explained that the costs were one reason he wanted to sell. In March 2020, the machine went to auction. The final reported price, including the buyer's premium, was three hundred and sixty thousand dollars.

It wasn't necessarily the last example of the project. In 2025, another retail-style prototype was photographed at the home of Sony engineer Ken Kutaragi. A different development machine surfaced in a museum acquisition in 2026.

The Diebold console mattered for a more specific reason. A family had found it, shown that it was genuine, and let engineers bring its abandoned disc system back to life. The project had failed in the shops. Decades later, people could finally sit down and play a game on it.
