What happens to a game console when the companies building it decide they can no longer work together? How do you prove that a machine from a canceled project is more than a case with the right logos? And what would it take to make that machine run a game from a disc, decades after the project ended?

The story begins at an auction that had nothing to do with video games.

In 2009, the American financial company Advanta went bankrupt. Terry Diebold, who worked there, saw some things he wanted among the company's property. He remembers bidding seventy-five dollars for a lot containing household and office goods. When the boxes were ready to collect, there were more of them than he expected. It took two trips to bring everything home.

One of the items was a cream-colored piece of electronics. There were controller ports on the front, a cartridge slot and a CD drive. The controller looked like the familiar one from the Super Nintendo, except for the Sony name. The words Play Station were there too.

You might recognize all those details separately. But they were never supposed to be together on a console in your living room. The machine had come from a project that dated back to a time when Sony was not yet selling its own successful game console.

Why had it ended up in an American financial company? One of Advanta's former executives was Olaf Olafsson. Before he worked in finance, he had held a senior position in Sony's game business. The auction's later account of the prototype's provenance traces it to him. The family did not know the entire journey the machine had made, but there was a plausible connection between the former employer and the device.

To understand what Terry had brought home, go back to the game shops of the early nineties.

A Super Nintendo game normally came in a cartridge. It was a familiar arrangement: insert the cartridge into the slot, turn the console on and the game could access its own memory. A compact disc worked differently. It carried data on an optical medium and required a drive. Its capacity opened the possibility of larger amounts of music, recorded voices and graphics. A company developing a disc system could offer software publishers a different way to distribute games.

Nintendo already had the customers and the games. Sony had experience with compact discs. The joint plan would let one machine accept cartridges while adding CD technology. For people who already owned Super Nintendo games, that combination was an obvious selling point.

The difficulty was not just fitting the two kinds of media into a single case. There was a business question too: who would control the licenses for the new disc software?

At the summer Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago in 1991, Sony announced a combined system under a Nintendo license. Nintendo announced plans for a CD attachment with Sony's competitor Philips. The contradiction was public. It was not clear that the companies could agree on the system they had been developing.

Discussions continued beyond that trade show, but the combination never made it to store shelves. The Sony PlayStation that arrived in Japan in December 1994 took its name into a different generation of hardware. It had a 32-bit processor and used CDs as its game medium. A player could not take a Super Nintendo cartridge and plug it into that PlayStation. That is what makes the older machine interesting: it represents a different route, not just an unusually shaped version of the console people later bought.

For years, Terry's machine stayed with the family. In 2015, his son Dan came across a discussion of the canceled console online. He said his father owned one. Pictures of the unit appeared, and he followed them with a video.

There were good reasons to be cautious. A collector could put logos on a custom-built case. A photograph of a cartridge slot did not prove that the machine behind it could run an original cartridge. And even somebody who knew that the project had existed might never have seen this exact version before.

Sony executive Shuhei Yoshida, who had joined Ken Kutaragi's game team in 1993, recalled using a system with both Super Nintendo cartridges and disc support. But his recollection did not authenticate the machine in the photographs.

In November 2015, Terry and Dan brought it to Hong Kong. They had been reluctant to risk damaging it by turning it on without checking it first. Once it was running, they could insert a Super Nintendo cartridge and show a game on the television. That answered one question. There was functioning game hardware inside the case.

It did not answer the other question. The disc mechanism would not work and the audio output had a fault. When the machine was inspected, it contained components associated with both Nintendo and Sony. Power reached the drive, but that did not explain why it could not read a disc. A photograph of an open case and a game running on a screen were evidence of a real prototype. They were not yet a demonstration of the full idea the companies had tried to build.

In 2016, repair specialist Ben Heckendorn took on the unit. Imagine trying to repair a Super Nintendo with a broken component. There are many ordinary consoles from which you can learn how the circuit is supposed to behave. Now try the same job on a machine with an additional disc system whose original design never went into mass production. You need to work out how the circuits cooperate before you can say why they don't.

Heckendorn's team used an oscilloscope to inspect the signals while a cartridge game played. One digital-to-analog converter was producing the expected game audio signal. The CD audio circuitry was not doing the same thing. The team worked through the problems and replaced capacitors. At one point, after repairs and changes had been made, the CD system started working. They could not identify a single intervention as the definite cause.

That was enough to try another kind of test. The drive could read an audio disc. But a music CD is not the same as a game that needs to load instructions and data through the machine's hardware.

Enthusiasts had written software for the hybrid. Some had worked using an emulator, a program that imitates how another machine operates. An emulator can make reasonable assumptions about hardware, but it does not prove that every assumption is right. This time there was a physical console on which to check.

A little puzzle game called Magic Floor was loaded from a disc. The first result was not perfect. Some of the display behaved incorrectly. Its developer changed the timing of the software, taking account of when the screen could safely be updated. The real machine then displayed the game properly.

A different homebrew game, Super Boss Gaiden, worked from a cartridge, but its disc version did not pass that demonstration. So the breakthrough was specific: at least one game designed by modern enthusiasts could run from a disc on the surviving prototype. This was not a library of finished, long-lost Nintendo disc games. It was a functional demonstration of the old design.

The family kept exhibiting the machine. By 2019, Terry was considering the cost of taking it from event to event. He told a reporter that he wanted to sell in part because of those expenses. The object he had brought home with some ordinary goods had become something people traveled to see.

On March sixth, 2020, Heritage Auctions sold the prototype. The auction recorded fifty-seven bids. Its reported final price, including the buyer's premium, was three hundred and sixty thousand dollars. That was the price of the sale, not a calculation of the Diebold family's profit.

Was this the only one that survived? That claim was common when the console first became famous. But in 2025, a photographer showed another retail-style version at Ken Kutaragi's home. In 2026, a distinct earlier development system appeared in a museum acquisition. The number of surviving systems is not settled.

What is settled is more interesting than a claim about rarity. We can follow the Diebold machine from an auction of unrelated property, through a family's photographs, to a cartridge demonstration, then to the restoration of disc functionality and a real game on that drive.

Sony and Nintendo never put this combined console in a shop. They went their separate ways. But one of the unfinished machines made its own journey. And a generation that had only read about the abandoned project finally had a chance to play on it.
