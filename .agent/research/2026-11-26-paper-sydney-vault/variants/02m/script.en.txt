At the Sydney Olympics, the women's gymnastics final got an extra round so that five athletes could vault again.

The competition normally had four rounds, one for each apparatus. During the first two rounds, some of the world's best gymnasts were falling on vault.

American champion Elise Ray fell on both attempts. Her score was just 7.618. Britain's Annika Reeder was injured and had to withdraw.

Then sixteen-year-old Australian gymnast Allana Slater arrived for the third round. From the runway, the vaulting horse looked too low. She asked the other gymnasts about it, but nobody answered.

She tried the apparatus during warm-up. Instead of approaching its side, she felt her hands coming down onto the top. She went back to her coaches and showed them how low it stood against her body.

They got the equipment checked. The horse was 120 centimeters high. It should have been 125. Those five centimeters changed where a gymnast met the horse with her hands.

Staff raised it. But eighteen women had already competed.

The judges offered them another chance at the correct height. Five accepted.

Elise Ray returned to the runway. This time, her vault score was 9.487, almost two points higher. She climbed to fourteenth place that night.

But she had also fallen on the balance beam. That score stayed. Reeder couldn't undo her injury, and Svetlana Khorkina, a favorite for the title, declined the offer after struggling on the uneven bars.

The equipment was fixed. The entire competition couldn't be.

How did officials give those five women another chance without making all thirty-six gymnasts start the final over?
