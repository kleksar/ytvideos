Why did the women's gymnastics final at the Sydney Olympics need an extra round? And how could an athlete improve one score by nearly two points without being able to repeat the rest of her competition?

On September 21, 2000, thirty-six women were competing for the Olympic all-around title. Each needed to perform on four different apparatus: vault, uneven bars, balance beam and floor. Their scores would be added together. One bad event could make the difference between a medal and a place halfway down the standings.

For American national champion Elise Ray, vault came first. The apparatus in Sydney was a padded horse. A gymnast ran toward it, took off from a springboard, pushed away with her hands and landed on a mat. Every part of the routine depended on a practiced sequence of movements.

Ray fell during the warm-up. When the judges were scoring her performance, she fell on both of her vaults. Her score was 7.618.

She wasn't the only one having trouble. Svetlana Khorkina, one of the favorites from Russia, also fell. Britain's Annika Reeder was injured and had to withdraw. Australian coach Peggy Liddick would later explain that people had initially put the unusual number of falls down to Olympic pressure.

The gymnasts moved on to their next events.

Then, halfway through the competition, a sixteen-year-old Australian stopped the whole process. Allana Slater was about to warm up on vault. From the end of the runway, she noticed that the horse seemed lower than usual.

She asked the other gymnasts whether they noticed it too. No answer.

Slater ran toward the horse. She recalled feeling that her hands were descending onto it, rather than approaching its side. She returned to the apparatus and showed her coaches where the top of it came against her body. Her coach Nikolai Lapchine checked it against his own height.

The equipment was measured. It was five centimeters too low.

Five centimeters is about two inches. On a piece of furniture that might barely register. In a vault, it changes where the hands meet the surface and the position of the body when it pushes away. A gymnast cannot adjust every part of a fast, twisting movement after she has already started it.

The staff raised the horse from 120 to 125 centimeters. From that moment, the remaining gymnasts had the correct apparatus. But eighteen athletes had competed before the adjustment.

The officials offered those women a second chance at the end of the final. Five took it.

Ray had to finish the other three events first. She scored well on the uneven bars, fell on the balance beam and completed her floor routine. Then she returned to the vault.

On the corrected horse, she scored 9.487. The new mark put her fourteenth on the night instead of leaving her near the bottom of the field. The difference between her two vault scores was 1.869 points.

But another line on the scoreboard still read 8.887. That was her balance beam score. The offer did not include another beam routine.

Khorkina faced a different choice. After her fall on vault she had also come off the uneven bars, one of her strongest events. She decided not to repeat her vault. Her final position that evening was eleventh.

Could one of them have won with correctly set equipment from the start? Nobody can establish the result of a competition that never happened. Romania's Andreea Raducan had also used the low horse and scored well. She finished first that evening; the gold later went to Simona Amanar following Raducan's separate disqualification.

The equipment error was real, the new scores were real, and so were the results that could no longer be changed.

The Olympic officials had found a way to give five women a second vault. They hadn't found a way to give any of them a second Olympic final.
