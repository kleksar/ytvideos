How did a teenager discover that an Olympic gymnastics apparatus was set at the wrong height? Why had some of the world's most experienced athletes struggled on it before anyone fixed the problem? And what could the officials actually repair once half the competitors had already used it?

The answer begins with the way a gymnastics all-around works. At the Sydney Olympics in 2000, thirty-six women reached the individual final. They weren't simply competing for the best vault. They had four events, and their total across those events would decide the winner.

Vault was particularly short. An athlete sprinted toward a springboard, used it to reach the padded horse, pushed away with her hands, turned in the air and landed. The entire action had to work as a continuous movement. By the time the athlete reached the horse, her speed, takeoff and the angle of her body were already linked.

The horse also looked different from the broad vaulting table used in modern gymnastics. Its narrow shape matters here because the athletes trained for the exact point at which their hands would meet it.

September 21 was the women's all-around final. American national champion Elise Ray was starting on vault. The previous evening had been the men's all-around, which used a higher horse. The apparatus had been adjusted between the two competitions.

During her warm-up Ray fell onto her back. When it was time for her scored vaults, she fell twice more. She received 7.618, putting her last at that stage of the competition. The problem was not confined to one athlete. British gymnast Annika Reeder was injured and could not finish the final. Brazil's Daniele Hypolito also had a rough landing.

It wasn't clear why. In a major competition, even strong athletes make mistakes. But the pattern was beginning to look unusual.

Years later, another British gymnast, Lisa Mason, described what had happened in her own warm-up. Vault was normally an event she enjoyed. That evening she kept falling. She told her coach that something was wrong with the apparatus. According to her recollection, he directed her to adjust her approach instead.

Mason's coach did not dispute that exchange when later contacted about it. He explained that the error was so unusual that he and other experienced coaches did not recognize it. The trouble was not invisible to all the athletes. Some could feel it, but nobody had yet established the cause.

Meanwhile, Russia's Svetlana Khorkina was one of the leading contenders for the title. She had been the world all-around champion in 1997. She began with a strong floor performance in Sydney, then moved to vault. Her warm-up went poorly. She fell on her scored vault.

The gymnasts did not all compete in the same order. Ray had begun on the problematic apparatus; Khorkina encountered it in her second event. Other women were still waiting for their turn. The competition continued.

By the third rotation, sixteen-year-old Allana Slater was due to vault. She stood at the end of the runway and looked at the horse. It seemed low. She asked the other athletes around her if they thought so too. There was no reply.

Slater tried a warm-up. In her later account, she described a sensation she hadn't experienced in training: her hands came down onto the horse from above rather than toward its side. That gave her a reason to trust what she had noticed from the runway.

She returned to the horse and used her own body as a reference for her coaches. The top was noticeably lower than where she normally expected it. Nikolai Lapchine compared its height against himself too.

Then came the measurement.

The horse stood at 120 centimeters. The required height was 125.

The staff adjusted it. In an ordinary training session that would have been the end of the problem. Here, eighteen women had already competed, and some had moved on to other events.

A second problem now lay with the officials. They could make every remaining vault take place at the right height, but what could they do about scores that had already been entered?

Their solution was to invite the eighteen affected athletes to vault again at the end of the competition. It created a fifth rotation in a four-event final. Five women accepted, including Ray and Mason.

For Ray, the two sets of scores show exactly what that remedy achieved. She had completed her bars routine with a mark of 9.750. Her beam routine included a fall, giving her 8.887. On floor she received 9.537.

Then she performed on the corrected horse. Her vault score rose from 7.618 to 9.487. The difference was 1.869, enough to improve her standing substantially. Her new total was 37.661, and she ended the evening in fourteenth place.

There was no corresponding correction to her beam score. The missed routine remained part of the final total. Ray later described a loss of confidence after the first event; that is her account of its effect, not a way to calculate how she would have performed in a different final.

Khorkina did not accept the additional vault. She had also fallen on the bars after her vault. Repeating one event could not remove the other mark. She ended the evening in eleventh place. Reeder, who had withdrawn, could not simply be restored to the competition by an equipment adjustment.

There was also another side to the result. Romania's Andreea Raducan had competed on the same low horse and received a high vault score. Her team took the first three positions that evening. Raducan was subsequently disqualified for a banned substance, an issue separate from the apparatus error. The revised medal order put Simona Amanar first, Maria Olaru second and China's Liu Xuan third.

That later change does not tell us what a correctly run gymnastics final would have looked like. Nor does Raducan's successful vault make the low horse acceptable. The athletes had different routines, techniques and physical proportions. The same change in the equipment need not produce identical consequences for all of them.

How had the horse been set to that particular number?

For years, 120 centimeters had indeed been the correct women's height. A change effective at the beginning of 1998 raised it to 125. A booklet of apparatus specifications issued in 2000 still printed the old measurement. A correction was circulated that April.

A copy with the old figure could therefore have led someone to set the equipment to exactly the wrong height. This is a plausible explanation, not an established reconstruction of the actions of the Sydney equipment crew. We do not know which copy they consulted or whether the printed error was the cause that night.

At the time, the competition manager blamed the equipment supplier for the incorrect setting. The international gymnastics federation subsequently reprimanded its Women's Technical Committee and the Superior Jury for inadequate supervision. The fact that one group adjusts an apparatus does not remove the need for someone else to verify the result.

In the end, Slater's intervention had a concrete effect. It stopped the use of incorrectly adjusted equipment before the remaining athletes had to compete on it. The additional rotation also gave five women an actual replacement score, not just an apology.

But the scoreboard tells the limits of that solution. Look at Ray's new vault mark next to her old beam mark. One of those numbers could be replaced at the end of the evening. The other remained part of the competition that had already taken place.

That was the difference between repairing the horse and repairing the final.
