How did an ordinary computer in somebody's home become the best hope for a Pixar movie? And why did Pixar abandon much of Toy Story 2 after going to such lengths to rescue it?

In the late nineties, a team at Pixar was making the sequel to its first feature film. The characters were familiar, but every new adventure meant creating digital sets, performances and lighting. The artists shared the working material on the studio's computer network.

One day, Oren Jacob, an associate technical director, and his colleagues were looking at the files for Woody. The contents of a directory began to disappear. They checked other characters and found the same thing happening.

Jacob called the machine room and asked for the server to be turned off. When the damage was assessed, he later recalled, roughly ninety percent of the project's data volume was missing. That doesn't mean ninety percent of a completed movie had been lost. It was the material the team needed to make the movie.

Pixar had backed up its data on tape. The technical team restored the files and tried rendering roughly two thousand frames, about one from each shot. The pictures looked right. The artists returned to work.

Then things began to go wrong. Some objects no longer attached correctly to characters. Some artists discovered that their latest files had been replaced by much older versions. Rendering a picture was not the same as proving that the whole working project had been recovered. Pixar's tape backup was unreliable.

That was when Galyn Susman, the supervising technical director, remembered the computer at her house. She had been working at home after having a baby, and her machine had a separate copy of the film's files.

She and Jacob collected the computer in her Volvo, protecting it with blankets and fastening it in place with seat belts. Back at the studio, the team copied its contents before working with them.

But the home copy was around two weeks behind. It couldn't simply replace everything. The staff had three collections to search: the files from tape, the files from Susman's home and material that individual artists still had on their own machines.

Jacob remembers that about seventy thousand files could be checked automatically, while another thirty thousand needed human eyes. People worked in shifts from Friday into Monday. Eventually, the project could render again and work on the movie could resume.

That was only the first crisis.

Toy Story 2 had originally been intended for home video. Pixar had decided it should be a theatrical film instead, with the same ambitions as the studio's other movies. But the filmmakers were not seeing the improvement they wanted as they reviewed successive versions.

After A Bug's Life was finished in 1998, John Lasseter and other senior creatives examined the sequel. They thought it needed major changes. Disney, according to Pixar president Ed Catmull, considered the existing version good enough and the remaining schedule too short for a complete overhaul.

Pixar went ahead anyway.

Some characters and locations survived. Much of the layout and animation did not. The studio had to make new material for the revised story. The movie's familiar opening, where Buzz Lightyear's adventure turns out to be a video game, was among the additions. So was Buster the dog. New work was also needed for the airport scenes.

The rebuilt Toy Story 2 opened in American cinemas in November 1999.

There had never been a copy of that exact finished film on the back seat of the Volvo. The computer held a version of a project that was still changing. The people who recovered its files made it possible to continue, and the filmmakers then decided what was worth keeping. The first loss was an accident. The second was a choice.
