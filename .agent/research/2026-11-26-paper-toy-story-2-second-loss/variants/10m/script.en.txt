What happens when an animated movie loses most of its working files? Why can a backup look perfectly usable while hiding serious damage? And why did the people making Toy Story 2 decide to start much of their work again after they had managed to save it?

To understand the second decision, we need to go back to the beginning of the sequel.

The first Toy Story had been a success. But the next film wasn't originally intended to follow it into cinemas. Disney had been making sequels for home video, and that was the initial plan for Toy Story 2. Pixar had another team working on A Bug's Life at the same time.

Ed Catmull, who led Pixar, later described a problem with that arrangement. People working in the same studio were aiming at two different standards. One film was supposed to match the quality of Toy Story; the other was being made for a different market. Pixar decided that the sequel should be a theatrical feature too.

But reaching that standard was going to take more than changing the way the movie was released.

Think about what an artist at Pixar needed to make a new shot. There was a digital version of Woody, with controls that allowed him to move. There were surfaces that gave his clothes and face their appearance, a set for him to stand in, lights and a virtual camera. A particular performance depended on many pieces of work created by different people. If important pieces were missing, the artist might not be able to make the shot at all.

Much of that work was available through the studio's shared computer network. That made collaboration possible. It also meant that a mistake affecting the shared files could travel far beyond one person's desk.

Oren Jacob, one of the technical directors on Toy Story 2, later recalled being with his colleagues when they noticed something strange about Woody's directory. The number of files was shrinking between one look and the next. They checked other directories, and other characters were disappearing as well.

He called the machine room and asked them to shut down the server.

That interrupted work across the production. Some employees could continue for a short while using data already on their machines. When they needed more material from the server, their work stopped too.

When the team assessed the damage, the project's directory occupied only about a tenth of its previous space, Jacob remembered. This is sometimes described as the loss of ninety percent of the movie. But that figure describes data volume, not how close the movie was to being finished.

The distinction matters. A picture from an earlier version of a scene does not necessarily give you the digital character, camera and other components required to alter that scene later. Pixar needed the working files.

The studio had a backup system using magnetic tape. The technical team restored the project and prepared a test. They rendered roughly two thousand frames, selecting around one from every shot. The process used the recovered material to produce pictures from across the film. It looked as though the team had successfully restored the project.

The artists went back to work.

Over the next several days, however, they encountered problems. Among them were errors involving the way objects were attached to characters. For example, when Woody removes his hat, the object must follow his hand rather than continue following his head. That's the kind of relationship that can be broken even when a single image still looks reasonable.

Some artists also encountered files that were nowhere near the version they remembered working on. The restored project could produce pictures, but it was not a faithful copy of the material they had lost.

The team discovered a problem with the backup system itself. This changed the task completely. They couldn't just restart the restoration and assume the result would be correct. Another source of files was needed.

At a meeting about the problem, Galyn Susman, the film's supervising technical director, remembered that she had a computer at home with a copy of the project.

She had been working away from the office after the birth of her child. To make that possible, the studio had copied the project's files to her workstation, and the machine received updates from Pixar over a relatively slow connection.

It was a separate copy made so that she could do her job. Now it was valuable for an entirely different reason.

Susman and Jacob travelled to her house in her Volvo. They put the machine in the back seat with blankets around it and secured it with seat belts. At the studio, a group of employees helped carry the computer inside using a sheet of plywood. When it started up successfully, the team copied the drive.

It wasn't a complete solution yet. The files on the home computer could be about two weeks old. If artists had changed their scenes since then, those changes might still be lost.

The recovery team put together three collections of material. One came from the tape restore, another from Susman's computer and the third from the files the artists had kept on their own machines. The staff looked for older saved copies and other local remnants of the project.

A file absent from one collection could exist in another. But if several copies existed, the team had to determine which one was useful. They couldn't simply choose a folder and declare the problem solved.

Jacob estimated that they could verify around seventy thousand files before being left with another thirty thousand that needed manual checking. The staff divided up the work and used comparison tools to examine the files. People kept working from Friday into Monday, taking turns. Jacob also remembers people from outside the recovery team providing food and blankets.

When they were done, the collection was still not identical to the original. Some files remained missing for reasons Jacob could not explain. Yet it could render, and the team could continue making the film.

Catmull describes Pixar's priorities in the aftermath: get the production back, fix the backup system and make accidental mass deletion harder. Rather than devote the response to finding and punishing the individual who had caused the accident, the studio addressed the conditions that had allowed the damage.

Now the technical staff had solved enough of the crisis to return to the original problem. Toy Story 2 still had to become a movie worth watching.

As 1998 went on, the people making the sequel were reviewing the work. Catmull recalled that the film wasn't steadily improving as they would have expected. After A Bug's Life was finished, John Lasseter came back to examine the sequel, and agreed that it needed major changes.

Pixar took its assessment to Disney. Catmull said Disney regarded the existing movie as good enough and considered the remaining time inadequate for a remake. Pixar's filmmakers disagreed with the first judgment and accepted the difficulty of the second.

They decided to rework the film anyway.

Production stopped in December and the story team worked on a new version for the return in January. Some essential pieces survived, including the principal toy characters, Andy's room and the toy shop. But much of the existing animation and the layout of scenes had to go. The team was faced with making a new film out of those surviving pieces and a great deal of new work.

Watch the first minutes of the movie that eventually appeared. Buzz Lightyear is in what seems to be another adventure, until the movie reveals the action is part of a video game. Jacob identifies that opening among the additions during the overhaul. Buster, Andy's dog, was a new character to build and animate. The human extras at the airport near the end needed to be created as well.

These were not simply files somebody had recovered. They were part of the material that the artists created because the story was changing.

The calendar had not expanded to match the new ambition. The film needed to be completed in a matter of months. On November twenty-fourth, 1999, Toy Story 2 opened in American cinemas.

What travelled in the Volvo was not the exact movie people saw that day. It was an earlier collection of working material. Recovering it let Pixar's artists continue, including the freedom to replace parts of the movie they had already spent time making.

The two crises left Pixar with very different kinds of loss. During the first, the artists had no control over what disappeared. During the second, they were choosing what would appear on screen. The people who saved the files made that second choice possible.
