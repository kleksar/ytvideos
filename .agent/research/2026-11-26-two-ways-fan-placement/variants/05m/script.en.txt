The sun has gone down, and my apartment is still hot. I have one window and one ordinary fan. The air outside is cooler than the air around me, so there's a chance to bring the temperature down without an air conditioner. But where should I put the fan?

I could aim it out of the window from nearby, or move it several metres into the room and let it drive air across the whole apartment. The second arrangement sounds useful because I want to cool more than the space beside the window.

I'm going to compare the two in a published computer model of a studio apartment. I haven't measured these temperatures in my own home. The same room, window, fan and outdoor conditions apply to both positions. It's about thirty degrees inside at the start and twenty-two outside. The fan faces outwards in both cases. For A, it is one metre from the opening. For B, it is five metres away, at the same height.

My target is the average air temperature in the entire apartment after sixty minutes, not the temperature in front of the fan. Which arrangement would you choose?

Before either fan starts, there's already a way for air to move. I open the only window. Warm indoor air tends to rise, and cooler outdoor air tends to settle below it. Two streams can use different parts of the same opening. One brings cool air into the room; the other carries warm air away. The simulation also includes outdoor air moving along the building, so this isn't a perfectly still night.

What happens if I leave everything like this for an hour? The average temperature comes down to twenty-six point seven degrees. The window is doing real work. But the room still has warmer air near the ceiling. A thermometer there would give me a different answer from one lower down.

Now I put the fan in position B, at the back. Its stream travels through the room. Instead of leaving cool air in one layer and warm air in another, it mixes them. That seems promising, until I follow the air through the opening.

The window isn't attached to a sealed pipe. Outside, the stream meets air moving along the wall. It spreads and bends. Some of the air that has just crossed the window can turn around and come back. In this arrangement, circulating air inside the room is easier than carrying heat far away from it.

At the end of the hour, B is at twenty-six point one degrees. Compared with leaving the window open alone, that's only about six tenths of a degree better.

Now I return the fan to A. It still points towards the same window, but the stream starts much closer to the opening. There's still space for incoming air beside it. The model shows more cooling through the room, including a reduction in the warm air above.

The final average is twenty-four point four degrees. That's about one point six degrees cooler than B. I didn't give A a more powerful fan or a larger opening.

There's also a third fan position in the study, close to the window but lower down. It finishes at twenty-four point nine. That is fairly close to A. I wouldn't use that small difference to claim there's one perfect height for every apartment.

The reason I care about where the fan goes is that my skin isn't a thermometer for the whole room. A breeze can make me feel cooler even if the apartment's average temperature hardly changes. And the walls may stay warm after the air cools. They continue giving heat back to the room, so the early temperature drop doesn't tell me how cool it will be at the end of the night.

For this specific setup, I would start with position A and see how the room responds. The numbers aren't a promise for my apartment. A different wind, window or second opening can change the result. The model didn't compare the fan pointing inwards or measure how much genuinely new air replaced the old air.

My answer to the original choice is simple: one metre wins this particular comparison. If I want to cool a whole room, moving air around it isn't the same as carrying its heat away.
