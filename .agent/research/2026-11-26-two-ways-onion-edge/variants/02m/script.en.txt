Sharp knife or dull knife? I want less onion juice in the air when I make dinner.

Pick A or B. A is the sharper blade, B is the blunter one. I’m comparing how many droplets they throw into the air, using published laboratory results rather than a new kitchen experiment.

The test uses peeled onion halves and blades of the same basic shape, moving at the same speed. The difference is the tiny rounded edge at the very tip.

On the sharp side, the edge begins to open the onion. On the blunt side, the surface bends down before it breaks. Why does that matter?

An onion ring has a surprisingly tough, thin skin surrounding its juicy interior. Even after I remove the brown peel, that skin is still there.

The blunt blade presses the softer tissue underneath it. When the skin finally splits, the compressed layer springs back. Juice shoots out, then little threads of liquid break into more droplets.

It all happens so quickly that a high-speed camera is needed to separate those events.

Here is the result: the bluntest blade in the comparison produced up to forty times as many droplets as the sharpest.

And I wouldn't try to make up for a dull edge by cutting faster. In a separate comparison, increasing cutting speed produced about four times as many droplets.

That doesn't mean forty times fewer tears. The experiment counted airborne liquid, not how much people cried. The onion also releases a volatile chemical that can irritate my eyes.

So I’d choose the sharp blade and cut at a comfortable, controlled pace. I have a better chance of limiting the spray without racing through dinner prep.
