Can the knife I use for dinner change how much onion juice flies into the air? And why might trying to cut faster make things worse?

I have an onion on my cutting board. I can pick a sharp blade or keep using one whose edge has become rounded. Which side would you pick?

I’m reconstructing a laboratory comparison. My main score is the number of liquid droplets thrown into the air during a cut. I’m not measuring tears, and I’m not conducting a new experiment in my kitchen.

First I need a fair comparison. I can't put a large onion under one blade and a small onion under the other, chop one fast, chop the other slowly, then blame the knife for the difference.

In the published test, the samples were halves of peeled onions of the same species. The researchers compared steel blades of similar shape at about the same cutting speed, just over a metre per second. For this comparison, the main variable was how rounded the cutting edge was.

Imagine looking straight at the end of that edge through a microscope. It isn't a perfect point. Even a sharp edge has a tiny curve. For the sharpest blade in this comparison, the radius of that curve was less than one micrometre. For the bluntest, it was just over thirteen.

I wouldn't be able to see the difference with my naked eye, but the onion responds to it.

Before the cut finishes, I want to stop the picture and look at the first juicy layer. A ring of onion is not just a lump of soft tissue. It has a thin, relatively strong covering. I can peel off the dry brown outside and still find that covering on the fleshy rings underneath.

As the wider, rounder edge pushes down, the covering resists. The softer part beneath it is compressed. The blade slows down as the onion deforms. Then the surface breaks, the blade moves forward, and the layer rebounds.

If I only look at the completed cut, I've already missed the most important part. Juice escapes in a rapid initial spray. Some liquid comes out in narrow threads. Those threads break up in the air, creating more drops even after the first burst.

The camera records thousands of frames every second. Suddenly, what looks like a single motion on my cutting board turns into a sequence I can follow.

So which side wins my comparison?

The bluntest edge produced up to forty times as many droplets as the sharpest edge. That number describes the extremes in the tested range, not every pair of kitchen knives. But it is a substantial difference when the cutting speed has been kept about the same.

Now I want to try a different question. I’ll leave the blade condition fixed and compare different cutting speeds, using a separate part of the published experiment.

As the speed rose from roughly half a metre per second to about two metres per second, the number of droplets increased around fourfold. I cannot simply multiply that result by forty. The two comparisons use different sets of conditions, not a single combined test of the extremes.

The result also doesn't mean the only thing that matters is droplet count. A large drop and a microscopic drop each add one to the counter. They can behave differently once they leave the onion. And the onion releases a volatile eye irritant when its cells are damaged.

That's why I won't call the sharper blade a guarantee against tears. The comparison gives me a way to reduce one source of spray, not a complete measurement of everything that reaches my eyes.

For my dinner, the choice is straightforward. If I'm deciding between the two edges, I'll take the sharp one. I'll use a steady pace rather than trying to finish the onion as fast as possible.

Both methods leave me with chopped onion. The difference is what happens to the juice during that brief instant when the blade first breaks through.
