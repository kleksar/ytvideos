I want to chop an onion without sending a cloud of juice into the air. Can I make a meaningful difference just by changing the blade? What happens inside the onion before the first drop escapes? And if I slow down, is that another way to get the same result?

I can choose between two knives on my cutting board. One has a fine cutting edge. The other has an edge that has become rounded. I want to compare A, the sharper blade, with B, the blunter blade.

My score will be the number of droplets released during a cut. I'll also look at their speed, because a drop that barely leaves the onion is not the same as one that shoots upwards. I'm working from published laboratory measurements and an explanation of the mechanics, not claiming I've tested these knives at home.

To understand the comparison, I need to look much closer than I normally would in a kitchen. The difference between the two edges isn't how wide the knife is at the handle. It's the shape of the microscopic region where steel meets the onion.

Imagine the cross section of the edge. Even when it looks pointed, the very end is rounded on a small scale. The comparison in the experiment kept the basic wedge shape similar while varying that tiny radius. The sharpest edge had a radius of about nine tenths of a micrometre. The bluntest was around thirteen micrometres. These values aren't ratings for the knives in my drawer; they're measured properties of the laboratory blades.

There is another problem. Onions vary, even when I buy them from the same store. I can't make two cuts on exactly the same piece of tissue, because the first cut destroys it. For the edge comparison, the experiment used different onions and collected averaged results. For the speed comparison, separate cuts on the same half helped reduce some of the variation. Neither approach makes the samples perfectly identical.

For the first comparison I'll focus on the edge while keeping the cutting speed at about 1.17 metres per second. Both sides start with peeled onion halves of the same species and similar blade geometry apart from the edge.

I could try to count the drops just by looking at the board. But the most interesting thing happens before I would have time to react. High-speed recordings in the experiment captured between five thousand and twenty thousand frames in a second.

At the upper rate, a single frame represents one twenty-thousandth of a second. The first half millisecond would occupy ten frames. Now I can separate moments which normally appear to be one motion.

The camera shows the blade meeting the onion. Before the surface opens, the onion gives way a little. This is where the two edges begin to behave differently. The sharper edge concentrates its contact into a smaller region. The blunter edge presses the surface down farther before the material breaks.

What is the surface actually doing? I'll take an onion ring in my imagined kitchen and look at its cross section. The ring has a juicy middle and a thin, tougher covering. It is not the dry brown peel I discarded. It belongs to the flesh of the onion.

A useful analogy is a liquid-filled pouch with a flexible covering. If I press it with something rounded, the covering can deform without opening immediately. That gives the material underneath time to compress. If the covering finally splits, some of the energy stored during that deformation can be released. An onion is not literally a bag of water, though. It contains living tissue with its own structure, and cells underneath the skin can be damaged before the skin itself fails.

There is direct evidence that the covering matters. In a separate experiment, a rounded test tool pressed onion tissue with and without that thin skin. The peak force needed to fracture the covered samples was typically two to three times higher. This isn't a second measurement of my two knife edges. It's a way to check why the surface can hold back the softer tissue.

I can follow the cutting motion again with that in mind. The blade slows as the surface deforms. Once the skin gives way, the blade moves forward rapidly and the onion surface rebounds. The compressed region underneath has been waiting for an exit.

Juice leaves the opening. Some of it is already in separate drops. Some is stretched into thin liquid threads that keep breaking apart. If I stop the counter too early, I miss those later fragments. If I count the same moving drop on every frame, I exaggerate the total. The analysis tracks the liquid through the sequence rather than treating every visible spot as a new drop.

The earliest drops can be surprisingly fast. The recorded initial speeds in the study reached forty metres per second under some conditions. That does not mean every droplet travels that fast, and it is not the speed of the knife. The pressure released inside the onion can accelerate a tiny mass of liquid far beyond the blade's own speed. As later liquid escapes, the initial ejection speeds generally fall.

Now I can return to my score. Under the edge comparison, the bluntest blade produced up to forty times as many droplets as the sharpest. The study also found a tendency toward larger, faster droplets with blunter edges. My result therefore isn't only about how many individual specks I can see.

It would be misleading to draw exactly forty dots on one side for every dot on the other and present them as a recording. I can use a relative index instead. If the sharp endpoint is one unit, the blunt endpoint reached up to forty units of droplet count. That illustrates the reported ratio, without pretending to know the actual count for a particular onion in my kitchen.

I still have to decide how quickly to cut. Suppose I keep a blade of the same edge radius and vary the speed. A separate published comparison did just that. As cutting speed increased from about 0.44 to 2.03 metres per second, droplet count increased around fourfold. The average kinetic energy of the droplets also increased with the blade's speed.

I can make two useful choices here: the edge I start with and the pace at which I cut. I can't combine the two reported multipliers to promise a hundred and sixty times fewer droplets. The experiment did not measure that exact combination as a joint result.

What about the advice to put the onion in the fridge? The same study compared cooled onions and room-temperature samples. The cooled ones had been held at one degree Celsius for twelve hours. No statistically significant difference in droplet speed was established, and the chilled samples actually showed a larger volume of ejected liquid. The authors said the mechanical effect of temperature needed more investigation. That doesn't settle whether cooling changes the chemistry responsible for irritation.

And that brings me to the limit of my score. Cutting onion cells also releases a volatile irritant. I can count droplets, but those counts cannot tell me precisely how much irritant reaches my eyes. A tiny drop may be carried by room air, while a heavier drop follows a different path. The camera observes a limited region, and the measurements are not a complete map of my kitchen.

I don't need to turn the experiment into a promise that I'll never cry again. Its useful result is much narrower and better supported. A sharp edge helps limit the amount of liquid spray generated during the cut, and faster cutting produced more droplets in the separate speed comparison.

So when I prepare dinner, I’ll take the sharp knife and use a comfortable, controlled cutting pace. I get the chopped onion I came for. And I understand why pushing a dull edge through that first fleshy ring can create a much bigger spray, even when both knives seem to do the same job.
