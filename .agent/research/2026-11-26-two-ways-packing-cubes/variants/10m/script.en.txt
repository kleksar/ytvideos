I've got one carry-on for a trip. If I can fit everything I need inside it, I don't have to find room for a second bag. I could fold my clothes directly into the carry-on. Or I could use compression packing cubes, the organizers with a second zipper that squeezes the clothes down.

The second option sounds almost too good to ignore. If my bundle gets smaller, surely I should have more space left in the bag. But I'm interested in something different from the size of an individual bundle. I want to know how many of my clothes I actually get to bring.

So here's my choice. A is folding directly into the carry-on. B is compression cubes. Before I show the results, which one would you pack with?

I don't have a new physical experiment to show. Instead, I'll reconstruct a published luggage test and use a simple model to explain why its results can look contradictory. I'll keep the reported results and my illustrative model separate.

First, the rules. In the published test, each method used the same clothing items in the same order. The testers also made sure their soft luggage didn't grow beyond the size limit. They filled remaining small spaces with socks. I'll track the number of garments as my main score, and pairs of socks as a separate score. I'm not measuring weight, packing speed or the number of complete outfits.

I'm starting with the small soft travel bag. I'll leave the outside dimensions unchanged between methods. That matters because if I can just stretch the bag until another jumper goes inside, I've changed the problem. I might end up with a bag I can't bring under the original size restriction.

A has no extra organizer between the stacks of clothes. I can fold the next garment into a shape that makes sense for the opening I have. With B, I first pack garments into an organizer. Once the first zipper is closed, the other zipper reduces its thickness. Now I have to place that bundle in the bag and fit the other bundles or loose items around it.

The useful question isn't whether the compressed clothes look smaller on my bed. It's whether I can find an opening for the next thing I need once the bundle is in my bag.

Let me make that visible with a very simple drawing. Imagine my bag is a flat rectangle, six squares wide and four squares high. This is a made-up grid, not a diagram measured from the published luggage.

I give my first collection of clothes a footprint of four by three squares. I'll place it in the upper left corner. It occupies twelve squares. Along the right edge there's still a rectangle two squares wide and three squares high. I have another garment that needs exactly that much space. It fits. At the bottom there's an entire row of six small squares left for six small items.

Now I take the first collection and imagine compressing it into a bundle five squares wide and two squares high. That occupies only ten squares. I've freed two squares in total compared with the first collection's original shape. But suppose I position the bundle halfway down the bag, with one empty row above and one below. Now I have a strip to the right and two narrow rows. There's more empty area, fourteen squares instead of twelve before the extra garment. Yet that garment doesn't fit in any of the available gaps, even if I turn it sideways.

If I can move the bundle to the top edge instead, I might find an arrangement that works. That's the point. The drawing doesn't prove that every layout with cubes must fail. It shows that saving space inside a bundle, arranging the bundle and accommodating the next item are separate problems. My little square items can use the narrow strips even when the larger item cannot. The shape of what remains matters as much as the amount of space.

Now back to the real published test. The reported result for the small soft holdall is twenty-two garments with folding and nineteen with compression cubes. A wins by three garments. The tester didn't publish a map of the contents that would let me replay their exact placement. I can't prove that the cubes were arranged like my drawing.

There is something particularly interesting about the second score. A fit five pairs of socks. B fit eighteen pairs. The organizer method had fewer garments but much more room for the small items. The report describes the spaces around cubes being used for socks, which is consistent with the idea I showed in the drawing.

Suppose I forget that garments and pairs of socks have different jobs and just count everything together. For the small bag, A would get twenty-seven and B would get thirty-seven. I could announce that B had won by ten items. All the arithmetic would be correct, but the headline would hide the fact that B left three more garments behind in the test. It would reward an extra pair of socks exactly as much as another garment. That's why I set my main score before looking at the numbers.

What if my trip actually requires a lot of socks? I'll keep the same results and introduce three hypothetical shopping-list targets. No new physical packing is taking place here. In the first, I need twenty garments and five pairs of socks. Only A meets both numerical targets. In the second, I need nineteen garments and ten pairs. Only B meets both numerical targets. In the third, I need twenty garments and ten pairs. Neither observed result meets both targets. I would have to change the list or try another layout, rather than declaring a winner based on whichever total is higher.

These are comparisons of counts. They don't tell me which particular clothes were the extra ones, and they don't prove a combination of items on my personal list will fit. But they make it clear that the word more needs a definition.

I also want to know whether this was peculiar to the small soft bag. The same published test included a larger hard suitcase, a larger backpack and a smaller wheeled case. Folding held forty-seven garments in the large case, versus forty-six with compression cubes. In the backpack it was forty versus thirty-five. In the little wheeled case, twenty versus eighteen. Folding had the higher garment count in each bag. On socks, there was a tie in the big suitcase, a small advantage for folding in the backpack, and an advantage for cubes in the two small bags.

That gives me a stronger reason to try folding first than a single unusual result would. It still doesn't tell me the average for every brand of cube or every kind of wardrobe. The report doesn't provide repeated trial distributions or the full packing order. I won't turn four luggage comparisons into a universal percentage of space saved.

There is a good counterexample. In a separate test for a business trip, all the specified clothes, footwear and accessories fitted a twenty-one-inch carry-on with rolling. It was a tight fit. Compression cubes, using the same list, left more room according to the tester and made closing the case easier, although some clothes were wrinkled afterward. The spare space wasn't quantified. That comparison changed the type of packing and used a different task: fitting a fixed list and looking at the room left, rather than adding garments until there was no room for more.

I would like to learn which outcome applies to my own trip. Here's how I can do that without buying a different carry-on. I put the exact clothes I need on the bed in a predetermined order. I make a list, keeping socks, shoes and other categories separate. I try A, check the outside dimensions and write down what remains outside. I empty the bag completely. I try B with the same priority list and size limit. I can repeat both attempts with the order reversed to see whether the result depends on practice or packing order. If I care about time or finding a particular shirt in a hotel, I can measure those separately. I can also record the total weight if my trip has a weight limit. Those would be new observations, not values already known from the published test.

Here's my decision for now. If I'm primarily trying to maximize garments in a bag like the small holdall, I'll start with folding. If I need more small items, a different wardrobe or a convenient way to pull out a group of clothes together, I'll consider the cubes and compare the actual list. In that published small-bag comparison, A won on garments, twenty-two to nineteen. B won on socks, eighteen to five. Both numbers deserve to be on the scorecard.

I'm not trying to win a contest for the neatest bundle on my bed. I'm trying to leave for my trip with the things I planned to bring. The smaller bundle only helps when it makes that possible.
