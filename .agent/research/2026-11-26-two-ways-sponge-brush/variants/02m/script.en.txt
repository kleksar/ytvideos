I want my dishwashing tool to dry before I use it again. Sponge or brush?

I've got a pile of plates to wash. Pick a side: A is the sponge, B is the brush. My score is how much water each tool holds when I'm done, and how much is still there tomorrow. I'm reconstructing a published comparison, not running a new experiment.

Both tools start dry and get the same exposure to water. After draining, the brushes in the test held about five grams of water. The three sponge types held roughly eighty to a hundred and eighty grams.

Four and a half hours later, the brushes had almost no water left. A day later, the unsqueezed sponges still held forty to a hundred and twenty grams.

But I don't normally put away a dripping sponge. In the same test, squeezing removed over eighty-five percent of its water. Now the gap was much smaller.

Does that difference matter beyond a wet countertop? In separate laboratory tests, potentially harmful bacteria survived longer on some sponges than on the brush.

There's a catch. A follow-up comparison with used brushes found that the advantage of drying was lost when the brushes were kept in a damp, closed bag.

That doesn't mean every sponge is dangerous or every brush is safe. These tests didn't measure how likely I am to get sick.

For washing dishes, I'd pick the brush and give it room to dry. I'd keep an absorbent sponge for jobs where I actually need to soak up liquid.

My plates are clean. It's the next wash I'm thinking about.
