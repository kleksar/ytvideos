If I wash the same stack of plates with a sponge or a brush, which tool will be in better condition when I come back tomorrow? And can a simple squeeze close the gap?

I've got both tools next to my sink. The sponge is good at picking up liquid. The brush has a handle and bristles with spaces between them. Both can help me clean a plate, but I want to compare what happens after the dishes are done.

Pick A for the sponge or B for the brush. I'm going to look at the weight of water left behind. Then I'll check whether the difference is relevant to kitchen hygiene. This is an illustrated reconstruction of published tests, not an experiment I've carried out myself.

Imagine the scale is set to zero for each dry tool. The tests gave the tools the same exposure to water and let the excess drain away. Three brush designs and three sponge designs were compared.

The first reading is already surprising. The brushes hold about five grams of water. The sponges hold roughly eighty to a hundred and eighty grams. If I were cleaning up a spill, I'd be pleased with the sponge. But I'm about to put my washing-up tool down and walk away.

Four and a half hours later, the brushes are almost dry. The sponges are still holding much of their water.

Now I come back the next day. The unsqueezed sponges have between forty and a hundred and twenty grams left. I might not see a puddle anywhere, because that water is inside the sponge.

That feels like an unfair picture of how I actually wash up. I usually squeeze my sponge. So let's use the other set of results, where the sponges were squeezed once before being left to dry.

Most of the water comes out straight away: over eighty-five percent. Four and a half hours later, the remaining amount is under ten grams. By the next day only the largest sponge in that set has measurable residual water, about a gram and a half.

The brush still wins on speed of drying, but the difference now looks nothing like a hundred grams of water against five. What I do with the sponge matters.

Now I want to know whether faster drying has a hygiene benefit. I can't tell that from the scale. In a separate controlled laboratory comparison, the tested brush lost detectable Salmonella sooner than two of the three sponge types. The slowest-drying sponge had the highest Salmonella levels during that comparison.

That is a result about the tested tools, not a prediction that my sponge is contaminated.

There's another surprise when I look at a follow-up study using brushes that had already been used in kitchens. It compared hanging brushes in the air with keeping them in a moist, closed bag. The hanging brushes had a significant decline in Salmonella, but the brushes in the bags did not show that decline. Buying a different tool is only part of my decision. It also needs a place to dry.

One more check before I throw every sponge away. A consumer test compared four kinds of cleaning tools, and the brush again had the lowest bacterial counts after use. Yet no disease-causing species were detected in any of those samples. A higher total bacterial count does not automatically mean a higher chance of getting sick. None of these comparisons measured that chance directly.

My choice for dishes is B, a brush I can hang up to dry. If I keep using A, I can at least avoid leaving it full of water. And for absorbing spills that don't involve raw food contamination, a sponge still has a job.

So I don't need to judge the tool by how clean my plate looks. I need to think about what I leave beside the sink for the next time.
