What makes a dish brush different from a sponge when I've finished washing up? Can I get the same benefit just by squeezing the sponge? And is a dry tool necessarily a clean tool?

I've got a small kitchen with a stack of dirty plates. I can wash them with either of two familiar objects. The sponge bends around the edge of a plate and absorbs liquid. The brush moves water across the surface with its bristles. Both belong in kitchens, and I don't want to declare either of them the winner before deciding what to measure.

I'm interested in the next time I wash the dishes. I want to find out which tool is more likely to dry between uses, and whether that matters for bacteria. I'll use an illustrated reconstruction of published measurements. I'm not claiming to have washed and tested these particular objects myself.

Choose A for the sponge or B for the brush. We'll start with the amount of water retained after the same exposure, then compare how each tool dries.

There's nothing strange about a sponge being more absorbent than a brush. What I find interesting is the size of the difference when the dishes are already done.

In one comparison of three types of brushes and three types of sponges, all the objects started dry. After exposure to water and a period for excess water to drain away, the brushes contained about five grams of water. The sponges contained roughly eighty to a hundred and eighty grams.

If I had a scale under each object, with its dry weight subtracted, I could read the extra mass directly. I don't need to know the weight of the plastic handle or the foam.

Let's pause here. The sponge has collected water. It hasn't necessarily performed badly. If my task were to pick up a puddle, that ability would be exactly what I wanted. My task in this comparison is to put away a tool that can dry before I need it again.

I leave the two sides alone, and the readings begin to fall. After four and a half hours, the brushes are nearly dry. The sponges are still carrying a substantial amount of water. After a full day the unsqueezed sponges contain about forty to a hundred and twenty grams.

The important part is that the water doesn't have to be visible on the counter. It can stay in the spaces inside the sponge. With the brush, most of the water is free to leave. The difference is built into the tools' design.

But I have hands, and I know how to squeeze a sponge.

Before I choose the brush, I want to give the sponge the benefit of that ordinary step. In another part of the same water test, the sponges were squeezed before drying. More than eighty-five percent of their water was removed. The readings after four and a half hours were below ten grams. A day later, the only measurable residual water in that set was about one and a half grams, in the largest sponge.

That changes the practical comparison. My unpressed sponge and my squeezed sponge aren't equivalent. If I'm looking at a chart with a huge gap between brush and sponge, I need to know which of those routines it describes.

The brush still has the drying advantage in these tests. I can also see why it is not the answer to every cleaning task. I don't have to decide that one tool is good and the other is useless.

Now I'll add the question of hygiene. I can't weigh the bacteria with the kitchen scale, and I can't infer their numbers just from the amount of water.

A different laboratory comparison looked at the survival of bacteria on three sponge types and one brush. Some of the bacteria tested can cause foodborne illness. On two sponge types, Salmonella could still be detected after a week. On the brush, it was below the test's detection limit by day three. Among the sponges, the one that dried most slowly had the highest Salmonella levels during the comparison.

There are two important details in those results. The water experiment and the bacterial comparison aren't one continuous home test. And below the detection limit does not mean that an item has been proven sterile.

So far, I'm looking at tools under laboratory conditions. What if I already have a brush that's been in my kitchen for weeks? It might not be in the same condition as a new one from the shop.

A follow-up study collected used tools from households in Norway and Portugal. The kitchen habits behind those tools were quite different. Most of the Portuguese sponges were used frequently for washing dishes. Several of the Norwegian sponges were used mainly for occasional scrubbing, in households that also used brushes.

I could compare their average readings, but I wouldn't know whether I was seeing the effect of the material, the way people washed up, or the types of dishes they washed. I need to keep those household observations separate from the controlled tests.

The follow-up also compared storage conditions for used brushes. In one group, the brushes were left hanging; the other group was kept moist in bags. Salmonella levels fell significantly in the hanging group, but that decline wasn't observed in the bagged group.

That's the part of the story that changes what I would do in my kitchen. I can buy the faster-drying tool and still fail to give it a chance to dry. A cupboard or holder isn't necessarily a problem by itself; the issue here is whether the brush is actually left wet in an enclosed environment. The published comparison was between the two specific storage conditions, not a test of every holder on the market.

A sponge advertised as antibacterial might seem like another solution. I would want to know whether the label makes a difference after ordinary use. In the follow-up study, a group of users received silver-containing sponges. After four weeks, the tests did not find a difference in overall bacterial counts or bacterial diversity compared with their conventional sponges. That is not a verdict on every product with an antibacterial claim, but it's a reason not to assume a label solves the drying problem.

And how worried should I be about all those bacteria in the first place? A separate consumer comparison looked at a standard sponge, an antibacterial sponge, a specialty sponge and a brush. The brush had the lowest bacterial counts at the later checks, but none of the samples had detectable disease-causing species. That's a useful counterweight to alarming pictures of bacteria beside a kitchen sink.

I can judge drying, and I can look at the bacterial results under the conditions that were tested. I cannot convert either result into my personal chance of food poisoning. That would need a different kind of evidence.

Here's what I would change. For routine washing up, I would use the brush and store it so it can dry. If I continue to use a sponge, I would squeeze out the water after use and avoid treating that as disinfection. For jobs where the point is to absorb a harmless spill, I would still use an absorbent tool.

The brush has won the comparison I set up at the beginning: drying between uses. Its advantage depends on being able to dry. The sponge hasn't stopped being useful. I have simply separated two different jobs, absorbing water and washing up, instead of judging both tools by one wet plate.
