A powered toothbrush moves its own bristles. A manual one relies on my hand. Does that save me time?

I want to compare how much plaque each brush removes. I'm using a published study, not testing my own teeth. My metric is the drop in plaque score: a bigger drop means better removal on this scale.

Forty people tried both brushes. Their starting scores were almost identical. I'll use a common starting score of three for the illustration.

First round, one minute each. Which would I pick: A, manual, or B, powered?

The manual brush reduces the score by 0.43 points. The powered one reduces it by 0.61.

Now I reset the comparison and give each brush three minutes. The manual score drops by 0.62. Look at those numbers: three minutes on the left, one minute on the right, almost the same reduction.

That's a tempting shortcut. But what happens when B gets three minutes too?

Its reduction reaches 0.93. The extra time improved both results. The motor didn't make the clock irrelevant.

Those close averages don't prove that a short session can replace a longer one. And this test used one particular powered brush, not every model on the shelf.

Separate studies comparing one and two minutes found that the extra minute helped with either type. I'll keep the recommended two minutes, twice a day, whichever brush I choose.

The powered brush won both equal-time rounds. I can change the tool, but I'll keep my timer.

So what am I actually paying for when I buy a powered toothbrush?
