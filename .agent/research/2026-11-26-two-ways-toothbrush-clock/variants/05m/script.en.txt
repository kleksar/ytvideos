Can a motor really save me time in the bathroom? I have a manual toothbrush, a powered toothbrush, and a timer. I want to know whether changing the brush is enough, or whether the time I spend brushing still matters.

I'll compare published measurements in an illustrated model, not a personal experiment. My finish line is a reduction in plaque score. Plaque is the film that builds up on teeth. An index is a way of assigning a score to what can be seen on the teeth. It isn't a percentage of teeth that are perfectly clean. And the difference between two scores isn't a direct measure of how much disease I prevented.

The study compared a manual brush with a battery-powered brush that had two moving heads. One turned back and forth and the other moved in a straight line. Forty people took part, and the same participants tried the different conditions. The people assessing plaque didn't know which brush had been used for the assessment.

The starting averages were 2.99 with the manual brush and 2.98 with the powered brush. For my illustration I'll start both bars at three. That's a common starting point for the model, not an exact record of an individual mouth. I will compare the reported drops in score, which were adjusted in the analysis for the starting conditions.

Before I see the results, I have to choose. A is manual. B is powered. They each get the same one minute. Is the movement of the powered brush enough to create a visible difference on my chart?

When the minute ends, A has a reduction of 0.43 points. B has a reduction of 0.61. The lead is 0.18 points. A's bar has moved, but not as far as B's.

Now the question changes. If I give A more time, can it catch up?

I reset both comparisons to their starting state. This time I compare three-minute brushing results. The manual brush reaches 0.62. That's an additional 0.19 points compared with its own one-minute result.

And here's the number that makes me want to stop the clock early with B. Its one-minute result was 0.61. A's three-minute result is 0.62. On this particular score, those two averages are separated by just one hundredth of a point.

Those close averages don't establish equivalence. Now I give B the same three minutes. Its score reduction is 0.93. Compared with its one-minute result, the extra time adds 0.32 points. The gap between brushes at the same time has actually grown, from 0.18 to 0.31.

Both brushes improved when the time increased. The powered brush led at both equal-time checkpoints. That is what this study established for these brushes, not a rule about every toothbrush on sale.

There is also a missing number. The test didn't report a two-minute result in its abstract. I could draw a straight line between the two checkpoints and invent a score for the middle, but teeth don't have to follow my line. So that space stays blank.

Instead, I look at a separate review of studies that directly compared one and two minutes. Across those comparisons, an extra minute improved the plaque scores for both types of brush. The direction is useful, even though the numbers from those studies cannot be pasted into the first chart.

This answers my original question. A powered brush can improve plaque removal, but the extra time isn't wasted simply because I switched brushes.

For my own routine, I will keep the general recommendation: a soft-bristled brush, fluoride toothpaste, and two minutes twice a day. I can choose either type and work through all areas of my mouth. I don't need to buy a motor in order to use a timer.

When I look back at the four bars, the useful surprise is not that a one-minute score happened to sit close to a three-minute score. It's that giving the powered brush more time moved its result further ahead. That is the result I would lose if I treated the motor as an excuse to stop early.
