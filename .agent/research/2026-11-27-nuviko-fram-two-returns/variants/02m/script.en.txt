Why would a captain let the sea freeze around his ship? And how could two men who left that ship make it home before the crew?

In 1884, people in Greenland found wreckage from an American ship that had sunk on the other side of the Arctic. Fridtjof Nansen, a Norwegian scientist, saw a possibility. If ice could carry the wreckage across the ocean, perhaps it could carry a whole ship.

In 1893 he sailed into the ice aboard the Fram. When the ice closed around her, the rounded hull rose between the blocks instead of being crushed. The men could stay aboard while the ice moved them across the ocean.

But the ship wasn't heading far enough north. Nansen wanted to reach the pole. He and Hjalmar Johansen left with dogs, sledges and kayaks, knowing they couldn't count on finding the ship again.

Ridges and open water slowed them down. In April 1895 they set a new record for the farthest north, but still had nearly four degrees of latitude to go. They turned south.

Winter caught them on Franz Josef Land. They made a shelter from stones, moss and walrus hide. By the following summer they were travelling again. At Cape Flora they met the British explorer Frederick Jackson by chance. His expedition's ship brought them to Norway.

They arrived on August thirteenth, 1896. That very day, hundreds of miles away, the Fram emerged from the ice north of Svalbard. Her captain brought the remaining eleven men home.

None of the thirteen had reached the pole. But their ship had crossed the Arctic in the ice, just as Nansen had predicted. They brought back evidence of a deep ocean where many had expected land. A failed race to the pole had become a successful experiment.
