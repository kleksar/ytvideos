Why would an explorer build a ship specifically to get trapped in the Arctic ice? What would make him leave it behind in the middle of the ocean? And how did both journeys end on the same day?

The idea began with a disaster. An American expedition aboard the Jeannette had lost its ship in the Arctic. Three years after the sinking, in 1884, Inuit in Greenland found pieces of wreckage thousands of miles from where she went down.

To Fridtjof Nansen, a Norwegian scientist, the discovery suggested a route through the Arctic. The ice wasn't fixed in place. It carried things with it. If he could build a ship that survived the pressure, he could travel with the ice instead of trying to sail through it.

He worked with shipbuilder Colin Archer on a vessel called the Fram. Its rounded hull was designed to slide upwards when ice pressed against its sides. In the summer of 1893, thirteen men set out from Norway. By September they were caught in the ice north of Siberia, exactly where Nansen wanted them.

Then came the real test. In January 1895 the ice piled up against the ship so violently that the crew carried supplies onto the ice and prepared to abandon her. They didn't have to. As the pressure continued, the hull rose out of the squeeze.

The ship was surviving, but there was another problem. The ice didn't move in a straight line, and the course it was taking would miss the North Pole. Nansen decided to try for the pole on foot.

He entrusted the ship to Captain Otto Sverdrup. He took one companion, Hjalmar Johansen. Their first two attempts to leave failed. The sledges were difficult to haul, so they changed the equipment and tried again. On March fourteenth, 1895, they finally set off.

A moving sheet of ice makes for a peculiar journey. You can travel north for hours while the ground beneath you moves south. And the surface isn't flat. The men had to haul sledges over ridges and make detours around water. In early April they reached eighty-six degrees, fourteen minutes north, farther than anyone before them. But they still had almost four degrees to go. They abandoned the attempt and headed south.

The Fram wasn't waiting somewhere behind them. It was still drifting. Their route home would have to take them towards islands.

By August they had reached Franz Josef Land, but they couldn't complete the journey before winter. They built a low hut from stone and moss, with a piece of driftwood supporting a roof of walrus hide. They survived on meat from the animals they hunted and burned fat for heat and light.

While the two men spent the winter there, Sverdrup's crew continued crossing the Arctic in the Fram. Neither group could send word to the other.

In May 1896, Nansen and Johansen left the hut. Their two kayaks could be joined together to cross open water, but that also meant the boats carried almost everything they needed to travel. On one occasion, the kayaks drifted away while the men were standing on ice. Nansen swam after them and managed to bring them back.

Then, on June seventeenth, they encountered people on a remote Arctic island. It was the expedition of British explorer Frederick Jackson. The two Norwegians were able to stay at his camp and leave aboard the British ship Windward.

On August thirteenth, 1896, Nansen and Johansen reached Vardø in Norway. On the very same date, the Fram broke free of the ice north of Svalbard. The men had spent about seventeen months travelling separately. A week after the two arrived home, Sverdrup brought the ship into Norwegian waters. They soon reunited.

Nansen had failed to reach the North Pole. But the crew had returned with something no record of latitude could give them: observations from a long voyage through the central Arctic. They had measured a deep ocean, followed the movement of the ice and found that it really could carry a ship from one side of the Arctic to the other.

The expedition's two routes told the same story in different ways. Nansen and Johansen found out how difficult it was to travel across moving ice. Sverdrup and the crew proved that the ice itself could do the travelling. All thirteen men made it home.
