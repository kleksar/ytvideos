Could a ship cross the Arctic Ocean without sailing through most of it? Why did the scientist who designed that voyage give up the safety of his ship for two tiny boats? And why did a journey that missed the North Pole change what people knew about the Arctic?

To understand what Fridtjof Nansen was trying to do, look at two places on a map. One is the sea north of Siberia, the other is Greenland, on the opposite side of the Arctic. In 1881, the American ship Jeannette sank in the ice north of Siberia. Three years later, Inuit on the coast of Greenland discovered wreckage from her. The objects had travelled across an enormous stretch of the ocean.

Nansen took the discovery seriously. The ice that had destroyed the Jeannette had also transported her remains. If the floating ice moved from one side of the Arctic to the other, a vessel that could stay afloat in it might travel through places that ordinary ships could never reach.

The problem was pressure. Sea ice is constantly shifting. When large pieces collide, there is nowhere for them to go except over or under each other, or against whatever happens to be between them. A ship with high, nearly vertical sides can become a barrier between enormous moving blocks. Nansen and the Norwegian shipbuilder Colin Archer designed a different hull. Its shape allowed the ice to push the vessel up, much as a slippery object can rise when it is squeezed from the sides.

There was no guarantee that the idea would work in every situation. And even if the ship survived, the expedition had to live on board for years. Supplies, the equipment for scientific observations and the means of moving around the ice all had to fit inside one relatively small ship. Archer built it at Larvik in Norway. Nansen's wife Eva gave it the name Fram, meaning forward.

On June twenty-fourth, 1893, thirteen men set out from Norway. They sailed east along the Siberian coast, then found their way into the ice near the New Siberian Islands. In September, the ship froze in.

For a while the voyage consisted of an unusual kind of progress. The crew could see the ice around them, but they had to measure their position to know how far they had travelled. Some days they moved north; on others the ice took them back south. They made observations of the weather, the ice and the water below. At Christmas they were only around seventy-nine degrees north. The drift was slower and less predictable than Nansen had expected.

Then the ship itself was tested. In January 1895, the surrounding ice threatened to overwhelm her. The crew brought supplies and equipment out onto the ice so they would have something to use if the vessel was lost. They prepared to leave the Fram.

But the hull did what it had been built to do. As the ice pressed in, the ship was lifted out of the squeeze. The men could stay aboard.

Nansen now had evidence that his extraordinary way of crossing the Arctic could work. Yet their actual track seemed unlikely to carry them to the pole. The expedition faced two very different objectives. It could study the ocean while drifting across it, or it could try to reach one particular spot on that ocean. Staying aboard gave them a chance at the first. Reaching the second would require leaving the vessel.

Nansen placed the Fram in the care of her captain, Otto Sverdrup. He chose Hjalmar Johansen to accompany him. Their baggage included sledges, skis and small kayaks, which would be necessary as soon as they encountered water they couldn't walk across. There would be no reliable way of locating the Fram once it drifted out of sight. On the way back they would have to aim for land.

Their first departure, in late February 1895, was a failure. They found it too difficult to move their heavy equipment across the ice. They returned to the ship and worked on the sledges. A second attempt failed as well. On March fourteenth they left for the third time, at a latitude of just over eighty-four degrees north.

Here the design problem shifted from the ship to the travellers themselves. They could carry enough provisions to give themselves a better chance of surviving, but the heavier the sledges, the harder they were to move. Ice ridges could force the men to lift a loaded sledge. Where water opened between floes, they had to find another route. The dogs, after a long period aboard the ship, fought and chewed through their harness lines, which had to be repaired in the cold.

Even their measurements could be discouraging. The ice moved under them, so the distance they travelled on foot wasn't necessarily the distance they gained towards the pole.

In early April, they reached eighty-six degrees, fourteen minutes north, a record at the time. They were still several hundred kilometres short of the North Pole. Going farther would leave less time and fewer supplies for the journey south. Nansen and Johansen turned around.

They had to change direction without changing the fact that they were still on drifting ice. As the season advanced, the pack became more difficult to cross. By May they were down to two sledges and twelve dogs from the original twenty-eight. They had to abandon more of what they carried as they went.

When they could travel across water, they used the kayaks. When the way ahead was blocked by ice, they hauled their boats back onto it. In August, while preparing to launch, Johansen was attacked by a polar bear. Nansen managed to kill it, and Johansen escaped without serious injury. Soon after, they reached land in the Franz Josef archipelago.

There was no settlement waiting for them. With winter coming, they had to build somewhere to live. They dug a hollow, made walls of stone, filled gaps with moss and used a log washed up on shore to support a walrus-hide roof. Animal fat gave them fuel, and the meat from their hunting supplied food. They adapted a sleeping bag so both men could fit inside.

Nansen's diary records a very different Christmas from the one they would have celebrated at home. They turned their shirts inside out, and Nansen managed a wash with a tiny amount of warmed water. Inside the hut, a change of clothing was something worth recording.

During the same months, eleven men aboard the Fram were still measuring their position and the ocean underneath. The ship eventually began drifting south towards the open sea. But as long as the ice held it, Sverdrup could not simply sail home.

In May 1896, the two men left their winter shelter. Their kayaks were often lashed together. Once, while they were on a floe, the boats drifted away from them. The boats carried the means to continue their journey. Nansen entered the icy water, reached them and recovered them.

In June they reached Cape Flora. There they came across members of an entirely different expedition, led by the British explorer Frederick Jackson. Neither Nansen nor Johansen had known they would find help there. Jackson took them into his camp. They could now return aboard the expedition's ship, Windward.

There is a photograph of Nansen and Jackson greeting each other on the island. It is a reconstruction of their meeting, made six days afterward, not a photograph of the instant they met. Both men were available to pose for it because Nansen had reached the camp alive.

On August thirteenth, Windward reached Vardø, Norway. That same day the Fram emerged from the pack ice north of Svalbard. On August twentieth, Sverdrup reached a Norwegian harbour. The two groups soon met again. All thirteen men had survived.

The geographic pole had eluded them, but the scientific experiment had succeeded. Their measurements showed that the central Arctic was a deep ocean, and their route demonstrated the large-scale movement of ice across it. Nansen also noticed that the drifting ice tended to move at an angle to the wind, an observation that helped inspire later work on ocean currents and the rotation of the Earth.

Years later, the same ship would carry another expedition towards a pole. Roald Amundsen used the Fram for his Antarctic voyage. But Nansen's achievement was already complete. The wreckage in Greenland had shown him that the ice could carry objects across an ocean. The thirteen people who returned from the Fram expedition showed that it could carry a living crew, and that the journey itself could be more valuable than the destination.
