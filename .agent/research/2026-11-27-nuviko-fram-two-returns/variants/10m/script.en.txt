How could a ship survive the ice that had crushed another vessel? Why did the man who planned the voyage leave that ship in the middle of the Arctic? And what did the expedition discover beneath the ice, even though it never reached the North Pole?

In 1881 the American ship Jeannette sank north of Siberia. Three years later, Inuit on the coast of Greenland found pieces of her wreckage. On a map, the two places lie on opposite sides of the Arctic. Something had carried the objects across the ocean.

The Norwegian scientist Fridtjof Nansen saw a way to use that movement. He would take a ship into the drifting ice, let it freeze in and wait for the ice to carry it across. But first he needed a vessel that could survive what had happened to the Jeannette.

Nansen worked with shipbuilder Colin Archer. Instead of upright sides for the ice to press against, their vessel had a rounded hull. As the ice squeezed in, the hull was meant to slide upwards. The crew could then live on the ship even while it was trapped. Archer built it in Larvik, and Nansen's wife Eva named it Fram, meaning forward.

On June twenty-fourth, 1893, thirteen men set out from Norway. By September they were in the ice near the New Siberian Islands. On some days the drift carried them north; on others it took them back south. By Christmas they were only around seventy-nine degrees north. But even when the ship hardly moved, the crew could investigate the sea beneath it.

They lowered a weighted line into the water, expecting to reach the seabed. It wasn't long enough. Nansen had expected a much shallower sea, so the ship lacked the right equipment. The crew took apart a steel cable and made a new sounding line out on the ice in temperatures as low as minus forty Celsius. With it they measured depths of more than three kilometres. Already, the map Nansen had imagined was beginning to change.

Then, in January 1895, enormous masses of ice pressed against the Fram. The men moved food and equipment onto the ice and got ready to abandon the ship. But the hull lifted between the floes. The vessel survived. The scientific voyage could continue.

The route, however, was unlikely to take the Fram to the North Pole. Nansen decided to leave the drifting laboratory and attempt to reach the pole on foot. Captain Otto Sverdrup would take charge of the eleven-person ship party. Nansen chose Hjalmar Johansen to go with him. Once the two had lost sight of the ship, they could not rely on finding it again. Their return route would have to take them towards land.

Their first departure, in late February 1895, was a failure. They found it too difficult to move their heavy equipment across the ice. They returned to the ship and worked on the sledges. A second attempt failed as well. On March fourteenth they left for the third time, at a latitude of just over eighty-four degrees north.

Three men from the ship accompanied the pair to their first camp, then watched them disappear across the ice. Five days after the departure, Sverdrup put his crew to work on a ridge piled against one side of the Fram. He was concerned that the ice could push the ship down if the ridge stayed there. It took them more than a week to cut and cart away enough ice. The rounded hull had survived January, but the crew still had work to do to keep it safe.

Meanwhile, Nansen and Johansen were hauling their equipment across a surface that kept breaking up. They had to repair harnesses damaged by the dogs, lift sledges over ridges and detour around open water. They could spend a day travelling north and discover that the ice beneath them had moved south. In early April they reached eighty-six degrees, fourteen minutes north. It was a record, but the pole was still hundreds of kilometres away. Continuing would leave less time and fewer supplies for the return, so they turned south.

Sverdrup kept up the scientific work. In April his crew tried to measure the depth again, but the sounding line broke and they lost hundreds of metres of it. They tried extending the steel line with hemp rope, only to lose more of their equipment. Sverdrup suspended the soundings until July. The discovery of the deep sea had made their research much harder than they had planned.

By May Nansen and Johansen were down to two sledges and twelve dogs out of the original twenty-eight. They paddled their kayaks where there was water and dragged them back onto the ice when the route was blocked. In August a polar bear attacked Johansen. Nansen killed it, and Johansen escaped without serious injury. Soon they reached land in the Franz Josef archipelago.

There was no settlement waiting for them. With winter coming, they had to build somewhere to live. They dug a hollow, made walls of stone, filled gaps with moss and used a log washed up on shore to support a walrus-hide roof. Animal fat gave them fuel, and the meat from their hunting supplied food. They adapted a sleeping bag so both men could fit inside.

Nansen's diary records a very different Christmas from the one they would have celebrated at home. They turned their shirts inside out, and Nansen managed a wash with a tiny amount of warmed water. Inside the hut, a change of clothing was something worth recording.

Back aboard the Fram, Sverdrup's men had also made preparations in case they ever lost their home. Earlier that year they had built spare sledges and kayaks and even adapted an ice saw so they could cut oak for snowshoes. The ship kept drifting. It came almost as far north as Nansen and Johansen had gone on foot, then slowly headed towards Svalbard.

In May 1896, the two men left their winter shelter. Their kayaks were often lashed together. Once, while they were on a floe, the boats drifted away from them. The boats carried the means to continue their journey. Nansen entered the icy water, reached them and recovered them.

In June they reached Cape Flora. There they came across members of an entirely different expedition, led by the British explorer Frederick Jackson. Neither Nansen nor Johansen had known they would find help there. Jackson took them into his camp. They could now return aboard the expedition's ship, Windward.

There is a photograph of Nansen and Jackson greeting each other on the island. It is a reconstruction of their meeting, made six days afterward, not a photograph of the instant they met.

On August thirteenth, 1896, the Windward reached Vardø in Norway. On that same day the Fram finally escaped the ice near Svalbard. Neither group had been able to tell the other what had happened. On August twentieth, Sverdrup brought the ship to Skjervøy in Norway. The two parties reunited in Tromsø. All thirteen men were alive.

Nansen had originally expected shallow water in much of the Arctic. He had come home with measurements of a deep ocean instead. The crew had also found warmer water beneath a colder surface layer and recorded how the ice moved. The same drifting ice that had carried wreckage to Greenland had now carried a working laboratory across the Arctic.

The Fram itself was still usable. Years later Roald Amundsen took it to Antarctica. The ship built in response to the loss of the Jeannette went on to serve another polar expedition. The North Pole had remained beyond Nansen's reach, but the experiment beneath the ice had worked, and everyone who had set out with him came home.
