Why did the first insulin injection fail to save a boy with diabetes? And what happened when his doctors tried again twelve days later?

In January 1922, Leonard Thompson lay in a hospital in Toronto. He was a teenager and weighed just sixty-five pounds. His diet had been cut to about four hundred and fifty calories a day. Even that had not brought his diabetes under control.

Without enough insulin, the body cannot use and store glucose normally. Sugar accumulates in the blood and spills into the urine. The body starts breaking down its own fat. Cutting back on food can delay the consequences, but it cannot replace the missing hormone.

On January eleventh, Leonard received an extract made from animal pancreases. His blood sugar fell somewhat, but his condition did not noticeably improve.

The researchers had a problem. They could get an active substance out of a pancreas, but they still needed a preparation that worked in a patient.

Biochemist James Collip refined the extract. On January twenty-third, Leonard received the new preparation. The difference appeared in his hospital records. On the day before the injection, he had lost nearly a hundred and forty-nine grams of glucose in his urine. On January twenty-fourth, that figure was under nine grams. He became more active and felt stronger.

Then came another important observation. When the injections stopped for several days in February, the sugar returned in large quantities. When treatment resumed, it fell again.

Insulin had not repaired Leonard's pancreas. It was replacing something his body could no longer supply.

He lived for another thirteen years. What began with a disappointing injection became a treatment that could be given again, day after day, to keep someone alive.
