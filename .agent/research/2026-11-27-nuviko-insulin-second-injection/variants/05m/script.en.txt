What changed between the first and second insulin injections given to Leonard Thompson? Why did a doctor in 1922 have to keep a patient with diabetes on a near-starvation diet? And how did an extract from an animal organ become something that could keep him alive for years?

In Toronto, at the beginning of January 1922, a teenage boy was being treated for severe diabetes. Leonard Thompson weighed sixty-five pounds. His hospital doctors were trying to limit the amount of sugar his body had to deal with. They put him on a diet of about four hundred and fifty calories a day.

The treatment was not working. His body was losing glucose in his urine, and the notes described a severely undernourished boy who was weak and listless. He had already been subjected to dietary restriction before arriving at the hospital.

To understand why this was so difficult, think about the problem from the patient's side. Food provides energy and material to keep the body functioning. Insulin helps the body manage the glucose that comes from food. When the pancreas fails to supply enough insulin, glucose accumulates in the blood. The body is also forced to rely heavily on fat, producing substances called ketone bodies. In severe diabetes this can become life-threatening.

Doctors could reduce the burden by restricting food. They could not give back the hormone Leonard needed.

A few months earlier, at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting and Charles Best had been studying extracts taken from animal pancreases in a laboratory provided by John Macleod. They could measure changes in the blood sugar of diabetic dogs. The experiments indicated that a substance made in the pancreas could bring that sugar down.

Getting an extract to affect a laboratory animal was one thing. Giving it to a sick child was another.

On January eleventh, Leonard received an injection of pancreatic extract. The blood sugar readings went down by roughly a quarter. But the boy did not noticeably improve. The first clinical attempt had failed to deliver the result the team needed.

There were two problems bound up in the same bottle: getting enough of the active substance and removing other material from the animal tissue. A biochemist, James Collip, worked on purifying the extract. Rather than starting over with another theory of diabetes, the team tried a better preparation.

On January twenty-third, the doctors gave Leonard the new extract. They had continued to collect measurements under the same restricted dietary regime. Those measurements now told a very different story.

The day before, Leonard's urine had contained about a hundred and forty-nine grams of glucose. On January twenty-fourth, it contained less than nine. The compounds associated with excessive fat breakdown disappeared during effective treatment. Leonard became more active. He reported feeling stronger.

One good day could still leave the doctors with a question. Had they found a treatment that would continue to work?

Leonard did not receive the extract for a stretch in February. The reason is not established by his clinical chart. But the result of the interruption is there: large amounts of glucose appeared in his urine again, along with traces of the compounds that had disappeared. After injections resumed, the glucose losses fell again.

This was not what a permanent cure would look like. It was evidence of something immensely valuable nevertheless. The doctors could repeatedly replace a missing part of the body's chemistry.

Other patients began receiving insulin. A new problem emerged: there were far more people who needed it than the Toronto laboratory could supply. Producing a dependable extract was now as important as discovering it.

In 1922, the university's researchers and manufacturing partners worked to increase production. Patents were assigned to the university for a nominal dollar each, and agreements with manufacturers helped move insulin beyond the research laboratory. Cheap patent transfers did not make it costless to manufacture, but they show how the researchers chose to organize the next stage of the discovery.

Leonard himself lived until 1935. He did not leave the hospital with a pancreas that suddenly worked. He gained another thirteen years of life with treatment.

The decisive advance was not simply that an injection lowered a number on a chart. The first injection had already done that. It was that a preparation could make a gravely ill patient better, and that giving it again could maintain the improvement.

That is what the second injection gave Leonard Thompson. It turned a laboratory discovery into the possibility of a life beyond the hospital.
