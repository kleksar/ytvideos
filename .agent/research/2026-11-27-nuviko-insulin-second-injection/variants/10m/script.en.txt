Why did the first insulin injection given to a boy with diabetes fail to make him well? How could his doctors tell that a second preparation was genuinely working? And why did a successful result in a Toronto hospital still leave the researchers with a problem big enough to require factories?

The first person in this story is not a scientist. He is a patient, Leonard Thompson, a teenager in Toronto with severe diabetes.

At the start of January 1922, his doctors were trying to control the disease with food. They reduced his diet to about four hundred and fifty calories a day. Leonard weighed sixty-five pounds. He had been on dietary restrictions even before admission to the hospital, and he was severely undernourished.

The hospital could record his weight. It could measure the sugar in his blood and urine. What it could not yet do was reliably provide the substance that his pancreas was failing to produce.

For a patient like Leonard, this created a vicious contradiction. He needed food in order to grow and maintain his body. Yet his body could not manage the glucose from that food normally. When insulin is lacking, glucose accumulates in the blood, and some of it is lost in the urine. The body also breaks down fat much more rapidly, producing substances called ketone bodies. In severe diabetes that process can become dangerous.

A doctor could try to slow the disease by reducing the patient's food intake. The price was obvious in Leonard's hospital notes. He was weak, inactive, and wasting away. The diet had not stopped the disease.

The idea of finding something in the pancreas that could control diabetes did not begin with Leonard. Earlier scientists had already linked the pancreas to the regulation of blood sugar. Other researchers had tried extracts from the organ in human patients before 1922. Obtaining a usable treatment had proved much more difficult than recognizing the organ's importance.

An organ contains a mixture of substances. If a scientist grinds up pancreatic tissue, the resulting liquid is not simply a bottle of insulin. The active substance must survive the preparation. There must be enough of it to have an effect, and the unwanted material needs to be removed as far as possible.

There was also a problem of evidence. Without a practical way to measure glucose repeatedly, it was much harder to compare preparations and find out which method was producing a useful result.

In 1921, Frederick Banting came to the University of Toronto to pursue the problem. John Macleod provided laboratory facilities and Charles Best joined Banting in the experimental work. They used animals with diabetes and measured their blood sugar as they investigated pancreatic extracts.

The immediate goal was limited but crucial: show that the extract had an effect that could be followed in measurements. The scientists were not yet trying to manufacture a finished medicine for thousands of people.

The team obtained preparations that lowered glucose in their experimental animals. That narrowed the question. A substance with the required activity was present in the pancreas. But the experiment had not established that a preparation could be given safely and effectively to a gravely ill person.

Now return to Leonard's hospital bed.

On January eleventh, 1922, the doctors gave him an extract from animal pancreases. His blood glucose declined by roughly a quarter. That might sound like a successful result. It was not enough. Leonard showed no noticeable clinical improvement.

A smaller number in the laboratory record was important, but the boy was still sick. The researchers needed more than a single favorable measurement.

James Collip, a biochemist, joined the effort to make the extract suitable for clinical use. His work used chemical separation to improve the preparation. Imagine the distinction between the two parts of the task. The first was finding the activity that could lower glucose. The second was getting that activity into a preparation which could help the patient, rather than merely demonstrating it.

Twelve days after Leonard's first injection, the doctors tried again.

The hospital records give us a way of comparing the result without having to invent a dramatic bedside scene. The doctors had kept measuring his glucose while his food intake remained restricted. In the twenty-four-hour collection recorded on January twenty-second, Leonard's urine contained nearly a hundred and forty-nine grams of glucose.

On January twenty-third, he received the refined extract. The record for the twenty-fourth lists less than nine grams of glucose in his urine. The blood glucose readings improved as well. The compounds associated with heavy fat breakdown disappeared during periods of effective treatment. Leonard was becoming more active and reported feeling stronger.

Consider what those measurements meant to the doctors. The amount of glucose lost in a single day had fallen by well over a hundred grams. There was a change in the patient's condition to go with the change in the measurements. This was a far more convincing result than the first injection.

The next part of the hospital record is particularly revealing. Leonard did not receive the extract during a period from February fifth to February fifteenth. We cannot establish from the record why treatment was interrupted, and it would be wrong to portray this as an intentional modern controlled trial.

But we can read what happened. Large amounts of glucose returned to his urine. Traces of the substances associated with excessive fat breakdown returned, too. When insulin treatment began again, glucose losses were reduced once more.

That was a vital distinction. If the injections had cured the underlying disease, the problem would not have returned simply because Leonard stopped receiving the extract. The treatment was replacing a function that the boy's own pancreas was not supplying.

An effective preparation was only the first stage of what he needed. He needed continued access to it.

The Toronto team described Leonard's progress alongside the results from other patients in a medical paper published in March 1922. The researchers now had evidence that the treatment was not limited to one boy.

Other young patients arrived in Toronto during the following months. Among them was Teddy Ryder, a little boy who had become severely underweight with diabetes. He began receiving insulin in 1922 and eventually lived into his seventies. Elizabeth Hughes was another young patient who had lost a great deal of weight while living on an extremely restricted diet. She received insulin and lived for decades afterward.

These were not cases of a disease simply disappearing. They were lives made possible by a continuing supply of treatment.

And that supply could not be guaranteed just because the experiments had succeeded.

By the spring of 1922, Toronto's production facilities were struggling to provide enough insulin. The raw material came from animal organs, and every step between obtaining those organs and delivering a usable preparation mattered. A laboratory might make a small batch successfully. A manufacturer needed to make many batches with dependable activity.

The university worked with its own production facilities and with industrial partners, including Eli Lilly. The challenge was to increase the amount of medicine without losing the properties that had made the new preparation effective in the hospital.

The researchers also made decisions about ownership. The insulin patents were assigned to the University of Toronto for a nominal payment of one dollar each. The university used arrangements with manufacturers and research funding to organize development and distribution. The famous dollar figure describes the patent assignment; it was not the price of making insulin, nor a guarantee that every patient everywhere could afford treatment.

In 1923, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Banting and Macleod. Banting shared his prize money with Best, and Macleod shared his with Collip. The awards and arguments over credit are part of the history. But the clinical result makes the importance of their different contributions particularly clear.

The experiments identified an active substance. Chemical work made a better preparation possible. The hospital team measured and cared for patients. Larger-scale production made it possible to provide more than a handful of injections.

None of those steps on its own would have been enough for Leonard.

He lived until 1935, another thirteen years after the first injections. His was not the longest life made possible by early insulin treatment, as the later lives of other patients showed. It was the beginning of an approach that would change what a diagnosis of severe diabetes could mean.

The small interval between the two January injections is where that transformation becomes visible. On the first occasion, a measurement moved, but Leonard did not get better. On the second, a much more effective preparation changed both the measurements and his condition. The interruption in February demonstrated something equally important: he still needed the medicine.

The doctors had not made his disease disappear. They had found something he could receive repeatedly in place of what his body was missing.

For a boy who had been living on a few hundred calories a day, that difference meant another thirteen years.
