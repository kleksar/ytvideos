Why did the first insulin injection given to a boy with diabetes fail to make him well? What changed twelve days later? And why, even after the doctors found something that worked, did they struggle to keep their patients alive?

In January 1922, Leonard Thompson was in Toronto General Hospital with severe diabetes. He weighed sixty-five pounds. From early January his doctors restricted him to about four hundred and fifty calories a day. His diet had been restricted before he arrived at the hospital, and he was severely undernourished.

A child needs food to grow. Leonard's body, however, could no longer use and store glucose normally. Without enough insulin, glucose builds up in the blood and some is lost in the urine. The body breaks down fat, producing ketones that in severe diabetes can accumulate to dangerous levels. Reducing the amount of food could delay the consequences, but it could not give Leonard the missing hormone.

On January eleventh, a hospital physician, Ed Jeffrey, administered an extract made from beef pancreas by Frederick Banting and Charles Best. The amount of glucose in Leonard's blood fell by roughly a quarter. Yet his condition did not noticeably improve. An abscess formed at an injection site, and the doctors discontinued that preparation.

For the Toronto researchers, this was a serious setback. They had already spent months developing extracts that could reduce blood sugar in diabetic dogs. In the hospital they were dealing with a boy who was still losing weight and strength despite a severely restricted diet. An extract that produced a result in an animal was not necessarily suitable for Leonard.

The search for a pancreatic treatment was older than the Toronto project. Other researchers had tried extracts on patients before 1922. The team needed a preparation that could survive the process of being separated from the animal tissue and could be given repeatedly without the problems that had defeated earlier attempts.

In 1921, John Macleod had provided Banting with laboratory facilities at the University of Toronto. Best worked with Banting on the experiments. Repeated blood sugar measurements let them compare the effects of different preparations. By the time of the first clinical attempt they had evidence of useful activity in the pancreas. The question now was how to get that activity into a reliable medicine.

Biochemist James Collip was working on purification. He found a way to separate more of the unwanted material while retaining the active substance. His preparation reduced blood sugar in experimental animals without the troublesome reaction that had accompanied Leonard's first injection. It was time to return to the hospital.

Twelve days after the initial attempt, physician Walter Campbell administered the new preparation to Leonard. The team continued to collect measurements while the boy remained on his restricted diet. The records give us a remarkable comparison across the two days surrounding the injection.

On January twenty-second, Leonard lost nearly a hundred and forty-nine grams of glucose in his urine. He received Collip's extract on the twenty-third. On the twenty-fourth, the amount in his urine was less than nine grams. The blood glucose readings improved as well, and during effective treatment the ketones disappeared. Leonard became more active and reported feeling stronger.

The doctors could now see the difference in their patient, not merely in his laboratory results. But they still needed to know how long the improvement would last.

In February, Leonard went without the extract for a period. The published clinical record does not explain why. Large quantities of glucose returned to his urine and traces of the ketones appeared again. When the injections resumed, his glucose losses fell once more.

That observation answered an important question. Insulin had not repaired Leonard's pancreas. His body still needed what the injections supplied. A treatment that could be repeated might keep him alive, but only as long as the medicine remained available.

In March, the Toronto team published their results, including the cases of six other patients. A supply sufficient for a small hospital trial now had to meet the needs of more and more people. By May, attempts to expand production in the university's Connaught Laboratories had failed to keep up. The university reached an agreement with the American manufacturer Eli Lilly, but shortages did not vanish overnight.

In July, there was another patient at Toronto General Hospital who made the consequences of a shortage painfully clear. Charlotte Clarke needed surgery for a badly infected leg. Banting temporarily reduced the insulin available to five other patients so that Clarke could receive treatment. She was able to undergo the operation. When the supply ran short again, however, her condition deteriorated.

Banting and chemist David Scott went to Indianapolis, where the Lilly team offered help and showed them its production equipment. Banting returned with insulin. Clarke received it again and recovered. This was another example of how the supply of the extract could determine the course of a patient's illness.

Back in Toronto, Banting wanted equipment that could improve production. He asked the university for money, but the request was deferred until the autumn. He travelled to New York, where a physician helped secure a donation of ten thousand dollars. The university accepted the money in August and the new equipment could be ordered.

Even when the extract was available, its strength could vary. Imagine what that meant for somebody trying to recover at home. The problem is particularly clear in the letters of another young patient, Elizabeth Hughes, who reached Toronto in August 1922.

Elizabeth had been living on an extremely restricted diet and weighed about forty-five pounds. Her mother arranged for Banting to treat her, while her nurse Blanche Burgess stayed with her. A week later Elizabeth wrote to her mother about the prospect of eating a much more substantial diet and eventually studying again. What had previously been impossible was becoming a realistic plan.

In September she wrote another letter. She had gained weight, but a new batch of insulin was only about half as strong as the preceding one. During the earlier course of treatment she had also experienced episodes of excessively low blood sugar. The letter describes the challenge of adjusting her care as the strength of the extract changed. Producing enough bottles was not the whole problem; the medicine in them had to be dependable.

At Connaught and at Lilly, chemists worked on improving the purification process and obtaining more consistent preparations. The equipment and the supply of animal pancreases both mattered. By the beginning of 1923, production and quality had improved enough for treatment to spread much further.

The researchers assigned their insulin patent rights to the University of Toronto for a nominal dollar each. That figure is sometimes confused with the cost of the medicine. It actually describes the transfer of the patents. Factories still had to buy raw material, produce and test the extract, and get it to the people who depended on it.

Elizabeth lived for decades after her treatment began. Another young patient, Teddy Ryder, also treated in 1922, lived into his seventies. Their lives give us a sense of the change that was beginning in Toronto. The medicine meant continuing care rather than an end to the disease.

And Leonard? He lived until 1935, thirteen years after the two January injections. The first attempt had shown the doctors what a pancreatic extract could do to a laboratory measurement. The second gave them something that could improve his condition. February revealed why he would need it again and again.

The boy who had been living on a few hundred calories a day had thirteen more years of life. What made those years possible was an effective medicine that could keep reaching him after he left the hospital.
