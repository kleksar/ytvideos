Jerrie Mock was flying toward Bermuda when she discovered that her long-range radio didn't work. She had never flown across an ocean before.

It was March 1964. Mock was a mother of three from Ohio, and she had spent more than a year preparing to fly around the world alone. Her little Cessna had one engine and three additional fuel tanks.

She had originally wanted to see the world. Then she learned that no woman had ever completed a solo flight around it. And just before she left, another pilot, Joan Merriam Smith, set off on a longer route.

Mock reached Bermuda. On her next crossing, ice built up on the wings. She got above the clouds, where the sun melted it.

Over Libya, the wire for her radio antenna started smoking. She switched off its reel, and it cooled.

In Egypt, she landed at the wrong airfield. Soldiers approached her plane. While she waited for permission to leave the military base, she ended up watching television with them.

In Saudi Arabia, a man looked into her cockpit to check whether a man was aboard. Finding none, he told the crowd, and they applauded.

She still had the Pacific to cross. She stopped on islands for fuel and finally flew from Hawaii to California.

On April seventeenth, after twenty-nine days and about twenty-three thousand miles, Mock landed back in Columbus. She was the first woman to fly solo around the world. Smith finished her own longer journey in May.

Mock's airplane is now in the Smithsonian collection. That record began with a very ordinary piece of equipment failing on her first ocean crossing. What went wrong?
