How did a pilot who had never crossed an ocean become the first woman to fly around the world alone? Why did she leave earlier than planned? And what happened when her little airplane landed at a military base that wasn't on her map?

In 1937, an eleven-year-old girl in Ohio followed Amelia Earhart's attempt to fly around the world on the radio. Earhart disappeared over the Pacific. The girl, Geraldine Mock, grew up wanting to fly beyond the places she knew.

In 1958, she and her husband earned pilot's licenses. Later, while visiting the island of Saint Pierre near Canada, she listened to pilots crossing the Atlantic over the radio. She wanted to make that crossing herself. From there, going all the way around the world seemed worth trying.

When Mock asked the American organization that certified aviation records about the rules, she discovered that no woman had completed a solo flight around the globe. But she couldn't simply point her airplane east and go. She had to arrange fuel stops, obtain permission to enter countries, and prepare for long stretches of water.

Her red-and-white Cessna 180 was an eleven-year-old, single-engine airplane. It was fitted with three extra fuel tanks and additional radio and navigation equipment. She earned her instrument rating, so she could navigate when she couldn't see the ground. A Columbus newspaper helped finance the journey.

Then she learned another pilot was going too. Joan Merriam Smith intended to follow a route closer to the equator, inspired by Earhart's journey. Smith departed from California. Mock brought her own departure forward to March nineteenth, two days after Smith. The newspapers now had two women to follow.

On Mock's first leg toward Bermuda, she discovered that her long-range radio wasn't working. She had never crossed an ocean before, and the equipment she had installed for this trip was already failing.

She made it to Bermuda, and the next crossing toward the Azores brought a different problem. Ice formed on the wings. She climbed above the clouds and the sun eventually cleared the ice.

On land, she finally had a chance to see some of the places she'd dreamed about. In Casablanca, friends took her out for a meal she would remember for the rest of her life. But there was pressure from home to spend less time sightseeing and keep moving.

As she flew over Libya, the wire associated with her trailing radio antenna began to smoke. There was fuel nearby. Mock recognized the source, switched off the antenna reel, and the wire cooled. No fire developed.

The next problem arrived after she touched the ground. Approaching Cairo, Mock landed at an airfield and found soldiers coming toward her. She had mistaken a military base for her destination. She couldn't simply take off again. While she waited for authorization to leave, she watched television with the soldiers. Then she flew to the airport she had intended to reach.

In Saudi Arabia, another unexpected reception awaited her. A man from the crowd inspected the cockpit, apparently looking for a male pilot. When he announced that there was no man aboard, the crowd applauded.

Mock continued east through Pakistan, India, Thailand and the Philippines. In Manila, her Cessna could be serviced at a Cessna repair shop before the long Pacific crossings. Smith, meanwhile, had been delayed by problems with her airplane's fuel tanks in South America. The two pilots were not flying the same course, and Mock's route was shorter.

After Guam, Wake Island and Hawaii came the last great stretch of water. Mock flew from Honolulu to California, nearly twenty-four hundred miles over the Pacific. Then there was only the American continent to cross.

On April seventeenth, the Cessna landed in Columbus. Mock had circled the globe in twenty-nine days, covering about twenty-three thousand miles. The record was certified. Smith completed her own longer solo journey in May, a remarkable flight in its own right.

President Johnson honored Mock the following month. She continued setting aviation records, but that first world flight answered the question she had asked herself years earlier in Saint Pierre. She could fly across an ocean, and she could keep going.

Her Cessna belongs to the Smithsonian today. The little airplane that left Ohio with one pilot and a typewriter came back with a world record.
