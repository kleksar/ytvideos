How do you prepare for a flight around the world when you've never crossed an ocean? What happens when the radio you bought for the trip goes silent? And how did an American pilot end up watching television with soldiers at the wrong airfield?

In 1937, an eleven-year-old girl in Ohio was following Amelia Earhart's world flight on the radio. After Earhart disappeared, she listened to the news of the search. Geraldine Mock went on to study aeronautical engineering, got married and had three children. She and her husband Russell earned private pilot certificates in 1958. On a trip to Saint Pierre, off Newfoundland, Mock discovered a radio room at her hotel. She heard pilots talking as they crossed the Atlantic. She decided she wanted to make that flight herself, and then thought she might as well go all the way around the world.

She wrote to the National Aeronautic Association to ask about the rules. The answer surprised her: no woman had yet completed the journey solo. In a later interview she recalled keeping her plans quiet. She knew women pilots in Ohio with wartime experience who might obtain a sponsor and get there before her.

At her dining-room table in Ohio, Mock worked out a route with maps and a globe. Some airfields were off limits, some countries were reluctant to admit her, and officials needed to clear her entry. An Air Force friend helped with the route; the Mocks visited embassies in Washington. To certify the record, the journey had to exceed roughly twenty-two thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine miles, with designated officials confirming her progress at stops. Mock later remembered that all this arranging had been harder than flying.

Her Cessna 180 had been built in 1953. She called it Charlie, after the last letter of its registration. The three spare seats were replaced by fuel tanks; the airplane also got extra navigation and radio equipment and an autopilot. She earned her instrument rating shortly before departure, with about seven hundred and fifty flight hours behind her. The Columbus Dispatch helped finance the journey and she was to write about it for the newspaper. She packed a typewriter and a skirt and sweater for formal arrivals.

Then came news of Joan Merriam Smith, another pilot preparing a solo world flight. Smith's twin-engine Piper Apache would follow a longer route, closer to the equator, in the spirit of Earhart's journey. Mock's completed paperwork gave her official sanction for the women's speed-record attempt, but that didn't stop newspapers from comparing the two pilots. She moved her departure forward to March nineteenth, two days after Smith left California.

On her first leg from Columbus toward Bermuda, Mock tried to contact the radio station serving flights over the Atlantic. Nothing came back. She tried to get her new long-range set working, but it was dead. She recalled in her memoir weighing whether to return home or land in Virginia, with the sponsors and the farewell crowd on her mind. She reported the trouble while she could still reach a station on land. Then she continued toward Bermuda.

She reached Bermuda in strong winds and ended up spending about a week on the island because of the weather. The delay gave the mechanics a chance to investigate. Getting to the radio wiring required removing a large fuel tank from beside her seat. Once a mechanic could reach the wiring, he discovered a disconnected lead. Mock suspected interference, but no one established who had disconnected it or why. With the radio repaired, the weather still determined when she could leave.

Her next crossing took her toward the island of Santa Maria in the Azores. Ice collected on the airplane's wings. That changes how well a wing works, and on a small airplane it is a serious problem. Mock got above the clouds, where sunlight eventually melted the ice. She made it to the islands.

When she landed, she found a place very different from Ohio. In her later recollection, she couldn't even make a telephone call home from the Azores. She visited a church associated with Columbus and his voyage. She had come across the Atlantic to see places like this, not just to accumulate miles.

At Casablanca, friends took her to a restaurant. Fifty years later she still remembered the couscous. Yet the adventure she'd planned was increasingly governed by a race she'd never set out to enter. According to Mock's later account, Russell urged her onward and sometimes gave her inaccurate reports of Smith's position. Smith had encountered fuel-tank trouble in South America. And even without any delays, her longer route was a different undertaking. Getting to Columbus first would not settle which pilot had flown farther.

Mock crossed North Africa, and over Libya something started to smoke inside her airplane. It was connected with the wire antenna used for her radio. There was fuel nearby. In an interview many years later, she explained that she recognized the problem and disconnected the power to the antenna reel. The wire cooled. The feared fire never occurred.

Near Cairo, Mock landed on a runway and saw soldiers coming toward her airplane. She had mistaken a military base absent from her map for her destination. Now she needed clearance to leave. While she waited, she watched television with the soldiers. They eventually let her fly to the airport she'd been trying to reach.

Even at the right airport, the paperwork could be absurd. Mock's memoir describes an immigration official who insisted she needed a plane ticket before she could leave Cairo. She was flying her own airplane, so there was no ticket to show him. She did get to see the pyramids, though the schedule left her only a few minutes at the Great Pyramid. She also got to ride a camel, one of the things she'd wanted to do since childhood.

At her stop in Saudi Arabia, a man looked into the cockpit. He wanted to establish that there was no male pilot hidden away inside. When he reported what he'd found, the people around the airplane applauded. The little Cessna had brought a woman flying alone into a country where women weren't then allowed to drive cars.

The route then took her through Pakistan and India. She had wanted to see the Taj Mahal, but according to her account she neither stopped there nor flew over it, because the airspace was restricted. By the time she reached Manila via Thailand, the long Pacific legs were next. She could get Charlie serviced at a workshop familiar with her type of airplane.

She flew on to Guam, Wake Island and Hawaii. Her last ocean leg took her from Honolulu to Oakland, about twenty-four hundred miles. She had crossed both oceans. But the map was still open: the record required her to return to the point where she had started.

On April seventeenth, a crowd was waiting at the airport in Columbus. The Cessna touched down twenty-nine days after it had departed. Mock had traveled about twenty-three thousand miles. The flight was certified, and she became the first woman to fly alone around the world.

Smith finished in May. She had flown almost twenty-eight thousand miles, farther south than Mock, and received that year's Harmon Trophy for a woman pilot. The two journeys had produced two different achievements.

President Lyndon Johnson honored Mock on May fourth. She kept flying and set more records, including a nonstop journey from Honolulu to Columbus in 1966.

Charlie is now on display in the Smithsonian. Years before her flight, Mock had listened to strangers crossing the Atlantic from a hotel radio room on Saint Pierre. Decades after her return, she remembered women telling her that her example had helped them decide to fly.
