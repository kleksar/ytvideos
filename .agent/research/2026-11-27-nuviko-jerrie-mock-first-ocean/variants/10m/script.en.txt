What does it take to fly around the world in a little airplane, with no other pilot aboard? How did a woman who had never crossed an ocean prepare for two of them? And why did the first woman to complete that journey end up watching television with soldiers at the wrong airfield?

In Ohio, in the spring of 1964, a red-and-white Cessna was being prepared for a journey that would take it far beyond the American Midwest. Its pilot was thirty-eight years old, had three children, and had been flying for only a few years.

Geraldine Mock had been fascinated by distant countries long before she became a pilot. At school, geography introduced her to places she could barely imagine from home. In 1937, she followed Amelia Earhart's progress around the world on the radio, and then the reports of the search after Earhart vanished over the Pacific.

Mock studied aeronautical engineering at Ohio State University, left college to marry, and started a family. She and her husband, Russell, earned private pilot certificates in 1958. Flying opened up a way to travel that she could actually control.

On a visit to the French island of Saint Pierre, just off Newfoundland, she found a radio room at the hotel. She listened to the pilots crossing the Atlantic. They were traveling between continents, not just between American cities. She decided she wanted to cross the ocean too.

A single crossing turned into a much more ambitious plan. Why not continue around the whole planet? She contacted the National Aeronautic Association to find out how a world flight was officially recognized. That was when she learned that no woman had yet completed the flight solo.

This discovery changed the project. There was a record available, but other women could claim it. She knew there were experienced women pilots who had flown during the Second World War. If one of them got the financial backing and arranged a world flight, she could finish first.

Mock's own plan involved a surprising amount of work that had nothing to do with operating an airplane. Where could she land? Which countries would issue permission? How far could she fly between fuel stops? For months she contacted officials, exchanged messages and visited embassies. An Air Force friend helped with the route. Mock later said that arranging the journey had been more difficult than flying it.

For a certified world flight, going around the planet wasn't enough on its own. There was a minimum distance of about twenty-two thousand eight hundred and fifty-nine miles. Her route had to satisfy that requirement, and designated officials at her stops would document her progress.

Her airplane, a Cessna 180 built in 1953, was a high-wing machine with one engine. The aircraft was called Spirit of Columbus, but Mock called it Charlie, after the final letter in its radio registration.

Three additional tanks gave the airplane the range needed for long flights. It was equipped with more radio and navigation gear, including a long-range set with a trailing antenna, and an autopilot. Mock earned a qualification to fly by instruments. She had about seven hundred and fifty hours of experience, but had never flown across an ocean.

The local newspaper, the Columbus Dispatch, helped support the expedition. Mock packed clothes and a typewriter. She chose a skirt and sweater for her arrival at foreign airports, where she expected to deal with officials and formal receptions. Her plane and her luggage reflected two completely different parts of the same trip: hours alone in the cockpit, followed by meetings with people she'd never met.

Then, shortly before the planned departure in April, she received news of another world flight.

Joan Merriam Smith had been preparing an expedition of her own. Smith flew a twin-engine Piper Apache. She wanted to travel nearer the equator, following the general ambition of Earhart's route. Both women had applied for official recognition of their planned flights. Mock's completed paperwork gave her the official sanction for the women's speed-record attempt.

The public, of course, could still see which woman arrived home first. Mock moved up her departure to March nineteenth. Smith had already left California two days earlier.

Mock took off from Columbus with her first stop in Bermuda. This was the beginning of her very first ocean crossing. And the long-range radio that had been installed for precisely this kind of journey didn't work.

She continued and reached Bermuda. Later she found a disconnected wire associated with the failure. She suspected someone had interfered with the equipment, but that suspicion has not been established as fact.

Her next crossing took her toward the island of Santa Maria in the Azores. Ice collected on the airplane's wings. That changes how well a wing works, and on a small airplane it is a serious problem. Mock got above the clouds, where sunlight eventually melted the ice. She made it to the islands.

When she landed, she found a place very different from Ohio. In her later recollection, she couldn't even make a telephone call home from the Azores. She visited a church associated with Columbus and his voyage. She had come across the Atlantic to see places like this, not just to accumulate miles.

In Casablanca, friends took her out to eat. Decades later she still remembered the couscous. Moments like that were getting squeezed by the timetable. According to Mock's own later account, her husband pressed her to move faster and at times gave her misleading information about how far Smith had traveled.

Smith was actually dealing with problems with her fuel tanks in South America. It is important to separate the two flights. Mock was taking a course that met the world-flight distance requirement. Smith was making a longer journey farther south. They didn't begin at the same airport and weren't following identical rules for a head-to-head race.

Mock crossed North Africa, and over Libya something started to smoke inside her airplane. It was connected with the wire antenna used for her radio. There was fuel nearby. In an interview many years later, she explained that she recognized the problem and disconnected the power to the antenna reel. The wire cooled. The feared fire never occurred.

Egypt brought a problem no instrument rating could solve on its own. Mock approached the Cairo area and landed at the wrong airfield. Soldiers came toward the Cessna. She had accidentally arrived at a military base that didn't appear on her map.

Her next task wasn't to fly anywhere. It was to get permission to leave. While the necessary clearance was being arranged, she watched television with the soldiers. Then she was allowed to take the airplane to the proper airport.

For all the time she spent navigating by instruments and dealing with officials, she still managed to see the pyramids and ride a camel. Those were the sorts of experiences that had inspired the trip in the first place.

At her stop in Saudi Arabia, a man looked into the cockpit. He wanted to establish that there was no male pilot hidden away inside. When he reported what he'd found, the people around the airplane applauded. The little Cessna had brought a woman flying alone into a country where women weren't then allowed to drive cars.

From there, Mock moved through Pakistan, India and Thailand to Manila. The last part of the trip would involve long crossings of the Pacific. In Manila she could finally have Charlie serviced at a shop for her type of airplane before those crossings.

She flew on to Guam, Wake Island and Hawaii. The islands gave her places to land in the vast stretch of ocean between Asia and the American mainland. On her last ocean leg, from Honolulu to Oakland, she covered about twenty-four hundred miles.

After that, she was back on the American mainland, but the official journey wasn't over. She needed to return to her starting point.

On April seventeenth, a crowd was waiting at the airport in Columbus. The Cessna touched down twenty-nine days after it had departed. Mock had traveled about twenty-three thousand miles. The flight was certified, and she became the first woman to fly alone around the world.

Smith completed her journey in May. It was nearly twenty-eight thousand miles and earned her a major aviation award. Her achievement belongs in the story too, even though Mock returned first.

In May, President Lyndon Johnson honored Mock. She went on to set other records, including a nonstop flight from Honolulu to Columbus in 1966.

Years later, she remembered women telling her that her example had helped them decide to fly. Her first world journey had begun with a girl listening to the radio, and later, a pilot listening to strangers flying across the Atlantic.

Today the actual red-and-white Cessna is in the Smithsonian collection. It is a small airplane to look at. The journey it made was not.
