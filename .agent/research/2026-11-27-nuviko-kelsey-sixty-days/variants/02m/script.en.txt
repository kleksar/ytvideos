Why did a sleeping pill already sold overseas fail to reach American pharmacies in 1960? And why did stopping it still leave American families at risk?

In 1960, Frances Kelsey joined the Food and Drug Administration. One of her first assignments was a drug called thalidomide. An American company wanted to sell it under the name Kevadon.

The company had a deadline on its side. Under the law at the time, a new drug application could take effect after sixty days. Kelsey had to act before the time ran out.

She found gaps in the safety studies. In November she formally asked for more evidence. The application was incomplete, and the clock started again.

The company supplied more information, but the questions remained. Its representative complained to her superiors. They kept her on the case.

Then a Scottish doctor reported something that did not fit the drug's reputation: patients who had taken it for months were developing nerve problems. Kelsey asked the company about those reports, too.

By late 1961, doctors in Germany and Australia had connected thalidomide taken during pregnancy to severe birth defects. Thousands of children worldwide were affected. In March 1962 the American company withdrew its application.

But the drug had already entered America through a different door. More than two million tablets had been distributed for investigational use. American children were harmed, and the FDA worked to recover the remaining supplies.

Congress tightened the rules that October. Manufacturers now had to demonstrate both safety and effectiveness before marketing a drug, and clinical investigations faced stronger controls.

Kelsey had not needed to know the final danger in advance. She had needed enough evidence to justify letting the drug through. And when that evidence was missing, she refused to let the deadline decide.
