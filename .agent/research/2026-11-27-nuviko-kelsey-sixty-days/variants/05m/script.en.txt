Why did an American drug company expect a sleeping pill to pass a government review almost automatically? How did one doctor keep that review open for more than a year? And how did the same drug reach American patients anyway?

In 1960, thalidomide was already a commercial drug in many countries. It was sold as a sedative and also given to pregnant women to relieve nausea. A company in Cincinnati, William Merrell, planned to bring it to American pharmacies as Kevadon.

The paperwork went to Frances Oldham Kelsey, a physician and pharmacologist who had only recently joined the FDA. Her job was to look at the evidence behind the claims.

There was a peculiar feature of American drug law. If a new drug application became effective at the end of the sixty-day review period, the manufacturer could market the product. The government did not yet have to issue the kind of affirmative approval we would expect today. A deadline could accomplish what the company wanted.

The application gave Kelsey plenty to read, but too little to rely on. There were deficiencies in the animal studies, in the human studies, and in the evidence about prolonged use. The fact that the product was popular abroad did not fill those gaps.

A colleague, Barbara Moulton, explained a useful option: the FDA could formally declare the submission incomplete and request the information that was missing. The manufacturer could respond, and the review period would begin again.

In November 1960, Kelsey sent the company such a letter. The expected clearance did not happen.

Merrell's representative, Joseph Murray, called to discuss the decision. In December he and a colleague came to Washington with additional material. The FDA still had questions. Murray eventually contacted Kelsey's superiors, but they left her in charge of the review.

Then a medical journal brought a new problem to the surface.

Leslie Florence, a doctor in Scotland, had noticed unusual symptoms in four patients. They had been taking thalidomide for a long time. Their hands and feet felt numb or prickled, and they experienced difficulties with muscle control. Their condition improved after stopping the drug.

This was not evidence of what the drug might do to a developing baby. It was evidence that a supposedly safe medicine deserved much closer scrutiny.

Kelsey questioned Murray about it. The British distributor had already received reports of these problems and had put warnings on its packaging. The American application was still unresolved.

Meanwhile, doctors overseas were facing an entirely different medical mystery. Children were being born with limbs that had failed to develop normally. In Germany, pediatrician Widukind Lenz investigated cases and compared what mothers had taken during pregnancy. In Australia, obstetrician William McBride reported a similar association with thalidomide.

By the end of 1961, the connection could no longer be dismissed as an isolated observation. The drug was being withdrawn in major markets.

In March 1962, Merrell abandoned its American application.

That should have been the end of the danger in the United States. But there was another part of the old system. A company could distribute experimental medicines to doctors before obtaining permission to market them. Merrell had already distributed more than two million thalidomide tablets for investigational use.

The FDA had to locate the remaining supplies and take them out of circulation. Some American families had already suffered harm. The absence of a commercial launch had limited the disaster, not prevented every case.

In August, President John F. Kennedy presented Kelsey with a federal civilian service award. In October, he signed a change to drug law that Congress had been considering in various forms before the thalidomide crisis. Manufacturers would have to establish effectiveness as well as safety. Investigational drugs and clinical trials would receive closer supervision, including requirements for informed consent.

The episode was not the last chapter in the drug's history. Thalidomide was approved in the United States in 1998 for a specific complication of leprosy, under strict controls. That was a different medical decision about a known risk, not a vindication of the original application.

The decisive thing Kelsey did in 1960 was to keep the burden of proof where it belonged. She asked for evidence before many more patients could be exposed, rather than waiting for injured patients to supply it.
