How could a drug company in 1960 gain the right to sell a new medicine without receiving an explicit yes from the American government? Why did a reviewer keep asking for information about a sleeping pill that doctors abroad were already prescribing? And why did blocking the proposed sales still leave the government with the task of recovering supplies?

By 1960, Frances Kelsey had trained as both a pharmacologist and a physician and joined the Food and Drug Administration in Washington.

One of her earliest assignments was a sedative. Its active ingredient, thalidomide, was already being sold in many countries. Unlike some older sleeping medicines, it had acquired a reputation for being unusually safe. It was also used for nausea in pregnancy.

The American company William Merrell was preparing to market the drug as Kevadon. The application was in the hands of the FDA, and a commercial launch was being planned.

The rules gave Merrell sixty days. Unless the FDA acted, the application could take effect without a formal yes. The company would then be able to sell the drug.

Kelsey examined the material. There were shortcomings in the studies of animals and people, and unanswered questions about long-term use. A large collection of observations was not necessarily the same thing as a carefully conducted safety study.

Kelsey discussed the situation with another FDA physician, Barbara Moulton. She learned how the agency could keep the application from taking effect without having to establish that the drug was poisonous. It could formally ask for the missing information.

In November 1960, Kelsey sent the company a letter explaining the deficiencies in its submission. The review would have to continue.

In a sense, this was a test of the law that had been written after another disaster. In 1937 a liquid preparation of sulfanilamide, an antibacterial drug, killed more than a hundred people. Its maker had used diethylene glycol as a solvent without establishing that the mixture was safe.

Kelsey had been a young researcher in Eugene Geiling's laboratory at the University of Chicago when he helped investigate that poisoning. The 1938 law that followed required safety evidence for new drugs. But it still left the government with a deadline like the one now facing her.

Joseph Murray, a bacteriologist representing Merrell, began pressing for a resolution. After the first letter, he called Kelsey. In December, he and another representative came to Washington and brought more information to the agency. It did not settle the questions. In January, Murray appealed to Kelsey's superiors to get the process moving. They kept her on the case.

At this stage, Kelsey was arguing mainly about the quality of evidence. She had not discovered a pattern of injured newborns. That investigation was going to take place on the other side of the world.

At the end of December 1960, the British Medical Journal published a short letter from Scottish physician Leslie Florence. Four of his patients had been taking thalidomide regularly. They had developed symptoms affecting their hands and feet, including abnormal sensations and problems with muscle control. It was not clear whether the damage would be reversible.

When Kelsey learned of the report, she questioned Murray. The problem was not just that an unexpected side effect had surfaced. The British distributor had already received reports of nerve symptoms and introduced warnings on the drug's label in 1960.

In her later account, Kelsey described another request to the company. She wanted to know whether doctors in the United States had noticed the same nerve symptoms. Merrell sent a list of thirty or forty investigators. The request was worded around prolonged use, so it did not cover everyone who had received the drug.

In Germany, a pediatrician and geneticist, Widukind Lenz, was trying to understand a growing number of unusual birth defects. Children had been born with severely shortened or missing limbs. A condition he might previously have encountered only rarely was appearing in multiple families.

Lenz began collecting histories of the pregnancies. What mattered was not just which medicines the mothers had taken, but whether they had taken them during the early stages of development. He found that exposure to thalidomide was common among the cases he was investigating.

He presented his findings to other doctors in November 1961.

Australia had its own warning. In Sydney, medical staff had observed unusual abnormalities in babies born to women who had used the drug. Obstetrician William McBride reported the association in a letter published in the Lancet that December.

The two lines of investigation mattered because they came from different places. And they were looking at a pattern that did not resemble an ordinary side effect in the person taking a sleeping pill. A woman might take the medicine without experiencing an obvious injury herself, while the developing fetus was exposed during a critical period.

As reports accumulated, the drug was removed from the German and Australian markets and then withdrawn in most countries. Thousands of children worldwide were affected. The precise number of pregnancies that had been exposed cannot be reconstructed.

Back in the United States, the commercial application had never become effective. In March 1962, Merrell withdrew it.

The review had stopped the commercial launch. But American doctors already had the tablets. More than two million had been distributed for investigational use. That number describes tablets sent out, not doses actually taken or people injured.

By then Helen Taussig, a pediatric cardiologist, had returned from Germany. She had spoken to doctors, researchers and families there. On April 6, 1962, Kelsey and her colleague John Nestor visited her in Baltimore. As Kelsey later recalled, Taussig showed them photographs and patient histories linking the birth defects to thalidomide. She had initially been unaware that the drug was being studied in the United States.

Five days after that visit the FDA sent Merrell a request for the complete distribution list. The reply arrived on April 25. There were more than a thousand physicians on it, far more than the thirty or forty investigators on the earlier list. The agency also received copies of the company's warning letters.

Those letters indicated that not everyone who had received the drug had been contacted in December. FDA staff sorted the names by state and specialty, preparing to visit the doctors individually, recover the supplies and ask about pregnancy exposures and birth defects. The work revealed that American families, too, had suffered harm. The exact total is uncertain.

Stopping the application had kept the drug out of routine commercial sale. Tracking it through the doctors' offices was a second task. Neither could undo exposure that had already occurred.

In Congress, Senator Estes Kefauver had already been examining the pharmaceutical industry before the danger of thalidomide became widely known. The disaster helped build support for legislation that had been under debate.

In July 1962, the American public learned more about Kelsey's role through newspaper reporting. In August, President Kennedy gave her a federal civilian service award. On October tenth he signed the Kefauver-Harris drug amendments.

The law required manufacturers to demonstrate both safety and effectiveness before a new drug could be marketed. Clinical investigations also faced new requirements, including informed consent and more detailed reporting to the FDA.

Kelsey went on to lead the agency's branch responsible for reviewing investigational drugs. The responsibility was closely related to the gap that had allowed thalidomide tablets to reach American patients.

Decades later, the drug itself came back into the American regulatory system. In 1998 it received approval for a particular complication of leprosy, under stringent restrictions intended to prevent exposure during pregnancy. A drug with a severe risk can still have a medical use if its benefits and risks are understood and its distribution is controlled.

In 1960, Kelsey had not been asked whether thalidomide could ever be useful. She had been asked whether Merrell had shown that it was safe to market as proposed. The company had sixty days on the calendar. What it lacked was the evidence, and she kept asking for it. The investigation that followed showed why both parts of the system needed to change.
