How do you prove that you've read a language nobody has understood for thousands of years? And what can a drawing of a cooking pot tell you?

In 1900, excavations at Knossos, on the island of Crete, began turning up clay tablets covered in strange signs. Some had recognizable pictures of animals or goods. The words beside them were a mystery.

An American scholar, Alice Kober, found groups of words with matching beginnings and different endings. She could work out relationships between their sounds without knowing what those sounds were.

A British architect, Michael Ventris, built on that work. He arranged the signs in a grid: consonants down the side, vowels across the top. If he could identify a few sounds, he could begin predicting others.

He tried reading some recurring words as names of places on Crete. One matched Amnisos. Its signs helped him read another name: Knossos.

More words began to look like ancient Greek. That was not the language Ventris had expected. In July 1952 he announced his discovery on the radio.

But could his system read a tablet he had never seen?

The following spring, archaeologist Carl Blegen tried it on a newly cleaned tablet from Pylos. The first word came out as ti-ri-po-de: Greek for two tripods.

At the other end of the line were a picture of a three-legged vessel and the number two.

Other entries matched the numbers of handles on the pots they described.

A palace inventory had become a test of the decipherment. Those little clay records pushed the known history of written Greek back to a time more than three thousand years ago.

The people who counted the vessels had left their own answer key.
