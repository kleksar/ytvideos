How do you start reading a script when you don't even know the language behind it? Why did a British architect have to abandon his own favorite theory? And how did a drawing of a pot help prove him right?

At Knossos on Crete, excavations led by Arthur Evans from 1900 brought thousands of inscriptions to light. They had been scratched into clay. A little picture of a horse was easy enough to recognize, and there were signs for quantities. These looked like records of goods and people, rather than letters written to us.

But the words were made of unfamiliar symbols. Evans named the script Linear B, and he dismissed Greek as its language. Scholars tried other possibilities, including Etruscan. There was no known translation waiting beside the original.

The strange thing was that people could begin solving the writing before they could translate a single sentence.

There were roughly eighty-seven signs that seemed to represent sounds. That's an awkward number for an alphabet, but it makes sense if each sign stands for a syllable, like pa or ti. The pictures for goods were a different part of the system.

In the United States, Alice Kober taught at Brooklyn College. She spent her spare time sorting words from the inscriptions. She noticed groups in which the beginning stayed the same and the final sign changed.

Think of the Spanish words niña and niño. The two endings have different vowels but share a consonant. That is just a modern example, not a translation of anything on the tablets. Kober could detect that kind of relationship between the unknown signs.

Her surviving archive contains around a hundred and eighty-six thousand small note cards. She was building a way to compare the writing without forcing it into any particular language.

Meanwhile, Emmett Bennett was working on tablets from Pylos, on the Greek mainland. He distinguished genuine differences between signs from differences in handwriting. That made it possible to count and compare signs reliably. His publication of the Pylos material in 1951 gave other scholars a much larger set of records to work with.

Kober died in 1950. But her work helped Michael Ventris, an architect who had been studying the writing for years, take the next step.

He arranged the signs in rows and columns. Each row shared a consonant and each column shared a vowel. The sounds themselves were still provisional.

He suspected that one sign represented a, because it occurred so often at the start of words. Another looked like a sign for ni in an already understood script from Cyprus.

Then he looked at recurring words in the headings on tablets from Crete. What if they were place names?

Amnisos, the ancient harbor near Knossos, could be written a-mi-ni-so. He found a group of signs that fit.

Now the grid mattered. The sound so suggested a column with the vowel o. The row containing ni supplied the consonant n. At their crossing was a sign that ought to be no.

There was another possible place name that ended in no-so. The remaining signs suggested Knossos itself.

As Ventris extended the readings, words that resembled Greek kept appearing. He had favored an Etruscan connection, but the results led him away from that idea. On July first, 1952, he announced on the BBC that the tablets were written in an early form of Greek.

John Chadwick, a Cambridge scholar of Greek who had worked in wartime codebreaking, heard the announcement, examined Ventris's work and joined him.

Then came a test neither of them had designed.

Carl Blegen had excavated more tablets at Pylos in 1952. After cleaning, he tried the proposed readings on a tablet of vessels.

The first word came out as ti-ri-po-de. In Greek, it meant two tripods. The entry ended with a drawing of a pot on three legs and the number two.

Further down, a word that suggested four handles matched a vessel with four handles. Other entries distinguished three handles and no handles.

One familiar word might have been luck. A series of readings fitting independent pictures was a different matter.

Chadwick and Ventris published their work together in 1953. The discovery opened a window onto Greek-speaking communities in the Bronze Age, centuries before the Greek literature that had previously been known.

An ancient clerk had counted the palace's pots. Thousands of years later, those same counts helped turn an unknown script into readable history.
