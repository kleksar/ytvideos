If an inscription has no translation and nobody knows what language it represents, where can you begin? Why might the shape of a word matter more than its meaning? And how can you tell whether a newly deciphered script really says what you think it says?

In the late nineteenth century, a Cretan antiquarian named Minos Kalokairinos excavated at Knossos. A clay tablet that Arthur Evans saw in 1895 probably came from that work. From 1900, Evans's own excavations brought thousands of inscriptions to light.

The finds included three different systems of writing. Evans gave two of them the names Linear A and Linear B. They resembled each other, but you could not simply assume that they recorded the same language.

Imagine examining one of the Linear B tablets. There are little marks in rows, some of them repeated. At the side you might recognize the shape of a horse. Some markings indicate a quantity. You can make a reasonable guess that somebody was keeping track of horses. But which word identifies an animal, a person, or a place? You cannot yet read it.

That distinction is where the story begins. A picture can give away the subject of a record without telling you anything about the pronunciation of the words. A drawing of a horse might sit beside English, Greek, or a completely different language.

Evans dismissed Greek as the language of these tablets. Etruscan, a language once spoken in Italy, became one of the possible explanations. Others looked toward languages of Anatolia.

There was a further complication. Finding a tablet and making it available to every scholar were two very different things. Evans died in 1941 before the main publication of his Linear B finds had appeared. Tablets had also been unearthed at Pylos on the Greek mainland in 1939, but the Second World War delayed publication. For years, scholars had an incomplete picture of the available records.

One of the ways into the problem was to count the signs. Linear B had roughly eighty-seven phonetic symbols. That was a plausible number for a system in which a sign represented a syllable, such as pa, ti, or a vowel on its own.

There were also separate picture signs for commodities and people. So the problem was not simply to find an English letter to put in place of every mark.

Even counting the signs was not straightforward. Look at the letter a in different fonts or different people's handwriting. If you count each shape as an entirely new letter, the alphabet seems much larger than it is. Emmett Bennett studied the material from Pylos and worked out which variations belonged to the same sign. In 1951 he published the Pylos tablets. A student of the script could now compare a larger and much more consistently organized collection.

Meanwhile, at Brooklyn College in New York, Alice Kober had been working on another part of the puzzle. She taught Classics, and in the time she had available outside teaching, she analyzed the inscriptions.

Her notes survive in enormous quantities. Among the papers are approximately a hundred and eighty-six thousand small cards. They were a means of keeping track of a mass of information that could not yet be read.

Kober found that certain groups of words began the same way, then changed at the end. This matters because in some languages the ending of a word changes according to things like gender or number.

Take a modern Spanish example: niña and niño. If you were writing those words by syllables, the first syllable would stay the same. The second would change, but its consonant would not. The two final signs could be completely different shapes, yet still share part of their sound.

That example is only an illustration of the method. Kober was not reading Spanish on a Cretan tablet. She was using recurring patterns to work out which unknown signs might be related.

Now consider the consequence. You can put two signs in a row together without knowing the consonant that they share. You can group signs that seem to contain the same vowel without naming that vowel. A table begins to take shape before you know the sounds that belong in its headings.

Kober died in 1950. The problem was not solved, but there was now a way to restrict what a possible solution could look like.

Michael Ventris, a British architect, took that approach further. In 1950 he circulated questions to twenty other specialists and put together a report on the state of research. He subsequently circulated working notes that exposed his changing theories rather than keeping his work a secret.

One of those notes, from February 1952, shows a handwritten grid. The rows stood for consonants and the columns for vowels. Some signs had uncertain positions. Others had possible readings written beside them. It was a working model, not a finished dictionary.

Ventris needed a few footholds. A sign that often appeared at the very beginning of words seemed a good candidate for a vowel. He provisionally read it as a. A resemblance to a sign in a known syllabic script from Cyprus suggested a reading of ni for another.

Neither guess was enough to identify the language. But Ventris had noticed something about the headings in the Knossos records. Certain groups of signs appeared repeatedly. Perhaps they were the names of the places from which the palace received its goods or with which it was doing business.

He looked for the name Amnisos, the harbor near Knossos. Written as syllables, the name would be a-mi-ni-so. The signs at the beginning and near the end of one candidate word agreed with his provisional readings. If the place name was right, the other signs would supply two more readings, mi and so.

Here is where the grid becomes useful. Suppose you know ni and so. On Ventris's table, a sign lying in the same row as ni ought to share its consonant, n. If it also belongs to the column of so, its vowel should be o. Together they give no.

Another recurring group of signs ended with so. The preceding sign now appeared to read no. The group suggested ko-no-so, Knossos.

Finding a name on a list of records from the very place where the name belonged made the hypothesis worth pursuing. But the bigger test was whether the same values worked elsewhere. Changing a sign's sound every time it produced an inconvenient word would make it possible to manufacture almost any answer.

Ventris had long favored an Etruscan connection. Yet the more he applied the proposed readings, the more recognizable Greek appeared. On July first, 1952, he announced on the BBC that he believed the tablets to contain an archaic form of Greek.

John Chadwick, a newly appointed Cambridge lecturer with knowledge of early Greek and experience in wartime codebreaking, heard the broadcast. He asked to see the work. Within days he was convinced enough to congratulate Ventris, and the two began collaborating.

The really persuasive test came in 1953. Carl Blegen, the excavator at Pylos, had new tablets from the previous year. They had been cleaned during the winter. When he tried Ventris's provisional syllabary, he found an extraordinary set of matches on a tablet listing vessels.

The first word was ti-ri-po-de. In the proposed Greek reading that meant two tripods. Farther along the same entry there was the picture of a vessel standing on three legs, followed by the number two.

There were more details. A group of signs read qe-to-ro-we, corresponding to four handles. The accompanying vessel had four handles. Other words distinguished vessels with three handles and vessels without handles. In other entries the word for tripod appeared in its singular form.

Blegen sent the findings to Ventris. Chadwick later recalled Ventris calling him from London with the news.

The picture signs and quantities had been there for centuries before anyone proposed those sound values. And the newly cleaned tablet was not among the records on which the original solution had been developed. It was a powerful check on the reading.

In 1953 Ventris and Chadwick published their findings. Their much larger book followed in 1956, shortly after Ventris's death. Chadwick continued working on the script.

The result did more than put familiar names on a map. Written Greek could now be traced back more than three thousand years. The records let historians study parts of Bronze Age society through its own administrative writing, rather than relying solely on later traditions and excavated objects.

That does not make every sign or damaged word easy to interpret. Nor does deciphering Linear B automatically give a translation of Linear A. The achievement was to find a system of readings that made sense across the records and could survive a new test.

It took decades of excavation, sorting, publishing, and checking. And when a freshly cleaned tablet finally offered the right kind of test, the decisive evidence was not a royal speech or a great poem.

It was a clerk's inventory, with two three-legged pots.
