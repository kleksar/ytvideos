How do you read words when you don't even know what language they're written in? Why did an architect spend years arguing for the wrong language? And what could an inventory of palace pots tell him that all his theories could not?

From 1900, Arthur Evans's excavations at Knossos on Crete brought thousands of clay inscriptions to light. There had been earlier discoveries too: a tablet Evans saw in 1895 probably came from the excavations of a Cretan antiquarian, Minos Kalokairinos.

Evans identified three systems of writing, two of which he named Linear A and Linear B. Some of the Linear B records had pictures of animals or goods beside rows of unfamiliar signs. A drawing of a horse told you something about the record, but it gave you no way to pronounce the words beside it.

In 1936, Evans was at an exhibition when a group of schoolboys came to see his finds. One of them, Michael Ventris, asked him about the unread tablets. Four years later the boy, now eighteen, published an article arguing that the language might be related to Etruscan, once spoken in Italy. He started training as an architect that same year.

Evans himself had dismissed Greek. To test any of the competing ideas, scholars needed enough examples of the writing to see how the same signs behaved in different words. And much of the material still had to be published.

More tablets were found at Pylos on the Greek mainland in 1939, but war delayed their publication. Evans died in 1941 with much of the Knossos material still unpublished. The task of preparing those records fell to another scholar, John Myres.

Linear B had roughly eighty-seven signs for sounds, alongside the pictures for goods and people. That number suggested syllables such as pa or ti rather than individual letters. But first the researchers had to agree on which marks were really different signs.

Emmett Bennett studied the Pylos material and distinguished separate signs from variations in handwriting. Ventris returned to architecture after the war, but kept in touch with Myres. In 1948, when Myres wanted his help with the Knossos publication, Ventris disagreed over how the signs should be classified and withdrew. He favored Bennett's system. Bennett's 1951 edition of the Pylos tablets eventually gave scholars a much firmer basis for comparing words.

Meanwhile, in New York, Alice Kober taught Classics at Brooklyn College. Outside teaching she sorted words from the inscriptions, looking for patterns she could demonstrate without deciding what language they represented.

Her papers include around a hundred and eighty-six thousand small cards. Some of her handwritten slips were filed in cigarette cartons. A scholar could bring together words that had appeared on separate tablets and examine their endings side by side.

Kober found that certain groups of words began the same way, then changed at the end. This matters because in some languages the ending of a word changes according to things like gender or number.

Take a modern Spanish example: niña and niño. If you were writing those words by syllables, the first syllable would stay the same. The second would change, but its consonant would not. The two final signs could be completely different shapes, yet still share part of their sound.

The Spanish words are only an analogy. Kober used the recurring endings on the actual tablets to establish relationships between the unknown signs. She could group signs with a possible shared consonant without assigning a sound to it.

Kober died in 1950. She had established relationships that could be used even if none of the proposed languages turned out to be right.

That year Ventris circulated a report on the state of research, based on questions he had sent to other specialists. He intended it to be his last contribution. Instead he gave up his architectural job to concentrate on the tablets and began sending out a succession of working notes.

One of those notes, from February 1952, shows a handwritten grid. The rows stood for consonants and the columns for vowels. Some signs had uncertain positions. Others had possible readings written beside them. It was a working model, not a finished dictionary.

Ventris needed a few footholds. A sign that often appeared at the very beginning of words seemed a good candidate for a vowel. He provisionally read it as a. A resemblance to a sign in a known syllabic script from Cyprus suggested a reading of ni for another.

Neither guess was enough to identify the language. But Ventris had noticed something about the headings in the Knossos records. Certain groups of signs appeared repeatedly. Perhaps they were the names of the places from which the palace received its goods or with which it was doing business.

He looked for the name Amnisos, the harbor near Knossos. Written as syllables, the name would be a-mi-ni-so. The signs at the beginning and near the end of one candidate word agreed with his provisional readings. If the place name was right, the other signs would supply two more readings, mi and so.

Here is where the grid becomes useful. Suppose you know ni and so. On Ventris's table, a sign lying in the same row as ni ought to share its consonant, n. If it also belongs to the column of so, its vowel should be o. Together they give no.

Another recurring group of signs ended with so. The preceding sign now appeared to read no. The group suggested ko-no-so, Knossos.

These place names were promising. If the proposed sounds were right, they should work in other records as well, including the ones from the Greek mainland. Ventris kept applying them.

Ventris had long favored an Etruscan connection. Yet the more he applied the proposed readings, the more recognizable Greek appeared. On July first, 1952, he announced on the BBC that he believed the tablets to contain an archaic form of Greek.

John Chadwick, a Cambridge lecturer who knew early Greek and had worked in wartime codebreaking, examined Ventris's results. In his first letter, in July 1952, he suggested pu for one unresolved sign. That allowed him to read the name Pylos itself. Ventris asked him to help publish the decipherment, and their collaboration began.

The really persuasive test came in 1953. Carl Blegen, the excavator at Pylos, had new tablets from the previous year. They had been cleaned during the winter. When he tried Ventris's provisional syllabary, he found an extraordinary set of matches on a tablet listing vessels.

The first word was ti-ri-po-de. In the proposed Greek reading that meant two tripods. Farther along the same entry there was the picture of a vessel standing on three legs, followed by the number two.

There were more details. A group of signs read qe-to-ro-we, corresponding to four handles. The accompanying vessel had four handles. Other words distinguished vessels with three handles and vessels without handles. In other entries the word for tripod appeared in its singular form.

Blegen included a copy of the tablet in his letter. When Ventris received it in London, he called Chadwick in Cambridge with the news.

The quantity, the pictures and the words fitted. And this tablet had only been cleaned after the original decipherment. The signs did not need new sound values to make the inventory fit the Greek reading.

The two published their findings in 1953 and kept working on the original tablets. In April 1955, Chadwick wrote to Ventris from Crete about a remarkable find. An old tablet from Knossos showed drawings of horses, but its beginning had been missing. Chadwick found a fragment that joined it. On that fragment was the word i-qo, an early Greek word for horse.

The same kind of test had happened again. A physical piece of clay, joined to another, brought a readable word and a recognizable picture together. In June, Chadwick sent Ventris a postcard about their joint book. He wrote the message in Linear B.

Their book came out in 1956, just after Ventris's death. Historians could now trace written Greek back more than three thousand years and learn about Bronze Age societies from their own records. Some readings were still uncertain, and the closely related Linear A script remained unreadable.

The schoolboy who had seen Evans's unread tablets lived long enough to receive a message written in their script. And the palace clerk who counted two three-legged pots had unwittingly left him a way to check his answer.
