Why did a man die after receiving the drug that would change the treatment of bacterial infections? And how did an Oxford laboratory turn that failure into a medicine hospitals could actually obtain?

In February 1941, a police officer named Albert Alexander lay in the Radcliffe Infirmary with a severe bacterial infection. The doctors tried something new: penicillin.

Within a day his condition began to improve. But the researchers making the drug had a problem. They could only produce tiny quantities.

Years earlier, Alexander Fleming had noticed that bacteria stopped growing near a patch of mould. The Oxford team had learned how to extract the active substance. Making enough of it was another matter.

Their laboratory was full of improvised containers, even biscuit tins and hospital bedpans, in which they grew the mould. The supply was so precious that they recovered penicillin from Alexander's urine and used it again.

Even that wasn't enough. After about five days the supply ran out. The infection returned, and Alexander died.

Howard Florey and Norman Heatley took the production problem to the United States. Researchers there found a much more productive mould and industry learned to grow it in enormous tanks.

In March 1942, a woman named Anne Miller received scarce penicillin at a hospital in Connecticut. Her fever came down by the following morning. This time the patient recovered.

For a while, doctors still had to request the drug from a limited national supply. But by March 1945, American pharmacies could obtain it through normal distribution.

The Oxford doctors had seen a treatment work, then lost their patient when the medicine ran out. The breakthrough that followed was learning how to keep the medicine coming.
