How could a hospital run out of a medicine while a patient was beginning to recover? Why did researchers begin making that medicine in biscuit tins? And how did it eventually reach ordinary pharmacies?

On February twelfth, 1941, a police officer named Albert Alexander received an injection at the Radcliffe Infirmary in Oxford. He was forty-three and had a severe bacterial infection. His condition began to improve within a day.

The drug was penicillin. Today that name sounds ordinary. In that hospital, it meant months of work in a laboratory across town.

In 1928, Alexander Fleming had seen a patch of mould growing on a dish of bacteria. The bacteria immediately surrounding the mould had stopped growing. There was something in the material released by the mould that could inhibit them. Fleming named it penicillin, but getting a stable, concentrated medicine out of that material was extremely difficult.

A decade later, the work was taken up at Oxford. Howard Florey led a team that included Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley. Heatley developed equipment to separate the useful substance and a way of checking how active it was against bacteria.

They tested their preparation in mice with a serious bacterial infection. The untreated animals died rapidly; some of those given penicillin survived. The team had a reason to try the drug in people. But mice were small. A human patient would require far more medicine.

It was wartime Britain. The researchers used whatever suitable vessels they could find, including old biscuit tins and bedpans. Heatley designed a ceramic container that could be stacked. Hundreds were made by a pottery company. The laboratory became a tiny pharmaceutical factory.

Then came Albert Alexander. The improvement after treatment was striking. But there was still a limit to what the laboratory could supply. Some of the penicillin passed out of his body in his urine. The researchers recovered it and returned it to the treatment.

After roughly five days they had no more to give him. Alexander's infection returned, and he died. The Oxford team's first treatment of a patient with a systemic infection had demonstrated the promise of the drug and the terrible cost of its scarcity.

Other Oxford patients did respond successfully. The team needed industry to take over a task that no number of clever makeshift containers could solve quickly enough. That summer Florey and Heatley went to America.

An agricultural research laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, became part of the effort. Its staff had experience producing substances with microorganisms. They improved the amount of penicillin they could get from the mould. A strain found on a spoiled melon in the local area was particularly valuable for increasing production.

But a discovery in a laboratory wasn't the same thing as a factory. Drug companies had to find a way to manufacture and recover the medicine on a scale far larger than anyone at Oxford had managed.

In March 1942, the supply was still desperately small. In New Haven, Connecticut, a woman named Anne Miller was suffering from a severe infection after a miscarriage. Her doctors used their academic connections to obtain penicillin. A few grams represented an enormous share of the American stock.

By the morning after her first dose, her temperature had returned to normal. She eventually recovered. The medicine had done what her doctors' previous efforts could not.

Cases like hers made the pressure to produce more penicillin even greater. The American government coordinated an industrial program. A former ice plant in Brooklyn was converted into a large penicillin factory, which opened in March 1944. Instead of rows of little containers, there were enormous tanks.

Production grew so fast that by March 1945 the United States could end its restrictions on distribution. A doctor no longer needed the same extraordinary connections to obtain the medicine.

Oxford's Museum of the History of Science still holds a small specimen of penicillin recovered from patients' urine in 1941. It belongs to a time when a dose could be too precious to use only once.

Albert Alexander never lived to see the drug become widely available. But his treatment had shown exactly what the new medicine could do, and what the people developing it still needed to accomplish. It took a laboratory, a hospital, agricultural researchers and factories to turn a promising substance into a treatment that would be there for the next patient.
