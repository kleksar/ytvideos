Why did one of the first patients treated with penicillin die even though he began to recover? How did a laboratory that grew mould in biscuit tins help launch an industry? And why did a few grams of the same medicine have to be flown to another hospital a year later?

There is a small object in Oxford's Museum of the History of Science that brings those questions together. It is a specimen of penicillin dated June twenty-fifth, 1941. This particular specimen was not obtained directly from a mould culture. It had been recovered from the urine of patients.

At the beginning of that year, Oxford doctors had been preparing to test a new treatment for severe bacterial infections. One of their patients was Albert Alexander, a forty-three-year-old police officer at the Radcliffe Infirmary. His case was particularly serious. On February twelfth, he was given penicillin.

The next day, his condition was already improving. But within about five days the drug was gone. The people who had made it had to find a way to turn a promising medical treatment into a reliable supply of medicine.

To understand why that was so difficult, go back to London in 1928. Alexander Fleming was studying bacteria. A mould contaminated one of his culture dishes, and a clear area appeared around it where the bacteria did not grow. Whatever the mould was producing could interfere with the growth of certain bacteria.

Fleming investigated the substance and published his findings. He called it penicillin. Yet the active material was difficult to separate and unstable. An observation on a dish could not simply be transferred to a syringe.

At Oxford, around a decade later, Ernst Chain returned to Fleming's work as part of a research program into antibacterial substances led by Howard Florey. The team needed both a way to isolate the drug and a way to find out whether a sample still worked. Norman Heatley helped solve both problems.

He devised equipment for extracting penicillin and developed a test in which its effect on bacteria could be measured. This mattered because an apparently identical amount of liquid might contain a very different amount of active drug. The Oxford team could compare preparations on the basis of their antibacterial activity, not merely their volume.

They eventually accumulated enough of a preparation for an animal experiment. Mice were infected with bacteria. Some received penicillin and some did not. The untreated mice died quickly; several treated mice survived much longer. Further trials supported that initial finding.

An injection in a mouse, however, was not a prescription for a human. The preparation still contained impurities, its safety needed investigation, and larger patients would require more material. Another member of the Oxford team, Edward Abraham, took on further chemical work to purify the drug.

To produce enough penicillin for human trials, the researchers turned their own laboratory into a manufacturing operation. The mould occupied the surface of the liquid in small vessels. A collection of flasks and containers yielded only small amounts of the substance that the researchers needed.

In wartime, even obtaining suitable containers became a challenge. Heatley collected whatever could be used: pie dishes, tins, and hospital bedpans. He designed a rectangular ceramic vessel that could fit into a stack. A pottery firm in Staffordshire manufactured hundreds of them, and on Christmas Day in 1940 the first batch was put to use. The resulting stock went toward the first six patients at the Oxford hospital in 1941.

Imagine the difference in scale. The museum's collection includes an original flask holding about three hundred millilitres of culture fluid. Its exhibition estimates that two thousand litres of culture fluid were required to make enough penicillin for a single case of sepsis in those early days. That is not the recorded volume for Alexander personally, but it shows the size of the obstacle.

Charles Fletcher administered the clinical treatment at the Radcliffe Infirmary under Florey's supervision. When Alexander began to improve, they had evidence of something far more important than the appearance of a clear patch on a dish. The substance was active in a seriously ill person.

They recovered some of the drug from Alexander's urine and used it again. Nonetheless, the supply was exhausted after several days. The infection returned and he died in March. The result was not a simple failure of the drug. It demonstrated the consequences of having too little of a drug that was showing an effect.

Further patients in Oxford improved with penicillin. The team had been able to show that the drug deserved much more attention, but it could not meet demand by continuing to work with the same equipment.

In 1941, Florey and Heatley went to the United States in search of industrial help. Britain was fighting a war and its manufacturing capacity was already under enormous pressure. In America the researchers made contact with an agricultural laboratory in Peoria, Illinois.

At Peoria the scientists brought experience of growing microorganisms for industrial purposes. They investigated how to get more penicillin out of the mould cultures. Their experiments raised the output substantially. They also searched for varieties of mould that would produce more of the substance. A particularly useful strain turned up locally, growing on a cantaloupe.

These advances changed what it was possible to contemplate. In Oxford the small containers had provided space at the surface of the culture for the mould. An industry would have to grow useful quantities inside vastly larger vessels and still extract the relatively small amount of medicine from all that material.

American pharmaceutical firms were approached. At first the clinical evidence was limited and producing the drug in bulk was uncertain. Government officials helped bring companies and laboratories together, and by late 1941 they were working toward much greater output.

Meanwhile, patients were still being admitted to hospitals with infections that ordinary treatment could not stop.

In March 1942, Anne Miller lay seriously ill in New Haven Hospital, Connecticut, after an infection following a miscarriage. Her physicians, John Bumstead and Orvan Hess, sought help. Bumstead had another patient in the same hospital, a Yale professor named John Fulton. Fulton knew Norman Heatley.

Through that connection, the doctors obtained 5.5 grams of penicillin, an extraordinary allocation. Yale's historical account puts it at roughly half the American stock at the time. A shipment was flown in, and a state trooper brought it to the hospital on March fourteenth.

Miller received the drug that day. Her temperature returned to normal by the next morning, and she was eventually discharged cured. She lived to the age of ninety. Penicillin had not yet reached a point where any doctor could obtain it on demand, but her recovery made the implications of larger supplies very clear.

The war accelerated the industrial program. The United States government coordinated production, selected manufacturers and gave military needs priority. But civilians needed the drug too. A physician in Boston, Chester Keefer, was responsible for deciding how much could be spared for civilian cases. Doctors had to submit detailed information about patients whose previous treatments had failed. Requests came from desperate relatives as well as from hospitals.

There was still a physical limit behind every decision.

In Brooklyn, Pfizer converted a former ice plant into a factory devoted to penicillin. It opened on March first, 1944, with large fermentation tanks. The company had experience in fermentation, but the new drug required a major industrial effort to obtain useful yields and separate the medicine from the huge volume of culture.

By early 1944, American output was rising rapidly. The increase continued through the year. Government planners had been working to build up reserves for the Allied invasion of Europe, and expanding production meant the medicine could reach more military and eventually civilian patients.

In 1943, the United States had produced about twenty-one billion units of penicillin activity. The following year, the figure exceeded one and a half trillion. These are units of drug activity, not numbers of injections or patients. They show how far the manufacturers had come from Oxford's improvised laboratory.

On March fifteenth, 1945, restrictions on the drug's distribution in the United States were lifted. Penicillin could now be supplied through ordinary channels. Later that year Fleming, Florey and Chain received the Nobel Prize for their work.

But look again at that little specimen in Oxford. It belongs to a stage of the story that the famous image of Fleming's culture dish cannot explain on its own.

Albert Alexander's treatment had made the shortage painfully visible. Anne Miller's recovery a year later showed what access to a scarce supply could mean. And the factories changed the scale of that access.

What began as a chance observation on a laboratory dish became a medicine people could obtain because scientists, doctors, engineers and manufacturers solved the problems that came after the discovery. For many patients in the United States, access no longer depended on a laboratory trying to recover a precious dose from urine.
