A bottle of water from a London street was delivered miles away. Two women drank from it and later died of cholera.

In September 1854, people living near Broad Street were falling ill. Many blamed the foul air. Doctor John Snow suspected the public water pump, even though the water he examined looked surprisingly clean.

He began asking where the people who died had been getting their drinking water.

The pattern around the pump was striking. But what about the people who lived close to it and survived? A nearby brewery had more than seventy workers, yet none suffered a severe attack. They had other sources of water and were given beer. A workhouse nearby also had its own water supply.

Then Snow heard about a widow in Hampstead. She liked the taste of the Broad Street water and had it brought to her. She and her visiting niece died after drinking it.

On September seventh, Snow took his findings to the local authorities. The next day, the pump handle was removed.

By then, far fewer people were falling ill with cases that would prove fatal. Removing the handle made sense, but it couldn't prove what had caused the outbreak.

A local clergyman, Henry Whitehead, had doubted Snow's explanation. He kept investigating, and found that a sick infant had lived right beside the well. Water used to wash the baby's soiled cloths had been emptied into a nearby cesspool. An inspection revealed damaged brickwork through which waste could seep toward the well.

The precise first infection remained uncertain. But the investigators now had a plausible physical route into the drinking water, as well as the pattern of illness. They weren't just looking for where people died anymore. They were following what people had swallowed.

What linked those deaths miles away to the affected streets?
