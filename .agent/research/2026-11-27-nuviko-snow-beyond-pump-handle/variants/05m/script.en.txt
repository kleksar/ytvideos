Why did a London brewery escape the worst of a cholera outbreak that devastated its own neighborhood? And why did a doctor investigate a bottle of water delivered to a woman miles away?

In late August 1854, cholera broke out around Broad Street in Soho. The streets were crowded with homes and businesses. People collected drinking water from public pumps, and the water from this particular pump was popular.

At the time, many people believed diseases like cholera spread through foul air. Doctor John Snow had a different explanation. He suspected that something from a sick person's body could get into drinking water and infect the next person who swallowed it.

He inspected the Broad Street water on September third. It didn't look particularly dirty. He couldn't simply hold up the sample and show what was causing the disease. Instead, he obtained a list of recent deaths and went looking for the people who could tell him what the victims had consumed.

One of the most useful clues was a place where almost nothing had happened.

A brewery stood near the pump. More than seventy people worked there, breathing the same neighborhood air as their neighbors. Not one had suffered a severe attack of cholera. The workers received beer, and the brewery had its own sources of water. They didn't rely on the public pump.

A nearby workhouse held more than five hundred people. Only five of its residents had died from cholera. The institution had its own water supply too.

Now compare those buildings with a nearby factory where workers drank water brought in from the Broad Street pump. Eighteen workers died at home.

For Snow, these differences mattered. The brewery wasn't some protected island outside the affected district. It was right there, and its workers didn't use the same drinking water.

Then the trail led away from Soho altogether.

A widow living in Hampstead had once lived near Broad Street. She preferred the water from the old neighborhood, so it was brought to her. A bottle arrived on August thirty-first. She drank from it that evening and the following day. Her visiting niece drank the water as well. Both women died of cholera, although Snow reported no comparable local outbreak around their homes at the time.

If water could carry the disease across London, the address where a person fell ill wasn't necessarily the place where the danger originated.

On the evening of September seventh, Snow presented his findings to local authorities. By the next day, the handle had been removed from the pump. No one could draw water from it in the usual way.

This is where the familiar story often ends. But Snow's own figures show that the outbreak had already been declining. There had been a mass departure of residents, and the contamination itself might have changed. The timing of the intervention alone couldn't establish that it ended the outbreak.

There was still a question that Snow's interviews couldn't answer. How had anything dangerous entered a well that supplied drinking water?

Henry Whitehead, a local clergyman who knew the people in the area, had initially doubted Snow's explanation. He continued questioning families. Months after the outbreak, his inquiries drew attention to a sick infant at number forty Broad Street, right beside the pump.

The infant had suffered from diarrhea. Her mother had washed the soiled cloths and disposed of the water into a cesspool.

The distance between that cesspool and the well was less than a metre. When the structures were inspected, defective brickwork and evidence of seepage were found.

This provided a plausible way for contaminated waste to reach water people were drinking. It didn't establish beyond doubt that this infant had been the first cholera patient. But it supplied a piece of physical evidence that neither the map nor the removed handle could provide.

Snow could show who used the water. Whitehead's inquiries and the subsequent inspection could help explain how that water had become dangerous.

The two women outside the affected neighborhood, the brewery workers inside it, and the damaged brickwork beside the well now belonged to the same explanation.

Closing the pump was a precaution worth taking. Understanding what had happened took something more: following the water, including when it went somewhere the disease wasn't expected to be.
