How could a Victorian doctor investigate the source of a deadly disease when he couldn't identify the infectious agent in a sample of drinking water? Why did two women outside an affected neighborhood die, while dozens of workers next to a suspected source escaped severe illness? And what would happen if the same question were asked of thousands of homes across London?

In 1854, cholera was spreading through the city. People needed an explanation that would tell them how to avoid it. One widely held theory was that disease was carried by foul air. In parts of London, where sewage and crowded housing made the smell almost impossible to escape, that explanation seemed to fit what people could observe.

But a doctor named John Snow had been investigating cholera for years. He had published a different explanation in 1849. He argued that infectious material from a person with cholera could be swallowed by another person. Drinking water contaminated with human waste would be a particularly effective way to carry it from one household to another.

In late August 1854, a concentrated outbreak in Soho gave him a chance to investigate.

People around Broad Street collected water from a public pump. In a city where the sources of drinking water varied from one building to the next, knowing the address of a patient didn't necessarily tell Snow what water the patient had consumed.

On September third, he examined a sample from the Broad Street pump. He found little visible organic impurity and initially hesitated. He was unable to demonstrate the presence of the infectious material he suspected just by examining the water.

So he turned to the records of the dead and to the people still alive who knew their habits.

Among the deaths registered in the preceding week, eighty-three had occurred in its last three days. Snow investigated recent cases around the pump. Where possible, he asked relatives or other people who knew the victims where they obtained water. Sixty-one of the recent victims were reported to have consumed Broad Street water regularly or occasionally. Information was missing in some cases, and in others he was told that the victims hadn't drunk it.

There were also people who lived nearer another pump. That could have undermined his explanation if distance were the only thing he was investigating. But some families preferred the Broad Street water. Some children went to school near the pump, even when they lived closer to a different one.

Snow had to distinguish the place where an individual was exposed from the place where they lived. And the people who didn't get sick could be as informative as the people who did.

Consider a brewery close to the pump. More than seventy people worked there, and not one suffered a severe attack. The workers received malt liquor and had access to other sources of water. They didn't use the public pump.

Does that mean beer prevented cholera? No. The relevant difference was that the brewery workers weren't drinking water from the suspected source. They worked in the same area as people who were dying.

The nearby workhouse had a different arrangement. Its five hundred and thirty-five residents had their own water supply. Five died from cholera.

Meanwhile, a factory employing about two hundred people brought water from the street pump into the building. Eighteen of its workers died at their own homes.

You could put a mark on a map at each of those homes and miss the fact that the workers had shared water in a single workplace. You could also draw a circle around the pump and miss the story of someone who hadn't visited the area at all.

Snow learned about a fifty-nine-year-old widow living in Hampstead. She liked the water from Broad Street, where she had formerly lived, and arranged to have it brought to her. On August thirty-first, a bottle was delivered.

She drank it that evening and the next day. A niece visiting her also drank from it. The widow died on September second. Her niece returned to her home in Islington, fell ill and died as well. According to Snow's report, neither locality was experiencing a comparable outbreak at the time.

Their connection wasn't simply where they lived. It was what had arrived in that bottle.

On September seventh, Snow put his findings before the local Board of Guardians. The next day, the pump handle was removed.

The closure became the most famous moment of his investigation. But look at the chronology he later published. Among the people whose onset of illness could be assigned a date and whose attacks proved fatal, one hundred and forty-three became ill on September first. The number was fifty-four on September third. By September eighth, when the handle came off, it was twelve.

Those were not counts of all infections, nor were they the numbers of people who died on each of those days. They tell us when illness began among the recorded fatal cases. And they establish an important limit on what the pump intervention can tell us.

Many residents had left the area. The contamination might also have subsided. Disabling a suspected source was sensible, but the fall in cases had begun before that decision. It would be wrong to treat the subsequent decline as an isolated experiment proving that Snow was right.

Nor was the famous published map the instrument that first led him to the pump. It organized the evidence from a hypothesis he was already investigating. He displayed a map later that year, and a version appeared in his book in 1855.

A separate local inquiry was getting closer to a physical explanation.

Henry Whitehead was a clergyman in the neighborhood. Initially, he doubted that the pump water was the cause. He knew local families and was able to make repeated inquiries. In the following months, he investigated the illness of an infant at number forty Broad Street, almost next to the pump.

The baby had suffered from diarrhea. Her mother had washed soiled cloths and emptied the water into a cesspool. On inspection, investigators found defective brickwork and indications of seepage. The cesspool was less than a metre from the well.

No one could establish with certainty that the infant's illness had been the original cholera infection. But the investigation now had evidence of a possible route from a household's waste to its neighbors' drinking water.

Snow's work did not stop at that one well. In South London, another investigation was tackling the same problem on a much larger scale.

Two water companies supplied homes in overlapping areas. The Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream in 1852, away from much of the sewage entering the Thames in London. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company continued drawing water from a polluted part of the river.

Families could live near one another and breathe the same air, yet receive water taken from very different places. Snow set out to determine which supply served households struck by cholera.

That wasn't as simple as asking everyone for the name of a company. Some residents didn't know. In those cases Snow looked for evidence such as water bills. He could also distinguish samples from the two sources by their very different salt content, using a chemical test.

A bottle labeled with the house it came from could help identify the supply even when the resident couldn't name the company.

The figures for the first four weeks of the 1854 epidemic were dramatic. Snow recorded two hundred and eighty-six fatal attacks among roughly forty thousand houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall. In roughly twenty-six thousand houses supplied by Lambeth, he recorded fourteen.

In round numbers, that was seventy-one versus five fatal attacks per ten thousand houses. The comparison used houses as its denominator, not individual people. It wasn't a randomized trial, and it didn't tell Snow the exact risk faced by every person.

But the difference between the two supplies was much harder to explain by neighborhood air alone.

Across London, the same pattern had emerged in different ways. People near one suspected source could escape severe illness by using other water. People far from the outbreak could fall ill after a delivery. And neighboring households receiving water from different stretches of the Thames could experience sharply different rates of fatal illness.

Those observations weren't identical pieces of evidence. The distant bottle linked exposure and illness, the damaged brickwork offered a physical route for contamination, and the water-company comparison tested the explanation on a far larger population.

That is why the story is more revealing than the familiar image of a doctor taking the handle off a pump.

The handle was the action local authorities could take on September eighth. The investigation, involving people who initially disagreed, made it possible to understand why that action was worth taking. It offered a way to investigate other outbreaks, too, long after that particular pump stopped being the center of attention.
