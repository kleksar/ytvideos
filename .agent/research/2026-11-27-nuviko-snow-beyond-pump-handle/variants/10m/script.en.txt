Why did a brewery escape the worst of a cholera outbreak on its own doorstep? Why did two women who lived miles away die? And how did a clergyman who doubted the doctor's explanation eventually help uncover what the doctor couldn't find?

In late August 1854, cholera struck the streets around Broad Street in Soho. Homes stood close to workshops and shops. People collected drinking water from public pumps. The one on Broad Street was popular.

Many Londoners blamed disease on foul air. John Snow, a doctor, had published a different explanation in 1849. He suspected that infectious material from a sick person could enter drinking water and be swallowed by someone else. On September third, he examined the pump water and found surprisingly little visible organic matter. He couldn't show anyone the infectious material he suspected.

A brewery near the pump presented a much more striking clue. More than seventy people worked there, and not one suffered a severe attack. The workers received malt liquor and had access to other sources of water. They didn't use the public pump. The workers breathed the neighborhood air, but drank different water.

The nearby workhouse had a different arrangement. Its five hundred and thirty-five residents had their own water supply. Five died from cholera. Meanwhile, a factory employing about two hundred people brought water from the street pump into the building. Eighteen of its workers died at their own homes.

Snow obtained records of recent deaths and asked relatives and acquaintances where the victims had collected water. Sixty-one were reported to have drunk from Broad Street regularly or occasionally. Some families could not provide an answer; others said the victims had not used the pump. Snow still needed to account for the cases that didn't fit.

There were also people who lived nearer another pump. That could have undermined his explanation if distance were the only thing he was investigating. But some families preferred the Broad Street water. Some children went to school near the pump, even when they lived closer to a different one.

Snow learned about a fifty-nine-year-old widow living in Hampstead. She liked the water from Broad Street, where she had formerly lived, and arranged to have it brought to her. On August thirty-first, a bottle was delivered.

She drank it that evening and the next day. A niece visiting her also drank from it. The widow died on September second. Her niece returned to her home in Islington, fell ill and died as well. According to Snow's report, neither locality was experiencing a comparable outbreak at the time.

The bottle had traveled farther than the outbreak. It gave Snow a possible link between two deaths outside Soho and the same water used by the factory workers. The investigation had to follow what people drank, even when their homes were nowhere near the pump.

On September seventh, Snow put his findings before the local Board of Guardians. The next day, the pump handle was removed.

By the time the pump was disabled, the outbreak was already declining. Snow's later table recorded one hundred and forty-three fatal attacks beginning on September first, fifty-four on the third, and twelve on the eighth. These were dates of illness among people who eventually died, not the total number of infections. People had fled Soho, and the contamination might have changed. Closing the suspected source was a reasonable precaution; the falling numbers did not prove that closing it had stopped the outbreak.

In September, inspectors looked inside the well. Its brick lining appeared intact. Even a later examination failed to identify a route by which waste could enter. Snow had evidence from the people who drank the water, but he still lacked evidence of how the water had been contaminated.

While he was investigating Soho, Snow also had a way to test the same explanation across South London. In fact, the records he used there began weeks before the outbreak around Broad Street.

Two water companies supplied homes in overlapping areas. The Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream in 1852, away from much of the sewage entering the Thames in London. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company continued drawing water from a polluted part of the river.

Families could live near one another and breathe the same air, yet receive water taken from very different places. Snow set out to determine which supply served households struck by cholera.

Snow and a medical colleague, John Joseph Whiting, traced the supply at homes where fatal attacks had begun. Whiting covered several districts where only one of the two companies operated, checking whether the water came from that company or another source. Snow worked in districts served by Lambeth, including streets where both companies supplied houses.

At some doors a resident could not say which company supplied the house. Snow checked receipts, and during the dry weather he could use a chemical test to distinguish water from the two companies by its salt content. He had a way of identifying the source without relying entirely on a person's memory.

His first four weeks of records, from July eighth to August fifth, showed two hundred and eighty-six fatal attacks in homes served by Southwark and Vauxhall, and fourteen in homes served by Lambeth. Using the previous year's counts of houses supplied, that was about seventy-one versus five fatal attacks per ten thousand houses. Other victims obtained water from the river, wells or ditches; in a few cases the source was unknown.

The figures compared houses, not individual people. This was no controlled laboratory experiment. Yet the contrasting supplies running through the same streets made the difference especially revealing. If the air were the whole explanation, why should the source of water inside neighboring houses matter so much?

In Soho, the task of finding a route into the well was taken up by a parish inquiry. Its questionnaires had produced too few useful replies, so investigators turned to interviews. One of the people who joined the inquiry was Henry Whitehead, a clergyman who had worked in the neighborhood during the outbreak.

Snow gave Whitehead a copy of his new book early in 1855. Whitehead still disagreed. He wrote to Snow that a closer inquiry would overturn the explanation that blamed the pump. He set out to investigate the people who had lived on Broad Street, including families who had moved away.

Some households were visited four or five times. Whitehead wanted to know when each fatal illness had begun, which rooms the victims had occupied and what water they had drunk. By June, his report accepted a connection between the pump water and the outbreak. But that wasn't the only discovery he had made.

In the spring of 1855, he found a death record that drew his attention to a five-month-old girl at number forty Broad Street, beside the pump. The entry described diarrhea, rather than cholera. The child's illness had begun before the worst of the outbreak. Whitehead questioned her mother.

She told him how she had washed the baby's soiled cloths and emptied some of the water into a cesspool in front of the house. The cesspool was close to the well. An investigator could now look for a particular route by which the waste might have reached the water.

In April, a surveyor examined the cesspool, the drain and the well from outside. Beneath the surface, the brickwork had deteriorated. Less than a metre separated the cesspool from the well, and the soil between them showed evidence of seepage. Looking inside the well had missed the problem in the ground around it.

No one could establish that the infant had definitely had cholera, or that this was the first infection. There was no reason to blame the mother. What the inquiry had found was a physical connection between household waste and a public drinking-water supply.

The brewery and the workhouse had other supplies. The factory workers and the two women miles away had shared the Broad Street water. The separate South London comparison made it harder to explain that pattern by the air alone. And now the investigation had a plausible route into the well.

The handle had been removed in a day. The explanation took months of work by people who had started out disagreeing. Whitehead had set out to disprove Snow's theory; by going back to the families and uncovering what lay beneath the street, he helped explain why a bottle of water could carry a disease across London.
