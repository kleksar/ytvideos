In 1995, the new Slinky Dog missed Christmas. Children could see him in Toy Story, but the company couldn't get the toy into stores in time.

That was not the first time the company had been in trouble.

During World War Two, engineer Richard James noticed how a falling coil of wire could turn end over end. He developed a spring that could repeat the movement. His wife Betty found a name for it in a dictionary: Slinky.

They borrowed five hundred dollars and persuaded a Philadelphia department store to let them demonstrate it. Four hundred springs sold in less than two hours.

But sales eventually slowed. In 1960, Richard left for Bolivia. Betty was left with six children, a company and its debts.

She moved the business back toward her hometown. In 1963 she risked her home to attend a New York toy show. The springs sold out again. Television advertising helped keep the toy recognizable, and Betty insisted on keeping the basic Slinky affordable.

Years later Disney called about a dog the company had actually stopped making. The old Slinky Dog had too many parts to manufacture economically. For Toy Story, the company needed new molds, and getting them right took longer than expected.

By December, customers were calling the factory looking for the dog. The toys finally reached stores in February 1996. In March, Betty said all the production planned through August, eight hundred and twenty-five thousand dogs, had been ordered.

She sold the company in 1998. Richard had invented a spring that could keep moving. Betty had kept a business moving for nearly four decades, even through the unusual setback that began its second comeback.
