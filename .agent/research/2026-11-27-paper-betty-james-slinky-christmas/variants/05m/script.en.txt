Why did a toy from one of the biggest children's movies of the nineties miss the Christmas shopping season? And why was the woman who ran its factory willing to bring back a toy she had already stopped making?

During the Second World War, Richard James was working on equipment for the navy. A coil of wire fell and turned over in a way that suggested an unexpected use. Instead of absorbing a shock, a spring could be made to walk down a staircase.

Getting a spring to do that reliably required more than a fortunate accident. Richard experimented with its proportions. His wife Betty chose the name from a dictionary. They borrowed five hundred dollars to get their business going.

There was a problem that no amount of packaging could solve. Look at a metal coil in a box and you might see a piece of machinery. Watch the same coil turn itself over, landing neatly on the next step, and suddenly you understand the toy.

A Philadelphia department store gave the couple a chance to demonstrate it during the Christmas season of 1945. They sold four hundred in less than two hours. At a dollar apiece, the springs were a gift that many families could afford.

The company started making other toys out of the same simple idea. A designer in Seattle, Helen Malsed, developed a pull-along dog whose front and back were joined by a spring. Children could pull it across the floor and watch its body stretch between the two halves.

But the original success did not last uninterrupted. By 1960 the business was struggling, and Richard left for Bolivia. Betty later said his donations to a religious group had put the company in a precarious financial position. She was left with six children, the oldest eighteen and the youngest two.

Betty sold the old factory and moved the business back toward the area where she had grown up. In 1963 she put her home at risk to get to a toy trade show in New York. The springs sold out. That same year a television jingle began advertising the toy, demonstrating the kind of movement that had been so difficult to explain on a shelf.

The company's product had not become complicated or expensive. Betty wanted a child without much money to be able to buy it. Half a century after the first demonstration, the price of the basic metal Slinky was still about two dollars.

Not every product was as simple to make. The company had discontinued the original dog because assembling its many separate pieces was too expensive. Then Disney called. The filmmakers wanted to put the dog in Toy Story, and they had redesigned it for the screen.

Betty said yes. This meant producing a physical dog that matched the new appearance. The molds needed repeated corrections. The company missed the Christmas shopping season of 1995.

This was the reverse of the problem at the first department store demonstration. Back then the Jameses had a product and needed customers. Now there were customers who had seen the product in a film, and the factory couldn't give it to them.

People called the company asking for the toy. The dogs finally shipped to stores in February 1996. In March, Betty told an interviewer that the factory's entire planned production through August had already been ordered: eight hundred and twenty-five thousand dogs. That was not the number already delivered. It was the amount the factory would have to make. Since the previous autumn, the workforce had increased from eighty to one hundred and twenty.

Betty sold the company in 1998, almost four decades after taking charge of it. The original spring entered the National Toy Hall of Fame in 2000. Betty herself received toy industry recognition the following year.

Her husband had discovered what a spring could do. She had worked out how to keep making it, how to show people why they wanted it, and how to make it affordable. When Disney came looking for an old dog, there was still a company to call.
