Why did a dog from Toy Story miss the biggest toy-shopping season of 1995? Why did the factory have to redesign a toy it had already sold for decades? And how did Betty James still have a company to run, thirty-five years after her husband left?

The dog began as something that looked nothing like an animal. During World War Two, Richard James was working on equipment for ships. A coil fell and turned over, and he began experimenting with a spring that could repeat that motion. When it walks down a staircase, gravity keeps supplying the energy for the next step. Richard's problem was making a spring with the right proportions to keep going. His patent, granted in 1947, even illustrated how it could descend steps.

Betty found its name in a dictionary, and the couple borrowed five hundred dollars to start the business. A shopper looking at a coil in a box could hardly guess what made it fun. So during the Christmas season of 1945, the couple demonstrated it at Gimbels in Philadelphia. At a dollar apiece, four hundred springs sold in less than two hours. The store had finally seen why the toy belonged on its shelves.

Fifty years later, the situation was reversed. The children who watched Toy Story already knew exactly what they wanted. They could see a long dog with a spring in the middle of its body. But when families asked James Industries for one, the company couldn't give them the new dog. It had stopped making the old version years earlier. The very product that Disney wanted to put in its film had been taken out of production because it was too expensive to assemble.

To understand why the company accepted that challenge, go back to the early fifties. In Seattle, a boy named Rick Malsed wondered what a Slinky would do with wheels. In the family's later account, his father fitted the wheels and his mother, Helen, used a cord to pull the contraption. She went on to turn the spring into several different animals. One of her designs was a dog. Its front and rear could pull apart, then draw back together.

James Industries put the idea into production. A surviving toy from 1955 has the two plastic body sections joined by a metal spring. Helen applied for a design patent the following year. Her patent drawings showed the dog in both its extended and compact positions. So the movie character had an ancestor that existed long before anyone at Pixar put it on a screen.

By the end of the fifties the original Slinky had lost some of its popularity. Richard had become involved with a religious group. According to Betty's later account, he had given away much of the money the business needed. In 1960, he left for Bolivia.

Richard offered Betty a choice: go to South America with him or take the business. Their six children ranged in age from two to eighteen. She chose to stay. For a while, a caregiver looked after the children while Betty spent part of each week away working. The decision meant that she had to find a way to support the family and keep a struggling factory operating at the same time.

She sold the factory near Philadelphia and moved the business toward Altoona, where she had grown up. The company eventually operated in nearby Hollidaysburg. The unpaid bills still had to be dealt with. A journalist who later knew Betty reported that she settled them over the following four years and thanked the creditors who had waited. That doesn't tell us whether the business ever went through a formal bankruptcy. It does tell us what Betty spent those early years doing.

In 1963, Betty took a financial risk involving the mortgage on her home to exhibit the spring at a toy trade show in New York. The Slinky sold out there. Retailers again had a reason to take the product seriously.

That same year, a jingle started airing on television. The medium suited this toy unusually well. A print advertisement could show a spring and tell you that it walked. A television commercial could let you see it descend. The tune gave the toy a recognizable identity even when you weren't looking at it.

In later years, large toy companies approached Betty about buying her business. She recalled turning down offers that would have meant closing the local factory. The people working there had helped her through the years when the business was struggling. Keeping that operation going was one of the reasons she gave for staying independent. She also wanted a child with only a little spending money to be able to buy the original spring.

In 1945 the spring had cost a dollar. In December 1995, a newspaper put the price at no more than two dollars. The company had kept using machines Richard had designed to wind springs. It also sold toys such as pinwheels and pompoms, which gave it products for more than one season of the year. Keeping the old spring cheap did not mean keeping every toy in the catalogue forever.

The old dog was one product Betty's company could do without. Around 1991 it was discontinued. An employee explained the problem to a reporter four years later: the dog had roughly a dozen separate parts, whereas some of the other spring animals were made with far fewer. Every piece was another item to manufacture or obtain and fit together. A toy that used the same basic spring could be much harder to produce than the spring itself.

Then Disney called about Toy Story. Betty agreed to let the company bring back the dog. This was not a case of opening a storeroom and finding boxes of finished toys. The movie dog had a different appearance, and that meant making new molds. If a molded part did not come out right, producing more of it would not solve the problem.

Betty explained in December 1995 that the company had had to correct the molds. It had a usable version by October, but couldn't get the dogs into stores for Christmas. At the factory's own outlet, visitors were already asking for the new toy. Other customers contacted the company to find out where they could buy it. In the movie they had seen the character moving; now they were trying to find the physical version.

There is another way to see the change the factory was dealing with. In February 1996, an inventor named Simon Cheng filed a patent for an articulated spring toy assigned to James Industries. His design used plates inside the body to hold the ends of the spring in place. Unlike Helen's design patent, this application concerned how the pieces were connected. It isn't a record of exactly which mold caused the Christmas delay, but it shows how the company was continuing to develop the construction of the dog around the time of its return.

The new dogs were finally shipped to stores in February 1996. In March, Betty reported that all the production planned through August had been spoken for. Eight hundred and twenty-five thousand units. That figure describes orders for the production period, not the number of toys that had already left the factory.

When a reporter visited Betty at the Toy Fair in March, her eldest son Tom was showing the product to buyers from Ireland. The film was due to open in Europe that month. The factory had increased its workforce from eighty to one hundred and twenty since the previous autumn. It now had to fill orders for a toy that had not even been part of its product line a few years earlier. And this time nobody needed to show the buyers what made a Slinky Dog interesting.

Betty sold the company in 1998. The spring was inducted into the National Toy Hall of Fame in 2000, by which time more than a quarter of a billion had been sold. Betty received recognition from the toy industry the next year.

The first time a Slinky sold out at Christmas, Betty and Richard had a new spring and needed a store to give it a chance. The second time, decades later, a store could have sold the new dog if only there had been enough dogs to sell. Betty had gone from finding a name for a spring to working out how to save the business that made it. Her decision to keep the factory alive meant that when the phone rang about Toy Story, there was still someone there to answer it.
