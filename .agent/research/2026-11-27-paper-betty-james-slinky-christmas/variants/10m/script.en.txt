Why was Slinky Dog missing from toy stores at Christmas, just when children could see him in Toy Story? How had a simple metal spring survived half a century of changes in the toy business? And who kept the company going after the inventor walked away?

Start with the original toy, before it had a face, a tail, or a part in a movie. There was just a coil of wire.

During World War Two, engineer Richard James was working on equipment intended for use aboard ships. The story of his invention begins with a coil that fell and turned over. He saw a possible toy in that movement.

If you have ever put a Slinky on a staircase, you know what he was trying to reproduce. The front end of the spring bends down toward the next step. The rest of the spring follows. When it settles, the end that had been at the back is now at the front, ready for another step. There is no motor in the coil. As the toy descends, gravity supplies the energy, while the spring repeatedly changes shape. Richard worked on a coil with the right proportions for this behavior.

His patent application, filed in 1946, described the toy together with the way it could be used on descending surfaces. The patent was granted the following January. But by then, Richard and Betty James had already learned that making an object and selling it were two different jobs.

Betty found the name Slinky in a dictionary. She and Richard borrowed five hundred dollars for their venture. The difficulty was how to explain the purpose of a coil of wire to a shopper who had never seen one moving. It wasn't an obvious plaything. In a department store, a demonstration could solve the problem more effectively than a description.

During the Christmas shopping season of 1945, a Philadelphia department store agreed to give them the opportunity. They brought four hundred toys. In under two hours they had sold the lot. Each cost a dollar.

The little spring was not a one-off trick. With a repeatable manufacturing process, a store could stock more of them. In 1951 the company moved its factory from Philadelphia to Paoli. Richard had designed machinery for the production of his springs, and the business kept using the principle behind the original invention.

Some of the most useful new ideas did not come from the inventor himself. In Seattle, a woman called Helen Malsed developed toys using the Slinky as a connecting part. A dog was among the designs she brought to James Industries. In place of an ordinary rigid toy on wheels, the front and rear of the dog could move apart and come back together.

The dog gave the company a way to sell the same kind of spring to a child who wanted an animal rather than a coil of wire. Other animal designs joined the product line. But having more products did not guarantee that the company would remain healthy.

By the end of the fifties the original Slinky had lost some of its popularity. Richard had become involved with a religious group. According to Betty's later account, he had given away much of the money the business needed. In 1960, he left for Bolivia.

Betty was now responsible for the company and six children. The youngest was two years old. The eldest was eighteen. At first she spent several days each week away from the children while a caregiver looked after them.

She sold the factory and moved the company toward Altoona, Pennsylvania, where she had grown up. Eventually James Industries operated in nearby Hollidaysburg. The move preserved a business that otherwise might not have had a future. But it didn't automatically create a new generation of customers.

In 1963, Betty took a financial risk involving the mortgage on her home to exhibit the spring at a toy trade show in New York. The Slinky sold out there. Retailers again had a reason to take the product seriously.

That same year, a jingle started airing on television. The medium suited this toy unusually well. A print advertisement could show a spring and tell you that it walked. A television commercial could let you see it descend. The tune gave the toy a recognizable identity even when you weren't looking at it.

Another part of keeping the company alive was resisting the temptation to turn its basic product into a more expensive object. Betty wanted children with very little spending money to be able to have a Slinky. At its introduction it had cost one dollar. A newspaper report in December 1995 put the price at no more than two dollars.

Think of the other costs behind that decision. Every new piece of machinery, every additional component, and every change to the manufacturing process had to be justified by what it would accomplish. The factory continued to rely on machinery Richard had designed because it worked well for the product. A new design would have to offer something more than novelty.

The company also broadened its product line, adding such things as pinwheels and pompoms in the seventies. Toys with different selling seasons made it possible to reduce dependence on the Christmas rush. The point was not to abandon the spring; it was to have more than one thing to sell.

That strategy didn't make all the products permanent. The dog in particular was difficult to manufacture economically. A company employee explained in 1995 that the old design contained a dozen or so parts, while some of the other spring animals had far fewer. The old dog was dropped from the line around 1991.

Now picture the problem from the filmmakers' side. Toy Story was going to feature recognizable playthings alongside its fictional characters. A spring with a dog's head and tail was particularly well suited to an animated world: the body could stretch between the two ends. Disney contacted James Industries and asked about including the dog.

Betty agreed. There was one awkward detail. The old toy no longer existed as a current product. Nor could the company simply produce another batch of its old dog. The movie's version had a different appearance, and the manufacturer needed new molds.

In December 1995, Betty described the difficulties to a reporter. The molds had required corrections, and the process had taken longer than the company expected. Workers had a version to produce by October, but the dogs were still not ready for Christmas.

Inside a movie theater, children could already recognize the new dog. At the factory's own outlet, visitors wanted to buy it. A longtime employee described people contacting the company in search of something it could not yet supply.

For many businesses, missing the season could be the end of the opportunity. The first surge of demand can be short-lived. In this case, the film continued to bring people to the toy.

The new dogs were finally shipped to stores in February 1996. In March, Betty reported that all the production planned through August had been spoken for. Eight hundred and twenty-five thousand units. That figure describes orders for the production period, not the number of toys that had already left the factory.

The company's workforce, meanwhile, had grown from eighty to one hundred and twenty since the previous autumn. The situation was almost the opposite of what the Jameses had faced at the department store in 1945. Then they had springs but needed to show people why they might want one. Now people knew the dog, and the challenge was to provide enough of them.

Betty sold the company in 1998. The spring was inducted into the National Toy Hall of Fame in 2000, by which time more than a quarter of a billion had been sold. Betty received recognition from the toy industry the next year.

The story began when Richard James saw a different purpose in an ordinary spring. It continued because Betty James managed to keep the price low, the factory working, and the product familiar, long after the first excitement had worn off.

The movie gave that work an unexpected reward. The Christmas it missed was not the end of the Slinky Dog. It was the moment when a factory that had nearly disappeared decades earlier found itself having to catch up with a new audience.
