A judge stopped Kodak from selling the film its instant cameras needed. The cameras already in people's homes still worked. But soon there was nothing to put inside them.

For years, Kodak had supplied photographic material to Polaroid. Then the two companies became rivals. In 1972, Polaroid introduced the SX-70. Press a button and a picture came out of the camera. You could watch it develop without peeling anything away.

Kodak introduced its own instant cameras four years later. Polaroid sued almost immediately, claiming that the new cameras and their film used its patented inventions.

The lawsuit dragged on while Kodak kept selling cameras. By the end of 1985, it had sold more than sixteen and a half million of them.

Then the court order took effect in January 1986. Kodak had to leave the instant photography business. Owners couldn't simply buy a pack of Polaroid film instead: the systems weren't compatible.

At first Kodak offered customers a different kind of camera, coupons, or a share of Kodak stock. The lawyers representing consumers rejected those offers. In 1988, a settlement offered owners fifty to seventy dollars in cash and discount coupons, depending on the model. To claim it, they had to send in a coupon and the nameplate from the camera.

That was a separate settlement from the money Kodak owed Polaroid. By 1991, Polaroid had collected nearly nine hundred and twenty-five million dollars.

The oddest thing was that the court didn't need to take a single camera out of anyone's hands. A working shutter could still open. The lens could still focus an image. Once the remaining film ran out, ordinary use of the camera became impossible. How could so many working cameras lose the very thing that made them useful?
