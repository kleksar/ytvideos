How can a camera become useless without a single part breaking? Why did Kodak eventually pay its rival almost a billion dollars, and offer its own customers money for the nameplate on their cameras?

For decades, getting a photograph meant waiting. You took the picture, finished the roll and had the film developed. Polaroid sold a different experience. Its early instant cameras could give you a picture while the people in it were still standing in front of you. You had to wait for the chemicals to work and peel the picture away from the rest of the film, but you didn't need to visit a photo lab.

One of the companies supplying material for this system was Kodak. Its relationship with Polaroid went back to the early nineteen-fifties. The two companies even had an agreement covering work on color photography.

Meanwhile, Polaroid was trying to eliminate the last awkward steps. In 1968, its founder Edwin Land showed a Kodak research executive pictures made using a new process. The companies discussed licensing and further supply arrangements but did not reach an agreement. The next year Kodak notified Polaroid that it intended to end their existing agreement. It was also starting a project to develop an instant photography product of its own.

Then, in 1972, Polaroid introduced the SX-70. It folded into a compact shape, and its film did not need to be peeled apart. A motor pushed a photograph out through rollers, which squeezed developing chemicals across the sheet. You could hold the finished picture by its border and watch the image appear.

Kodak had to compete with that experience, not with the older peel-apart camera. One of its early designs used a cord to eject a photograph. It was considerably bulkier. Kodak abandoned a separate project for film compatible with the older Polaroid system after spending ninety-four million dollars on it. Work continued on Kodak's own film and camera.

In April 1976, Kodak finally brought out its instant photography line. Buyers now had another major brand to choose from. But the two systems needed different film.

Polaroid filed a patent lawsuit that April. It wasn't arguing that no one else should ever be allowed to make an instant camera. It claimed that particular technical inventions inside Kodak's products were covered by its patents. Kodak disputed those claims. For years, while the companies fought over the patents, people bought Kodak cameras and used Kodak film.

In October 1985, a federal court ruled that Kodak had infringed valid patent claims. An order barring Kodak from the business was due to take effect in January. Kodak tried to delay the order while it appealed. The court refused.

On January ninth, 1986, the order took effect. Kodak's instant camera and film business was finished. By then more than sixteen and a half million Kodak instant cameras had been sold. People who had one could use whatever compatible film they still had, but they could not replace it with Polaroid film. The object they had bought for taking pictures now depended on a disappearing supply.

Kodak offered three alternatives: a different kind of camera, coupons worth fifty dollars, or one share of its stock. The lawyers representing consumers rejected the options. A camera owner might have to spend more money to redeem a coupon, and selling a single share could involve a fee.

A consumer settlement followed in 1988. Owners were offered fifty to seventy dollars in a mixture of cash and coupons, with the amount depending on the model. Kodak expected roughly four million people to receive compensation. The unusual proof they had to send was the camera's nameplate, together with a coupon. The camera didn't have to disappear from the world; its badge could be removed and mailed away.

Polaroid's own damages were dealt with separately. In 1991, after years of litigation, the company collected almost nine hundred and twenty-five million dollars.

The two sides of the dispute ended in very different ways. Polaroid was paid for the economic harm of the infringement. A Kodak customer could be compensated for a camera that no longer had its normal supply of film. The patent dispute had led to two different kinds of payment. But a consumer payment could not restore the thing customers had actually bought: a camera they could keep loading with fresh film.

The technology still worked. It was the system around it that had been shut down.
