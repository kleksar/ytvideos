Why did one of the biggest names in photography stop selling film to people who had already bought its cameras? Why did some of those customers have to take the brand name off the camera to obtain compensation? And how did the company that used to supply Polaroid end up paying it nearly a billion dollars?

Before instant photography, taking a picture and getting a print were usually two separate jobs. A roll of film went to a darkroom, and the person in the picture might be far away by the time the print came back. Edwin Land wanted to bring the darkroom into the camera.

Polaroid's camera went on sale in December 1948. Its first pictures were sepia. Later came black-and-white prints, and in 1963, color. You could get a photograph on the spot, but you still had to pull the sheets apart after the chemicals had worked. Kodak, a giant in conventional photography, helped supply the material that made that possible.

In 1957 the two companies agreed to work together on negative material for color instant pictures. Polaroid shared some of its color technology, and Kodak helped turn that technology into a product. A company making instant cameras was relying on the company whose name was already on film boxes around the world.

In October 1968, Land showed Kodak's head of research, Henry Yutzy, pictures made with a new process. The two companies discussed licensing and further supplies but reached no agreement. The following spring, Kodak notified Polaroid that it intended to end their 1957 arrangement. By then Kodak had started its own instant photography project. The supplier was becoming a competitor.

Kodak initially pursued two products. One was film that would fit the older Polaroid cameras. The other was an entire Kodak system, in which the company would make both the camera and the film. Then Polaroid changed the product that Kodak was trying to compete with.

In April 1972, Polaroid announced the SX-70. The camera could fold down when not in use. To take a picture, you pressed a button, and the camera's motor advanced the exposed sheet through a pair of rollers. A small pouch containing processing liquid was squeezed as it passed between them. The developing liquid spread over the picture. Instead of peeling the image away from another sheet, the photographer watched the photograph develop in daylight.

The trick was keeping the unfinished image safe from light. One patented Polaroid design sent the sheet along a curved path before it emerged; the arrangement helped protect the film and distribute the developing liquid. A customer could see a simple white-bordered picture coming out. Inside the camera, even the route that piece of film took mattered. That sort of detail would eventually become the subject of a lawsuit.

Kodak's engineers had a very different prototype. To get the picture out, the photographer pulled a cord. The Lanyard camera also had mechanisms to remove the chemical pouch and cut the edges of the picture. Kodak's marketing staff had accepted the size of that prototype, but the new Polaroid camera made it look bulky. The company reconsidered the design.

When the SX-70 reached stores in October 1972, Kodak bought cameras and film in large quantities and had its teams study and test them. By January, the managers' assessment of their own project was bleak: matching the competing product would still take more than two and a half years, and even then they expected an inferior design. Polaroid had already put its new system on sale.

The other project, the film for the earlier Polaroid system, was abandoned toward the end of 1972. Kodak had spent ninety-four million dollars on it. Its researchers had unresolved chemical problems, and the new SX-70 made the old type of product look outdated even before Kodak could get it onto the shelves.

Kodak concentrated on its own camera and film. At the peak of development, between thirteen hundred and fourteen hundred people were assigned to the project. A team that could not solve a problem with one technology tried another. In April 1976, Kodak finally had two cameras, the EK-4 and EK-6, and its own PR10 film ready for customers.

For a buyer, the decision extended beyond the price of the camera. Each packet of photographs used up material, and each new packet had to fit the camera. Buying Kodak meant returning to the shop for Kodak-compatible film. That continuing supply was part of what made the original purchase valuable.

Polaroid sued on April twenty-sixth, 1976. The dispute covered specific inventions used in cameras and film, not the abstract idea of taking a photograph quickly. Kodak argued that the patent claims were invalid or that it had not infringed them. The case took years to work through the evidence. The trial itself occupied seventy-five days between October 1981 and February 1982.

While the companies argued over their inventions, Kodak continued selling instant cameras. More than sixteen and a half million were sold between 1976 and 1985. Every sale added another camera to a household or a business that would need a supply of compatible film.

On October eleventh, 1985, the district court ruled that Kodak had infringed valid claims from seven Polaroid patents. The order would stop Kodak manufacturing and selling the offending instant photography products in the United States. Kodak tried to get the injunction suspended during its appeal. On January ninth, 1986, it took effect.

There was no need for a court official to visit every home and collect the cameras. Once the normal film supply was withdrawn, the effect would reach those homes through the shops. A remaining packet of film still worked. Polaroid's packets, though, were incompatible. When an owner ran out of compatible film, a perfectly good shutter and lens were no longer enough.

Kodak announced an exchange program. Camera owners could register by telephone and trade in their camera for a Kodak Disc camera with film, a fifty-dollar merchandise rebate, or a share of Kodak stock. A Disc camera took a different kind of photograph; it did not restore the instant camera's function. And getting cash from a single share could mean paying a broker.

Twelve days after the ban took effect, Joyce and William Sampson filed a lawsuit on behalf of camera owners. They challenged the adequacy of Kodak's exchange offer. Thirteen similar suits followed in other courts. On February thirteenth, the Illinois court certified the Sampson case as a class action and stopped Kodak from carrying out its original exchange plan. The first patent ruling had removed the film; a second court was now dealing with what Kodak owed the people who had bought the cameras.

Meanwhile, the legal route back to the instant business narrowed further. In April 1986, the federal appeals court affirmed the appealed portions of the judgment. In October, the Supreme Court declined to take up Kodak's challenge.

Negotiations over the consumer claims continued for more than two years. In May 1988 a proposed settlement offered camera owners between fifty and seventy dollars in cash and discounts, depending on the model. Notices went to more than four million class members. Around two hundred formally objected to the proposal; the court examined its fairness in September and approved the settlement. Those notices were not a count of every camera sold or every person who actually collected money.

To claim the payment, a camera owner needed to mail back a coupon and the nameplate from the camera. A little badge bearing the manufacturer's name could now be used to obtain compensation. The lens and shutter could stay at home. The new settlement had found a way to compensate owners without making the instant film available again.

Even after the camera owners' case was settled, Kodak and Polaroid were still arguing over the money owed for the patents. The final judgment amounted to about eight hundred and seventy-three million dollars, including interest accumulated before judgment. In July 1991, Kodak paid that award plus further interest: altogether nearly nine hundred and twenty-five million dollars. The patent litigation that began in 1976 was finally over.

Polaroid used the money to pay taxes, reduce debt, buy back stock and give employees a special bonus. It received an enormous payment, but that money did not go to the households whose cameras had lost their normal source of film. Those owners had a different settlement for a different loss.

For the camera buyer, however, the outcome was still visible in the empty film compartment. A consumer could get money for the badge, but not a new supply of the original film. The former partners had spent decades perfecting a photograph that could develop in someone's hands. Their legal dispute ended by showing how much that photograph depended on something the owner had never bought outright: the next packet of film.
