Why did one of the biggest names in photography stop selling film to people who had already bought its cameras? Why did some of those customers have to take the brand name off the camera to obtain compensation? And how did the company that used to supply Polaroid end up paying it nearly a billion dollars?

To understand that ending, it helps to start with what a photograph used to require. With a conventional film camera, the light entering the lens creates an image that you cannot yet see. Chemicals are needed to turn that invisible image into a visible one. In the days when most people used film, the processing normally happened somewhere else, after the photographer had finished taking pictures.

Edwin Land and Polaroid set out to bring those steps into a single photographic system. In 1948, Polaroid put an early instant camera on the market. It produced sepia pictures. The company developed black-and-white film and later a color system known as Polacolor. The picture was available quickly, but the photographer still had to separate the finished image from other parts of the film.

For this business Polaroid needed photographic materials. Kodak supplied negative material from the early nineteen-fifties. In 1957, the companies entered an agreement involving color instant photography. They were not simply two competing camera brands looking at each other across a shop counter. Their businesses were connected.

By the late nineteen-sixties, Polaroid was developing a much more ambitious kind of film. In 1968, Land showed photographs made with the new process to a Kodak research executive. Discussions about licenses and continued supply did not produce an agreement. In April 1969, Kodak announced its intention to terminate the earlier agreement. Kodak had also begun its own instant photography development project.

That decision created a problem that could not be solved just by making a better lens. Kodak needed film chemistry, a mechanism that could handle the film and a camera that could deliver the picture customers wanted. There were two possible approaches. One project, called P-129, was developing color instant film compatible with existing Polaroid cameras. The other, P-130, aimed to make a complete Kodak system, with a Kodak camera and Kodak film.

In April 1972, Polaroid announced the SX-70. The camera could fold down when not in use. To take a picture, you pressed a button, and the camera's motor advanced the exposed sheet through a pair of rollers. A small pouch containing processing liquid was squeezed as it passed between them. The developing liquid spread over the picture. Instead of peeling the image away from another sheet, the photographer watched the photograph develop in daylight.

Every stage had to work with the stage before it. The film had to advance one sheet at a time. The rollers had to squeeze the liquid across the image rather than leave a patch uncovered. The undeveloped portion of the sheet had to be protected from light while the front of the picture was already leaving the camera.

Take that last problem. A photograph is stiff enough to slide out of a slot, but light entering the wrong part of the material can spoil the image. One of Polaroid's patented designs bent the film's path inside the camera before it came out. The longer path through a protected space helped keep unwanted light from reaching the exposed film. It also helped the processing liquid spread more evenly. The shape of the film path was not just a styling choice. It did two useful jobs.

Kodak's own developers had already been working on these kinds of problems. One camera prototype, known as the Lanyard, relied on a cord to move the picture out. It had mechanisms for removing the chemical pouch and trimming the picture. The result was relatively large. After Kodak saw what the SX-70 offered, that approach was no longer considered desirable.

The other project, the film for the earlier Polaroid system, was abandoned toward the end of 1972. Kodak had spent ninety-four million dollars on it. Its researchers had unresolved chemical problems, and the new SX-70 made the old type of product look outdated even before Kodak could get it onto the shelves.

So Kodak put enormous resources into the complete Kodak system. At the peak of the project, between thirteen hundred and fourteen hundred people were working on it. Researchers tackled different parts of the system at the same time. Kodak eventually brought its EK-4 and EK-6 cameras and PR10 film to market in April 1976.

Imagine the choice from the other side of the shop counter. An instant camera was not a one-time purchase. You bought a camera and then returned to buy packets of film. The film was specific to the system. Each new Kodak customer was therefore also a potential customer for future Kodak film sales.

Polaroid sued on April twenty-sixth, 1976. The dispute covered specific inventions used in cameras and film, not the abstract idea of taking a photograph quickly. Kodak argued that the patent claims were invalid or that it had not infringed them. The case took years to work through the evidence. The trial itself occupied seventy-five days between October 1981 and February 1982.

While those engineers and lawyers examined the details, Kodak kept selling its instant cameras. More than sixteen and a half million were sold between 1976 and 1985. A household buying one in the middle of that period could not know how a federal court would eventually determine whether the manufacturer could keep supplying its film.

The district court issued its judgment on October eleventh, 1985. Polaroid prevailed on infringed claims from seven patents. Not every claim it asserted survived the case. The judgment nonetheless included an order stopping Kodak's instant camera and film business, effective January ninth, 1986.

Kodak asked the appeals court to delay that order while it challenged the judgment. On January sixth, the court denied the request. Three days later, the injunction took effect.

Now consider the camera owner's situation. The machine still contained a lens, a shutter and a mechanism for processing the film. The order did not make those components stop functioning. But the film packs Kodak normally sold for them were withdrawn from the market. Polaroid's packs were incompatible. A remaining pack could still make photographs, but using the last one left the owner facing a problem no repair shop could solve by replacing a broken part.

Kodak's first compensation plan offered three choices: a Kodak Disc camera, fifty dollars in coupons, or a share of Kodak stock. The camera was a different photographic system; coupons involved buying other Kodak products; selling a share could entail a brokerage commission. Lawyers for camera owners rejected the choices.

Meanwhile, the legal route back to the instant business narrowed further. In April 1986, the federal appeals court affirmed the appealed portions of the judgment. In October, the Supreme Court declined to take up Kodak's challenge.

Owners pursued their own claims. In 1988, Kodak agreed to a consumer settlement offering fifty to seventy dollars in cash and coupons, according to the model. It sent information to about 4.3 million people who had responded to its efforts to identify owners. That is not the same as sixteen and a half million cameras sold, and not every person who had bought a camera necessarily collected compensation.

One condition made this an unusual exchange. Owners had to return the nameplate from the camera and a coupon. Think of the two objects side by side: a camera missing its badge, and the form that could obtain compensation for it. Neither was a replacement pack of film.

Polaroid's claim for damages was separate. In the final order, about two hundred and thirty-three million dollars represented lost profits, about two hundred and four million were calculated using a royalty measure, and more than four hundred and thirty-five million represented interest before judgment. Additional interest brought the amount Polaroid actually collected to just over nine hundred and twenty-four and a half million dollars in 1991.

Polaroid used the money to pay taxes, reduce debt, buy back stock and give employees a special bonus. It received an enormous payment, but that money did not go to the households whose cameras had lost their normal source of film. Those owners had a different settlement for a different loss.

The whole story began with companies working together on materials for a photograph. It ended with Kodak paying both a former partner and customers who had bought its rival system. The former partner sought compensation for patented inventions that Kodak had used; the latter had simply wanted to keep taking pictures.

For the owners, the decisive part of an instant camera had always been the part they consumed with each photograph. Without a reliable supply of that material, the rest of the machine could be perfectly intact and still be unusable in its intended way. The most expensive argument about the camera ended up taking away something that wasn't permanently inside the camera at all.
