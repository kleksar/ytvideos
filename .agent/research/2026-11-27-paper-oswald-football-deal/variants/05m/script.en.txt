Why would a television network give up a cartoon rabbit to hire a football commentator? And why would Disney ask for that particular rabbit, nearly eighty years after it stopped making his cartoons?

The answer begins in a small animation studio in California. Walt Disney and his brother Roy had been making short films in which a real girl appeared in a drawn world. In 1927, their next series needed a completely animated leading character. Walt and his animator Ub Iwerks created Oswald the Lucky Rabbit for Universal.

In September, audiences saw a film called Trolley Troubles. Oswald went on to become a popular character, and the Disney studio produced twenty-six shorts featuring him. For a young studio, a successful series meant more work and a chance to negotiate better terms.

So in February 1928, Walt went to New York. He wanted the distributor, Charles Mintz, to pay more for the next batch of films.

The meeting revealed a much bigger problem than the price of a cartoon.

Mintz had already been arranging to make the series with other animators, including people from Walt's own studio. Walt sent a telegram to Roy, asking him to secure contracts with the staff. Most refused. Meanwhile, the rights to Oswald belonged to Universal. Walt couldn't take his character to a competing distributor and keep making the same series.

He went home without a new contract. The studio still had existing Oswald films to finish, even as it prepared to lose people who had helped make them.

One important animator stayed. Ub Iwerks worked with Walt on a new character: a mouse. They developed the film Plane Crazy while the rabbit cartoons were still being completed. A second mouse cartoon followed, but getting those silent films distributed proved difficult.

Then came Steamboat Willie, with synchronized sound. It reached theaters in November 1928 and turned Mickey into a success. Universal, meanwhile, continued making Oswald cartoons without Walt.

That could have been the end of their connection.

Decades later, the problem of who could work for whom appeared in a completely different part of the entertainment industry. Al Michaels had been calling Monday Night Football for twenty years. In 2005, he agreed to stay with ABC and ESPN as the Monday broadcast moved to ESPN. But he subsequently wanted to join his broadcasting partner John Madden and other members of their team at NBC, which was launching a Sunday night NFL broadcast.

He needed his existing employer to release him.

Disney owned ABC and ESPN. NBCUniversal controlled Oswald. And Disney's chief executive, Bob Iger, had already told Walt's daughter that he wanted to bring the rabbit back.

Here was an opportunity to make an agreement that served both companies. ESPN president George Bodenheimer opened talks with NBC Sports chairman Dick Ebersol. From NBC's perspective, getting Michaels was useful. Ebersol said the Oswald rights weren't bringing his company money in the United States.

On February ninth, 2006, the agreement was announced. Michaels could move to NBC. Disney gained Oswald. ESPN also obtained Friday coverage rights for four Ryder Cups, more Olympic highlights and other programming and promotional benefits.

The popular version of this story is that a football commentator was traded for a cartoon rabbit. The actual deal was a bundle of rights and contractual permissions. That is precisely what made it possible: the two companies wanted different things.

And Oswald's return produced something that Walt Disney and Ub Iwerks could never have made in 1928.

In 2010, the video game Epic Mickey brought the rabbit and the mouse together. Its fictional world imagined Oswald as an older brother who resented Mickey for taking the life that might have been his. The game turned a real separation into a story in which the two characters could meet.

There was another reunion in 2015. A researcher recognized an entry in the British Film Institute's archive catalogue. It was Sleigh Bells, an Oswald cartoon from 1928 thought to be lost. A restored print was shown in London that December.

A contract had separated the rabbit from his creators. Another contract made it possible for their studio to work with him again. And nearly a century after their original films, the rabbit and the mouse had become part of the same story.
