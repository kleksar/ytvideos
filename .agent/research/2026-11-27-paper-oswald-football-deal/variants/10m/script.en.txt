Why did a football commentator's move between television networks bring a forgotten cartoon rabbit back to Disney? And why did the people making a video game decades later go looking for the rabbit's old films? The answers begin with a studio that was still trying to establish itself in the 1920s.

In the 1920s, Walt and Roy Disney ran a young animation studio in California. One of its series was the Alice Comedies. A real girl appeared in an otherwise animated setting. For their next series, the central figure would be drawn too. Walt worked with Ub Iwerks, an animator who had been working with him since their days in Kansas City. They created Oswald the Lucky Rabbit for Universal.

The first film they completed for the series was Poor Papa. Universal rejected it. The studio's next attempt, Trolley Troubles, made it into theaters in September 1927. Poor Papa had to wait until June of the following year. By then the rabbit had an audience, and the Disney studio ultimately made twenty-six Oswald cartoons.

That success gave Walt a reason to ask for better terms. His studio made the films, Charles Mintz arranged their distribution, and Universal owned the character. In early 1928, Walt went to New York to negotiate.

A later historical account based on records from the time puts his requested fee at two thousand five hundred dollars per film, up from two thousand two hundred and fifty. But the negotiations were about to cost him far more than the difference between those two figures.

Instead, he learned that several members of his own team had been approached by Mintz's side of the business. They could continue making Oswald cartoons without him.

Walt sent Roy a telegram, asking him to get the animators under contract. Most would not sign. He also tried finding another business arrangement in New York, without success.

Even if Roy hired a new team, Walt couldn't take Oswald to another distributor. Universal owned the character.

When Walt returned to California, he had an awkward job to finish. He still needed to deliver the remaining Oswald cartoons, some of them made with the very people who were preparing to leave.

Meanwhile, Iwerks was already drawing a new character: a mouse. Recollections differ on how he concealed the work from other animators, but some of the production took place at Walt's home. Walt's wife Lillian, Roy's wife and other helpers worked on the drawings while the studio finished its rabbit films.

Their first Mickey film was Plane Crazy. It used an idea familiar to audiences of the time: the fascination with aviation. The mouse builds a plane. The flight becomes chaotic. One of the film's visual effects put the viewer in the position of the pilot, looking at the ground as the aircraft falls. A member of the team later recalled the painstaking work of changing the position of a painted background between photographs.

The team completed the film and gave it a preview, but couldn't readily find a distributor. A second silent Mickey film ran into the same problem.

Walt tried a different approach with the third film. Steamboat Willie synchronized the sounds with the events on screen. Mickey's movements now had a musical and comic rhythm that an audience could hear as well as see. In November 1928, the film reached cinemas and Mickey found his audience.

Oswald had a career of his own after that. New shorts continued to appear, and the rabbit later appeared in comic books. His creators, meanwhile, built a studio that became famous for a mouse they could keep.

The Disney company eventually grew far beyond animation. By 2005 it owned ABC and ESPN, which put an entirely different contract in its hands. This one belonged to a man whose voice millions of sports fans knew.

Al Michaels had spent roughly three decades at ABC, twenty years of them calling Monday Night Football. John Madden had worked alongside him for the last four seasons. In July 2005, Michaels agreed to stay as the Monday games moved to ESPN.

Then Madden and members of the broadcast crew headed for NBC. Michaels later explained how much he wanted to stay with that group, including producer Fred Gaudelli and director Drew Esocoff. NBC was preparing Sunday night football, and Michaels asked to join them instead.

ABC and ESPN had to release him from his agreement. NBC, in turn, belonged to the group that still owned Oswald.

Bob Iger had told Walt Disney's daughter Diane that he wanted the rabbit back. She confirmed that conversation when the agreement was announced. Now there was a reason for the two businesses to negotiate.

In January 2006, ESPN president George Bodenheimer called NBC Sports chairman Dick Ebersol. Ebersol later said that the rabbit was not generating US revenue for his company. But Disney also wanted things that would be useful on its sports channels.

On February ninth, the companies announced the result. Michaels could join NBC, and Disney acquired Oswald's rights.

The sports arrangements went in both directions. ESPN received rights to Friday play in four Ryder Cups and broader access to Olympic highlights. NBC was to promote ESPN's Monday games during Sunday football, and obtained additional rights to highlights from ABC and ESPN events.

Michaels himself joked about becoming the answer to a trivia question. And NBC was where he had made his first television broadcast, back in 1971. He was returning to a network where his career had begun; the rabbit was going back to its original studio. Neither return was a literal one-for-one trade.

For the people who would make new work with Oswald, there was a different problem: how do you introduce a character whose earliest films are missing in part?

Game designer Warren Spector and his team went through the available original films and animation tests. In 2010 he described having thirteen of Walt's twenty-six Oswald cartoons available at the time. They discovered a rabbit with an unusual physical vocabulary: he could turn his ears into paddles or use his tail as a cleaning tool. Those old gags helped them understand what made him different from Mickey.

Epic Mickey, released in 2010, made the separation itself part of the fictional story. The two characters became brothers. The older rabbit blamed the younger mouse for the life he had missed. Players could help the characters reconnect. It was a fictional family conflict drawn from the history of the two characters, not a reconstruction of what anyone felt in 1928.

Some original Oswald films were still turning up. Five years after the game, a researcher browsing the British Film Institute's online catalogue spotted an entry for Sleigh Bells, a 1928 cartoon that had been considered lost.

A surviving print had been preserved in the archive. Disney's animation studio used it to make new film and digital preservation copies. On December twelfth, 2015, audiences at BFI Southbank in London could see that six-minute cartoon again, eighty-seven years after it was made.

The discovery was a separate archival event, not a condition of the football deal. But Disney could now take part in both restoring the old work and making new work with the character.

For the company's centenary, Disney released a new one-minute Oswald short, the studio's first new cartoon starring him in ninety-five years. A character whose first film had been turned down in 1927 was once again the leading figure in a new Disney production.

Walt never saw that reunion. But the two characters he helped create could finally appear in the same story, after a football contract opened a door that an animation contract had closed.
