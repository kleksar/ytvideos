What do a rabbit drawn in the 1920s, a football broadcast in 2006 and a film reel found in Britain have in common? Why did Disney negotiate for a character it hadn't animated for generations? And how did that decision change the way two of Walt Disney's earliest creations could appear together?

To understand the answer, we need to go back to the time when there was no Mickey Mouse.

In the 1920s, Walt and Roy Disney ran a young animation studio in California. One of its series was the Alice Comedies. A real girl appeared in an otherwise animated setting. For their next series, the central figure would be drawn too. Walt worked with Ub Iwerks, an animator who had been working with him since their days in Kansas City. They created Oswald the Lucky Rabbit for Universal.

The first released Oswald film, Trolley Troubles, reached theaters in September 1927. The character was recognizable from film to film. Audiences could come back to see another adventure involving the same rabbit, and the studio had a continuing source of work. In all, Walt's studio produced twenty-six Oswald cartoons.

But there were three businesses involved. Walt's studio made the films. Charles Mintz handled distribution arrangements. Universal had the rights to the character. Those distinctions did not matter much to an audience watching a rabbit on a cinema screen. They mattered enormously when Walt wanted a new contract.

In early 1928, Walt traveled to New York. A later historical account, drawing on records from the period, describes him asking for a fee increase from two thousand two hundred and fifty dollars to two thousand five hundred dollars per film. The series had been popular. He wanted better terms for continuing it.

Instead, he learned that several members of his own team had been approached by Mintz's side of the business. They could continue making Oswald cartoons without him.

Walt sent Roy a telegram, asking him to get the animators under contract. Most would not sign. He also tried finding another business arrangement in New York, without success.

He couldn't solve the problem by taking Oswald to another company. Universal owned the character. Even if the Disney brothers could put together another animation team, the rabbit would not be theirs to use in a competing series.

When Walt returned to California, he had an awkward job to finish. He still needed to deliver the remaining Oswald cartoons, some of them made with the very people who were preparing to leave.

At the same time, he and Iwerks needed to develop something new. They could not simply stop work on the rabbit and start drawing the mouse in front of everyone.

Accounts of that period describe a parallel production. Iwerks worked on the drawings away from the other animators. There are different recollections of exactly how he kept them hidden, so the precise arrangement isn't certain. But the secrecy extended beyond the studio. Walt's house became part of the production, with Lillian, Roy's wife and other helpers working on the drawings.

Their first Mickey film was Plane Crazy. It used an idea familiar to audiences of the time: the fascination with aviation. The mouse builds a plane. The flight becomes chaotic. One of the film's visual effects put the viewer in the position of the pilot, looking at the ground as the aircraft falls. A member of the team later recalled the painstaking work of changing the position of a painted background between photographs.

The finished film showed what the remaining team could do. Yet making a film and getting it into theaters were separate problems. After a preview of Plane Crazy, the studio made another Mickey cartoon. It struggled to secure distribution for both.

Walt tried a different approach with the third film. Steamboat Willie synchronized the sounds with the events on screen. Mickey's movements now had a musical and comic rhythm that an audience could hear as well as see. In November 1928, the film reached cinemas and Mickey found his audience.

The studio went on to become identified with the mouse.

What happened to Oswald? He wasn't simply erased from the world. Universal continued making films featuring him. The character appeared in later publications as well. But he was no longer part of Walt's ongoing animation career.

The separation remained in place as the original studio became the Walt Disney Company, and that company expanded into other businesses, including sports television.

That expansion matters to what happened next.

In 2005, Al Michaels was one of the most established voices in American sports broadcasting. He had worked with ABC for about three decades, including twenty years as a Monday Night Football commentator. Viewers heard him describe the action alongside analyst John Madden.

The Monday night broadcast was moving from ABC to ESPN. Michaels had agreed to continue under the new arrangement. But Madden and other colleagues were moving to NBC, which was preparing its own Sunday night football coverage. Michaels wanted to join them.

A team can move from one broadcaster to another, but an existing employment agreement does not disappear when someone decides to follow their colleagues. Michaels needed ABC and ESPN to agree to let him go.

The two companies were now dealing with a contractual question, just as another part of the entertainment business had been in 1928. Only this time, the Disney company had something that NBC wanted.

There was also an old piece of Disney history on the other side of the negotiating table. NBCUniversal still controlled Oswald. Bob Iger, Disney's chief executive, had expressed a wish to return the character to the studio that had created him. Walt's daughter later confirmed that he had discussed this with her.

The negotiations gave Disney a way to pursue that goal as part of a much bigger business deal.

In January 2006, ESPN president George Bodenheimer contacted NBC Sports chairman Dick Ebersol. They worked toward terms acceptable to the companies. Ebersol later explained that his company wasn't earning money in the United States from Oswald's rights. Giving them up would mean something different to NBC than acquiring them would mean to Disney.

On February ninth, the public learned what had been agreed.

Michaels was free to join NBC. Disney acquired the rights to Oswald. ESPN gained the ability to show Friday play from four Ryder Cup golf championships and expanded use of Olympic highlights. Other promotional and sports highlight arrangements were included, too.

This was not a literal exchange of a person for a fictional rabbit, nor was the rabbit the only consideration. Different rights and contractual concessions made up the agreement. Michaels could go where he wanted to work; the networks received assets that mattered to their own businesses.

For Disney's animation history, the result was extraordinary. An old creation could once again appear in the work of the company that had made him.

Game designer Warren Spector found a way to make the historical relationship meaningful to a modern audience. In Epic Mickey, released in 2010, the rabbit and mouse are imagined as brothers. Oswald has been pushed aside and resents the younger character's success. The game lets Mickey enter Oswald's world and gives their relationship a chance to change.

That family drama is fiction. It is not evidence of anyone's real feelings in 1928. But it takes an actual business event, which separated two characters made by the same people, and turns the consequences into something the player can explore.

And some of the old work itself was still waiting to be found.

In 2015, the British Film Institute announced that a researcher had spotted a catalogue entry for Sleigh Bells, an Oswald short from 1928 that had long been considered lost. The archive had preserved a film print. Disney's animation studio made a new preservation print and digital copies. In December, the approximately six-minute film was screened in London.

That rediscovery was not part of the football negotiations. It was a separate archival event. But it shows what the reunion of character and studio could accompany: both new creative work and preservation of old animation.

For Disney's centenary, the company even released a new one-minute Oswald cartoon, its first new short starring him at the studio in ninety-five years.

At the start of this story, Walt had a character he could draw but couldn't take with him. By the end, his successors had acquired the rights to that character because another business wanted something entirely different.

And the rabbit that once made way for Mickey could share a story with him instead.
