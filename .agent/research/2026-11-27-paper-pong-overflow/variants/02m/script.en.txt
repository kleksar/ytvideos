The first Pong machine stopped accepting money because its coin box was full.

In 1972, Atari hired an engineer named Al Alcorn. His boss, Nolan Bushnell, told him General Electric had ordered a game that people could play on their television. Two paddles, a ball, and a score. Alcorn had never designed a video game before.

He spent three months building one. He bought an ordinary black-and-white TV and converted it into a monitor. He made the ball square to save circuitry, and found a way to get those little electronic sounds out of the hardware.

There was just one problem. His circuit needed dozens of chips. It was too expensive for the home market. There was another problem with the project. General Electric hadn't ordered anything. Bushnell had invented the order to give his new engineer a practice assignment.

But the game was fun enough for Atari to try it in a bar. Ted Dabney built a cabinet, and the machine went into Andy Capp's in Sunnyvale, California.

When Alcorn received a repair call, he discovered quarters blocking the coin mechanism. Alcorn later recalled that the machine was bringing in over a hundred dollars a week, against an expectation of about twenty-five.

Atari started selling the arcade machines. Other companies began making similar games. And a few years later, Atari's engineers managed to fit the game logic onto a custom chip, making a cheaper home version possible.

Sears put it in its stores in 1975. The project that had started with a made-up order now had a real order for about a hundred and fifty thousand units.

Pong wasn't the first video game. What it did was help turn video games into a mass-market business. How did Atari first learn that strangers would pay to play a game this simple?
