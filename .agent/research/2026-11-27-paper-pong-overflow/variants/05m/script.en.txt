Why did Atari invent a customer for its new engineer? What made a game with two lines and a square ball worth manufacturing? And how did that game end up in American living rooms?

In the early seventies, playing a video game usually meant having access to equipment most people couldn't afford. Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney had already worked on a coin-operated game called Computer Space. You controlled a spaceship and tried to hit other ships. It reached the market, but it wasn't the widespread hit Bushnell wanted.

In June 1972, an engineer named Al Alcorn joined their new company, Atari. He knew how television circuitry worked. He'd been repairing TVs since his teens. But he hadn't designed an arcade game.

Bushnell gave him what sounded like a serious job. General Electric wanted a game for the home, he said. It would show something like table tennis on the TV. Each player would turn a knob to move a paddle. The machine would keep score and make sounds.

Alcorn spent three months figuring out how to build it. There was no modern game processor to run the whole thing. He had to make electronic circuits generate the picture and control the ball. A square ball cost less circuitry than a round one. A proper monitor was expensive, so he converted a cheap television instead.

Bushnell and Dabney wanted sound, too. Alcorn produced a few simple tones using parts of the video circuitry. Those little beeps became part of the game.

By the time it worked, Alcorn had used more than seventy integrated circuits. He didn't see how it could be sold cheaply enough to families. But the assignment had never been what it appeared to be. There was no General Electric contract. Bushnell had invented the customer so the new engineer would take his practice project seriously.

Yet the game was good enough to try as a commercial machine. Dabney built a cabinet. Atari placed it in Andy Capp's, a bar in Sunnyvale, California.

Alcorn later recalled asking what a good weekly take would be. Bushnell's answer was about twenty-five dollars. The machine was taking more than a hundred.

Then the bar reported a breakdown. Alcorn went over to find out what had failed. He found the coin mechanism jammed by quarters. The customers hadn't lost interest. The machine had simply run out of room for their money.

Atari could now sell a game that operators had reason to buy. Alcorn estimated the early units cost roughly five hundred dollars to make, and sold for about twelve hundred. There was a new business in electronic games, and competitors soon wanted their share. Similar machines appeared, while Magnavox challenged Atari over its earlier television-game technology. Atari settled the dispute.

The next question was how to get the same game into homes. Alcorn, Harold Lee and Bob Brown worked on a design whose game logic could fit on a custom integrated circuit. A family wouldn't need to buy the expensive arcade cabinet or all the electronics inside it.

Atari approached retailers. Several weren't interested. But Tom Quinn, a sporting-goods buyer at Sears, saw a game that might fit alongside the table-tennis equipment people bought for home recreation.

Atari still had to convince Sears to place a big order. During a demonstration at its Chicago headquarters, Alcorn connected the prototype to a television. The game didn't appear. The building's own television transmission was interfering with the prototype's signal. He opened the unit, made an adjustment, and got the game running.

Sears ultimately ordered about a hundred and fifty thousand home Pong units for the Christmas season. Atari needed new manufacturing capacity and financing to meet the order. The game was a success under the retailer's Tele-Games brand.

So the strange part of this story isn't that somebody invented video table tennis. Games like that existed before Pong. It's that a practice assignment became a product, then a business, and finally an affordable game for families. A costly collection of integrated circuits had become unnecessary. A cabinet full of quarters had become a reason to make thousands more machines.
