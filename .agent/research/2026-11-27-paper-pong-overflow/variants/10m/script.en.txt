Why did Atari's boss invent a contract with a huge corporation? How did his engineer build a television game without a modern game processor? And why did a breakdown in a California bar help create a market for a product that many retailers didn't know how to sell?

Nolan Bushnell had worked at an amusement park and had also played games on computers at college. In an arcade, each machine had to earn enough money to justify its cost. At the time, a computer powerful enough to play games was expensive. Even if the software was good, there was little point in putting thousands of dollars of equipment behind a coin slot.

When smaller computers became available, Bushnell considered a system in which one computer would serve several screens. The idea was to spread its cost across several paying customers. But the computer struggled to keep up with the games as they were being played. Each screen needed its own electronics to help produce the moving pictures.

Bushnell kept shifting work away from the central computer until he realized the machines could run without it. Instead of having a general-purpose computer calculate every move, he could build hardware for one particular game.

Computer Space, the result of the work with Ted Dabney, arrived in 1971. The machine put a space battle inside a futuristic cabinet. You needed to learn how to control a ship while aiming and firing. It sold, but it was not the great breakthrough that Bushnell had hoped for.

Then, in 1972, Bushnell and Dabney formed Atari. They brought in an engineer who had worked with them at Ampex, a company that made professional recording equipment. His name was Al Alcorn. He had been fixing television sets since he was thirteen, and he understood the electronics that produced a television picture. He hadn't designed a video game.

Bushnell told Alcorn that General Electric had contracted Atari to build a game for consumers. He described what would appear on the screen. A ball would move between two paddles. The players would operate knobs, the machine would keep score, and there would be sound.

There was no cheap memory to store the whole picture. Alcorn had to make circuits control when the screen lit up as the television drew each line. Changing those positions made the ball move. Each new feature meant buying more electronic parts.

One trade-off was the ball itself. A square was much simpler to generate than a circle. There was a similar choice over the score display. Alcorn recalled replacing a five-dollar component with simpler circuitry worth about three dollars. It made the numbers less elegant, but they still told the players who was winning.

Even the sound was a problem. Bushnell wanted something resembling a cheering crowd; Dabney suggested a different crowd reaction. Rather than build elaborate new hardware, Alcorn found useful tones in the circuitry that already generated the timing for the screen. Pong got its characteristic electronic sounds.

And the screen was an ordinary black-and-white television that Alcorn had bought for seventy-five dollars and adapted for his machine. A dedicated monitor would have cost him roughly four times as much.

These choices made sense for a product that had to be cheap. Yet after about three months, Alcorn still needed more than seventy integrated circuits. Just the electronic parts were already expensive for a small home game, before adding everything else the customer would need.

There was never an order from General Electric. Bushnell had chosen the simplest game he could think of to give the new engineer a chance to learn the technology. The impressive customer had been invented.

But the exercise was playable, and Atari decided to test it where the money could be counted. Dabney assembled a cabinet and the team installed the game at Andy Capp's, a bar in Sunnyvale.

This was a different test from the one Alcorn had been working toward. He no longer needed to persuade an engineer that the circuitry worked or get the price low enough for a family. He needed to make people who walked past the machine stop, put a quarter into it and play.

Pong had an advantage over Computer Space. If you've ever played table tennis, the two paddles and a bouncing ball tell you most of what to do. There are two players, each looking at the same screen. A missed ball turns into a point for the opponent. You don't have to learn how to fly a spacecraft to understand the contest.

Alcorn remembered being told that twenty-five dollars per week would be a good result for an arcade machine. According to his later account, Pong was already taking more than a hundred.

Then came a report that the machine had stopped working.

When Alcorn went to investigate, it wasn't a fault in the circuits that he had spent months designing. The quarters had filled the coin receptacle and jammed the mechanism.

There was a reason to build more machines, but Atari had started out intending to sell game designs to established manufacturers. Alcorn remembered sitting with the founders in the same bar while Bushnell argued that they should make the machines themselves. Alcorn and Dabney initially resisted the idea. The small team had to learn how to run a factory.

Atari went ahead anyway. In Alcorn's recollection, a school that had assembled circuit boards for them sent over a group of graduates. Atari hired them. Alcorn began teaching two of the new workers to diagnose faulty boards. The engineer who had been hired to design a game now had to teach other people how to build it. Alcorn later estimated a cost of around five hundred dollars for the earliest machines and a selling price of around twelve hundred. That gap wasn't pure profit; a company still had overhead and other expenses. But buyers had a reason to pay for a machine that could take money week after week.

Pong also had a limitation from Atari's point of view. A competitor didn't have to come up with a completely new game to compete with it. Other manufacturers put similar games into their own cabinets. And Atari had not invented the general concept of electronic table tennis. Ralph Baer's work had already led to the Magnavox Odyssey home system. Bushnell had seen a demonstration of it. Magnavox challenged Atari's use of the earlier technology, and Atari reached a settlement rather than going to trial.

The arcade boom was valuable, but the idea of a game in the living room hadn't gone away.

Harold Lee, Bob Brown and Alcorn worked on reducing the game logic to a custom integrated circuit. It was a way to get away from assembling the large collection of separate components that had made Alcorn's original assignment unsuitable for the home.

According to Alcorn, when the first home-game chip worked, they didn't even have a case for the device or an arrangement to distribute it. Atari knew how to sell to arcade operators. Selling to families meant finding an entirely different kind of customer.

Atari found Tom Quinn, who bought sporting goods for Sears. He was already familiar with the Odyssey home game. He saw where a product like Pong could fit in the store.

At a demonstration for Sears executives in Chicago, Alcorn connected the prototype to a television. The game failed to appear on the screen. This time, a broadcast signal from an antenna on the building was interfering with the signal the prototype used. Alcorn opened the machine, adjusted it and got the demonstration working.

The problem of retail distribution went beyond the demo. Atari wanted the freedom to sell through other retailers. Alcorn recalled a New York toy trade show where buyers examined Pong but placed no orders. Sears turned out to be the partner willing to back the product.

Ultimately Sears ordered about a hundred and fifty thousand home Pong units for the Christmas season of 1975. That meant more than getting a working circuit onto a conference-room table. Atari needed facilities, workers and capital to deliver at that scale. The company obtained financing, and the game sold successfully under Sears' Tele-Games label.

Alcorn had set out to design a cheap game for a customer who did not exist. Three years later, the company had a real customer that wanted enough machines to fill a production line. The same engineer had gone from adapting a television on a workbench to helping build a consumer business. The first machine, the one that had jammed with quarters in a bar, had given Atari something that no imaginary contract could: evidence that people would actually pay to play.
