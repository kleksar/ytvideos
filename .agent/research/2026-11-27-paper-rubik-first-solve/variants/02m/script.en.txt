The man who invented the Rubik's Cube couldn't solve it.

In 1974, Ernő Rubik was teaching design in Budapest. He wanted to build something that could change shape on the inside without falling apart. He made wooden pieces that could move around each other while staying together.

Then he gave the six faces different colors and started turning them. The mechanism worked. The colors, however, were all over the place.

He couldn't simply put one color back at a time. A turn that brought a piece into place could take several others out of theirs. And there was no instruction booklet. Nobody had written one yet.

Rubik eventually found a solution. It had taken him about a month.

Getting the puzzle into shops was another challenge. Hungary had a centrally planned economy, and a toy with no established market was difficult to introduce. Production began in 1977. At a toy fair in 1979, the puzzle caught the attention of a man who helped bring it to an international audience.

By 1982, Budapest was hosting the first world championship. Nineteen competitors came to solve the same kind of puzzle. An American competitor named Minh Thai won with a time of just under twenty-three seconds.

That's what makes the first solve so remarkable. The competitors could practice methods that people had already discovered. Rubik had to discover a method before anyone could practice it.

In 2010, a team even proved that every possible scramble made with ordinary face turns can be solved in at most twenty moves, if a half turn counts as one move.

Twenty moves or fewer, and yet the first solution took weeks. If you've ever picked one up and found yourself completely stuck, you're in good company.
