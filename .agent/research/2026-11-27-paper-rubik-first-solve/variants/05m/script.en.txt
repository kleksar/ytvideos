Why did the man who invented the Rubik's Cube need about a month to solve it? And how did a puzzle made in a Hungarian apartment end up at a world championship where the winning time was less than twenty-three seconds?

In Budapest, in the spring of 1974, Ernő Rubik was teaching design. He was interested in the way objects fit together in three dimensions, and he wanted to build a structure that could move without coming apart. A collection of little blocks was easy enough to make. The difficulty was keeping them together while allowing groups of them to change places.

Rubik experimented with wood. One of his early working models was not even colored. The construction itself was the problem he was trying to solve.

When he gave the faces different colors, however, he discovered a second problem. He could twist the cube to mix up the colors. Getting them back where they belonged was much harder. The man who understood how every part of the object fit together had made something he couldn't solve.

Try putting a white corner into place on an ordinary cube. The piece has three colors, so matching the white face is not enough. The other two colors also have to match their neighboring faces. You can get the corner right with one turn and ruin something else you already finished. What you need is a way to move particular pieces without permanently disturbing the ones you want to keep.

There wasn't a book or a video Rubik could follow. He had to work out his own method, and by his account it took about a month to get the colors back.

A puzzle that was hard to solve turned out to be an appealing product. But Rubik was living in a country with a centrally planned economy. Getting a new kind of toy into production meant finding a place for something nobody had been making before.

He applied for a patent in 1975. In 1977, production began in Hungary under the name Magic Cube. It also began attracting people who saw it as an interesting mathematical problem. In 1978, cubes reached a mathematics meeting in Helsinki. One of the people who encountered the puzzle there was David Singmaster, who went on to help introduce it to other solvers.

Meanwhile, the cube was selling at home. By the end of 1979, roughly three hundred thousand had been sold in Hungary.

At a toy fair in Nuremberg that year, the Hungarian puzzle came to the attention of Tom Kremer, who helped arrange its international distribution. Ideal Toy Company brought it to a much wider market. Magic Cube became Rubik's Cube.

In 1980, Rubik traveled to New York. He had never been outside the Eastern bloc before. One of the things he had to establish for his new audience was something he now knew from experience: the puzzle really could be solved.

Two years later, the first world championship took place back in Budapest. Nineteen competitors took part. Minh Thai, representing the United States, recorded a solve of twenty-two point nine five seconds and won.

The result doesn't mean that Thai worked out the original solution in twenty-three seconds. By then, solvers could learn sequences of turns and practice recognizing where to use them. Rubik had started without that accumulated knowledge.

And the fastest hands were solving a different problem from the mathematicians. The mathematicians wanted to know how short a solution could be, even for the most difficult possible arrangement. In 2010, a team proved that no position obtainable by ordinary turns requires more than twenty moves, counting a half turn as a single move. That does not make those moves easy to find.

The cube's journey even came back to its starting country. On November tenth, 2026, a factory in Jászberény began making Rubik's Cubes in Hungary again, with part of the global production returning. Rubik attended the opening.

The wooden experiment had become a product that people could make by the million. Its solution had become a skill that people could teach, practice and compete at. Yet neither achievement would have been possible without somebody first working out how to undo a few innocent turns.
