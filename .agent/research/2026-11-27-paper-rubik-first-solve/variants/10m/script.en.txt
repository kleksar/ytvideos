Why did the inventor of the Rubik's Cube need weeks to solve his own creation? How did people learn to do it in seconds, and why did mathematicians still have a problem to solve decades later? The answers begin with a wooden model on a table in Budapest.

In the spring of 1974, Ernő Rubik was teaching design in Budapest. He was working on a three-dimensional problem. Imagine making a large cube out of small blocks. You can take those blocks apart and rearrange them, but then you no longer have a single object. Could you make a cube whose parts moved around one another while the whole thing stayed together?

Rubik experimented with wood in his family's apartment. One of the early working models still exists, and it has no colored faces. Look at a normal cube today and you can see the problem he had overcome: the corner and edge pieces can travel around the central structure without the object falling to pieces. A group can move with one turn and then some of those same pieces can move with the next.

Rubik gave its faces different colors. Now it was possible to follow those movements by looking at the outside of the object. He started turning the faces, and the colors mixed. His mechanical problem was solved. He had just discovered a new problem: he could not easily put the colors back.

Picture a corner with white, red and blue stickers. Even if you can make a whole white face, that corner is wrong if its red and blue sides don't match the surrounding faces. Move it into the right place, and the turn may undo work on another part of the cube. You can make progress in one spot and lose it in another. Rubik had no instructions explaining how to get around that problem.

Decades later, in a question-and-answer session marking the cube's fiftieth anniversary, Rubik confirmed something specific about his first solution. He had started with the corners. Once those eight pieces are in place, the remaining edges can be treated as a separate problem. Finding sequences that move only certain pieces is one way to make that possible. The exact turns Rubik used are not known from that account.

Rubik has recalled taking about a month to find a solution. His later memoir also describes experimenting intermittently over a longer period in 1974, so we cannot reconstruct a daily timetable. But the result changed what he had made. Another person could mix the colors again, and the object presented a fresh problem each time. A finished design exercise had become a game.

In 1975 Rubik applied for a patent. The puzzle still had to get out of the apartment. Hungary's centrally planned economy made it difficult to introduce a new kind of consumer product. By the end of 1977, it had reached Hungarian toy stores under the name Magic Cube. Rubik later remembered travelers carrying the cubes out of the country in their luggage.

One of those who spread the puzzle was the mathematician David Singmaster. He encountered it at a meeting in Helsinki in 1978 and later wrote a guide. His notation assigned a letter to each face: U for up, D for down, and so on. Now a person could describe a sequence of turns on paper without drawing the cube after every move. Someone who had never met Rubik could follow instructions from a book.

Meanwhile the toy was selling at home. By the end of 1979, around three hundred thousand had been sold in Hungary. A separate challenge was getting a mass-produced version onto shelves in other countries.

That year, at the toy fair in Nuremberg, Tom Kremer encountered the Hungarian invention. He helped arrange for its distribution abroad with Ideal Toy Company. The new international name was Rubik's Cube. In 1980, Rubik himself traveled to New York, his first trip beyond the Eastern bloc. His invention could now reach people on the other side of the Cold War divide.

The new audience could also exchange discoveries. In July 1980 a student at MIT sent the first message to an electronic mailing list called CubeLovers. Its participants could discuss the same little object across great distances, using a notation that made those moves easy to write down. They no longer needed to be in the same room with a cube on the table.

On June fifth, 1982, nineteen competitors gathered in Budapest for the first world championship. The official results list three attempts for the American competitor Minh Thai. His first recorded solve took twenty-seven point one six seconds. Then came twenty-two point nine five. His third was twenty-seven point nine seven. The middle attempt won the competition; the runner-up was only about a second and a half slower.

The contrast with the inventor is striking. Rubik's month included discovering what to do. A contestant can learn how to recognize a particular arrangement, practice a sequence and move on as soon as it appears. A successful method, once shared, can save thousands of other people from having to make the same discovery.

The commercial boom, however, ended almost as quickly as it had arrived. By November 1982 a New York Times headline was already announcing the end of the craze. The family of the man who arranged the international deal recalled unsold stock in 1983. There was a revival in the nineties as the World Wide Web gave enthusiasts another way to find one another. The toy had outlived its first fashion.

And there was a question the competition could not answer. What is the worst possible scramble, if the goal is to get back to the solved cube in the fewest turns? There are more than forty-three quintillion reachable arrangements. Knowing that a practiced solver can restore one of them quickly tells us nothing about how many moves the hardest position really needs.

In 1995 researchers had an important clue. Imagine all eight corners correctly placed and all twelve edge pieces in the right slots, but each edge turned the wrong way. The cube looks almost finished, yet this position, called the superflip, was proved to need twenty moves. So nineteen could not be a universal guarantee. The harder part was to prove that twenty would always be enough.

A programmer named Tomas Rokicki began working on that problem in 1999. He and other researchers could reduce the search by treating equivalent arrangements together. If two cases are the same after a rotation of the whole object, you need not solve both independently. In 2010, with computer time supplied by Google, the team finally finished the proof. The computations used roughly thirty-five CPU-years, shared across machines. Every legal arrangement can be solved within twenty moves when either a quarter turn or a half turn of one face counts as one move.

That limit does not tell you which moves to make when a friend hands you a cube. And even after the proof, the hardest positions remained interesting to study. At a mathematics meeting in San Francisco in January 2024, Rokicki was still searching for them. The same meeting brought Rubik in by video link, fifty years after the original wooden experiment, to discuss the puzzle with people who had spent years exploring it.

There was one more development in the story of the physical object. Production had shifted toward Asia as demand grew, but automation and proximity to European customers made a partial return practical. On November tenth, 2026, Rubik attended the opening of a production line in Jászberény, Hungary. The target for the first year was around a million classic cubes. That was a production plan, not a million cubes already made.

When the inventor first mixed up the colors, there was no book of moves, no stopwatch and no computer proof to help him. He found a way to get the pieces back. Other people learned to explain that kind of solution, carry it out faster, and eventually prove how short it could be. The factory in Hungary now makes the object that set all those different people to work. The challenge it gives you when you pick it up is still the one Rubik had to face himself.
