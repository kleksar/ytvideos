How did an engineer trying to improve refrigeration end up with America's bestselling toy? And why did an award of almost seventy-three million dollars to the company of the Super Soaker's inventor actually involve Nerf?

Lonnie Johnson worked on spacecraft at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. When he went home, he had projects of his own. One was a cooling system that could use water instead of a conventional refrigerant.

In 1982, during an experiment in his bathroom, a jet of water traveled across the room. Johnson saw a much simpler potential product. A water toy that performed like that wouldn't need a complicated explanation to a customer.

He already had a patent and had struggled to find people willing to finance his other inventions. If a toy could bring in enough money, he reasoned, he might be able to become an inventor full time.

He returned to the Air Force and set up a workshop in his basement. His young daughter got an early prototype and used it to soak her friends.

Then Johnson approached a manufacturer. The estimate he received was about two hundred thousand dollars to set up production and deliver the first thousand toys. A working invention had brought him face to face with a bill he couldn't afford.

He changed his plan. Instead of owning a factory, he would find a toy company that could manufacture and sell his product under a license. He received his first water gun patent in 1986. There were still false starts, including a company he had worked with that went out of business.

In 1989, Johnson went to the big toy trade show in New York. An executive at Larami suggested he visit the company's headquarters in Philadelphia if he happened to be nearby.

Johnson's previous prototype hadn't been returned to him, so he made another version. He arranged the meeting and traveled to Philadelphia.

According to Johnson, the company president and other staff were there. He opened his suitcase, took out the toy and sent a stream of water across the conference room. The people at Larami knew the water toy business. They had just seen something they could compare with their own products.

Larami licensed the invention. In 1990, the Power Drencher went on sale. The following year, the redesigned and renamed Super Soaker generated more than two hundred million dollars in sales. That was the value of toys sold, not Johnson's personal income.

Hasbro eventually bought Larami. Johnson's patents became the basis of another familiar toy line, Nerf N-Strike. Now two separate licensing agreements mattered to his business.

A license can specify which products trigger a payment to the inventor's company. By 2013, Johnson Research and Hasbro disagreed about how broadly those two agreements applied. Johnson's company said that more products qualified than Hasbro believed.

The Nerf agreement required arbitration, a process in which an appointed decision maker resolves a dispute. The Super Soaker claims went to federal court instead.

On October twenty-ninth, the Nerf arbitrator issued an award. Hasbro announced a total of almost seventy-three million dollars, including damages, interest and legal costs. The award covered the Nerf dispute, not the separate Super Soaker case.

Hasbro sought to overturn the decision. Before that challenge was resolved, on February seventh, 2014, the parties reached a settlement. Hasbro agreed to pay Johnson Research fifty-eight million and forty thousand dollars to end both disputes. Johnson Research agreed to release its claims under the two agreements.

So there are three different numbers in this story: the value of a hit toy's sales, an arbitration award in a different product line, and the amount agreed to settle both disputes. None tells us exactly how much money Johnson personally took home.

By then, his work had extended far beyond toy design. His companies developed battery technology and investigated ways to turn heat into electricity. A cooling experiment had led to a toy, and the toy became a business that helped support a career in invention.
