Why did an engineer working on a mission to Jupiter decide to sell a water toy? Why did it take eight years to reach the shops? And what was the seventy-three-million-dollar award that made headlines decades later actually about?

Lonnie Johnson grew up in Mobile, Alabama. His father could repair household appliances and showed his children how things worked. Lonnie liked taking toys apart, including toys that belonged to his siblings. Years later he built something of his own on a much larger scale: a robot named Linex.

In 1968, Johnson took first place at a science fair at the University of Alabama. He later studied mechanical and nuclear engineering at Tuskegee University. He went on to work in government research, served in the Air Force, and in 1979 joined NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, where engineers were preparing Galileo, a spacecraft that would explore Jupiter.

But the problems Johnson chose to work on after he went home were his own. He had already obtained a patent before he had his famous toy idea. He had also discovered how hard it was to turn an idea into a business. Being able to make something work did not mean he could find an investor willing to pay for it.

One of the ideas he was pursuing in 1982 was a heat pump that would use water rather than Freon. He later described doing experiments in his own bathroom. During one of them, water traveled across the room.

Johnson saw another possibility. He could make a water toy whose appeal was immediately obvious. And unlike some of his engineering projects, it could potentially finance his next invention without his having to persuade someone to understand a complicated technology first.

That was the plan. The next several years would show how much lay between a demonstration and a product.

Johnson returned to active duty in the Air Force. Once his family was settled, he equipped a basement workshop and began developing his idea. One of his early prototypes went to his seven-year-old daughter. She used it to soak her friends on the military base.

Johnson initially intended to manufacture it himself. He approached a company capable of making molded plastic parts. When the estimate came back, it was about two hundred thousand dollars for a production line and the first thousand toys.

He could make the prototype in a workshop, but he couldn't put a thousand of them in stores without the resources for tooling and production. He would have to find a business partner or somehow raise a considerable amount of money.

He chose to pursue licensing. With a license, another company could bring its manufacturing and distribution capabilities to the project, while the inventor could earn money from the rights he granted. Johnson wouldn't need to start by building a toy factory himself.

That introduced a new difficulty. To interest potential partners, he had to show them his idea. Johnson remembers being conscious of the risk that a company could see what he had done and pursue it without him. But if he kept his prototype hidden, nobody could buy it either.

His first water gun patent was issued in 1986. A patent wasn't a factory, and the product still hadn't reached shops. He recalled trying more than one company; one of the businesses he had been dealing with went out of business.

Then came the Toy Fair in New York in 1989. Johnson was trying to get in front of the people who made and sold toys. He later described walking the corridors in search of a conversation.

He met Al Davis, a vice president of Larami, a company already familiar with water toys. Davis indicated that the fair wasn't the place to evaluate the invention. He suggested Johnson visit the company's Philadelphia headquarters if he was in the area, without making a trip especially for it.

There was another problem: Johnson didn't have his earlier prototype back from the companies he had approached. So he went home, made another version, arranged an appointment and went to Philadelphia anyway.

According to Johnson's account, Larami's president Myung Song, Davis and other staff were in the meeting room. Johnson took the toy out of his suitcase and sent water across the room. The company's staff began discussing its possibilities.

This time he got a licensing deal. The Power Drencher appeared in stores in 1990. The following year it was redesigned and renamed Super Soaker. It became the country's bestselling toy, with sales of more than two hundred million dollars.

After that success, other companies sold similar products. Johnson said that his patent enabled him to obtain court orders against infringers, including stopping shipments in Los Angeles. He had acquired something more useful than a sample toy: a legal means of protecting the invention.

Hasbro eventually bought Larami. And Johnson's patent work found a new application. The Nerf N-Strike line, with foam darts instead of water, was also based on his patents.

For a customer, those might look like two different toy aisles. For Johnson Research, the company that licensed the inventions, there were two different agreements. How much the company earned depended in part on which products each agreement covered.

This distinction became particularly important in 2013. Johnson Research and Hasbro disagreed about the scope of both licenses. The inventor's company believed that royalties should have been paid on a wider range of products than Hasbro accepted. The dispute concerned which products qualified; it was not a finding that Johnson had never received royalties before.

The two agreements had different dispute-resolution provisions. The Nerf dispute had to go to arbitration. An arbitrator would hear the arguments and issue a decision. The Super Soaker dispute was pursued through a separate complaint in a federal court in Georgia.

In February 2013, Johnson Research initiated both proceedings. The Nerf claim concerned certain products sold over the period from 2007 through 2012. In August 2013, the arbitration hearing took place.

On October twenty-ninth, the arbitrator ruled in favor of Johnson Research. Hasbro's announcement two days later put the award at just under seventy-three million dollars. This was a total that included damages, interest, fees and expenses. It wasn't a calculation of Super Soaker sales.

The decision didn't end the matter. Hasbro publicly disagreed with it and subsequently asked a federal court in Rhode Island to vacate the award. Its annual report later described the award as about seventy million dollars.

There was still the separate lawsuit about the water toys. Both disputes finally came together in a settlement dated February seventh, 2014. Hasbro agreed to pay Johnson Research fifty-eight million and forty thousand dollars. In return, Johnson Research agreed to release its claims arising from the two licenses. That was the agreed amount to resolve both disputes, not a second payment on top of the original award.

The two hundred million dollars from the early Super Soaker boom describes sales. The nearly seventy-three million dollars announced in 2013 describes an arbitration award in the Nerf case. The fifty-eight million dollars agreed in 2014 describes the settlement of both cases. The public record doesn't tell us Johnson's personal take-home amount.

And what happened to the engineer who wanted a way to finance his other ideas? Johnson continued working on energy technology. His ventures included research into batteries and devices intended to convert heat into electricity. He was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2022.

He had once been trying to get people to invest in inventions that were hard to explain. The toy gave him a business built around something children could understand without a lecture. It took a workshop, a patent, a manufacturing partner and years of negotiations to turn that idea into an independent enterprise. The water that crossed his bathroom in 1982 was the easy part.
