Why did an engineer working on a mission to Jupiter decide to sell a water toy? Why did it take eight years to reach the shops? And what was the seventy-three-million-dollar award that made headlines decades later actually about?

In 1982, Lonnie Johnson worked at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, where Galileo was being prepared for its journey to Jupiter. At home, he was pursuing a different project: a heat pump that could use water instead of Freon. He made some nozzles and attached them to his bathroom sink.

The stream coming out of one nozzle was so impressive that he turned it across the room. Then he realized what else he could do with it. A toy like that could appeal to people who knew nothing about refrigeration. He had already struggled to find investors for his inventions. If he could make money from something as accessible as a toy, he might be able to develop his own ideas full time.

He had been building things since childhood. His homemade robot had won first prize at a regional science fair in Alabama in 1968. He later trained as an engineer. But now the problem was how to get one of his creations out of a workshop and into stores.

Johnson returned to active duty in the Air Force. Once his family was settled, he equipped a basement workshop and began developing his idea. One of his early prototypes went to his seven-year-old daughter. She used it to soak her friends on the military base.

Johnson went to a plastics manufacturer with a plan to make the toys himself. The manufacturer quoted about two hundred thousand dollars to set up the production line and deliver the first thousand. Johnson didn't have that money. His professional experience was in government engineering, not in running a factory.

He decided to find a company that would make the product under license. That meant showing other businesses an invention they might copy. Johnson later explained that he wanted to learn how a product was brought to market, even if the venture failed. What he learned could help him on his next project.

In 1986, he received his first water gun patent. It gave him a way to protect the invention, but it didn't make companies want to manufacture it. One manufacturer examined his prototype, dismantled it and failed to return it in working order. The thing Johnson needed to demonstrate his idea was now missing.

Another of his inventions did reach shops. It was a toy airplane called the Jammin' Jet. But the commercial version had a defect, and later accounts describe the planes crashing. The toy failed commercially. Getting a company to manufacture an invention, Johnson was learning, didn't necessarily mean it would succeed in the hands of customers.

In 1989, Johnson tried again at the Toy Fair in New York. This was a trade show, but many of the displays he wanted to visit were in private rooms. Companies were keeping their new products secret. He walked the corridors looking for someone willing to hear his proposal.

He met Al Davis, a vice president of Larami, a company already familiar with water toys. Davis indicated that the fair wasn't the place to evaluate the invention. He suggested Johnson visit the company's Philadelphia headquarters if he was in the area, without making a trip especially for it.

Johnson went home and spent a couple of weeks building a new version because he hadn't recovered his previous prototype. This time he put the water bottle on top, giving the toy a much larger supply of water. He arranged the appointment and made the journey to Philadelphia especially for it.

According to Johnson's account, Larami's president Myung Song, Davis and other staff were in the meeting room. Johnson took the toy out of his suitcase and sent water across the room. The company's staff began discussing its possibilities.

Larami licensed the invention, and the Power Drencher appeared in stores in 1990. The toy was redesigned and relaunched as the Super Soaker the following year. A television commercial showed two teenagers disrupting a pool party. The product could be demonstrated in seconds, exactly the sort of accessible invention Johnson had been trying to sell.

The Super Soaker became a hit, with sales exceeding two hundred million dollars. Other companies began selling competing products. Johnson recalled that he obtained injunctions against companies infringing his patent, including stopping shipments at the docks in Los Angeles. The patent was now helping protect the business he had spent years trying to build.

Hasbro eventually bought Larami. And Johnson's patent work found a new application. The Nerf N-Strike line, with foam darts instead of water, was also based on his patents.

For shoppers, the two products might have been in different aisles. For Johnson Research, the company licensing his inventions, there were two different agreements. How much it earned depended in part on which products each agreement covered.

This distinction became particularly important in 2013. Johnson Research and Hasbro disagreed about the scope of both licenses. The inventor's company believed that royalties should have been paid on a wider range of products than Hasbro accepted. The dispute concerned which products qualified; it was not a finding that Johnson had never received royalties before.

In February 2013, Johnson Research filed a claim about Nerf products sold between 2007 and 2012. That license required arbitration, so the claim went before an arbitrator. The Super Soaker complaint was filed separately in a federal court in Georgia. Two different proceedings involved the same two companies.

The Nerf hearing took place in August. On October twenty-ninth the arbitrator ruled in favor of Johnson Research. Two days later, Hasbro announced the amount: almost seventy-three million dollars, including damages, interest, fees and expenses. That was the number that made headlines. It related to Nerf, not to the toy that had made Johnson famous.

The decision didn't end the matter. Hasbro publicly disagreed with it and subsequently asked a federal court in Rhode Island to vacate the award. Its annual report later described the award as about seventy million dollars.

There was still the separate lawsuit about the water toys. Both disputes finally came together in a settlement dated February seventh, 2014. Hasbro agreed to pay Johnson Research fifty-eight million and forty thousand dollars. In return, Johnson Research agreed to release its claims arising from the two licenses. That was the agreed amount to resolve both disputes, not a second payment on top of the original award.

Neither the early sales figure nor the arbitration award tells us how much Johnson personally earned. The settlement documents describe an agreement with his company. What we can see is that he continued working on technologies for storing energy and turning heat into electricity.

In 2015, the Super Soaker was inducted into the National Toy Hall of Fame. The inventor himself entered the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2022. The stream of water that crossed his bathroom had opened a new way for him to keep working on the very kinds of problems that had brought him there in the first place.
