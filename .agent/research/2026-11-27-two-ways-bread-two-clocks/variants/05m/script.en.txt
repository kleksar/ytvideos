I buy a loaf for sandwiches. Half of it will probably still be here in a few days. Do I leave the bag on the counter so the bread stays soft, or put it in the fridge to stop it spoiling so soon? And why do people who have tried both give such different answers?

I want to separate two clocks. One measures how the crumb changes. The other measures when I have to throw the bread away because of visible mold.

Before picking A or B, I'm going to reconstruct a published ten-day storage experiment. I haven't run it myself. The illustrated kitchen stands in for the actual test.

The bread I'm comparing is a wheat pan loaf, made with flour, water, salt and yeast. The samples came from the same production batch. They were put into identical plastic bags. In the original setup there were two wheat loaves per storage container, so there was enough bread to take samples.

A sits at roughly twenty-two degrees Celsius. B stays at roughly eight degrees, the refrigerator temperature used in the experiment. I'm not recommending that setting for a household fridge. Each day a new slice is taken from the exposed end, with careful handling.

To check softness, the experiment doesn't rely on someone's opinion of a sandwich. It uses a machine that compresses stacked slices and measures their resistance. The samples are allowed to rest so the result isn't simply the difference between a cold and a warm piece of bread.

For the other clock, I look for mold on the loaf. There are laboratory tests as well, so an empty-looking surface isn't the only evidence.

Here's where the results separate. In the room-temperature bag, visible mold appeared on day four or five. The refrigerated bag went through all ten days with no mold detected, including the laboratory checks. If I judge solely by this result, the fridge wins.

But I bought that bread to make a sandwich, and the other measurements are going in the opposite direction. The cold bread firms faster. By day ten, the reported firmness of the refrigerated wheat bread is about four and a quarter times its starting value. In the room-temperature bag, it is about four times its starting value. Those are instrument readings, not ratings of how good the bread tastes. And the room-temperature loaf is already moldy, so I am not comparing two edible sandwiches on day ten.

What surprises me is that the bags had almost the same humidity. Both held moisture relatively well. Staling isn't just a loss of water into the kitchen air. After baking, the starch molecules in the crumb gradually form more rigid structures and redistribute water. At refrigerator temperatures that process is faster. Putting the bread in a better bag doesn't stop it.

Now suppose I change the loaf instead of the storage method. The experiment also compared a mixed rye and wheat sourdough. None of its four storage methods produced visible mold during the ten-day test. Acidity may have contributed to that difference. Even here, however, the chilled bread became firmer more quickly.

So I can't use the wheat loaf's day-four result as a universal deadline. The bread recipe, the kitchen and the way I handle the loaf matter.

I also can't keep a moldy piece of bread and just cut off the green spot. Bread is porous; contamination can extend beyond the visible patch. I discard the loaf if I see mold. Lack of visible mold alone is not a guarantee of safety.

Here's how I would use the two results. If I'm going to finish plain bread soon, I can keep the portion I'll eat at room temperature according to its storage instructions. If I have no freezer and mold is the main problem, the fridge offers a useful trade-off: slower spoilage, potentially faster staling. If I'm buying enough bread for longer, I'd rather freeze the extra slices while they're fresh and take out only what I need.

I don't have to win both clocks with one storage location. I can make the decision for each portion before the loaf gets old.
