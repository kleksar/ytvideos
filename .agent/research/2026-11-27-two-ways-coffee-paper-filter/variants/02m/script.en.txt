I can pour the same coffee two ways: straight into my cup, or through a paper filter. Which would leave less coffee oil in my drink? Pick A or B before we see the numbers.

I'm using a model based on a published experiment, not testing my own blood or brewing coffee in a lab. Both methods start with the same coarse ground coffee and hot water. The brew stands for ten minutes. Then the liquid is poured off the grounds. In A, that's it. In B, the liquid passes through paper as it goes into the second container.

I'll compare equal cups of a hundred and fifty milliliters. The experiment measured about one gram of lipids per liter in the coffee poured off the grounds. That gives my first cup about a hundred and fifty milligrams of coffee lipids. The paper-filtered brew contained only twenty milligrams per liter. My second cup gets about three milligrams. That's ninety-eight percent less in this particular comparison.

The interesting part is what the filter doesn't remove. The measured caffeine concentrations were quite similar. This is not a way to make decaf.

And there's a reason the oils matter. In a trial with sixty-four healthy volunteers, the group drinking the unfiltered brew had a greater rise in LDL cholesterol than the group drinking the paper-filtered brew. The difference was about sixteen milligrams per deciliter after seventy-nine days. That's a group result, not a prediction for me or a recommendation to drink six cups a day like the trial participants.

So B wins for the oil in these two cups. Taste and other health outcomes weren't settled by that comparison. If I keep my beans and brewing method, what can I actually change?
