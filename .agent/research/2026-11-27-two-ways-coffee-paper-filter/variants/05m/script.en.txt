What if the biggest difference between my two cups of coffee happened after the coffee was already made? Could I take something out of my drink without taking the caffeine out with it? And would that difference be large enough to show up in a blood test weeks later?

I have two options. A is coffee poured off the grounds. B is the same brew passed through a paper coffee filter. I'm not comparing a dark roast with a light roast, an espresso with a large mug, or two different machines. I'm keeping the preparation the same and changing the last step. Which one would you pick?

I want to compare the amount of coffee lipids that reaches an equal-sized cup. These are fats and related substances that can travel with the brew. Some of the compounds in that mixture can affect cholesterol in the blood. To follow them, I'll use measured concentrations from a controlled experiment and calculate what they mean for two cups. This is an illustrated model, not a new laboratory experiment.

Here's how the coffee in that experiment was made. Hot water went over coarsely ground beans in a flask. The mixture was left for ten minutes, and the coffee was then transferred to another flask, leaving most of the grounds behind. For the paper-filtered version, that transfer went through an ordinary white coffee filter. The two methods shared the same preparation up to that point.

I could imagine the first pour solving the problem on its own. After all, I've left most of the visible grounds behind. And it does make a difference. The measurements before that transfer were close to two grams of lipids per liter. After the liquid had been poured off, the concentration was about one gram per liter. So the grounds and fine particles left behind account for part of the change.

But now I have two cups holding a hundred and fifty milliliters each. The first contains about a hundred and fifty milligrams of coffee lipids. If I want the second cup to match it, I'd expect roughly the same amount. It doesn't. The paper-filtered brew had just twenty milligrams per liter. At the same serving size, that works out to three milligrams.

There's my result: a hundred and fifty versus three. The second cup contains one fiftieth as much, or ninety-eight percent less, according to the measured concentrations in this experiment. Those are calculated amounts, not measurements of my two imaginary cups.

Does that mean the second drink is ninety-eight percent weaker? Not according to its caffeine. The researchers measured about eight hundred and sixty milligrams of caffeine per liter before paper filtration, and eight hundred and eighty-seven in the filtered coffee. They were close. I wouldn't interpret that small difference as the paper adding caffeine, and I certainly wouldn't call the result decaffeinated.

Now for the question I couldn't answer by looking at a cup. What happened when people drank these versions for weeks? All sixty-four healthy volunteers first spent seventeen days drinking the paper-filtered version. Then they were randomly assigned to three groups. One switched to the unfiltered brew, one stayed with the filtered coffee, and one stopped drinking coffee. That third group gave the investigators something important: a comparison with no coffee at all.

After seventy-nine days, the rise in LDL cholesterol in the unfiltered group exceeded the rise in the filtered group by about sixteen milligrams per deciliter. The filtered and no-coffee groups did not show a statistically significant difference in the measured blood lipids. A separate small trial also found higher LDL during periods of drinking unfiltered boiled coffee than during periods of drinking the same kind of brew after paper filtration.

I can't use those results to calculate a personal cholesterol reading. The volunteers followed a particular recipe and drank six small cups daily; that amount is not a recommendation. Neither study was a test of which coffee prevents heart attacks. And different beans, filters, preparation and serving sizes can produce different numbers.

But the two cups do answer my original question. I don't need a different type of coffee to change the amount of oil that reaches the cup. In this experiment, B wins on that specific measure, while leaving the caffeine concentration broadly similar. If that measure is the reason I'm choosing between these two methods, the useful change happens at the very last pour.
