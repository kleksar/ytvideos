I can wash my hands before the tap gets warm. But am I giving up a cleaner pair of hands?

Lunch is ready, and the water in my kitchen is still cool. I can start now, or let the tap run until it's warm. Which would you pick?

I'm comparing two temperatures using results from a laboratory, not testing bacteria in my kitchen. My first score is how much of the test bacterium is removed.

Twenty volunteers washed their hands under controlled conditions. In these two versions they used the same amount of ordinary soap and rubbed it in for five seconds. Only the water temperature changed: fifteen or thirty-eight degrees Celsius.

Imagine each wash starts with ten thousand recoverable bacteria. Applying the average reductions from the experiment leaves about forty-six in the cool-water picture and a hundred and twenty-three in the warm one.

That looks like a win for cold water. But those are illustrations of the average reductions, not actual counts from two hands. The difference wasn't statistically significant. Neither temperature earned a reliable cleaning advantage.

There's another detail: five seconds was the laboratory setting, not how long I should scrub. With ordinary soap, I should spend at least twenty seconds working it over my hands, then rinse and dry.

Now I add a water meter to my imaginary sink. If it runs at 3.8 litres a minute and I wait twenty seconds for warm water, another 1.27 litres go down the drain before I even begin. At ten such waits a day, that's over twelve litres.

If I have instant warm water, that extra flow disappears. And if the cold water makes me rush, I'll choose a comfortable temperature.

I'm keeping the soap and the full scrub either way. So do I really need to stand at the sink waiting for warmth just to remove germs?
