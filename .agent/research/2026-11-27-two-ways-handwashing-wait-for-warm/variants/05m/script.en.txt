I've just finished getting lunch ready. Before I sit down, I turn on the kitchen tap. The first water out is cool. Do I start washing my hands, or wait for the warm water to arrive?

I'll compare the two choices in a model based on published handwashing experiments. I want to know which temperature removes more of the test bacteria and, separately, how much water I might use waiting. Pick A, wash with cool water, or B, wait for warm.

I need the same job on both sides. I'm not going to give the warm-water side a longer scrub or an extra squeeze of soap. Then I wouldn't know whether the temperature made the difference.

In a laboratory study with twenty volunteers, hands were contaminated with a strain of E. coli used for testing. The investigators recovered bacteria before and after washing, so they could compare the change. For the two conditions I'm using, the amount of plain soap was one millilitre and the lather time was five seconds. The warm water was thirty-eight degrees Celsius, about a hundred Fahrenheit. The cool water was fifteen degrees, or fifty-nine Fahrenheit.

Here's how I can make those results visible. I put ten thousand imaginary dots on each side of the screen. The dots stand for a common starting amount of recoverable bacteria, not the actual starting counts of the volunteers.

With the cool-water average, about forty-six dots remain. With the warm-water average, about a hundred and twenty-three remain. I would have expected the warmer water to win if the temperature were doing the cleaning by itself. Instead, the sample averages point the other way.

I can't award A a victory for that. Results varied between people, and the difference between these two temperature conditions wasn't statistically significant. The data don't establish that cool water removes more bacteria, or that every pair of hands will come out equally clean. They give me no demonstrated advantage from choosing the warmer temperature in this comparison.

I also notice another result from the same study. When the time spent lathering with plain soap was increased from five to twenty seconds in the baseline warm condition, the measured reduction improved significantly. On my ten-thousand-dot scale, that longer-lather average corresponds to roughly twenty-nine dots left. That's a separate comparison, not a new test of cool versus warm at twenty seconds.

It changes the way I'd spend my time. Five seconds was a test setting, not a recommendation. In everyday life I wet my hands with clean running water, use plain soap, scrub all the surfaces for at least twenty seconds, rinse, and dry them. Water at a comfortable temperature helps me do that properly. Water hot enough to reliably kill germs directly on my hands could injure my skin.

Now I go back to the imaginary kitchen. I give both sides the same proper washing routine. The warm side has an extra step before it starts. I'll assume that this tap releases 3.8 litres per minute and takes twenty seconds to deliver comfortably warm water. If I let that water go straight down the drain, that's about 1.27 litres spent waiting.

One trip to the sink doesn't look like much. But suppose I have ten of those waits in a day. They add up to about twelve and two-thirds litres. I could fill more than twelve one-litre bottles with water that never touched my hands.

I can change that outcome without changing the handwashing routine. If the hot water is already at the tap, the wait costs nothing. If I collect the water for another useful purpose, it isn't automatically waste. And if the cold water is so uncomfortable that I cut my washing short, choosing it just to save water isn't a good trade.

The point isn't that I should always turn the cold tap. It's that I'm free to choose a comfortable temperature without assuming the warmer option gets my hands cleaner. The soap, coverage and time are the things I shouldn't skip.

At my modeled kitchen sink, A avoids the extra water drawn during the wait. Neither A nor B has a proven advantage on the primary bacterial-removal comparison. So if the cool water already feels fine, I'll start with it, keep the twenty-second scrub, and go eat lunch.
