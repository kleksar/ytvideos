If I wait at the sink for warm water, will my hands actually come out cleaner? How much water could I save by starting right away, and how can I find out what the wait costs in my own kitchen?

Picture my kitchen just before lunch. I've put the food on the table and I need to wash my hands. When I open the tap, the water is cool. I know that if I leave it running for a while, it will become comfortably warm.

I can start now with cool water, A, or wait for warmth, B. Which would you pick? I'll give both choices the same amount of soap and the same scrub. I'll use published laboratory results for bacterial removal, then an explicitly hypothetical kitchen model for water use.

In a 2017 study, twenty volunteers washed hands that had been deliberately contaminated with a nonpathogenic test strain of E. coli. The researchers compared how much they could recover before and after washing. I pick two conditions with one millilitre of plain soap and five seconds of lathering: fifteen degrees Celsius versus thirty-eight. Those five seconds are a laboratory setting, not my recommendation.

On my imaginary screen I start with ten thousand dots on each side. I apply the average reduction reported for each temperature. The cool side ends up with about forty-six dots. The warm side has a hundred and twenty-three. The answer I expected has just flipped.

Before I give A the trophy, I need to know what those dots mean. A reduction of one on the study's log scale leaves a tenth of the starting amount; a reduction of two leaves a hundredth. The reported means were 2.34 for cool water and 1.91 for warm water. My dots are a picture of those means on an equal starting scale, not counts from two real hands or arithmetic averages of the final counts.

And the laboratory results varied. The uncertainty around the difference was large enough that the investigators could not establish a statistically significant difference between the two temperatures. The averages alone do not prove that the cool water is better. Nor do the data prove that the two methods must always have identical effects.

While the laboratory score stays undecided, I can already compare the waiting step. In my model, the tap delivers 3.8 litres per minute. If I leave it running for twenty seconds before washing, B draws an extra 1.27 litres. A starts straight away. Both still get a complete wash; I am not saving water by doing less cleaning.

But what if the first result was a quirk of that experiment? Another team tested a related question in 2024 with fifteen volunteers. Each person went through all five treatments. Four of them involved rubbing the hands under running water without soap, at four or forty degrees Celsius, for ten or twenty seconds. I need to keep these results separate from the soap experiment.

At twenty seconds the mean reductions were 2.23 logs in cold water and 2.39 in warm water. This time the warm side comes out slightly ahead. But neither the ten-second nor the twenty-second temperature comparison found a statistically significant difference. The two experiments disagree about the direction of the averages, yet neither establishes a winner for temperature.

One change did matter in that second study: both temperatures removed more test bacteria at twenty seconds than at ten, with a significant difference. The separate one-minute soap reference did better than all four water-only conditions. It changed both soap and duration, so I cannot tell from that comparison how much of the improvement came from soap alone.

So what do I do with the time I might otherwise spend waiting at my tap? I return to the 2017 study and change something other than temperature. In a separate warm-water condition with plain soap, increasing the lather time from five to twenty seconds produced a statistically significant improvement over the five-second baseline. The average reduction increased from 1.91 to 2.54 logs.

That longer-lather average leaves about twenty-nine dots on the imaginary scale, compared with a hundred and twenty-three at five seconds. This is still a comparison at the warm temperature, not a test of cool versus warm with twenty seconds of soap. I have useful evidence about lather time without pretending I know the outcome of an experiment nobody in this study performed.

Back at the sink, I wet my hands with clean running water, turn off the tap and apply plain soap. I scrub for at least twenty seconds, including the backs of my hands, between my fingers and under my nails, then turn the tap back on to rinse and dry my hands. I don't need water hot enough to kill germs directly; that could scald me. If I work under special hygiene rules, I follow them instead of replacing them with this model.

There is a second way to save water without shortening that scrub. In the same 3.8-litre-per-minute model, closing the tap for those twenty seconds avoids another 1.27 litres compared with leaving it running. That is a different comparison. If both A and B close the tap during lathering, neither gets an advantage from it. The difference between them is still just the wait.

If I repeat those exact conditions ten times in a day, the extra volume is about 12.67 litres. Over 365 days that's about 4,623 litres, just for waiting. That's a scenario, not a measurement of a real household. The waiting time, flow and frequency all have to be true for that number to apply.

Now I change the situation. Say I return to the same sink shortly after somebody has used hot water there. Warmth might arrive almost immediately. The pipe between the heater and my tap already contains water; the volume it holds depends on its length and internal diameter. Once it has cooled, I may need to displace that water before warmth arrives. This is why the number on the waiting meter need not stay the same on every visit.

If every wait really lasted five seconds instead of twenty, the annual extra draw in my ten-waits-a-day scenario would be about 1,156 litres. If there is no wait, that part drops to zero. I cannot multiply one cold-start observation by every handwash and call it my annual consumption.

I could replace my assumed 1.27 litres with a number from my own sink. For a future check, I would put a measuring jug under the tap before opening it, start from a cool pipe, and collect the water until it reaches a chosen comfortable temperature. I would record the volume and repeat after a recent warm-water use. No bacteria experiment or hot-water exposure is needed. These are proposed measurements, not results I have collected.

If I use the collected water for something that would otherwise need fresh water, that volume need not become additional waste. And measuring litres alone cannot tell me the energy bill. Heating one kilogram of water from fifteen to thirty-eight degrees requires roughly twenty-seven watt-hours of heat. My heater may need a different amount of input energy because of its efficiency and pipe losses; some water in the wait might never have been heated. I cannot add the litre count to the heat figure and call it a bill.

There is a reason I might still choose B. If the water feels so cold that I rush through the scrub, I should choose a comfortable temperature rather than sacrifice the wash. At a sink with instant warm water, there isn't even a waiting penalty to weigh against comfort.

My lunch is waiting. The laboratory comparisons have not established a bacterial-removal advantage for warmer water. At the modeled sink, starting immediately avoids the extra draw before washing, provided the first water is comfortable. I keep the soap, the full scrub, the rinse and the drying. I can go eat lunch instead of standing at the tap waiting for a cleaning advantage the experiments didn't establish.
