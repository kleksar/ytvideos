How much cleaner are my hands if I wait for warm water? And if there isn't a measurable cleaning advantage, what exactly am I paying for while the tap runs?

Picture my kitchen just before lunch. I've put the food on the table and I need to wash my hands. When I open the tap, the water is cool. I know that if I leave it running for a while, it will become comfortably warm.

I have two choices. I can wash now with the cool water, or wait until the warm water reaches the sink and then wash. Which would you choose? I'll build a comparison from laboratory measurements and a separate model of the kitchen plumbing. I'm not going to pretend I can measure bacteria by looking at my hands.

The first score is bacterial removal. The second is the water drawn before the wash begins. They aren't the same thing, and I won't turn them into a single winner until I've seen what each tells me.

Before I put numbers on the two sides, I need to separate temperature from everything else I'm doing at the sink. If I spend longer rubbing my hands with warm water, use more soap, or rinse more thoroughly, that's a different wash. I want to know what changes when the temperature alone changes.

A handwashing study published in 2017 gives me a way to make that comparison. Twenty volunteers took part. Their hands were deliberately contaminated with a test strain of E. coli that isn't pathogenic. The investigators measured how much of it they could recover from the hands before and after washing.

The amount of bacteria present before a wash can vary, so simply comparing two final counts would be misleading. I need the reduction relative to the starting amount. The researchers expressed their results on a logarithmic scale. That lets me compare reductions even when I don't begin with precisely the same count.

For my A and B, I use two conditions with plain soap. Each uses one millilitre of soap and five seconds of lathering. That's a controlled laboratory condition, not the routine I'm recommending at home. The cool water is fifteen degrees Celsius. The warm water is thirty-eight degrees, which is roughly a hundred degrees Fahrenheit.

I can turn the reported average reductions into a simple picture. On each side of an imaginary screen, I begin with ten thousand recoverable units. If the amount falls by one decimal log, one tenth remains: a thousand. If it falls by two, a hundred remain. The bigger the reduction, the fewer dots stay on my picture.

For A, the cool-water result is 2.34 logs. On this normalized scale, that's about forty-six dots left. For B, the warm-water result is 1.91 logs. That leaves about a hundred and twenty-three dots. The warm side has more dots remaining, not fewer.

If I were making a video just to declare a winner, I could stop here and tell everyone that cold water beat warm water. But I wouldn't be reporting the experiment correctly. These dots aren't counts recovered from an actual pair of hands. They're what happens when I apply each mean logarithmic reduction to the same hypothetical starting amount.

And the laboratory results varied. The uncertainty around the difference was large enough that the investigators could not establish a statistically significant difference between the two temperatures. The averages alone do not prove that the cool water is better. Nor do the data prove that the two methods must always have identical effects.

Even at the same sink, people don't wash their hands in exactly the same way. The amount of friction, the areas they cover and the way they rinse can all matter. In the study, substantial variation was associated with the participants themselves.

I want to check whether another experiment gives me a completely different answer. In a 2024 study, fifteen volunteers had their hands contaminated with a test strain of E. coli, then rinsed and rubbed their hands under running water. This time, the four temperature-and-time conditions did not use soap. I can't substitute them for the original handwashing conditions.

The investigators compared water at four degrees Celsius with water at forty. They tested ten and twenty seconds of rinsing. At ten seconds, the reported mean reductions were 1.93 logs for the colder water and 2.01 for the warmer water. At twenty seconds, they were 2.23 and 2.39.

This time the sample averages lean slightly toward the warm side. But neither temperature difference was statistically significant. And for both temperatures, increasing the duration from ten to twenty seconds produced a significant improvement in bacterial removal.

There was also a separate reference wash with soap lasting one minute before rinsing. Its mean reduction was greater than the reductions in the four water-only treatments. That comparison changed both soap use and duration, so I cannot use it to calculate how much improvement was due to soap alone.

I certainly wouldn't replace a thorough wash with soap with a quick rinse, and I'm not going to recommend ice-cold water because it happened to be a laboratory test condition.

So what do I do with the time I might otherwise spend waiting at my tap? I return to the 2017 study and change something other than temperature. In a separate warm-water condition with plain soap, increasing the lather time from five to twenty seconds produced a statistically significant improvement over the five-second baseline. The average reduction increased from 1.91 to 2.54 logs.

On the same imaginary scale, that corresponds to roughly twenty-nine dots remaining instead of a hundred and twenty-three. It isn't a direct test of both temperatures with twenty seconds of soap. I can't simply combine numbers from the two experiments to manufacture that result. It does show me why the way I wash deserves attention.

For a normal household wash, I want clean running water, plain soap, at least twenty seconds of scrubbing, coverage of all the hand surfaces, a rinse and complete drying. Soap helps lift material off my skin, and the rinse carries it away. I don't need water hot enough to kill microbes by heat; water that hot can scald me.

Now both A and B get that same everyday routine. The laboratory scores stay on the screen for what they actually measured. I switch to the second score, the water that flows before the routine starts.

I assume my modeled faucet releases 3.8 litres each minute and the warm-water option takes twenty seconds to reach the desired temperature. I let the waiting water go down the drain. A begins immediately with comfortable cool water. B spends about 1.27 extra litres before washing.

If I repeat those exact conditions ten times in a day, the extra volume is about 12.67 litres. Over 365 days that's about 4,623 litres, just for waiting. That's a scenario, not a measurement of a real household. The waiting time, flow and frequency all have to be true for that number to apply.

If I cut the wait to five seconds at the same flow, it falls to about 1,156 litres a year. If the tap already delivers warm water, it falls to zero. And if I collect the waiting water and use it to replace water I would otherwise draw, the extra draw doesn't necessarily become extra waste.

There's also a distinct energy question. For an illustrative litre of water, raising its temperature from fifteen to thirty-eight degrees takes about twenty-seven watt-hours of heat. That isn't twenty-seven watt-hours read from my electricity meter. The actual input depends on the heater, the incoming water, the pipes and other losses. The waiting-water model and the heating model describe different quantities, so I won't simply add them together as a bill.

I don't need to forbid warm water. If my hands feel uncomfortable in the cold, warmth can help me stick with a thorough wash. If warm water arrives immediately, there is no extra waiting volume to avoid. If I work somewhere with specific hygiene procedures, I follow those requirements.

Back at the lunch table, I've made the decision I actually needed. The laboratory comparisons didn't establish a bacterial-removal advantage for warmer water. Starting immediately avoids the modeled waiting volume when the initial water is comfortable and the alternative is running it down the drain.

I can choose the temperature that lets me wash properly. I won't shorten the scrub, skip soap or confuse a quick rinse with a proper wash. On this particular sink, there's no reason to let perfectly usable water run away while I'm waiting for it to do a job that belongs to the soap and my hands.
