I have a pot of water to heat and a lid that fits it. I could put the lid on and save time, but how much time? And why might that same lid make a much bigger difference after I stop trying to raise the temperature?

I'm going to make a decision I can actually use when I cook. I'll look at the time it takes to get water hot, how much electricity I need to keep it hot, and what happens to the water itself. For every comparison, I'll use the same starting conditions within that round. A is the open pot. B is the covered pot. Which would you choose?

I'm working with a replay of published experiments on an induction cooker. I haven't run these tests myself. I also won't pretend that plain water tells me whether a particular meal tastes better or is ready to eat. It gives me a way to isolate the effect of a lid.

For the first round I choose a small stainless steel pot containing about one point nine liters of water. The comparison uses a target of ninety degrees Celsius, starting from twenty. The researchers used fitted heating curves to compare the same temperature range because they couldn't control the exact initial temperature of every run. The numbers I put on my clock are those comparable estimates, not a stopwatch recording I made.

I want to know what happens if I don't change the heat setting at all. Both versions get the lower of the two tested induction power settings. I put the lid on version B and leave version A open.

The first surprise is how close the times are. The open pot takes thirteen minutes and twenty-three seconds. The covered pot takes twelve minutes and seven seconds. My lid has saved seventy-six seconds, a little under ten percent of the waiting time. The published energy figures come to about zero point two three and zero point two one kilowatt-hours, respectively. The saving is about nine percent.

Now I increase the power for both versions. I don't mix a high-powered covered pot with a low-powered open one and call the difference a lid effect. At the higher common setting, the times are eight minutes and five seconds for the open pot, and seven minutes and forty seconds for the covered pot. The lid saves twenty-five seconds. The difference in energy is also smaller, at around five percent.

I can already make one choice. For a quick pot of water, I shouldn't count on a huge time saving. It depends on the setup. But getting water hot isn't usually the last step of cooking. If I have something that needs a long, gentle cook, I'll spend a lot of time trying to keep the water near the same temperature.

That's a different problem. I need to replace the heat my pot loses, not heat the entire contents from room temperature again. I can replay a separate set of published tests with a much bigger pot. I choose seven and a half liters of water in the fifteen-liter pot used in that experiment. This time my starting point is ninety degrees. I want the water to stay near that temperature for forty-five minutes.

I won't keep the power identical this time. My goal is an identical temperature target, so I'll compare how much power each method requires to achieve it. If I feed both pots the same amount of electricity regardless of the result, one might get hotter than I want. Then I wouldn't be comparing the same task.

The open pot needs around eight hundred and sixty-five watts. Under the lid, the power needed in the test is around two hundred and seventeen watts. That's about one quarter as much. In both cases the temperature is held near ninety degrees.

Now I look at the energy for the whole holding period rather than just the power at a given moment. The published figures for this volume give about zero point six four kilowatt-hours for the open pot and zero point one six for the covered pot. I've saved almost half a kilowatt-hour in forty-five minutes. That is about seventy-five percent of the energy for this stage, not seventy-five percent of all the energy I use making dinner.

There is a second way to see what happened. I compare the reported water losses from these tests. The open pot loses nearly nine hundred grams, almost the weight of a full liter bottle of water. The covered pot loses about eight grams. That doesn't mean every covered pot will have exactly eight grams of evaporation loss. It means that, in this particular comparison, keeping the lid on changed the amount of water that escaped by a very large margin.

As water evaporates, it carries energy away. On the underside of an ordinary lid, some of that vapor condenses and drips back into the pot. The lid also changes the heat exchange with the surroundings. The published measurements can't separate every part of that mechanism, but I can see the total effect in the power required to maintain the temperature.

This explains why looking only at a timer during initial heating could lead me to overlook the more important use of the lid. During the first stage, much of the electricity goes toward raising the temperature of the water. During the second stage, I'm paying to replace ongoing heat losses.

Before I carry that result into my own kitchen, I need to check the limits. The heating round used a different, smaller pot. I can't simply combine the two measured stages and call it one measured cooking cycle. And the holding test used a particular induction cooker with discrete settings. In the other pot sizes the available settings didn't always allow a matching temperature in both configurations. That matters because I want a fair comparison, not a deliberately hotter pot on one side.

I can still use a simple calculation to see how the length of the holding stage changes the stakes. Suppose, only for illustration, I keep the published power difference constant. Eight hundred and sixty-five watts minus two hundred and seventeen watts is six hundred and forty-eight watts. For ten minutes, that difference would correspond to about zero point one one kilowatt-hours. For forty-five minutes it would be about zero point four nine. That's a model using the reported power settings, not additional measured runs. The calculated forty-five-minute value is close to, but not the same as, the saving from the published energy table.

The longer I hold the same temperature under those assumptions, the more the savings accumulate. For an imagined electricity price of twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour, the actual reported energy difference for the forty-five-minute comparison works out to around twelve cents. If my price is different, or my pot loses heat at a different rate, my saving changes.

Now I come back to the food. If I want broth to stay hot while vegetables cook, keeping moisture in may be exactly what I want. But if I'm making a sauce and I need the liquid to evaporate so it thickens, leaving it uncovered is part of the recipe. In that case a lid that prevents evaporation doesn't necessarily save me money on the whole job. It may just stop me reaching the result I'm after.

I wouldn't put a lid on a pot at full power and walk away. I need to adjust the setting after reaching the desired temperature, follow the recipe, and leave room for the contents so they don't overflow. And I mean an ordinary kitchen lid, not a sealed pressure vessel.

So I have two different answers to the two clocks I started with. The covered pot got the water to ninety degrees a little sooner, and the size of that lead changed with the heating power. But for the second clock, the one that ran while the water stayed hot, the difference was much larger.

If I need to retain water and keep a pot hot, I'll use the lid and turn the heat down to what the food actually needs. If I need water to leave, I'll keep it open as the recipe requires. The point isn't to cover every pot. It's to choose the method that does the cooking job I actually want, and not pay for heat I'm only sending out of the kitchen.
