If I wash rice ten times instead of not washing it at all, will I get a noticeably less sticky dinner? And why might the answer change when I open a different bag?

I want the grains to separate easily. At the sink I have a simple choice. A goes straight into the cooker. B gets ten rinses. Which would you pick before seeing the result?

I'm reconstructing a published test, so the numbers I'll show are laboratory readings, not a new kitchen experiment. The researchers bought three kinds of rice: jasmine, medium-grain and glutinous rice. The last one is the kind that is supposed to hold together.

For each variety they compared small portions weighing ten grams. Each wash used about eighty millilitres of distilled water. They stirred for twenty seconds, drained the water without losing any grains, and used fresh water for the next wash. There was also a third treatment with three washes, to see whether a shorter routine made a difference.

Ten rinses in this setup mean about eight hundred millilitres of wash water and more than three minutes of stirring alone. That's for a tiny sample, not a suggested recipe for my kitchen.

If I stop the comparison here, B looks convincing. I can see loose particles leaving the grain. But my dinner isn't a bowl of rinse water. I need to know what happens once the rice is cooked.

Each portion of the same variety gets the same final ratio of rice to cooking water. The researchers seal the containers and steam them together under the same thirty-minute cooking procedure. Now the texture can be compared.

Instead of asking someone to judge a photograph of two plates, they use an instrument. It presses a half-gram layer of cooked rice. As it moves back up, the sticky grains resist separation. The reading gives me a way to compare adhesion. A lower number means less stickiness on this test.

I start with jasmine rice. On the published graph, the unwashed portion is around twenty-eight, and the one washed ten times is around nineteen. Those are approximate readings from the graph, not exact individual measurements. The result looks promising for B.

Medium-grain rice shows a similar direction. The ten-rinse portion is less sticky on average. But the middle option, three rinses, is already close to it. Extra effort doesn't necessarily buy much more separation.

Then I switch to glutinous rice and run the same choice again. Now B has the higher mean: roughly forty-seven instead of forty. That's the opposite direction.

Across all three varieties, the washing treatment didn't have a statistically significant overall effect on stickiness. Nor did it establish an overall hardness effect. The combination of variety and washing treatment did matter for stickiness. I can't call the methods identical, or declare ten washes the winner for every bag.

To understand why, I need to separate what I removed at the sink from what the grains released during cooking. The experiment collected the particles from the raw rice and, separately, material washed off the cooked grains. It found that these two materials had different compositions. The starch structure differed too. Yet the material released during cooking was broadly similar across the different washing treatments.

There is a fresh supply of starch inside the grain. As the rice heats in water, some of that starch leaves the grain and forms a layer on its surface. A branched type of starch called amylopectin plays an important part in how strongly cooked grains stick together.

That explains how I can remove loose material before cooking and still create a sticky coating in the pot.

My goal matters too. If I'm trying to form a piece of sushi, adhesion can be useful. If I want separate grains for another dish, it may be exactly what I don't want. The same reading doesn't mean the same thing to both cooks.

So my practical answer isn't to stop washing rice. I'll check the kind of rice, the dish and the instructions on the packet. If I want to see what an extra rinse changes, I'll compare two portions from the same bag with the same cooking water and method.

I won't judge the winner while I'm standing over the sink. I'll judge it after the rice is cooked.
