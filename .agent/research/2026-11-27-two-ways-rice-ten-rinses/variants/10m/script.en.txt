Will ten rinses give me rice that falls apart easily on my plate? Why might the same routine give me the opposite result with another bag of rice? I want to find out before I spend another evening at the sink.

I'll start with jasmine rice. I'm choosing between A, straight from the bag, and B, washed ten times. I want separate grains, so I'm judging how much the cooked grains stick. Pick a side. I'll keep that choice on the screen when I change the variety later.

I'm working from a published laboratory experiment, not claiming a new kitchen test. It compared three kinds of supermarket rice: jasmine, medium-grain and glutinous. In the reconstruction, the portions of each kind start alike. A stays dry. B gets ten washes. A third portion gets three.

The original test used ten grams of rice per portion. For each wash, the grains were stirred in about eighty millilitres of distilled water for twenty seconds, then drained without losing grains. So ten washes take more than three minutes of stirring alone, versus one minute for three. The corresponding wash-water amounts are about eight hundred and two hundred and forty millilitres. These are small laboratory portions, not kitchen instructions.

Before the rice goes into the steamer, I make sure the washed portions don't get extra cooking water by accident. The experiment adjusted the final amount of water within each variety and steamed the sealed containers for thirty minutes. The glutinous rice used a different ratio from the other two, so I'm comparing washes within each variety, rather than pretending that all three kinds cook identically.

To judge the result, I watch a circular probe press a small layer of cooked grains, then lift away. The stronger the adhesion, the larger the stickiness reading. It doesn't tell me whether the rice tastes good. I'll compare the approximate group averages from the published graph; the individual readings vary.

Let's see what happens to my jasmine rice. The graph shows about twenty-eight for the unwashed sample, about thirty for three washes, and about nineteen for ten. I'm reading approximate heights from the published figure, so those numbers aren't exact measurements. In this variety, the ten-rinse average is lower. If I only saw this comparison, I might decide every rice needed the full treatment.

Now I switch to medium-grain rice. The unwashed average is around twenty-six, and both washed groups are around eighteen. Three washes and ten washes look nearly the same on this graph. More washing doesn't show a steady improvement from one group to the next.

With glutinous rice, my prediction fails in the other direction. The average starts around forty without washing and rises to roughly forty-seven after ten washes. That doesn't prove every individual glutinous rice sample gets stickier when washed; the readings vary. It does mean I can't claim the same direction of effect across the varieties.

So which method won? On these graphs I can point to differences in averages, but I can't treat each difference as a proven effect. Across the three varieties, the statistical test found no significant overall washing effect on stickiness or hardness. It did find that the stickiness response differed with the variety. That is why I'm not going to average away the reversal and tell everyone to stop rinsing, or to rinse ten times. I still need an explanation for what changed on the surface of the cooked grain.

I return to the sink. The test actually collected material in two places: from the raw grains during washing, and from the cooked grains afterward. Both were recovered for analysis. I can now ask whether the stuff I washed off at the beginning is the same stuff that makes my dinner sticky.

For jasmine rice, starch made up about sixty-one percent of the recovered raw surface material. In the recovered material from cooked rice, it was around ninety percent. Those percentages describe the collected material, not the proportion of starch lost from the whole grain. The two samples differed in starch structure as well. Yet the cooked surface material had broadly similar characteristics whether the raw rice was washed or not. The starch on the finished rice hasn't simply survived ten washes.

When the grain absorbs water and heats up, starch swells and some of it escapes. Part of that material is amylopectin, a starch molecule with branches. The amount that escapes and the structure of the escaped molecules help determine adhesion. The grain is supplying a fresh coating during cooking. To find out how much that coating matters, I need a different experiment: change the surface after cooking, when the coating has already formed.

In a separate published comparison of twelve varieties, the surface of cooked rice was washed with hot water. Take two very different samples from that study. Khao Niao, a Thai glutinous rice, had a mean stickiness reading of seventy-five point four before the cooked-rice wash. Afterward, the mean was ten point three. An Indian variety, Swarna, started at only two point seven and ended at zero point six. These readings are from that separate test, measured in gram-seconds, not from my ten-rinse graph.

The striking thing is that removing material after cooking greatly reduced the measurement for both, but the starting points were already worlds apart. Across the twelve varieties the reported reduction ranged from sixty-five to eighty-six percent. This is evidence for the importance of the cooked surface, not an instruction to wash the rice on my plate. Nor can I apply that percentage to washing raw grains. That would be mixing up the two experiments.

Why were the starting points so different? Another measurement in the same study helps. The recovered dry material ranged from roughly fifteen to fifty-five milligrams for each gram of initial rice. Varieties with more amylose, another component of starch, tended to release less material and were generally less sticky in that study. I can't pick a universal winner from the name of the molecule alone; other properties of the grain also matter. But now I have a reason to look at the variety before I reach for the tap.

Now imagine I'm making sushi rather than trying to separate every grain. My goal has changed. If I remove too much adhesion, the rice may not hold its shape. A separate study on a particular Italian-grown japonica rice found an appropriate washing duration of two hundred and thirty seconds for its nigiri-forming process. That's a different outcome and a specific preparation method, not a contradiction of the three-variety experiment. Neither study supplies a magic number of rinses for every recipe.

There is one important boundary. Washing can have purposes besides changing texture, including removing surface debris or following the packet instructions. This comparison did not measure food safety, arsenic, nutritional value or how clear the rinse water became. A result about adhesion cannot answer those questions.

If I still want to test my own rice at home, here's what I would do. I'd split one bag into two equal portions, choose zero or ten rinses, and account for retained wash water so that both portions start cooking with the same amount of water. I'd keep the cooking and resting times and serving temperature the same. I'd ask someone to shuffle the labelled bowls so I don't know which one I'm trying. I could compare how easily the grains separate, then repeat the test another day. Those are proposed steps, not measured results. A single tasting could reflect my own preference as much as the washing routine.

Back at the sink, I no longer have a reason to insist on ten rounds just for separate grains. The jasmine average went down, the glutinous average went up, and the combined test did not establish an overall washing effect. The separate cooked-rice experiment shows why the surface at serving time matters so much. If I want a different texture, I start with the rice itself and the intended dish. I'll still follow the preparation instructions. And when I compare methods, I'll judge the rice on my plate, where the meal actually ends.
