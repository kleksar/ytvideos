I'm making rice for dinner, and I have a choice that seems obvious: rinse it until the water looks clear, or save time and cook it straight from the bag. But does the extra work actually make the grains less sticky? Why can the same washing routine produce a different result with a different rice? And if the water isn't a reliable texture test, what should I look at instead?

I'll start with one bag of jasmine rice. I want separate grains, so the score I'm interested in is how strongly the cooked grains stick. On side A, I don't wash the sample. On side B, I wash it ten times. Which side would you choose?

I'm not going to pretend I made these measurements in my kitchen. I'll use an illustrated reconstruction of a controlled experiment that tested three kinds of supermarket rice. It included glutinous rice, medium-grain rice and jasmine rice. There were three treatments for each kind: zero, three or ten washes.

Before we cook anything, I need to make the two methods comparable. If I grab one handful of rice for A and two handfuls for B, I'm already testing the wrong thing. The experiment used ten grams in each portion. Each wash used about eighty millilitres of distilled water. The grains were stirred with a glass rod for about twenty seconds. Then the water was poured away without losing grains.

One round is straightforward. Ten rounds use around eight hundred millilitres of wash water and two hundred seconds of stirring. That's more than three minutes, before I count pouring and draining. It's a calculation for a small lab sample, not an estimate of how much water anyone needs at home. I also keep the three-rinse group in the picture. If I get the same useful result in three rounds, there's little point spending seven more just for texture.

At the cooker I have a second problem. Rinsed grains can carry water into the next stage. If I ignore that and compare two portions with different amounts of water, I won't know whether the texture changed because I washed the rice or because I cooked it with more water. In the experiment, the final rice-to-water ratio was adjusted to one to one point six for the medium and jasmine rice and one to one point three for the glutinous rice. Within each variety, all washing groups had the same target. The containers were sealed with foil and steamed for thirty minutes.

Now the rice is ready. How do I compare stickiness without relying on what it looks like from across the room? The experiment used a texture analyser. Imagine a circular plate coming down onto a small layer of cooked grains, pressing them, then lifting away. If the rice resists being separated from the plate, that resistance contributes to the stickiness reading. The study compressed the sample by forty percent and performed four measurements for each sample. That is a specific measure of adhesion, not a score for taste, and the four measurements are not necessarily four independent cooking batches.

Let's see what happens to my jasmine rice. The graph shows about twenty-eight for the unwashed sample, about thirty for three washes, and about nineteen for ten. I'm reading approximate heights from the published figure, so those numbers aren't exact measurements. In this variety, the ten-rinse average is lower. If I only saw this comparison, I might decide every rice needed the full treatment.

Now I switch to medium-grain rice. The unwashed average is around twenty-six, and both washed groups are around eighteen. Three washes and ten washes look nearly the same on this graph. More washing doesn't show a steady improvement from one group to the next.

With glutinous rice, my prediction fails in the other direction. The average starts around forty without washing and rises to roughly forty-seven after ten washes. That doesn't prove every individual glutinous rice sample gets stickier when washed; the readings vary. It does mean I can't claim the same direction of effect across the varieties.

There is a useful distinction in the statistical results. Overall, the study did not find a significant main effect of washing on stickiness or hardness. But it did find a significant interaction between the variety and the washing treatment for stickiness. In plain language, the response to washing wasn't uniform across the three kinds of rice. A failure to establish an overall effect is not proof that rinsing and not rinsing are equivalent. The test didn't cover every type of rice, either.

What makes this especially interesting is that the experiment didn't stop at the texture measurements. It collected the material rinsed from raw rice, dried it, and compared it with the material recovered from the surface of cooked rice. That second sample was collected after cooking, using hot water. So there are two distinct collections, and I shouldn't confuse either with the water I'm adding to cook dinner.

In the jasmine sample, around sixty-one percent of the surface material collected before cooking was starch. After cooking, the material recovered from the grains contained roughly ninety percent starch. The material from the sink was not simply the same substance waiting to be released in the pot. Its composition and starch structure differed. Across the pre-cooking wash treatments, however, the materials released during cooking had broadly similar characteristics.

To picture the mechanism, imagine the grain as a package of starch. A rinse removes some of the material already outside that package. Heating the grain with water changes the starch inside; some of it moves into the water and onto the grain surface. One important component is amylopectin, which has many branches. The amount and structure of that released starch influence how much effort it takes to pull the grains apart.

There's a separate experiment that helps me understand this. In a study of twelve varieties, researchers measured freshly cooked rice, then washed cooked grains with hot water and measured them again. In that experiment, measured stickiness fell by roughly sixty-five to eighty-six percent across the varieties. That's not a recommendation to rinse my finished dinner. It's evidence that material released during cooking plays a big role in the sticky surface. It also explains why washing raw rice and washing cooked rice are two different interventions.

Now imagine I'm making sushi rather than trying to separate every grain. My goal has changed. If I remove too much adhesion, the rice may not hold its shape. A separate study on a particular Italian-grown japonica rice found an appropriate washing duration of two hundred and thirty seconds for its nigiri-forming process. That's a different outcome and a specific preparation method, not a contradiction of the three-variety experiment. Neither study supplies a magic number of rinses for every recipe.

There are other reasons to wash rice besides texture. I might want to remove surface particles, or the packet may tell me to rinse. And if I'm worried about arsenic or nutrients, I need a different test altogether. The US Food and Drug Administration reports that rinsing alone had little effect on arsenic in its tests and can remove added nutrients from enriched rice. Cooking in excess water and draining can reduce inorganic arsenic, but can also reduce those added nutrients. I can't use a stickiness test to decide whether a food is safe or nutritionally better.

So what would I actually do with that bag of rice at home? I'd check the variety and the package instructions, and decide whether I want separate grains or a cohesive texture. If I still want to find out whether extra rinses help, I can design a small home comparison: two equal portions from the same bag, the same final amount of cooking water, equivalent cooking and resting times, and the same serving temperature. I'd label the bowls underneath so I could compare them without knowing which one was washed. I'd look at separation and texture, and repeat the comparison before drawing a conclusion. That's a proposed test, not a result I've measured.

The answer to my original question is that ten rinses are not a reliable universal route to separate grains. In the published experiment, the average went down with jasmine rice but up with glutinous rice, and the overall washing effect wasn't established. I can change what's on the surface before cooking, but the rice can produce a new sticky layer as it cooks.

I'll choose the rice for the dish first, then follow the cooking instructions. If I'm comparing methods, I'll compare what's on my plate, not how clear the last bowl of water was.
