My washing machine has two settings: eight hundred revolutions a minute, or fourteen hundred. Which one would get my laundry out of the dryer sooner?

I'll compare two identical seven-kilo test loads, using published dryer measurements rather than pretending I've run the machines myself. A gets the slower spin. B gets the faster one. Both go into the same dryer on the same storage-dry program. I'm timing the drying stage, and I'll check its electricity use too. Pick a side.

Before either dryer starts, there's already a difference. In the measurements, A carries five litres of water into the dryer. B carries three and a half. That extra litre and a half has gone down the washer's drain instead of travelling into the dryer.

I start both drying clocks at zero. B finishes after one hundred minutes. A still has half an hour to go. At the end, A has used nearly five kilowatt-hours. B has used three point six six. That's one point three two fewer units of electricity in the dryer.

So should I just turn the spin all the way up? The same table has a sixteen-hundred setting. Compared with fourteen hundred, it cuts only another two minutes and two hundredths of a kilowatt-hour.

And there's a catch. My clocks didn't include the washer. If the faster spin adds thirty minutes there, the time advantage disappears. I also have to subtract any extra washer electricity from the saving.

For suitable fabrics, fourteen hundred looks like a useful option in this example, not a rule for every machine or garment. Before I even touch the dryer, I have a decision to make.
