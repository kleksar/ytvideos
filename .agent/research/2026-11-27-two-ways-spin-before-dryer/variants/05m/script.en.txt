I need a basket of dry laundry, and I have two buttons I can change before the drying even begins. If I use a faster spin on the washing machine, how much work can I take away from the dryer? And does the highest speed always make sense?

I'm going to model two ways of finishing the same laundry, not conduct a new appliance test. I have the published consumption table for an ETNA dryer. It gives drying times and electricity use for loads prepared at different washer spin speeds. I'll start with its seven-kilo test load and keep the drying program and target dryness the same.

For A I choose eight hundred revolutions per minute. For B I choose fourteen hundred. My first measure is how long the dryer is occupied. My second is how much electricity that machine consumes. Choose A or B before I reveal what the table says.

The difference begins inside the washer. Spinning doesn't need to turn all the water into vapour. It pushes liquid out of the fabric and through the holes in the drum. The washer can pump that liquid away. Whatever stays in the clothes has to travel with them to the dryer.

In the table, A starts drying with five litres of residual moisture. B starts with three and a half. Imagine a one-litre bottle and a half-litre bottle next to the basket. That is the difference between the two test loads. The clothes are still damp on both sides, but the dryer has less water to deal with in B.

Now I reset the clock at the beginning of each drying cycle. An hour passes in the model. Neither basket has reached the finish. At one hour and forty minutes, B reaches the published end of the program. A needs another thirty minutes. That's two hours and ten minutes of drying rather than one hour and forty.

The electricity difference is just as interesting. The slower-spin load needs four point nine eight kilowatt-hours in the dryer. The faster-spin load needs three point six six. I haven't changed the dryer or bought a different machine. The published difference is one point three two kilowatt-hours, just from preparing the load differently.

At an imaginary flat electricity price of twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour, that would be thirty-three cents off the dryer portion of one load. This isn't a claim about my own electricity bill. It gives the difference a scale.

Now I want to know whether I should turn the speed up again. The table goes as high as sixteen hundred. There, residual moisture falls to three point four litres. The drying time falls from one hundred minutes to ninety-eight. The electricity use falls from three point six six to three point six four.

The first change saved half an hour. The second saved two minutes. There is no reason to assume that another two hundred revolutions per minute will save the same amount every time. The washer has less water left to remove.

But I still need to count the part of the job that happened before my drying clocks started. If fourteen hundred takes ten extra minutes in the washer, for example, I would keep twenty minutes of the drying time saving. If it takes thirty extra minutes, I'd break even on total time. And if it adds forty minutes, I would get the finished laundry ten minutes later, even though the dryer was faster. Those are scenarios, not washer measurements from this table.

The electricity works the same way. One point three two kilowatt-hours is the gross dryer saving. I can subtract the difference between the two washer cycles to find the net saving. Without that measurement I can't tell you the exact total cost.

I would also check the washing instructions on the garment and the settings the washer actually permits. A fabric that needs a gentle cycle doesn't become suitable for a fast spin because another load dried faster.

So my answer for this particular drying test is B. The dryer is free thirty minutes sooner, with less electricity used. But when I judge the whole laundry job, I would count both machines and keep the fabric safe. The useful choice is not the biggest number on the dial. It's the setting that gets the work done without simply moving more of it somewhere else.
