I have a pile of washed laundry and an empty dryer. I'm trying to get the clothes ready without leaving the dryer running for longer than necessary. What if the best time to do something about that is while the clothes are still in the washing machine?

There are two numbers on the washer's spin dial that I want to compare: eight hundred and fourteen hundred revolutions per minute. I want to find out what happens to the drying time, what happens to the electricity consumption, and whether it makes sense to chase the biggest spin number available. I'll also check what changes when I include the washing machine in the calculation.

Rather than claiming I've tested appliances at home, I'm going to build a comparison using published dryer test results. The first set comes from an ETNA dryer manual. It reports measurements for three load sizes and five spin speeds, with the dryer set to its storage-dry program. That's useful because I'm not comparing a basket that's ready to put away with a basket that's deliberately left damp for ironing.

I'll choose the seven-kilo test load from that table. On the A side, the washer finishes at eight hundred revolutions a minute. On the B side it finishes at fourteen hundred. The model starts with the same laundry before the final spin. I keep the drying machine, program and target dryness the same. I will first compare the time between starting the dryer and the end of its program, and the electricity the dryer uses over that period. Which side would you choose?

The most important part of this comparison isn't a faster-moving dryer. It's what's inside the clothes when I open the washer.

Picture the perforated drum during the spin. The clothes press against the side, and liquid water can leave through the holes. The pump carries it away. The spin doesn't make the clothes perfectly dry. Some moisture is still held in the fabric. That's the part I have to deal with next.

In the published measurements, the load prepared at eight hundred revolutions per minute contains five litres of residual moisture. The one prepared at fourteen hundred contains three and a half. I could represent the difference with a litre bottle and a half-litre bottle. Neither bottle is part of the load; they simply show the volume of water that isn't going to the dryer on side B.

This is why weighing the baskets immediately after spinning would not be a fair way to decide how much clothing I had on each side. The water contributes to the weight. I want the same amount of laundry, not two baskets of equal wet weight that might contain different amounts of fabric.

Now I put the two measured starting conditions on a pair of drying clocks. These clocks begin when the dryer program begins, not at the start of the wash. The A clock runs to two hours and ten minutes. The B clock stops after an hour and forty minutes. At that point A still needs another half hour.

The dryer on side A used four point nine eight kilowatt-hours. On B it used three point six six. The gross difference is one point three two kilowatt-hours. As a proportion of A's dryer consumption, that's about twenty-seven percent. The time reduction is about twenty-three percent. These percentages belong to this test table, not to every laundry room.

Could I make B even better? There are measurements at intermediate speeds too. At a thousand revolutions the dryer needs two hours. At twelve hundred it needs an hour and fifty minutes. At fourteen hundred it's an hour and forty. Looking at those three steps, I might expect the next two hundred revolutions to take away another ten minutes.

Instead, at sixteen hundred the measured drying time is ninety-eight minutes. I get just two minutes more. The residual moisture only changes from three and a half litres to three point four. The electricity use changes from three point six six kilowatt-hours to three point six four. I would not decide that a washer with a sixteen-hundred setting is worth buying on the strength of these two minutes alone.

What if my basket is smaller? In the same ETNA table, a six-kilo load prepared at eight hundred takes a hundred and fifteen minutes in the dryer. At fourteen hundred it takes eighty-five. The thirty-minute difference remains in that row, while the electricity difference is one point two kilowatt-hours. With an eight-kilo load, the corresponding times are a hundred and fifty and a hundred and twenty minutes, and the electricity difference is one point four nine kilowatt-hours. Those are separate measured rows, not predictions for every size between them.

I also want to make sure I'm not taking one machine's consumption figures as a universal constant. Another published table, for a Siemens heat-pump dryer, gives different numbers. Its nine-kilo cotton load, cupboard dry, takes a hundred and eighty-one minutes after an eight-hundred spin. After fourteen hundred it takes a hundred and thirty-three. That is forty-eight minutes saved, with a dryer electricity difference of zero point seven six kilowatt-hours. A half-sized cotton load in that table saves thirty minutes and zero point four three kilowatt-hours.

Both tables show the same direction of change for those comparisons. But they do not give me the same answer in kilowatt-hours, or in minutes for every load. I can't mix the Siemens electricity result with the ETNA time result and call that my own dryer. I would look for the figures for my actual model, if they're available.

So far B is winning the drying-stage contest. But I've left something out of the clocks. I didn't time the final spin. Imagine that the washer's B cycle ends ten minutes after A's. B still saves twenty minutes from the point where the two options began. If the B cycle ends thirty minutes later, both finish at the same time. If the gap is forty minutes, A actually finishes ten minutes earlier. The drying-stage result didn't change; the boundary of the comparison did.

I can use the same reasoning for the bill. Suppose a flat price of twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour. The ETNA seven-kilo dryer saving would be thirty-three cents per load before counting the washer. Across one hundred such hypothetical loads that's thirty-three dollars in gross dryer savings. If each faster washer cycle used another zero point two kilowatt-hours, the net saving would be one point one two kilowatt-hours per load, or twenty-eight dollars over a hundred loads. Zero point two is an example input, not something measured by that manual. If the extra washer energy were equal to one point three two kilowatt-hours, the electricity advantage would vanish.

How would I find my real numbers? I would use the same permitted wash program and comparable loads of the same fabric, change only the spin setting, and record the complete elapsed time and the consumption of each appliance using a suitable, safely rated meter if available. I'd repeat the comparison rather than depend on one unusually balanced or unbalanced load. I would use the same drying program, check that both loads were actually dry enough, and avoid changing the dryer filter condition or the environment between trials. A faster displayed finish isn't worth much if the second basket still needs to go back into the dryer.

There is also a limit that has nothing to do with the clock. I'd check the care labels and the permitted spin settings. If the clothes require a gentle treatment, I would not force a high spin. I have no measurements here of fabric damage, wrinkles, vibration or noise. Those would be separate things to check, not points I can award to either side out of thin air.

Here is the result I can defend. For the original seven-kilo storage-dry comparison, side B frees the dryer thirty minutes earlier and saves one point three two kilowatt-hours in that machine. Moving to sixteen hundred barely changes that dryer result. My whole-job winner depends on what the washer adds and whether the clothes can safely take the spin.

The reason I care about this is simple. The water can leave through the washer's drain before the clothes move, or it can be removed later in the dryer. If I can measure that trade-off, I can make a better decision than just turning the drying dial up and hoping the laundry will finish sooner.
