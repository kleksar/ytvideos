I have a pile of washed laundry and an empty dryer. I'm trying to get the clothes ready without leaving the dryer running for longer than necessary. What if the best time to do something about that is while the clothes are still in the washing machine?

There are two numbers on the washer's spin dial that I want to compare: eight hundred and fourteen hundred revolutions per minute. I want to find out what happens to the drying time, what happens to the electricity consumption, and whether it makes sense to chase the biggest spin number available. I'll also check what changes when I include the washing machine in the calculation.

Rather than claiming I've tested appliances at home, I'm going to build a comparison using published dryer test results. The first set comes from an ETNA dryer manual. It reports measurements for three load sizes and five spin speeds, with the dryer set to its storage-dry program. That's useful because I'm not comparing a basket that's ready to put away with a basket that's deliberately left damp for ironing.

I'll choose the seven-kilo test load from that table. On the A side, the washer finishes at eight hundred revolutions a minute. On the B side it finishes at fourteen hundred. The model starts with the same laundry before the final spin. I keep the drying machine, program and target dryness the same. I will first compare the time between starting the dryer and the end of its program, and the electricity the dryer uses over that period. Which side would you choose?

The most important part of this comparison isn't a faster-moving dryer. It's what's inside the clothes when I open the washer.

Picture the perforated drum during the spin. The clothes press against the side, and liquid water can leave through the holes. The pump carries it away. The spin doesn't make the clothes perfectly dry. Some moisture is still held in the fabric. That's the part I have to deal with next.

In the published measurements, the load prepared at eight hundred revolutions per minute contains five litres of residual moisture. The one prepared at fourteen hundred contains three and a half. I could represent the difference with a litre bottle and a half-litre bottle. Neither bottle is part of the load; they simply show the volume of water that isn't going to the dryer on side B.

This is why weighing the baskets immediately after spinning would not be a fair way to decide how much clothing I had on each side. The water contributes to the weight. I want the same amount of laundry, not two baskets of equal wet weight that might contain different amounts of fabric.

Now I put the two measured starting conditions on a pair of drying clocks. These clocks begin when the dryer program begins, not at the start of the wash. The A clock runs to two hours and ten minutes. The B clock stops after an hour and forty minutes. At that point A still needs another half hour.

The dryer on side A used four point nine eight kilowatt-hours. On B it used three point six six. The gross difference is one point three two kilowatt-hours. As a proportion of A's dryer consumption, that's about twenty-seven percent. The time reduction is about twenty-three percent. These percentages belong to this test table, not to every laundry room.

So far B wins the drying-stage contest. But I have a problem with my two clocks: I started each when its dryer was ready, even if one washer might have finished later. To find out which basket is ready first, I'll put the washer and dryer on one timeline. Imagine that B's washer cycle ends ten minutes later than A's. B still wins overall by twenty minutes. Move the B basket thirty minutes to the right and they tie. Move it forty minutes and A wins by ten. I haven't measured any of those washer delays; I'm moving the start time to find the exact point where the answer changes.

Now I can make a more useful decision about the spin dial. Suppose my clothes or my washer permit only twelve hundred revolutions a minute. In the seven-kilo table, that setting leaves four point two litres of water, takes a hundred and ten minutes in the dryer and uses four point zero two kilowatt-hours. I would still save twenty minutes and zero point nine six kilowatt-hours against the eight-hundred setting. If I can use fourteen hundred safely, the extra gain over twelve hundred is just ten dryer minutes and zero point three six kilowatt-hours. I can compare those smaller margins with the added washer time and energy, instead of treating the maximum speed as my only alternative.

Next I move the dial from fourteen hundred to sixteen hundred. The residual water in the published table falls from three and a half litres to three point four, and the drying time falls from a hundred minutes to ninety-eight. Just two minutes. The energy saving is two hundredths of a kilowatt-hour. If this extra step takes more than two additional washer minutes, it actually makes my whole job longer. If it costs more than those two hundredths in the washer, the total energy goes up. I would need very little extra effort from the washer to lose the final step's benefit. Those are break-even limits, not measured costs of spinning.

But is my thirty-minute result tied to one particular load? In the ETNA table both the six-kilo and eight-kilo comparisons also save thirty minutes when I move from eight hundred to fourteen hundred. The dryer electricity savings differ: one point two kilowatt-hours for six kilos and one point four nine for eight. That's what these rows say. I can't assume the same thirty minutes for an untested basket.

Suppose I move the same question to a different laundry room. A published table for a Siemens heat-pump dryer gives a nine-kilo cotton load a hundred and eighty-one minutes after an eight-hundred spin, and a hundred and thirty-three after fourteen hundred. Here the dryer saves forty-eight minutes, but only zero point seven six kilowatt-hours. Both are measurements for that dryer and that program. In the smaller four-and-a-half-kilo row, the savings are thirty minutes and zero point four three kilowatt-hours.

I'll put the two full-size comparison examples side by side, without pretending that the load sizes or dryers are identical. Imagine the same hypothetical extra washer delay of forty minutes in each case. The ETNA seven-kilo job would finish ten minutes later; the Siemens nine-kilo job would finish eight minutes earlier. The dryer winner is still the fast spin in both cases. The winner for the whole job can reverse. This is why I need my own washer delay and dryer figures rather than a single rule copied from another machine.

I can do the same test for electricity. Imagine an extra washer cost of zero point eight kilowatt-hours. How much sooner can I finish, and how much electricity can I actually save?

To put the energy difference in terms of money, I'll use a fictional flat rate of twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour. A different example: if the ETNA washer adds zero point two kilowatt-hours, the combined saving is one point one two. At my example rate that is twenty-eight cents per load, or twenty-eight dollars across a hundred comparable loads. That is a conditional calculation, not an actual bill or a measured annual saving.

How would I find my real numbers? I'd choose the same permitted wash program, weigh comparable loads before they are wet, and keep the fabric and drying program comparable. I'd record the finish time of the entire job and the energy of each machine, using its own reliable energy report or a suitable meter explicitly rated and permitted for that appliance. I wouldn't improvise electrical connections. I'd repeat both choices with the same filter condition and similar room conditions. If the second basket still needs drying after the program ends, I'd include the extra cycle rather than declare it the winner on the display alone.

Before any home comparison I'd check the care labels and the washer's permitted settings. I have no results for fabric damage, wrinkles, vibration or noise. If a gentle program is required, I'd leave that limit in place and compare only the permitted choices. I wouldn't make the fabrics pay for two minutes on a timer.

Here's what I'd do with the results. For the seven-kilo table, fourteen hundred beats eight hundred by thirty dryer minutes and one point three two kilowatt-hours. I'd try it with suitable fabric and measure the whole job. If twelve hundred is my limit, I still have a quantified alternative. And I wouldn't push to sixteen hundred merely because the dial goes that high. The time and energy I gain at the dryer have to be larger than what I spend at the washer. That's the decision I can take back to the laundry basket.
