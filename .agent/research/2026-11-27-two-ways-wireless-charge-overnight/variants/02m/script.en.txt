Cable or charging pad, my phone says one hundred percent. But which one pulls more electricity from the wall?

Tonight I have seven hours to charge an empty phone. A is a USB-C cable. B is a magnetic wireless charger. Same iPhone 15 Pro, same power adapter, same goal. Pick A or B for the lower electricity bill.

I'm modeling two nights from published meter readings, not running a new experiment. I count the energy at the socket for the entire day.

First, the cable. It takes just over two hours and uses 18.25 watt-hours to fill the battery. The wireless charger reaches the same result using 23.33. That's about 28 percent more energy.

But I'm asleep. I don't disconnect either charger as soon as the phone reaches a hundred percent.

For the rest of the night, both setups keep drawing a little electricity. In the morning I take the phone away. The cable setup stops drawing measurable power in this test, but the magnetic pad keeps checking for a device throughout the day.

Now I add everything up. About 24 watt-hours for the cable, 34 for the pad. The wireless setup uses roughly 45 percent more.

That sounds expensive. At an example electricity price of thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, though, repeating this exact day for a year adds only about a dollar and fifteen cents to the bill.

So the cable wins on electricity. I wouldn't replace a charger just to save that amount. And different chargers and charging habits can change the numbers.

The meter knows the difference. What does my phone tell me the next morning?
