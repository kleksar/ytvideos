I'm going to bed, my phone is empty, and there are two ways to charge it on the nightstand. I can plug in a cable, or I can put it down on a magnetic charging pad. I want to know how much that little convenience costs me in electricity. And I want to count what happens after the battery is full, because by then I'll probably be asleep.

A is a USB-C cable. B is a fifteen-watt magnetic wireless charger. I give both versions of the night seven hours, starting at zero percent on the same iPhone 15 Pro. Both have the same power adapter. I take the phone off in the morning and leave the charger plugged in. Which would you pick if the goal were the lowest electricity bill?

These are two simulated days based on an actual published charging test. I haven't measured these chargers myself. The daily totals are estimates built from the reported readings. I won't treat the power printed on a charger as the amount of energy it uses.

Here's why. A watt tells me the rate at which electricity is being used. A watt-hour tells me how much energy that adds up to over time. If one device pulls more power for a shorter time, it doesn't automatically cost more. I need the total from the meter in the wall socket.

In version A, electricity travels through the adapter and cable to the phone. Just over two hours later, the battery reaches one hundred percent. The meter has counted 18.25 watt-hours.

Now I reset the model to zero percent. I put the same phone on the magnetic charger. There are two coils: one in the pad, one in the phone. The pad creates a changing magnetic field, and the coil inside the phone picks up energy from it. The magnets help line them up. There are more steps between the socket and the battery, and some of the energy is dissipated along the way.

This time the reading is 23.33 watt-hours. Both screens show a full battery. The wireless setup has taken an extra five watt-hours, or about 28 percent more energy, to get there.

If I pulled the plug the moment the phone was charged, I could stop the comparison here. But my alarm won't go off for nearly five more hours.

The reported cable setup still draws around 1.1 watts with the full phone connected. The magnetic setup draws around 1.5. I add that to the energy it took to fill each battery. By the time I get up, I've used about 23.6 watt-hours on the cable and 30.7 on the pad.

Then I take the phone with me. The wireless pad is empty, but it still has a job. It periodically checks for a device. In the reported setup it averages a fifth of a watt while waiting. For seventeen hours, that adds another 3.4 watt-hours. The wired setup has no measurable additional draw in the reported test once the phone is disconnected.

My full-day scoreboard is now about 23.6 versus 34.1 watt-hours. The cable wins, and the wireless option takes roughly 45 percent more electricity.

I can see the percentage on a giant bar chart. But I can't pay an electricity bill with a percentage. I need to convert that extra energy to money.

Suppose I repeat the entire day, every day, for a year. That gives me about 3.84 extra kilowatt-hours with the pad. At an illustrative price of thirty cents for each kilowatt-hour, that's an additional dollar and fifteen cents per year. Not thirty percent of my household bill. Not a reason to throw away a working charger.

The same test also reported more energy for a different, deliberately misaligned pad. That is a different charger and a different placement, so I can't use it to tell how much one small movement costs on my own pad. But it explains why I shouldn't assume every wireless charger has the same result.

If my only goal is to use the least electricity in this modeled day, I choose A. If I value the convenience of B, the cost difference in this example is small. My actual bill depends on my equipment, how much I recharge and how long I leave it connected. The percentage is real arithmetic; the yearly bill is a scenario, not a measurement of my home.
