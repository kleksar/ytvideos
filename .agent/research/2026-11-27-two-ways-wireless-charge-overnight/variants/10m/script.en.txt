I have a charging cable and a wireless pad on my nightstand. In the morning, either one can leave me with a full phone. So what exactly am I paying for when I choose the pad? I want to know how much more electricity it needs, what happens while I'm asleep after the battery reaches a hundred percent, and whether the difference is worth worrying about when the bill arrives.

I'll compare two versions of the same day. In A, I plug a USB-C cable into an iPhone 15 Pro. In B, I attach a magnetic fifteen-watt wireless charger to that phone. The battery starts empty. I have the same wall adapter, the same seven hours before my alarm, and the same target: a full battery. Once I get up, I take the phone with me and leave the equipment plugged in for the next seventeen hours. The winner will be the method that draws the least energy from the socket during the day. Pick your side before I start adding the numbers.

I'm using published measurements from a comparison of these charging setups and applying them to a model of my routine. I didn't put a meter in my own wall socket. The amounts drawn up to a full charge come from the test; the rest of the day is an estimate based on the reported power readings. That distinction matters whenever I extend the result beyond the original charging session.

First, I need a scoreboard that makes sense. If my charger says fifteen watts, that doesn't mean I pay for fifteen watts every hour it's plugged in. Watts describe how fast energy is being drawn at a particular moment. Watt-hours describe how much energy accumulates over a period. A lamp using ten watts for two hours takes twenty watt-hours. A charger whose power rises and falls needs a meter that adds up all those changes. I care about the number at the socket, because some energy never reaches the phone.

In A, I connect the cable. The phone regulates its own charge. In the published test, the power drawn from the socket climbs, reaches around twenty watts when the battery is thirty percent full, then falls as the battery fills. After two hours and ten minutes the charge reaches one hundred percent. The meter total is 18.25 watt-hours. The battery has a nominal energy capacity of 12.7 watt-hours. I can't measure the exact stored energy simply by reading that label, but I can see why the nameplate capacity and the wall-meter reading are different.

In B, I reset the starting condition and let the magnets position the pad on the back of the phone. I can now lift the phone off the charger without pulling a connector out of the port. But the way electricity gets to the battery has changed. Inside the pad, current in a coil creates a changing magnetic field. A coil inside the phone receives energy from that field. Electronics in the phone convert that into a form the battery can use. The adapter and the circuitry still use some energy, and this extra transfer introduces further losses. Aligning the coils helps the transfer, but it doesn't make it lossless.

At a hundred percent, the wall-meter reading for B is 23.33 watt-hours. Subtract the cable's 18.25 and I have 5.08 extra watt-hours. Relative to the cable reading, that's about twenty-eight percent extra. Notice the denominator: if I ask what fraction of the wireless total I could save by choosing the cable, I divide by 23.33 instead. I get about twenty-two percent. Both answers describe the same two readings, but they answer different questions. If I saw a headline with either percentage, I'd want to know which comparison it meant.

I could make the first decision now. If I disconnected the equipment at the instant the battery filled, the cable would win by those five watt-hours. But for my overnight routine there are nearly five hours left. The full-battery icon isn't the same thing as a wall socket switched off.

In the wired test, the connected setup drew about 1.1 watts after the phone reached full charge. Over four hours and fifty minutes, that adds a little over five watt-hours. On the magnetic pad the reported draw was about 1.5 watts for the remaining four hours and fifty-five minutes. That adds about seven and a half watt-hours. So at breakfast, before I remove either phone, my modeled totals are about 23.6 and 30.7 watt-hours. The comparison has already changed. It isn't enough to compare the first charge and ignore the hours after it.

Then I take the phone away. The cable and the adapter remain at home. In the published setup the disconnected cable had no measurable draw. The wireless pad, however, still checks whether a compatible device has appeared. The tested magnetic pad averaged around 0.2 watts in this empty state. That is barely anything if I look at it for a minute. But I leave for seventeen hours, so it adds around 3.4 watt-hours. Now my estimate for a complete day is 23.6 watt-hours for A and 34.1 for B. About 45 percent more for the magnetic setup.

This time, I don't stop at the percentage. I imagine repeating the day 365 times. My cable would use about 8.6 kilowatt-hours in a year. The magnetic charger would use about 12.5. The difference is roughly 3.84 kilowatt-hours. At an assumed electricity price of thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, the extra charge on my bill is about a dollar and fifteen cents. At fifteen cents, it's about fifty-eight cents. At sixty cents, it's about two dollars and thirty-one cents. These are examples, not local tariffs. My electricity supplier charges me for energy, not for how dramatic the percentage looks on a graph.

There are two changes I can make without buying another charger. Suppose I keep the seven-hour night but switch off the equipment when I leave in the morning. Under this model the magnetic charger loses its seventeen hours of empty-pad consumption. The remaining difference is roughly seven watt-hours a day instead of ten and a half. Or suppose I disconnect both chargers as soon as the phone is full. The difference goes back to 5.08 watt-hours for the charging session. Both are conditional calculations, not measured daily routines. I can't assume I do either of them while I'm sleeping.

There's another reason not to apply my numbers to every charging pad. The published comparison also tried a different, nonmagnetic pad with the phone deliberately placed off center. The energy used to fill the battery was 33.93 watt-hours, and the charging session took nearly four hours. That result illustrates a possible poor setup, not the average wireless charger. Both the charger and its positioning changed. I cannot isolate the cost of a millimeter of misalignment from that comparison. The test also observed different battery temperatures. It did not measure how many months of battery life I would gain by changing methods, so I won't put a fictitious replacement cost on my scoreboard.

What if I wanted my own numbers? I would start with a plug-in energy meter capable of resolving small loads. I would use the same phone, adapter, starting charge and surroundings as far as possible, record the total energy at the same ending charge, and repeat each method instead of treating a single run as the truth. If I wanted to compare overnight charging, I would start and stop the meter at the same times and include the empty pad. I would check the meter's resolution before assuming a zero reading meant literally zero power. I would also record charging settings and whether I actually reached the target. Until then, my model has a very specific job: it explains the reported comparison, not every phone in my house.

The cable wins on energy in both the full-charge comparison and my modeled overnight day. The magnetic pad offers a different convenience: I can put the phone down and pick it up without dealing with the connector. In this example the price of that convenience in electricity is small, despite the sizable percentage. I can use less energy with a charger I already own; I don't have to buy a new one to chase a dollar of savings. And if my actual routine includes only short top-ups, charging limits, or a charger with a different standby draw, I need different readings before predicting the bill. When I see a full battery in the morning, I know exactly what that icon can't tell me: how much electricity it took to get there.
