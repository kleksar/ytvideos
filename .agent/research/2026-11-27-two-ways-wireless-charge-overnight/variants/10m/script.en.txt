My phone can be completely empty when I go to bed. By breakfast, a cable and a wireless charger can both give me a full battery. But how much more electricity am I paying for when I take the easier option? And if I leave the pad plugged in while I'm away at work, is it costing me money even when there's no phone on it?

I'm setting up two versions of the same day. In A, I plug a USB-C cable into an iPhone 15 Pro. In B, I attach Apple's fifteen-watt magnetic wireless charger to the same phone. The adapter is the same, the battery starts at zero, and I have seven hours before my alarm. At breakfast I take the phone away and leave the equipment connected for the next seventeen hours. I want the lowest total energy from the wall socket. Which side would you pick?

I'm building this comparison from published meter readings, not measuring two chargers in my own bedroom. The original testers modeled a full recharge every day without adaptive charging. That's an unusually demanding routine, so I'll keep it the same on both sides and see how changing my habits affects the result.

First I plug in the cable. In the published test, the meter briefly reaches around twenty watts, then the power drops as the battery fills. Two hours and ten minutes after the start, the phone is at a hundred percent. The wall meter has counted 18.25 watt-hours. That's energy, not the peak power of the charger. I write the number down and start the other version of the night from zero.

I attach the magnetic pad. The cable is still connected to a wall adapter, but it ends at the pad instead of going into the phone. The magnets position two coils close to each other. One coil produces a changing magnetic field; the other receives energy from that field. The phone then has to convert the received electricity into a form the battery can use. There are extra conversion steps, and the process loses energy along the way.

The battery reaches the same endpoint. This time the wall meter has counted 23.33 watt-hours. I have paid for just over five extra watt-hours to fill the same phone. Compared with the cable, that's about twenty-eight percent more. And the phone hasn't gained another twenty-eight percent of battery. Both screens say one hundred.

At this point I could stop and declare a winner. But both charging sessions finish a long time before my alarm. The cable finishes two hours and ten minutes into my modeled night, and the magnetic charger finishes after roughly two hours and five minutes. I don't get up to disconnect either one.

The published test reports a draw of about 1.1 watts for the full phone on the cable. My model adds just over five watt-hours during the remaining four hours and fifty minutes. On the magnetic charger the draw after a full charge is around 1.5 watts. Over the rest of the night that adds about seven and a half watt-hours. At breakfast I have used about 23.6 watt-hours with the cable and 30.7 with the pad. Even though both batteries have been full for hours, neither total has been standing still.

I take the phone with me when I leave. The cable setup has no additional measurable consumption in the published test. On the other side the pad is empty, but it periodically checks whether a compatible device is there. Its reported average draw is around a fifth of a watt. A single minute of that would barely register on my bill. Seventeen hours adds 3.4 watt-hours.

So when I come back at night, the modeled totals are 23.6 and 34.13 watt-hours. The pad has used around forty-five percent more than the cable. I can separate that difference into three parts: the five extra watt-hours to fill the battery, about two more while I slept, and the 3.4 while the pad sat empty. Nearly a third of the gap comes from a charger that has no phone to charge.

That percentage looks big on my scoreboard. But electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours, and I need to turn the difference into money. If I repeat these exact days for a year, the cable uses around 8.6 kilowatt-hours and the magnetic pad around 12.5. The extra energy is about 3.84 kilowatt-hours. At a hypothetical thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, the difference is a dollar and fifteen cents a year. If the price doubles, the difference becomes around two dollars and thirty-one cents. That's not a forty-five percent increase in my household electricity bill.

Now suppose I decide to keep the magnetic charger and change just one thing about my routine. Before going to work, I switch the equipment off at the wall. The empty pad no longer adds its seventeen-hour standby consumption. The two sides still need different amounts during the night, but the extra annual cost falls from about a dollar fifteen to seventy-eight cents at the same hypothetical rate. If I happened to unplug both as soon as the phones were full, the same calculation would give about fifty-six cents extra over a year of daily full charges. That last option isn't realistic if I'm asleep. I can make the morning change without setting an extra alarm.

There's a catch if I own a different pad. The testers also used a fifteen-watt nonmagnetic charger and deliberately placed the phone off center. It took nearly four hours to fill the battery. The wall meter recorded 33.93 watt-hours before the phone even came off the pad. For that setup, their modeled day reached 57.93 watt-hours, including ten watt-hours while the charger was empty. Against the original cable's 23.6, that would add about three dollars and seventy-six cents a year at the same example price. I'm not using it as a third competitor in my original A/B test. The hardware and the positioning both changed, and I can't assign that extra cost to one small movement of the phone. What it does show is why the number for my magnetic pad isn't a number for wireless charging in general.

The researchers also measured battery temperature, using a probe against the battery rather than estimating it from the surface of the case. The wired test peaked around thirty degrees Celsius; the magnetic test approached forty. The intentionally misaligned pad spent much of its charging session above forty. That's a separate observation from the energy totals. The researchers did not run a battery-lifetime trial. I don't know how much earlier, if at all, I'd have to replace my battery because of either charger, and adding a replacement bill to my electricity comparison would give a false impression of precision.

If I want to find out what my own charger actually costs, I can use the same idea without opening a phone. I'd put an energy meter at the socket, keep the phone and its charging settings the same, start both tests at the same charge level, and record the energy at the same ending charge. I'd repeat the comparison, because the phone may use electricity for other things and the room temperature can change. Then I'd measure another period with the phone removed to see whether my pad uses enough standby power for my meter to register it. A displayed zero on a meter with limited resolution doesn't guarantee that literally no power is being drawn. Until I have those readings, the most honest number I can use is the estimate for the particular equipment and routine I've just compared.

For this day, I pick the cable if electricity is the deciding factor. But I wouldn't buy a replacement for a magnetic pad that already works just to save a dollar a year. I can keep the convenience and turn off the pad when I'm not using it. The result could change in size if I top up instead of charging from empty or use different charging settings. What I can take away from this comparison is a way to judge both the energy and the actual cost, instead of trying to read either of them from the battery icon.
