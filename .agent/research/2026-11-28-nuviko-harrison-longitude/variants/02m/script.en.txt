How could a watch tell a sailor where his ship was? And why wasn't one successful watch enough to win the reward?

In the eighteenth century, finding your position north or south at sea was relatively straightforward. East or west was another matter. If you knew the time back home when the sun reached its highest point above your ship, the difference between the two times could tell you how far east or west you had sailed.

But you needed a clock that wouldn't lose its accuracy on a moving ship.

John Harrison, a carpenter who taught himself clockmaking, spent years building large sea clocks. Then a small watch made to his design worked so well that he changed direction.

His fourth timekeeper, H4, was finished in 1759. His son William took it across the Atlantic. On the way to Jamaica, the watch helped him predict landfall at Madeira before the crew expected it.

A second trial confirmed that H4 met the toughest accuracy requirement of Britain's longitude reward.

Yet the commissioners wanted something more: another maker had to be able to build a working version. Harrison believed he had already earned the money.

A watchmaker named Larcum Kendall made a copy, finished in 1769. Captain James Cook took that copy on his second Pacific voyage. After three years at sea, Cook praised its performance.

Harrison received further compensation from Parliament, but not the full reward he believed was owed.

The watch had answered one question. Kendall's copy answered another: the method could work even when Harrison wasn't the man who built the instrument.
