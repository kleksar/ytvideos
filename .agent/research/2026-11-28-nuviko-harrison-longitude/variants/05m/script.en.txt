Why would a captain want to buy a watch that was too big to fit comfortably in his pocket? And why, after proving it worked at sea, did its inventor still have to fight for his reward?

Imagine you're crossing the Atlantic in the eighteenth century. You haven't seen land for days. Your compass tells you which direction you're sailing, but it doesn't tell you how far west you've already travelled.

The sun can help. At local noon, the sun is at its highest point. Now imagine you have a watch that still shows the time at your departure port. If it reads three in the afternoon when the sun reaches its highest point at sea, you're about forty-five degrees west of your reference longitude. Every hour of difference corresponds to fifteen degrees.

The catch is keeping that watch accurate for weeks on a rolling ship.

In Britain, Parliament offered rewards for a reliable way of finding longitude in 1714. A carpenter named John Harrison took on the problem. He had taught himself to make clocks and built instruments remarkably accurate on land.

His first marine clock had two balances that swung in opposite directions. In 1736 he took it on a voyage to Lisbon. Coming back to England, the officers identified a headland as the Start. Harrison told them it was the Lizard, farther west. He was right. The ship was about sixty miles away from where the officers thought it was.

The commissioners gave him money to develop the idea. He built a second clock, found a flaw and didn't take it to sea. He then spent nineteen years working on a third. It still couldn't deliver the accuracy he wanted.

Meanwhile, he had asked the London watchmaker John Jefferys to make him a much smaller watch to a new design. The result suggested that Harrison had been approaching the problem on the wrong scale.

Instead of trying to perfect another large sea clock, he began work on H4. It looked like an oversized pocket watch, but its fast-moving balance helped it resist the disturbances that made ordinary watches unreliable. He finished it in 1759.

Two years later, his son William took H4 aboard a ship bound for Jamaica. On the way, he predicted that they would reach Madeira sooner than the crew expected. The captain was impressed enough to ask to buy the family's next timekeeper.

Back in England, the commissioners decided one voyage wasn't enough.

They arranged a further trial, this time to Barbados. Astronomical observations provided a way to establish the island's longitude independently of the watch.

When the commissioners considered the result in 1765, H4 had met the strictest accuracy standard in the original act. But the terms for getting the money now included two separate steps. Harrison could receive the first ten thousand pounds after explaining how H4 worked. To get the remaining portion, it had to be shown that other craftsmen could build timekeepers like his.

From the Harrisons' point of view, they had done what the original offer demanded. From the commissioners' point of view, a navigation method that depended on one man's workshop wasn't yet ready for general use.

The original H4 underwent more testing at Greenwich. Its performance there was disappointing, and Harrison disputed the results.

Then came a test of the design rather than just the original instrument. Larcum Kendall, another watchmaker, finished a close copy of H4 in 1769. The copy became known as K1.

Captain James Cook carried K1 on his second Pacific expedition, from 1772 to 1775. It earned his confidence over a voyage lasting years rather than weeks.

Meanwhile Harrison, who had also built a fifth timekeeper with his son, secured additional compensation through Parliament. He didn't get everything he believed he was entitled to.

Kendall had demonstrated that another man could build a successful marine watch. That didn't mean every captain could afford one. Other makers would have to simplify the design before these instruments could spread widely.

Harrison's accomplishment was the breakthrough. The copy turned it into a method other craftsmen could take forward.
