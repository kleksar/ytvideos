Why would a sailor need to carry the time from a city thousands of miles away? Why did John Harrison abandon a clock he'd worked on for nineteen years in favour of something that looked like a pocket watch? And after his invention proved accurate at sea, why did the people offering him a reward ask for another watch?

Imagine an eighteenth-century ship approaching a coastline. The crew know the direction they've been sailing and have an estimate of how far they've travelled. They've also been observing the sky to work out their position north or south. But when they see a headland, they still have to decide which headland it is.

Two places on roughly the same latitude can lie a long way apart. A compass needle won't tell the crew which of them they're approaching. And their estimate of distance may have accumulated errors from the movement of the water and changes in their course.

There is another way to get at the answer. The Earth turns through a full circle in roughly twenty-four hours. That's fifteen degrees in one hour. Two observers at different longitudes see the sun reach its highest point at different times.

Suppose your reference is a port in England. When the sun reaches its highest point where you are, imagine that it's already three in the afternoon back at the port. In this simplified solar-time example, the difference tells you you're forty-five degrees farther west. There's no need for the coastline to be in view.

But what if the instrument carrying the reference time has gained or lost two minutes? That's half a degree of longitude, or thirty nautical miles at the equator. And a mechanical clock has to survive weeks of travel before you even know how much its reading has drifted.

In 1714 Britain offered rewards for finding longitude reliably at sea. One of the people who took up the challenge wasn't a sailor or an astronomer. John Harrison was a carpenter who had taught himself clockmaking. By the middle of the 1720s, he had already built clocks that were unusually accurate on land.

He went to London, where the astronomer Edmond Halley directed him to the respected clockmaker George Graham. Harrison needed people who could assess his work, and eventually the support to take a new machine to sea.

His first marine timekeeper, H1, was a large device. A pendulum clock that hung quietly on a wall was not a good starting point for a ship pitching in the waves. Harrison used two connected balances whose movements opposed one another.

In 1736 he took H1 on a voyage to Lisbon. The outward journey didn't start well for the clock, but it became more reliable by the time they arrived. On a different ship for the return journey, Harrison got an opportunity to demonstrate what it could do.

As they approached England, the officers thought they had identified the Start. Harrison's calculations put them at the Lizard instead. His identification was correct. The officers' estimate was off by around sixty miles.

The experiment gave the commissioners a reason to support further work. They agreed on five hundred pounds, half to be paid upfront, and Harrison promised a new instrument within two years.

He built H2 within that period but discovered a fundamental flaw. It never went to sea for a trial. In 1740 he began H3, a new large timekeeper. He worked on it for nineteen years, making adjustments and developing new solutions to mechanical problems. Yet he couldn't get the accuracy he wanted.

During those years something smaller had started to look promising. He commissioned the London watchmaker John Jefferys to build a pocket watch to a new design. The watch was made in 1753. Its performance made Harrison reconsider the kind of machine he needed.

A large clock wasn't the only way to build a good reference for navigation. Harrison began work on H4 in 1755. Four years later, the result looked remarkably like a large pocket watch. Its dial was about ten centimetres across. Inside, a fast, energetic balance oscillated at a rate that produced five audible ticks a second.

In 1761 his son William took this watch on a ship bound for Jamaica. On the way to the Caribbean, he used it to predict when they would reach Madeira. He expected the island sooner than the crew did. The captain was impressed enough to ask if he could buy the next timekeeper they made.

After that voyage the commissioners still wanted another test. And Harrison was no longer the only person with a serious proposal.

Astronomers had been working on a method involving the moon. As the moon moves across the sky, its angular distance from the sun or certain stars changes. With sufficiently accurate tables, a navigator could compare that measured distance with a predicted distance and infer the reference time. Then the navigator could compare it with local time to find longitude.

This method needed difficult observations and calculations, but it offered a way to get the reference time without carrying a costly mechanical timekeeper.

For the next trial, the destination was Barbados. Astronomer Nevil Maskelyne was sent ahead to establish its longitude using observations of Jupiter's moons. These observations from land provided a reference against which the different methods could be assessed. William arrived with H4 in 1764.

In February 1765, the commissioners confirmed that Harrison's watch had satisfied the most demanding accuracy requirement set by the original act. Now there was a dispute about what counted as delivering a solution.

Under the new terms, Harrison could receive ten thousand pounds once he explained the principles of his watch. The remaining amount, with earlier payments deducted, depended on showing that other makers could produce timekeepers like his.

The Harrisons believed they had already earned the reward and that the rules had changed unfairly. The commissioners wanted an invention that others could make, not merely a single successful machine. Their recommendations became law in May 1765.

It wasn't the end of the testing. H4 was handed to Maskelyne in a sealed box in 1766. He tested it at Greenwich for ten months. The results weren't good, and Harrison challenged them. The controversy cannot be settled by pretending the disappointing measurements never happened, or by assuming that disagreement proves deliberate interference.

A different kind of evidence was on its way. The commissioners had commissioned Larcum Kendall, an experienced watchmaker, to reproduce Harrison's design. Kendall finished the instrument in 1769. He had made a close copy, not just a case that resembled the original.

The finished watch cost hundreds of pounds. It received the name K1. In 1772 it went to sea aboard Captain James Cook's ship on his second Pacific expedition. The test would run for years.

During this period Harrison and his son had made another watch, H5. It was tested with the involvement of King George the Third. Harrison eventually secured further compensation through Parliament, although it fell short of what he believed was owed to him.

In 1775 Cook returned from his expedition and spoke highly of Kendall's watch. The instrument that had proved itself over those years was not the original H4. Another craftsman had made it.

There was still a practical barrier. A captain couldn't simply order an inexpensive clock from the nearest workshop. Later watchmakers had to develop simpler designs and cheaper ways to make them. Astronomical methods also remained useful; the choice was not an overnight victory of clocks over the sky.

What Harrison had done was turn a question about position into a question about time that could be answered by a machine on board a ship. Kendall's voyage-tested copy showed that the answer didn't depend forever on one man's hands.

It had taken a generation to move from the first large sea clock to a watch that another maker could reproduce. Getting reliable time into the hands of ordinary navigators was the next achievement.
