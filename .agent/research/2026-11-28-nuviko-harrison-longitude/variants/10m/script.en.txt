Why did a captain need to know what time it was thousands of miles away? How could a carpenter build a clock that helped him find his position at sea? And why, even after the clock worked, did another man have to build it again?

Imagine approaching the coast of England in 1736. You've kept track of your course and estimated the distance you've travelled. From the sky, you can work out your latitude. But currents and changes of course can throw off your estimate of longitude. When a headland appears, you still have to identify it.

That was a real problem on a ship returning from Lisbon. The officers identified the headland as the Start. A passenger named John Harrison told them it was the Lizard, much farther west. He was right. They were about sixty miles from the position the officers had estimated. His evidence came from a large clock he had brought aboard.

Here's why that clock mattered. The Earth turns through a full circle in roughly twenty-four hours. When the sun is at its highest point above your ship, it's local noon. Suppose your clock still shows the time at an English port, where it's already three in the afternoon. In this simplified solar-time example, you're forty-five degrees west of the port. Every hour of difference corresponds to fifteen degrees of longitude.

But what if the instrument carrying the reference time has gained or lost two minutes? That's half a degree of longitude, or thirty nautical miles at the equator. And a mechanical clock has to survive weeks of travel before you even know how much its reading has drifted.

That was the problem Britain had offered rewards for solving in 1714. Long before the Lisbon voyage, Harrison had taught himself to make clocks. He was a carpenter by trade, and by the middle of the 1720s his clocks on land were remarkably accurate. But to win the money he needed to carry that accuracy onto a ship.

He went to London, where the astronomer Edmond Halley directed him to the respected clockmaker George Graham. Harrison needed people who could assess his work, and eventually the support to take a new machine to sea.

His first marine timekeeper, H1, was a large device. A pendulum clock that hung quietly on a wall was not a good starting point for a ship pitching in the waves. Harrison used two connected balances whose movements opposed one another.

The first sea trial of H1 hadn't been an immediate success. It ran poorly on the outward journey in 1736 and improved by Lisbon. The clock was moved to a different ship for the return. That was the voyage on which Harrison corrected the officers' identification of the headland.

The experiment gave the commissioners a reason to support further work. They agreed on five hundred pounds, half to be paid upfront, and Harrison promised a new instrument within two years.

He built H2 within that period but discovered a fundamental flaw. It never went to sea for a trial. In 1740 he began H3, a new large timekeeper. He worked on it for nineteen years, making adjustments and developing new solutions to mechanical problems. Yet he couldn't get the accuracy he wanted.

During those years something smaller had started to look promising. He commissioned the London watchmaker John Jefferys to build a pocket watch to a new design. The watch was made in 1753. Its performance made Harrison reconsider the kind of machine he needed.

In 1755 Harrison began H4. When he finished four years later, it looked like an oversized pocket watch, with a dial about ten centimetres across. Its large, rapidly oscillating balance produced five ticks a second. The new arrangement made it less susceptible to a ship's movement. After years of working on large machines, Harrison now had something his son could carry across the Atlantic.

In 1761 his son William took this watch on a ship bound for Jamaica. On the way to the Caribbean, he used it to predict when they would reach Madeira. He expected the island sooner than the crew did. The captain was impressed enough to ask if he could buy the next timekeeper they made.

The commissioners wanted another trial. Back in Britain the argument spilled out of meeting rooms: Harrison's supporters defended his case in newspapers and pamphlets. Meanwhile, astronomers had developed a rival way to find the reference time.

Instead of carrying that time on a clock, you could measure the angular distance between the moon and a star, or the moon and the sun. Compare it with the values in astronomical tables and you can work out the reference time. The calculations were demanding, but the method mattered even to sailors who would later carry watches.

For the next trial, the destination was Barbados. Astronomer Nevil Maskelyne was sent ahead to establish its longitude using observations of Jupiter's moons. These observations from land provided a reference against which the different methods could be assessed. William arrived with H4 in 1764.

In February 1765, the commissioners confirmed that Harrison's watch had satisfied the most demanding accuracy requirement set by the original act. Now there was a dispute about what counted as delivering a solution.

Under the new terms, Harrison could receive ten thousand pounds once he explained the principles of his watch. The remaining amount, with earlier payments deducted, depended on showing that other makers could produce timekeepers like his.

The Harrisons believed they had already earned the reward and that the rules had changed unfairly. The commissioners wanted an invention that others could make, not merely a single successful machine. Their recommendations became law in May 1765.

In 1766 the astronomer Nevil Maskelyne received H4 in a locked box with three seals. He took it to Greenwich and tested it for ten months. When the results were published, they showed disappointing performance. Harrison challenged them. The original watch was still at the centre of a dispute, even as the Board was arranging a different sort of test.

The Board had commissioned a watchmaker named Larcum Kendall to build a copy of H4 in 1766. He completed it three years later. From the front, the two watches look remarkably similar. But the inscription on the copy's mechanism names Kendall and gives the year 1769. It was a new instrument made by a different craftsman, using Harrison's design.

The finished watch cost hundreds of pounds. It received the name K1. In 1772 it went to sea aboard Captain James Cook's ship on his second Pacific expedition. The test would run for years.

During this period Harrison and his son had made another watch, H5. It was tested with the involvement of King George the Third. Harrison eventually secured further compensation through Parliament, although it fell short of what he believed was owed to him.

On Cook's expedition the watch was tested against astronomical observations made by William Wales. In New Zealand in November 1774, Cook recorded just how much the answer depended on using an up-to-date estimate of the watch's daily error. Based on the rate observed there almost a year earlier, the discrepancy would have been nearly twenty minutes of time, or almost five degrees of longitude.

Cook also recorded a much smaller discrepancy for the recent leg, using rates measured at more recent stops. The watch could carry a reference between observations. The sky could help check how that reference had changed. The two approaches could now work together.

In April 1775 Cook left the Cape of Good Hope for a small island in the Atlantic, St Helena. He wrote that his confidence in Kendall's watch had led him to try a direct course. The ship sailed through days of weak winds. On the morning of the fifteenth of May the island came into view.

Cook compared the watch's estimate of the difference in longitude between Cape Town and the island with observations made on land. The two results differed by only about two miles in his account. And he pointed to the accuracy he could achieve by combining lunar observations with a good watch. The copy had become an instrument a captain could actually navigate with.

The voyage was over in 1775. But building another watch as elaborate as K1 still cost hundreds of pounds. Later craftsmen, including John Arnold and Thomas Earnshaw, worked towards simpler designs that could be produced in larger numbers. Widespread use took time.

Think back to that ship approaching England. Harrison had been the passenger telling the officers that they were looking at the wrong headland. Four decades later, Cook set out for a distant island relying on a watch made by someone else. The knowledge that had once depended on Harrison's workshop could now travel with the ship, even when Harrison stayed ashore.
