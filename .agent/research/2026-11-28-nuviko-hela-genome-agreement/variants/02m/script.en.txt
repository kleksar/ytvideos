In 2013, scientists put the DNA sequence of a dead woman's cancer cells online. Her grandchildren had reason to be concerned.

Sixty-two years earlier, Henrietta Lacks had been treated for cervical cancer at a hospital in Baltimore. A sample of her tumor went to a laboratory without her consent.

Growing human cells outside the body was notoriously difficult. Her cells kept dividing. Scientists could send some to another laboratory and grow more from the original culture.

They called the cells HeLa. Henrietta died that October, at thirty-one. The cells went on to help researchers study diseases and test vaccines.

Her family did not know what had happened to them for years. When scientists eventually approached relatives for blood samples, the family had trouble understanding what the researchers wanted.

Then came a new kind of experiment. Scientists could read the genetic material inside the cells, turn it into digital information, and share it across the world.

The cancer had changed Henrietta's DNA, and decades of growth in laboratories had changed it further. But parts of that DNA could still reveal information about the family she had left behind.

The researchers withdrew their public data. Another team held back a study.

The National Institutes of Health met with the family. They could keep the genetic data closed, make it public again, or let researchers apply for access.

They chose the third option.

Two family members joined a six-person group reviewing requests. Scientists could continue their work under conditions meant to protect the family's privacy.

The agreement did not give them ownership of every HeLa cell. It did give them a part in decisions about the genetic data.

What finally brought the family into that conversation was one more decision made without them.
