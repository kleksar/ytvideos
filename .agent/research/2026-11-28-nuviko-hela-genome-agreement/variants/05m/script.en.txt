How could a sample taken from a patient in 1951 become a privacy problem for her grandchildren more than sixty years later? And why did scientists take one of the most useful sets of genetic data in medicine off the internet?

Henrietta Lacks was a mother of five. In 1951, she went to Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, where a doctor found a tumor on her cervix. She received radiation treatment. During her medical care, tissue from the tumor was sent to George Gey's research laboratory. She had not agreed to that use of her tissue.

Gey had been trying to grow human cells outside the body. The idea was to have living material that scientists could study in a laboratory without repeatedly obtaining tissue from patients. But many samples stopped growing. The cells from Henrietta's tumor behaved differently. Given suitable laboratory conditions, their population could double in about a day.

That meant a small culture could supply another laboratory without the original one giving up all its cells. The new laboratory could grow more and send them on again. The cells were called HeLa, after the first letters of Henrietta's names.

Henrietta died of cancer in October 1951. She was thirty-one. The cells became a research tool that outlived her. Scientists used them in studies of the virus that causes polio, among countless other experiments.

But the success of HeLa created an unusual problem. The cells could spread into other cultures in laboratories. In the nineteen-sixties, genetic markers suggested that a number of apparently different human cell lines were actually contaminated with HeLa. Scientists needed better ways to tell one culture from another.

In the nineteen-seventies, researchers approached Henrietta's relatives for blood samples. Comparing inherited characteristics could help identify the cells. For the family, however, the purpose of the blood collection was poorly communicated. They were learning about research that had been going on without their knowledge for more than two decades.

The next major change came when scientists could read the DNA in a cell. A cell culture was now also a potential source of information about the person from whom it came.

In March 2013, a research team in Europe published the sequence of a HeLa genome and made the data publicly available.

A person did not have to be sent a tube of cells anymore. They could obtain a digital description of the cells' genetic material.

Henrietta's cancer cells were not identical to her healthy cells. Their DNA had changed during the disease and as the cells grew over many years. That limited what anyone could infer about her original genetic makeup. But it did not remove the inherited information altogether. And inherited DNA connects a person to their biological relatives. Henrietta had died, but members of her family were still alive.

After concerns were raised, the European researchers removed their publicly accessible data. A separate American research team had sequenced another HeLa culture. Its study was also held back while a solution was discussed.

Francis Collins, then director of the National Institutes of Health, met with the Lacks family. There were three broad options: keep the data private, put it back in the open, or give researchers access under specific conditions.

Closing the data would restrict valuable research. Making it public again would leave the family's concerns unresolved. The middle option required a way to decide who could use it.

In August 2013, NIH announced the agreement. Researchers could request access to the full genome data. A six-person working group would review those requests. Two of the six seats were for members of the Lacks family. The group would make recommendations within the NIH approval process.

Researchers receiving the data had to follow conditions on its use and sharing. Their resulting publications would recognize Henrietta and her family's contribution.

The agreement was not a transfer of ownership of HeLa cells. It did not require family permission for every experiment with the cells, and it did not provide financial compensation. Its focus was the use of full genome data under the NIH arrangement.

What had started with tissue leaving a hospital room without a patient's consent had become an international scientific resource. The 2013 decision could not undo the way that resource had been created. But for the first time, Henrietta's descendants had a formal place in deciding how this particularly sensitive kind of information would be shared.
