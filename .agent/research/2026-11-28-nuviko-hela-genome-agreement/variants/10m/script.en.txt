What happens when a piece of a person's body becomes a scientific resource used all over the world? How much can researchers learn about the relatives of someone who died decades ago? In 2013, a set of genetic data was taken offline while a family and one of America's largest medical research agencies worked out an answer.

The story begins at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore in 1951. Henrietta Lacks, a Black mother of five, came to the hospital with unusual bleeding. A doctor found a malignant tumor on her cervix. She began treatment with radiation, which was an established treatment for that cancer at the time.

A small sample of her tumor went to a research laboratory run by George Gey. Henrietta had not given consent for the sample to be used in research. The medical care she received and the scientific work on the tissue were two different things. The second would continue long after the first had ended.

Gey's problem was a basic one. How could a researcher keep human cells alive and growing outside a person's body? He wanted to observe cells, change the conditions they grew in, and see what happened. If each batch stopped growing after a short time, the laboratory would constantly need new material. An experiment begun in one place would also be difficult to repeat with exactly the same starting material somewhere else.

Then the laboratory found that the cells from Henrietta's tumor kept multiplying. Their numbers could double in roughly twenty to twenty-four hours. Instead of using up a limited sample, the researchers could maintain a population of cells and start new cultures from it.

The name they gave the cells, HeLa, came from Henrietta's first and last names. It was a practical way of labeling a culture. The name soon appeared in laboratories far from the hospital where the tissue had been obtained.

Henrietta herself was becoming more seriously ill. She died in October 1951 at thirty-one. The cells in the laboratory were descendants of cells from her cancer; they were not a living copy of Henrietta as a person. Her death did not stop them from growing.

There was a useful consequence for scientists. Imagine two laboratories studying how a virus affects human cells. If they start with cultures from the same line, each can test its ideas without having to find another person from whom to obtain a tissue sample. One laboratory can send some of its culture to another, and both can keep growing it.

HeLa became widely distributed. In particular, it was used in research connected with the development and testing of the polio vaccine. The hospital describes its own distribution of the cells as free of charge. The research material traveled far beyond its original laboratory.

The same ability to grow could produce trouble. Cells do not carry visible name tags. If some from one culture get into another, researchers may be looking at something different from what the label on the container says.

In the nineteen-sixties, geneticist Stanley Gartler compared inherited markers in twenty human cell lines. The striking similarities he reported supported the possibility that apparently different cultures had been contaminated with HeLa. The problem was not that all experiments with HeLa were bad. It was that an experiment with mislabeled cells might no longer answer the question the researcher thought it was answering.

To improve the identification of the cells, researchers in the nineteen-seventies sought blood samples from members of Henrietta's family. A person's relatives may carry some of the same inherited characteristics. Those shared features could help scientists work out which cultures contained HeLa cells.

The family, however, did not have the same understanding of the situation as the scientists who were collecting the blood. They had not been properly informed about the original use of Henrietta's tissue. The purpose of the new blood samples was also poorly communicated. To understand a scientific identification procedure, they first had to understand that material from Henrietta's body was still being used in laboratories.

This was a consequence of the way the research had begun. Decades of work could build on a sample that a patient had never agreed to give for that purpose. When scientists eventually needed information from her relatives, the missing conversation became important again.

In 2010, writer Rebecca Skloot published a book about Henrietta and her family. By then, the public could put a full name and a family history to the letters HeLa. Researchers had long recognized the cells. Now many people outside science learned whose tissue they had come from.

Meanwhile, another scientific change was taking place. Laboratories had acquired techniques for determining the order of the chemical letters that make up DNA. Once that sequence was recorded on a computer, parts of it could be searched and compared without having to repeat the entire laboratory procedure.

For a cancer researcher, the genome of a cell line could be an extremely useful reference. If an experiment depended on a particular gene, knowing the genetic characteristics of the cells could help explain the result. A sequence could be made available to many researchers, just as the original living cultures had been.

But DNA is also inherited. A sequence from one person can contain clues about their parents, children, and other relatives. And in this case, the source of the cells was no longer anonymous.

In March 2013, a European research team published work describing a HeLa genome. They made the sequence available to the public. Anyone with the relevant access to the public resource could obtain the information without asking a member of the Lacks family.

There is an important distinction here. The genome of a HeLa culture is not a perfect record of the DNA Henrietta was born with. Cancer alters genetic material, and cells can change further as they multiply in laboratories. Different HeLa cultures can have differences. Reading the tumor cell sequence does not automatically reveal a relative's medical future.

Nevertheless, some of the underlying inherited information can survive those changes. The sequence could provide clues that researchers might combine with other information. And the consequences of publishing it would no longer be limited to someone who had died in 1951.

Concerns were raised about the family's privacy. The European team removed its data from public access. A different group of researchers in the United States, working on another HeLa genome, also held back publication while the question was being addressed.

It would have been possible to leave the cells exactly where they were and discuss the future of this new kind of information separately. There was no need to destroy a culture in order to change who could obtain a digital sequence.

Francis Collins, the director of the National Institutes of Health, met relatives of Henrietta Lacks several times between April and July. One option was not to make the genome data available to other researchers at all. Another was to release it without restrictions. The third was controlled access.

The first option would protect against disclosure through the new resource, but scientists would lose an important reference. The second would preserve the convenience of an open scientific database but would not address the family's concerns. The third needed a practical procedure and people to oversee it.

The family agreed to the controlled-access approach. In August, NIH announced the new arrangement. Researchers who wanted the full data could submit a request for a particular biomedical research purpose. A six-member working group, including scientists, a bioethicist and two members of the family, would review the requests and make recommendations. The ultimate approval remained within NIH.

There were conditions. Researchers could not freely pass the data to others. They were to respect the family's privacy rather than contact relatives in connection with the data. Publications using it were to recognize Henrietta and her family's contribution.

The arrangement covered the two studies involved in the negotiations. NIH also expected researchers it funded who produced further full HeLa genome sequences to put the data into the controlled resource; researchers outside that funding relationship were encouraged to participate.

It was not a worldwide ban on sequencing HeLa cells. The agency could not impose its own funding rules on every laboratory on Earth. The family did not receive a veto over every cell experiment. Nor was the agreement a payment or a settlement of who owned the physical cells. These limits matter because the controversy over the original sample was much larger than the decision about two digital datasets.

The agreement was a specific response to a problem that did not exist in 1951 in the same form: it had become possible to make a known person's genetic information widely accessible in digital form, with potential consequences for people who shared some of that DNA.

The story of HeLa is often told through the extraordinary success of the cells. But another story runs alongside it. Scientists created a tool that could be used again and again, while the people connected to the original patient had little say in its use. In 2013, the research could continue, but a decision about access to a particular kind of data now had to take the family's interests into account.

The cells still bore the first letters of Henrietta Lacks's name. At the table where access to the genome was considered, two of the seats were finally occupied by members of her family.
