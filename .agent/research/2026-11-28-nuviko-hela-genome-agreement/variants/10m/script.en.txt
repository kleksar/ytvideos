Why would the publication of a dead woman's cancer genome affect people who were alive more than sixty years after her death? Why would two research teams put their work on hold over the same family? The answer begins with a hospital sample and ends with six people reviewing requests to use its genetic data.

In 1951, Henrietta Lacks, a thirty-one-year-old mother of five, went to Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore because of unusual bleeding. Doctors found a tumor on her cervix and began radiation treatment. During her care, a sample of the tumor was sent to George Gey's laboratory. Henrietta had never agreed to that use of her tissue.

Gey was trying to solve a problem that had frustrated researchers for years. Human cells taken out of the body often stopped growing before scientists could finish studying them. Henrietta's tumor cells kept dividing. In suitable conditions, their numbers could double in about a day. A laboratory could start a new culture from a little of the existing one without using up the original.

The laboratory called the cells HeLa, after the first letters of her first and last names. Henrietta died in October of that year. As the cells were distributed, one of the uses scientists found for them was research into the virus that causes polio. The hospital says it supplied cells to researchers free of charge. Other laboratories could grow their own cultures and use them again.

Then, in March 2013, a research team in Germany published a sequence of the DNA in HeLa cells and put the data in a public database. The Lacks family had not been consulted. Relatives learned about the publication through the writer Rebecca Skloot. The data were subsequently taken down. And there was already another paper, from a team in Seattle, waiting to be published.

The two projects were caught in a dispute over who ought to decide how the information was shared. HeLa had been traveling from laboratory to laboratory for decades. Why was a file on a computer suddenly a problem? The answer has something to do with another experiment, carried out long before anyone had posted the genome online.

As HeLa cultures spread in the nineteen-fifties and sixties, scientists discovered a disturbing side to their ability to grow. A few cells could accidentally find their way into a different culture and multiply. A researcher might then conduct an experiment on cells that did not match the name on the container.

In the nineteen-sixties, the geneticist Stanley Gartler compared inherited markers in twenty supposedly different human cell lines. The similarities he reported suggested that a number of these cultures had been contaminated with HeLa. A laboratory studying the wrong cells might reach conclusions about a tissue it was not actually testing. Researchers needed a better way to establish which cells they had.

In the nineteen-seventies, researchers sought blood samples from Henrietta's relatives. Some inherited features of her cells might also be present in the blood of her family members. Comparing them could help identify HeLa. The family, however, had not been properly informed that Henrietta's cells were still growing in laboratories, or why new samples were being collected. A procedure that made sense to the researchers was difficult for the relatives to understand.

By 2010, Skloot had published a book about Henrietta and her descendants. For many people, the label on a laboratory culture now had a name and a family attached to it. And as genetic sequencing advanced, a laboratory no longer had to send a living culture to share some of the most useful information about its cells. It could send a digital sequence.

There was a good reason to do that. If two researchers were using HeLa to study the effects of a drug, the genetic characteristics of their cells could help them understand the results. The sequence could serve as a reference that other laboratories could consult without repeating the whole sequencing procedure.

But part of a person's DNA is shared with their biological relatives. Some of that inherited material can still be present in a tumor. Cancer changes DNA, and so does the long growth of cells in laboratories. HeLa cultures can differ from one another, so their genome is not a complete, unchanged record of Henrietta's healthy DNA. Nor could reading it tell someone everything about the health of her descendants. Yet it could contain clues about them, and the public knew which family the cells came from.

That was the dilemma in March. A scientific database had been made available to people who had no reason to speak to the Lacks family first. Removing the German team's data from public view did not solve everything, because the American project had also sequenced HeLa. It would need to deal with essentially the same problem before its own paper could appear.

Between April and July, Francis Collins, then the director of the National Institutes of Health, met the family three times. He later described the relatives' questions about what the sequence could reveal about Henrietta and about themselves. The NIH offered to put them in touch with genetics specialists. Collins also reported that the family valued the medical advances made possible by HeLa. The issue was how to continue that work while taking their privacy seriously.

There was a choice to make. The data could go back into a public database, available for research without anyone's special permission. That would leave the relatives with very little ability to influence further use. Or the data could be kept entirely closed. That would deprive researchers of a useful reference, without stopping other laboratories from generating their own sequences from cells already in circulation.

There was a third possibility. Researchers could explain what they intended to study and apply to use the full genome data under particular conditions. That meant someone had to read the applications, weigh the proposed work against the rules, and recommend whether access should be granted. The family agreed to this approach.

On August seventh, 2013, the NIH announced the arrangement. A new six-member working group would review applications. At the announcement, three relatives represented the Lacks family. Two of them, Henrietta's grandson David Lacks Junior and her great-granddaughter Veronica Spencer, would serve on the group. They would be involved alongside scientific and ethics representatives; the final approval decision remained within the NIH.

An applicant would be expected to explain the biomedical purpose of the study, observe conditions on sharing the data, and avoid contacting family members in connection with the information. Publications using the data were to recognize Henrietta and her family's contribution. The rules concerned the two genome projects at the center of the dispute, and NIH-funded researchers producing additional full HeLa sequences were expected to use the controlled database.

Within weeks, the working group had actual requests to consider. On September sixteenth, the NIH approved the first uses of the data. The group had received six applications. It recommended approval for four and requested additional information about the other two. For the first time under the new arrangement, the question of who could obtain the data was being answered one research proposal at a time.

The family had not acquired ownership of every HeLa culture. A laboratory could still conduct ordinary experiments with its cells, and the NIH could not impose its funding conditions on every scientist worldwide. The August agreement contained no financial compensation for the family's loss of control over the original tissue. Other disputes about that tissue were outside its scope.

Sixty-two years earlier, a laboratory had received tissue from Henrietta Lacks without her consent. Later, researchers had collected blood from her relatives without clearly explaining what they were doing. By September 2013, scientists seeking access to the two genome datasets were submitting proposals for review. Two of the people on the new working group were members of the very family that had once been left out of the conversation.
