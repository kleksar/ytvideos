The first clue to a spinning dead star was a mark barely a quarter of an inch long.

In 1967, Cambridge graduate student Jocelyn Bell was searching for distant objects called quasars. She had helped build the radio telescope herself. Every day, it produced rolls of paper covered in traces of radio signals.

Among the familiar interference, Bell noticed something she couldn't identify. It kept appearing when the telescope was looking at the same part of the sky. Her supervisor, Antony Hewish, wanted a clearer recording.

Bell increased the speed of the chart recorder whenever that patch of sky came into view. For a month, she caught nothing useful. Then the signal returned. The pen recorded a series of pulses, about one point three seconds apart.

The next day, Hewish saw the same pattern. Another telescope picked up the signal too. Further measurements indicated that it had traveled through space. But what could send radio waves so regularly?

Bell found a second source in a completely different direction. Its pulses had a different rhythm. She went on to find two more.

The explanation turned out to be extraordinary without requiring anyone to be broadcasting a message. A neutron star, the dense remains of a collapsed star, can spin while emitting beams of radiation. When a beam sweeps across Earth, we detect a pulse, much like seeing a lighthouse flash from the shore.

These objects became known as pulsars. In 1974, Hewish shared a Nobel Prize with Martin Ryle; Bell was not among the recipients. In 2018, she received a three-million-dollar physics prize of her own and announced that she would use the money to fund students from underrepresented groups.

Astronomers had gained a new way to observe the universe. And how big was the first clue that gave it away?
