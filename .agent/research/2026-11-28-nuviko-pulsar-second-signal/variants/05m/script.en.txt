How do you tell whether a signal comes from a distant star or from the equipment in front of you? And why did finding a second signal help solve the mystery of the first?

In the summer of 1967, a radio telescope at Cambridge began recording the sky. It looked more like a field of posts and wires than a telescope. One of the people who had spent two years building it was a graduate student named Jocelyn Bell.

She was studying quasars, powerful sources of radiation far beyond our galaxy. Radio waves from compact objects like these can appear to flicker as they pass through the solar wind. The telescope was designed to pick up that flickering, so astronomers could locate more quasars.

Bell didn't have a computer to sort through the recordings. A pen drew the signals on paper, and she examined the charts. On a normal day, she could have nearly a hundred feet of new paper to read.

After a while, she knew what ordinary radio interference looked like. She also recognized the sort of flickering she was looking for. But in among those signals was a mark that didn't belong to either group. It was about a quarter of an inch long, and it didn't appear on every observation.

Bell went back through the data. The odd mark was associated with one position in the sky. That made it worth investigating, but a tiny scribble could tell her very little about its structure.

Her supervisor, Antony Hewish, suggested recording the signal on a faster chart. Imagine moving a sheet of paper beneath a pen that jumps every second. If the sheet moves slowly, the jumps bunch together. Move it faster, and each jump gets room of its own.

Bell couldn't keep the recorders running at high speed all the time. She went to the observatory for the relevant part of the sky's passage. Day after day, the recording contained only noise. A month went by without a useful result.

Then she finally caught it. The pen traced a succession of pulses separated by about one point three seconds. Hewish joined her for the next observation, and the same interval appeared again.

That regularity made the discovery harder to explain, not easier. An electrical device could produce a repeating signal. Bell and Hewish even considered the possibility of a signal from another civilization.

They needed evidence that didn't depend on Bell's own equipment. Paul Scott and Robin Collins observed the source using another radio telescope. They detected it as well.

John Pilkington then examined the signal at different radio frequencies. The lower frequencies arrived later than the higher ones, as they do when radio waves travel through a region containing free electrons. The amount of delay suggested a source within our galaxy, well outside the observatory.

There was still a question. Why would a natural object produce such a regular signal?

Bell found another one. It came from a different region of the sky and had a slightly different interval between pulses. By early 1968, she had identified four sources in all.

The group published its first discovery in February. At the time, there were two plausible kinds of explanation: a star oscillating or an object rotating. A rotating neutron star turned out to be the key.

When a massive star collapses, it can leave behind a neutron star: an object with roughly the mass of the Sun packed into a space comparable to a city. Some of these stars emit narrow beams of radiation. As the star rotates, its beam can cross our line of sight again and again.

Think of a lighthouse. From the shore, you see flashes even when the lamp itself is shining continuously. The flashes tell you how quickly the light is turning. In the same way, a pulsar lets us follow the rotation of a star we cannot see in ordinary detail.

Bell's contribution to the discovery was not reflected in the Nobel Prize awarded to Hewish and Martin Ryle in 1974. Decades later, in 2018, Bell Burnell was awarded a special physics prize worth three million dollars. She announced that she would direct the money toward scholarships and research studentships for people who were underrepresented in physics.

The original telescope had been built to find more examples of something astronomers already knew about. Bell's second discovery made it possible to recognize that the strange little mark on the paper belonged to an entirely different kind of object. Astronomy had found some of nature's most remarkable clocks.
