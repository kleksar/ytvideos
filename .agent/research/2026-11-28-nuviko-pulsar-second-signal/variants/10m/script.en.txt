How could a graduate student find a new kind of star on a strip of paper? How did she establish that the signal came from space? And why would a navigation system half a century later listen to stars instead of relying on satellites around Earth?

In the summer of 1967, Jocelyn Bell began operating a radio telescope outside Cambridge. She had helped build it over the previous two years. The team had planted more than a thousand posts in a field and connected the antennas with miles of wire. The equipment was designed to locate distant sources of radiation called quasars.

Quasars were useful targets because they are so compact that their radio signals appear to flicker as they pass through the solar wind. Bell was looking for this particular sort of flicker. Four recorders drew the radio signals with pens, producing nearly thirty meters of paper every day. The team wanted to understand the instrument before handing the analysis to a computer, so Bell read the charts herself.

A few weeks into the survey, she noticed a disturbance barely six millimeters long. It looked a little like the signals she wanted and a little like ordinary interference, but it did not quite match either. She pulled out earlier charts. Whenever the mark appeared, it came from the same patch of sky. Somewhere inside that tiny mark there was a signal that nobody on the team could explain.

This was an instrument built in a field, not a telescope that could simply be turned to follow a star all night. The rotation of Earth brought a source into view. Bell had to arrange a faster recording when the relevant patch of sky passed the antenna array. The paper moved faster so that features which normally crowded together would appear separately.

She made those special observations for weeks and kept getting noise. On one occasion she went to a lecture instead. When she checked the ordinary chart the following day, there was the little mark again. The signal had returned on the day she missed the fast recording.

On the twenty-eighth of November, Bell finally watched the pen draw a succession of separate peaks. Once she had the paper off the recorder, she could check the spacing. A pulse was arriving roughly every one and a third seconds.

She contacted her supervisor, Antony Hewish, who was teaching in a Cambridge laboratory. His immediate suspicion was that the signal came from human equipment. That was a reasonable objection: a device can repeat a pattern at a fixed interval, and no ordinary star was known to pulse so quickly. He joined Bell for the next observation, and fortunately the peaks appeared again.

Hewish examined the earlier charts. The source followed the timing of the stars rather than the timing of everyday life on Earth. The group considered radar signals bouncing off the Moon, unusual satellite orbits, and even possible effects from a large metal building near the antenna field. None explained the pattern they had measured.

Two colleagues, Paul Scott and Robin Collins, used a separate telescope with its own receivers and detected the pulses. Bell's recorder could no longer account for the observation. But both telescopes could still receive the same radio transmission. The next test had to establish where it had traveled from.

John Pilkington measured how the pulse arrived at different frequencies. The high frequencies reached the telescope first; the low ones followed. Radio waves crossing the thin gas between stars travel through free electrons, which affect the timing of their arrival. Given an estimate of how many electrons the waves had encountered, the delay suggested that the source was inside our galaxy but far beyond the solar system. The distance was approximate.

There was another test to make. If a transmitter were sitting on a planet orbiting another star, its motion might make successive pulses reach Earth a little closer together and then farther apart. Hewish made precise measurements of their spacing. The timing changes he detected were consistent with Earth's own orbit. He found no evidence of comparable orbital motion at the source. That was one more reason to look for a natural explanation, though it did not establish exactly what the object was.

By the time Christmas approached, Bell's ordinary chart analysis was falling behind. As she later recalled, she interrupted a meeting of senior scientists discussing how to announce the result. They had not established the nature of the signal. How could they tell the world what they had found without claiming something they could not prove?

After dinner that evening Bell returned to her charts. In a recording from a completely different region of the sky, close to the trace of a known strong radio source, she spotted another irregular mark. She checked the older records and found it on some of them too. There was just enough time to prepare another fast observation before that region of the sky came into view in the early morning.

There was a problem at the observatory. The temperature was low, and the receiver system was losing sensitivity in the cold. Bell later recalled working the controls and trying to warm the equipment with her breath. She managed to get it functioning for five minutes. This time the pen traced pulses separated by about one point two seconds.

She left the recording for Hewish before going away for Christmas. Now they had two objects, in different directions, each with its own rhythm. It was considerably harder to explain them as a single artificial signal. The very thing that made the first discovery so difficult to interpret was turning out to be a repeatable property of objects in the sky.

Hewish kept the telescope running over the holiday and left the charts for Bell. When she returned, she found two more suspicious marks on the same sheet. The second source was confirmed by further observations, and then so were the third and fourth. Bell had a class of objects to investigate, although nobody had yet established how they produced the pulses.

The first paper appeared in Nature in February 1968. It had five authors: Hewish, Bell, Pilkington, Scott and Collins. The paper dealt with the first object, for which the group had accumulated only about three hours of actual observation. A vibrating white dwarf was one possibility; a rotating compact star was another. At a Cambridge seminar, Fred Hoyle suggested that the source could be the remnant of a supernova.

A neutron star can form when the core of a massive star collapses. It can contain roughly the mass of the Sun in a sphere the size of a city. Its rotation and strong magnetic field can produce beams of radiation. We see a pulse whenever one of those beams sweeps past us.

Imagine a lighthouse at night. The lamp can shine continuously, but from the shore you see flashes as its beam turns. You can measure the rotation without seeing the machinery. Likewise, the intervals between the flashes from a pulsar reveal the rotation of the star. Some pulsars are stable enough that, with careful measurements of their changing rhythm, astronomers can predict the arrival of future pulses.

Bell went back to the work she had originally been doing. She finished the analysis of her quasar data and wrote her thesis. In her account, the pulsars ended up in an appendix. In 1974, Hewish and Martin Ryle received the Nobel Prize in Physics; Bell was not a recipient.

Half a century later, in November 2017, a NASA experiment on the International Space Station put pulsar timing to a very different use. An instrument called NICER observed X-rays from four millisecond pulsars. They were different objects from the ones Bell had found. The navigation experiment, called SEXTANT, began with a deliberately degraded estimate of the station's position and velocity.

A pulse arriving a little sooner than expected can indicate that the spacecraft is closer to the source along that direction. One pulsar cannot establish the whole position. By comparing predicted and measured pulse arrival times from several directions, the software could progressively correct its estimate.

The experiment collected seventy-eight measurements over a little more than two days. In about seven and a half hours its combined three-dimensional position error fell below ten kilometers and remained below that level for the rest of the experiment. It did not replace GPS; it demonstrated a way a spacecraft might navigate autonomously far from Earth.

In 2018 Bell Burnell received a special physics prize worth three million dollars and announced that she would use the money to support students from groups underrepresented in physics. The discovery that once threatened to derail her original research had given astronomy a new kind of star, and, eventually, a navigation technique based on the timing of its pulses. It began with a student who took a second look at a mark on a piece of paper.
