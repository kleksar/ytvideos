How could a graduate student find a new kind of star using a pen and rolls of paper? What finally persuaded astronomers that the pulses she recorded came from space? And how did that discovery eventually help a spacecraft work out its own position?

In the mid nineteen-sixties, a strange new class of objects was attracting astronomers' attention. Quasars were immensely powerful sources of radiation in the distant universe. They were so far away and so compact that there was little detail to see, yet their radio signals offered a clue to their nature.

There is a difference between the way a small point of light and a broad patch of light appear when you look through moving air. A star can twinkle in Earth's atmosphere, while a planet often seems steadier. Something related can happen to radio waves as they travel through the disturbed plasma of the solar wind. Compact radio sources can appear to flicker.

At Cambridge, Antony Hewish obtained funding to build an instrument that could survey the sky for that effect. His graduate student Jocelyn Bell was part of the construction team. Her work included the cables, connectors and transformers. The field covered about four acres, and the installation required roughly a hundred and twenty miles of wire and cable. Getting the telescope ready took two years.

The sky moved across the instrument as Earth turned. The team could choose different north-south viewing directions, but it didn't have enough receivers to use all the possible beams at once. Bell was the first person to carry out a research program with the new telescope.

Her job was to find quasars. Yet the signal that would transform her research did not resemble the familiar pattern of a quasar.

The instrument produced paper charts. If it collected a hundred feet of paper, Bell had a hundred feet of observations to examine. There was no computer automatically flagging the anomalies for her. By the end of her six months of observing, she had examined more than three miles of chart paper.

Think about what a new radio source had to compete with for her attention. An instrument can pick up ordinary human-made interference. And this telescope was new, so the possibility of an equipment fault had to be taken seriously. An unusual mark on a graph didn't immediately deserve an announcement.

Bell noticed a small disturbance, only a quarter of an inch across. It appeared intermittently, but in observations of the same position in the sky. That connection mattered. When a telescope relies on Earth's rotation to scan, a distant object passes through its field of view at a predictable point in the sky's daily movement.

Now the task was to see what was hidden inside the little mark. Hewish recommended making a more detailed recording. The simplest way to enlarge the pattern in time was to move the chart paper faster. If you doubled the speed of the paper while recording the same signal, the space between two marks would double. No new radio energy would be arriving at the telescope; the same event would simply be spread over a larger piece of paper.

Bell couldn't leave the recorder at the higher speed indefinitely. She needed to be at the observatory around the passage of that particular patch of sky. For about a month, the source refused to show up in a useful recording. Her careful timing produced sheets of noise.

When the signal returned, the faster paper exposed something she had never seen in the other data. The little mark contained a train of distinct pulses. The gaps between them were about one point three seconds.

Hewish came to the observatory for the next observation. The pulses appeared again, with the same interval. Now there were two people who had seen the pattern with their own eyes. That didn't settle whether the instrument itself was responsible.

The first real advantage of involving another observer was that the other telescope wasn't connected to Bell's recorder. Paul Scott and Robin Collins used their own instrument to observe the source, and it detected the signal too. Whatever was causing the pulses, they were not merely marks created inside Bell's chart recorder.

Then John Pilkington looked at the signal in two parts of the radio spectrum. This was a way to ask a different question: what had happened to the signal before it reached the telescopes?

Radio waves travel through the thin plasma between stars. Its free electrons affect the way the waves propagate. In particular, the low frequencies can lag behind the high frequencies. A single burst of radio energy arriving from far away can therefore be stretched out across the frequency range.

It is a little like receiving the same message through two channels with different delays. You compare the arrival of corresponding features, instead of merely looking at the total strength of the two signals. With an estimate of the density of electrons along the path, the measured frequency-dependent delay gave the group an estimate of distance. The result suggested a source somewhere out in our galaxy. It wasn't an exact distance, because the estimate depended on how much intervening material there was.

An artificial transmission from another civilization was among the possibilities the team considered. There was no evidence of a message, however, just an extremely regular sequence of pulses. The team needed to explain the signal, not assume an explanation from its rhythm.

Bell then found another source. This one was not in the same direction, and its pulse interval was different. In early 1968, she identified two more. Finding a second source had changed the nature of the problem. Astronomers now had to explain several similar phenomena in widely separated parts of the sky.

The first paper appeared in Nature in February 1968. It had five authors: Hewish, Bell, Pilkington, Scott and Collins. The interpretation was not yet settled. Hewish considered a vibrating white dwarf, a dense stellar remnant. Rotation was another possibility. At a talk in Cambridge, Fred Hoyle suggested that a neutron star could be responsible.

That was a very different object from the quasar Bell had set out to find. A neutron star can form when the core of a massive star collapses. The material that remains can pack a mass comparable to the Sun into a sphere only about the size of a city. Rotation and an intense magnetic field can produce beams of radiation that sweep through space. When Earth lies in the path of a beam, the signal appears to pulse.

Imagine walking around a lighthouse at night. Stand where its beam passes you and you see a flash at regular intervals. You could measure the rotation of the lamp without being close enough to inspect the motor. For a pulsar, measuring the arrival of those flashes reveals the star's rotation.

That gives astronomers something quite unusual: a clock they don't have to build. They still need to measure how its rhythm changes. Some pulsars are remarkably stable, and careful observations make it possible to predict when their pulses should arrive.

In November 2017, half a century after the discovery, a NASA experiment on the International Space Station put that property to use. The instrument was called NICER. A navigation experiment named SEXTANT observed X-rays from four millisecond pulsars. These were different sources from Bell's original discovery.

The experiment deliberately began with degraded knowledge of the station's position and velocity. Without fresh observations, the estimate would drift away from its actual position. The navigation software compared observations of the pulsars with predictions of pulse arrival times. Arriving a little earlier or later than expected can tell you something about where the spacecraft is along the direction toward that star. Observations from several different directions can help constrain its position.

The system used seventy-eight measurements over an experiment lasting a little more than two days. In about seven and a half hours, the combined three-dimensional position error dropped below ten kilometers and stayed below that level for the rest of the experiment. That did not replace GPS. It demonstrated an autonomous navigation technique that could be useful far from Earth, where ordinary satellite navigation is not available.

There is another part of the discovery's legacy. In 1974, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Hewish and Martin Ryle, for contributions to radio astronomy including the discovery of pulsars. Bell was not a recipient. In September 2018, she was awarded a special Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics worth three million dollars. Her announced plan was to fund scholarships and research studentships for people from groups underrepresented in physics.

The same investigation that began with a tiny inconsistency in a paper chart ultimately gave us a new class of stars and a new way to navigate. The important step wasn't recognizing a familiar signal. Bell kept following the one that didn't fit.
