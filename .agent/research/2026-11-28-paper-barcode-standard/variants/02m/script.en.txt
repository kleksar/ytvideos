Why did a barcode that worked in a supermarket lose to a completely different design? And why did some people try to keep the winning invention out of their stores?

In 1949, Joe Woodland and Bernard Silver applied for a patent on a machine-readable code. Their idea was to turn information into black and white bands. Arrange those bands in a circle, and a reader can approach them from any direction. It was like printing a little target on every product.

By 1972, RCA had put circular codes to work at a Kroger supermarket in Cincinnati. The idea was no longer just a patent.

But the symbol had to survive the printing press as well as the checkout. Smudged ink could change the width of the rings. IBM engineer George Laurer designed a rectangle of parallel stripes instead. The scanner could do the work of reading them at different angles. Even Woodland joined IBM's effort.

The industry chose the striped design in 1973. The following June, cashier Sharon Buchanan scanned a pack of Wrigley's gum at a Marsh supermarket in Ohio.

Yet a scanner was expensive if most packages still came without codes. Manufacturers had little incentive to change their packaging if stores had no scanners. Shoppers also worried about losing individual price stickers; unions feared lost jobs.

The trick was that the stripes didn't contain the selling price. They identified the product, allowing the store to look up its current price and record the sale. As manufacturers and retailers adopted the same code, that record became useful well beyond the checkout.

The little target had lost. The stripes won because a whole industry could use them as a shared language, one pack of gum at a time.
