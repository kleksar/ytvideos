Why did the first supermarket barcode look like a target? How did a rival design beat it, even though the target was already working in a real store? And why wasn't winning the contest enough to get the new barcode onto every shopping basket?

Go back to the late 1940s. At a busy grocery checkout, a cashier had to find each price and enter it. Employees spent time marking individual goods. Keeping track of what the shop had sold was another laborious job. A machine that could recognize the products would help with both.

Bernard Silver heard a grocery executive asking a dean at Drexel Institute of Technology for help. He took the problem to another student, Joe Woodland.

Woodland later recalled sitting on a beach in Florida, thinking about Morse code. A dot and a dash could distinguish letters. What if you made them into long marks of different widths instead? He dragged his fingers through the sand and turned the lines into rings.

The rings were a clever solution to the angle problem. You wouldn't have to hold every item the right way up. A scanner passing through the center would meet the same pattern of dark and light bands.

Woodland and Silver filed a patent application in 1949. Getting the code into a store was a different matter. Their early reading apparatus, according to later accounts, needed a powerful lamp and was roughly the size of a desk. The equipment was hardly suited to every grocery counter.

More than twenty years passed. Lasers and smaller computers made the idea much more practical. RCA picked up the circular design and, in July 1972, installed automatic checkouts at a Kroger supermarket in Cincinnati. Now there was something that could be demonstrated outside a laboratory.

Here was the catch. A working scanner in one supermarket was not enough. The grocery industry wanted a code that different manufacturers could print on their packages, and that different retailers could read on their own equipment. It had to be small, accurate and compatible with the printers already used to make labels.

IBM was one of the companies competing to supply the symbol. Its engineer George Laurer didn't want to follow the circular design. The geometry that made the target easy to read was also a problem on a printing press. A smear could run across the curved bands, altering the spaces the scanner needed to distinguish.

Laurer used straight bars and made the scanning system handle orientation. According to his later account, IBM even tested the reader by having a skilled softball pitcher toss beanbag ashtrays with symbols on their undersides past the scanner. The reader could identify them as they passed.

One of the people helping the IBM team was Joe Woodland himself. The inventor of the circle was now supporting its rival.

In 1973, the industry committee selected the rectangular design. On June 26, 1974, a new system went into service at Marsh in Troy, Ohio. Staff had spent the previous night labeling goods. The first customer in the ceremonial purchase was a Marsh executive, Clyde Dawson. He chose gum partly to show that the code could fit on such a small package. Sharon Buchanan passed it over the scanner.

The contest was over, but the commercial problem wasn't. A retailer had to buy equipment before most goods carried the new label; a manufacturer had to change its packaging before most retailers could read it. And the new system challenged familiar ways of shopping.

Paul McEnroe, who led IBM's team, later remembered union picketers preventing his engineers from entering a Virginia store. Consumer advocate Carol Tucker-Foreman fought to keep prices printed on individual items so customers could compare them. Without those tags, how would shoppers know the price displayed by the machine was the one they had agreed to pay?

That question points to what the barcode actually does. The stripes represent a product number, not a price. When a store scans two identical boxes, it can retrieve the same product record twice. It can change the price in its computer without changing the pattern on the box.

During the 1980s adoption spread. A scan could now do more than add a price to a total: it could help a business count sales and decide what to order again.

Look at the stripes on your next grocery purchase. The machine identifies what you're buying; the shop's records determine what you pay. The big invention was a way for the physical product and the digital record to meet, no matter which company made the scanner.
