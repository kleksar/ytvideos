Why did a working supermarket barcode lose a national competition? Why did its inventor help the rival team, and what stopped the new winner from taking over stores?

In July 1972, RCA installed automatic checkouts at a Kroger supermarket in Cincinnati. The labels on the groceries looked like small targets. The system worked, and the retailer's trial produced encouraging results.

But the next year the grocery industry selected a different design: black and white stripes in a rectangle. Even the man who had helped invent the target was working on the stripes. The design that worked in one store wasn't necessarily the one that could work across an entire industry.

To understand why, go back to 1948. Every price in a grocery basket had to be read and entered by a cashier. Employees also spent time marking goods and counting stock. Bernard Silver overheard a grocery executive asking a dean at Drexel Institute of Technology for help. Silver took the problem to Joe Woodland, who left graduate school and moved to Miami Beach to work on it.

Woodland later recalled thinking about Morse code at the beach. He drew his fingers through the sand, making long marks of different widths, then swept them around into rings. Imagine a beam passing through the center of a target. Rotate the target, and the beam still meets the same sequence of rings.

A cashier wouldn't have to hold every package the right way up. Woodland and Silver filed their patent application in October 1949. It was granted in 1952.

According to later accounts, they built a crude reader with a powerful incandescent lamp. An oscilloscope displayed the changing electrical signal. But the apparatus was bulky. Ordinary supermarkets still lacked the compact light source and affordable computing equipment necessary for a practical checkout.

In 1966, the grocery chain Kroger was still looking for a way to automate checkout. RCA, a large electronics company, took up the challenge and found Woodland and Silver's patent.

Now RCA had access to lasers. A narrow light beam could sweep over closely spaced bands. A detector converted changes in reflected light into a signal a computer could interpret.

RCA still needed to print accurate rings. Its developers even worked with a rotating arrangement of pens to improve the markings. The effort led to the Cincinnati pilot.

With an operating store to show the selection committee, RCA had a good reason to expect its design to win.

But the committee wanted a shared standard. Suppose two supermarkets bought scanners from rival companies. Both needed to read the number printed by a third company on a can of beans. Manufacturers couldn't make separate packaging for every type of scanner.

The code also had to fit on small labels and be printable on ordinary packaging production lines. The committee had specified a maximum area of one and a half square inches, and fewer than one undetected reading error in twenty thousand. Speed alone was useless if the machine silently identified the wrong goods.

Seven companies submitted designs. At IBM, the engineer George Laurer was expected to work on something like the established bull's-eye. Instead, he investigated the printing problem.

Imagine ink spreading in one direction as it is printed. Somewhere on a ring, that direction crosses the bands and changes their widths. A smear along parallel stripes need not change their widths the same way. This illustrates the design advantage; straight bars are not immune to printing defects.

Laurer proposed a compact rectangle of alternating dark and light bands. That left another challenge: the reader needed to find a suitable path across the stripes even when the package was turned. The laser scanner and the symbol had to be designed as parts of the same system. In his own account, Laurer proposed crossing two scanning lines on the glass, like an X. When the package was turned, the reader had another direction in which to cross the bars. The orientation problem had moved from the printed label to the equipment.

Laurer eventually got permission to proceed. He recalled that in one demonstration, after the prototype had read the labels correctly, a skilled softball pitcher was brought in to throw soft beanbag ashtrays with codes attached to their bottoms past the scanner. They were read successfully.

The circular code's original inventor was meanwhile working for IBM. According to Paul McEnroe, the head of the IBM development team, Woodland came to Raleigh to help them get the rectangular symbol selected. His own patent was no longer the design he was backing.

The industry committee chose IBM's rectangular symbol in 1973. A council could now assign product numbers that participating manufacturers and retailers would agree on. It was called the Universal Product Code, or UPC. McEnroe later recalled that the competition required participants to give up exclusive ownership of the new standard. A technology company could sell the equipment, while the marks on a package would not tie its manufacturer to a single equipment vendor.

A scanner from the next chapter survives in the Smithsonian's collection. Inside, a laser shone onto a rotating mirror, which directed light through the glass counter window. A detector picked up the reflection; a connected register looked up the product.

The scanner was one of the machines installed at the Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio. On the night before the system opened, employees attached barcodes to hundreds of items. Then came the first official purchase on June 26, 1974.

Clyde Dawson, a Marsh research executive, brought a pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum. He later explained why he chose it: there had been doubts that the code would fit on a package that small. Sharon Buchanan was the cashier. The scanner read the package and the register recorded the purchase.

The first UPC purchase was a demonstration. The difficult question now was: who pays to go first?

A retailer with expensive scanners but few coded goods would still process the rest manually. A supplier with new labels but few scanner-equipped stores would gain little. To recover its investment, a store needed codes on a large majority of goods. IBM's own history puts the estimated break-even threshold at seventy-five percent of grocery items, and describes how the industry pressed suppliers to adopt the labels and IBM helped them obtain the necessary technology. This was a coordinated change, not an instant consequence of the contest.

There were people who questioned the change for a different reason. A supermarket offered jobs to cashiers and employees who marked prices. Labor unions feared losing work. McEnroe recalled that his engineers were kept outside a store in Tysons Corner, Virginia, by union picketers.

Consumer advocate Carol Tucker-Foreman opposed getting rid of the price on each item. Her objection is easy to understand. A sticker on a can stays on the can as you walk from the shelf to the checkout. A shelf label does not. The issue reached public debates and Senate hearings. According to McEnroe, some states required shops to retain individual price labels even after installing scanners. Stores in those states could not immediately eliminate the labor of marking prices that had helped justify their investment.

For example, if a packet of biscuits goes from two dollars to three, the barcode doesn't change. Its number still identifies the same kind of biscuits. The shop changes the price in its records. If the shelf label is outdated, the customer has to catch the discrepancy at checkout.

The scanner solved recognition, not every price dispute. Adoption needed trust as well as engineering.

Adoption spread in the 1980s. Large retailers such as Kmart took the technology beyond groceries. Sales records helped shops see which products needed restocking instead of knowing only how much money had passed through the till.

In 1989, barcodes were used in more than half of United States grocery sales. The little marks could now serve as the connection between goods on shelves and records in computers across a large part of the economy. In 1992, Woodland received the National Medal of Technology for his barcode work. The contribution that earned him recognition had outlasted the circular shape he once patented.

Today, barcodes come in many forms, and not every barcode is a UPC. A library can use a code to identify a particular copy of a book; a supermarket UPC usually identifies a type of retail product. The important distinction is what the printed identifier refers to.

The target worked at Kroger. The rectangle won the contest. What made the stripes transformative was agreement on which numbers meant which products, how to read them, and how to use that information.

Next time you hear a checkout beep, notice how little information is actually being carried across that glass. It's an identity. Everything the machine can do with it depends on what people have built on the other side.
