Why did a barcode that could be read from any direction lose a nationwide competition? Why did the inventor of that code help build the one that defeated it? And what stopped the new striped barcode from taking over the supermarket as soon as it was ready?

Imagine buying a carton of milk in the late 1940s. Someone has to mark the price on it. At the checkout, another person has to read that price and enter it. Repeat this for a whole basket, and then for every customer in the store. At some point the shop also needs to find out which goods are running out. The bigger the supermarket, the more work there is behind that deceptively simple transaction.

In 1948, Bernard Silver overheard a grocery executive asking a dean at the Drexel Institute of Technology in Philadelphia to investigate a way of automating this process. Silver brought the idea to Joe Woodland. Woodland left graduate school and moved into an apartment belonging to his grandfather in Miami Beach. He had sold some of his stocks to support himself while he worked.

The problem followed him to the beach. In Woodland's later recollection, he was thinking about the dots and dashes of Morse code when he drew his fingers through the sand. He could make a wide line stand for one kind of mark and a narrow one for another. Extend the marks, and they become easier to find with a moving reader.

Then he swept his fingers around in a circle. It wasn't just a different shape. A line of writing has a preferred direction. A circle has no top or bottom. Imagine a beam sweeping across a target through its center. Turn the target around, and the beam still passes through the same sequence of rings.

For a cashier handling one package after another, that mattered. Woodland and Silver filed their patent application in October 1949. The patent was granted three years later.

The design was only one piece of the puzzle. How would the machine turn printed marks into useful information? Later accounts describe Woodland and Silver building a crude reader with a powerful incandescent bulb. An oscilloscope, a device for displaying electrical signals, let them see the changing signal. The apparatus was bulky. An ordinary supermarket didn't yet have the compact light source and affordable computing equipment needed to turn this into a practical checkout.

There was another problem that would take decades to become clear. A code might look perfectly convincing on a patent drawing, but groceries came in millions of packages that had to be printed quickly and cheaply.

By the mid-1960s the grocery chain Kroger was still looking for a way to automate checkout. It appealed for help with an optical system. RCA, a large electronics company, investigated the problem and found the earlier circular design.

Now it had a technology Woodland and Silver hadn't had in 1949: the laser. A narrow beam of light could pass over closely spaced bands. A light-sensitive detector could turn the changes in reflected light into a signal for a computer. The computer could recognize a number and look up what it meant.

RCA still had to get the code onto products. Printing accurate rings proved troublesome. Its developers even worked with a rotating arrangement of pens to improve the markings. By July 1972, the company was ready to install automated checkouts in a Kroger store in Cincinnati.

The pilot worked. At last, circular codes could be read at a real grocery counter. RCA had a good reason to expect that the grocery industry would select its design for wider use.

But the people choosing the code represented much more than that one store. They wanted a shared standard. Suppose two supermarkets bought scanners from rival companies. Both supermarkets ought to be able to read a number printed by a third company on a can of beans. Manufacturers couldn't reasonably maintain a separate version of every package for every type of scanner.

The symbol also had to fit on small labels. And it had to work with ordinary packaging production. A code perfectly printed by a specialist on a laboratory bench was not the test that mattered.

Seven companies submitted designs. At IBM, the engineer George Laurer was expected to work on something like the established bull's-eye. Instead, he investigated the printing problem.

Think of a print smear moving in one direction. On a circular symbol, somewhere on the ring that movement will cut across the bands and distort their widths. With parallel bars, a smear running along the bars need not change their widths in the same way. This is an illustration of the design advantage, not a guarantee that a striped barcode can survive every printing defect.

Laurer proposed a compact rectangle of alternating dark and light bands. That left another challenge: the reader needed to find a suitable path across the stripes even when the package was turned. The laser scanner and the symbol had to be designed as parts of the same system.

Laurer eventually got permission to proceed. He recalled that in one demonstration, after the prototype had read the labels correctly, a skilled softball pitcher was brought in to throw soft beanbag ashtrays with codes attached to their bottoms past the scanner. They were read successfully.

The circular code's original inventor was meanwhile working for IBM. According to Paul McEnroe, the head of the IBM development team, Woodland came to Raleigh to help them get the rectangular symbol selected. His own patent was no longer the design he was backing.

The industry committee chose IBM's rectangular symbol in 1973. A council could now assign product numbers that participating manufacturers and retailers would agree on. It was called the Universal Product Code, or UPC.

There is an actual machine from the next chapter in the Smithsonian's collection. A laser inside the scanner projected light via a rotating mirror through the glass window on the counter. A detector picked up the reflected light, and a connected cash register matched the signal against its stored product data.

The scanner was one of the machines installed at the Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio. On the night before the system opened, employees attached barcodes to hundreds of items. Then came the first official purchase on June 26, 1974.

Clyde Dawson, a Marsh research executive, brought a pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit gum. He later explained why he chose it: there had been doubts that the code would fit on a package that small. Sharon Buchanan was the cashier. The scanner read the package and the register recorded the purchase.

That should have been the moment when every other supermarket started ordering scanners. Instead, the companies faced an awkward question: who pays to go first?

If a shop installed the equipment and only a few suppliers printed codes, employees would still have to process the rest of the goods manually. If a supplier redesigned its packages while very few stores could scan them, it would pay for a change that provided little immediate value. IBM's historical account recalls a requirement that a large majority of grocery items would need to be coded for retailers to recover their investment.

There were people who questioned the change for a different reason. A supermarket offered jobs to cashiers and employees who marked prices. Labor unions feared losing work. McEnroe recalled that his engineers were kept outside a store in Tysons Corner, Virginia, by union picketers.

Consumer advocate Carol Tucker-Foreman opposed getting rid of the price on each item. Her objection is easy to understand. A sticker on a can stays on the can as you walk from the shelf to the checkout. A shelf label does not.

Here is a little example, not a historical sale. Say a shop sells a packet of biscuits for two dollars. Tomorrow the shop changes the price to three dollars. It doesn't need a new barcode, because the number points to the same packet of biscuits. The shop changes the price in its records. That is efficient for the shop, but if the shelf sign has not been updated, the customer has to notice the discrepancy somewhere else.

The scanner solved recognition; it didn't settle every question about price accuracy. The new system needed trust as well as engineering and an agreement between suppliers and shops.

By the early 1980s, adoption was spreading. Big retailers such as Kmart helped take it beyond the grocery checkout. The barcode gave stores something they could use after the customer went home: a record of which product had sold. Knowing which type of cereal was bought is more useful for replenishing a shelf than simply knowing how much money came through a register.

In 1989, barcodes were used in more than half of United States grocery sales. The little marks could now serve as the connection between goods on shelves and records in computers across a large part of the economy.

Today, barcodes come in many forms, and not every barcode is a UPC. A library can use a code to identify a particular copy of a book; a supermarket UPC usually identifies a type of retail product. The important distinction is what the printed identifier refers to.

A target worked at Kroger. A rectangle won the competition. But neither shape could have changed shopping on its own. People had to agree on the numbers, learn to print and read them reliably, and build stores where customers could trust the results.

Next time you hear a checkout beep, notice how little information is actually being carried across that glass. It's an identity. Everything the machine can do with it depends on what people have built on the other side.
