Why did shoppers leave Tupperware on the shelf? And how did the woman who taught America to buy it end up losing the business she had built?

In the late nineteen forties, Earl Tupper was selling plastic bowls with an unusual lid. You had to press the lid into place and lift one edge to let some air escape. It was a clever seal, but it wasn't obvious to a shopper looking at a bowl in a store.

Brownie Wise showed people how it worked in their own living rooms. At a party, she could fill a sealed bowl with liquid and toss it across the room. Now the lid made sense.

A hostess invited her friends and received merchandise in return. The seller bought the bowls at wholesale prices and made money on the difference. For women who wanted an income, the party was also a business opportunity.

In 1951 Tupper hired Wise and took his products out of retail stores. The company built a whole network of sellers and managers around home demonstrations.

Wise became its public face. By 1954 the company's sales had reached twenty-five million dollars.

Then the two executives fell out. Tupper fired her in 1958. She owned no shares in the company.

Wise started a cosmetics business. At that year's gathering of Tupperware dealers, her replacement distributed an advertisement for her new venture and invited anyone who wanted to join her to leave.

A former employee later recalled that nobody stood up.

Wise's new business lasted about a year. Tupper sold his company for millions. She received none of the proceeds.

The sales network Wise had created was now strong enough to carry on without her.
