How do you persuade somebody to buy an expensive-looking piece of plastic when they already have bowls at home? And why, after building a national sales force, couldn't Brownie Wise take that sales force with her?

After the Second World War, Earl Tupper developed lightweight plastic containers with a flexible lid. There was a trick to closing one. Press the lid down, raise an edge to let some air escape, then close it again. The seal was useful for storing food.

In a department store, though, a customer could pick up the bowl and never discover the trick. The material was unfamiliar. There was nothing about a bowl sitting on a shelf that explained why it was worth buying.

Brownie Wise had experience selling household products at home parties. Instead of trying to explain the lid in an advertisement, she could show it working in front of a roomful of people. A sealed bowl could be tossed across the room with liquid inside. If it stayed shut, the audience had seen the advantage for themselves.

The party also gave people a reason to arrange the next demonstration. A woman invited her friends to her home and received products as a reward. A dealer did the selling, buying at wholesale and keeping the difference between her purchase price and the retail price.

And some of those guests could become dealers themselves.

One woman, Lavon Weber, later remembered wanting a coffee table for her house. Hearing how much a dealer had made at a party, she began working out how many parties it would take to buy the table. There was a cost to getting started, but she had found a way to earn money herself.

In 1951, Tupper made a remarkable decision. Instead of keeping his containers in stores and treating the parties as extra sales, he hired Wise as an executive and withdrew his products from retail stores. The living room became the main place to buy a Tupperware bowl.

That meant the business needed more than a good demonstrator. It needed people who could train new sellers, supply them with stock and keep them organising parties.

Wise built that system. At annual gatherings in Florida, dealers attended training sessions and received prizes for their achievements. She celebrated the women who sold the products rather than hiding them behind the company name. Sales reached twenty-five million dollars in 1954. The following year, Wise became the first woman to appear on the cover of BusinessWeek.

For a while, the inventor and the salesperson had complementary jobs. Tupper made the containers. Wise got them into people's homes.

By 1957 that relationship was breaking down. The factory was struggling to supply orders, and the two executives disputed business decisions. Their correspondence shows disagreements about spending, including thousands of copies of a motivational book that Wise had ordered.

Tupper wanted greater oversight of the business in Florida. Wise didn't own any shares, despite being the person many dealers associated with the company. In January 1958, she was dismissed.

She launched a cosmetics venture called Cinderella. If the women who had learned to sell bowls could sell cosmetics in the same way, perhaps she could build another national network.

That year, Tupperware held its big annual gathering without her. Her successor, Hamer Wilson, did something surprising. He distributed an advertisement for Cinderella and offered the assembled dealers the chance to leave and join Wise.

Pat Tahaney, who had worked for the company, later recalled that nobody in the audience got up. Another dealer explained that her commitment was to the product she sold.

Cinderella lasted about a year. Tupper sold his company in 1958 for millions of dollars. Wise got a settlement after her dismissal, but because she owned no shares, she received nothing from the sale.

The home parties did not disappear. The woman with a bowl in her hands had proved that a salesperson could create demand that a shelf couldn't. She had also built something that no longer needed its founder to be in the room.
