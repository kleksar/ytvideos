Why did a company that sold millions of dollars' worth of kitchenware end up burying its sales chief's own book? And what happened when that sales chief tried to take her business with her?

In the late 1940s Earl Tupper had an unusual problem. He had made a lightweight plastic bowl with a flexible lid. The lid worked best if you pressed it down, lifted part of its edge to let air out, and closed it again. A customer who picked up the bowl in a department store might never discover how to use it.

Brownie Wise knew a different way to sell things. She was a divorced mother who had worked as a secretary and sold cleaning and household products through Stanley Home Products. Her customers could see the products being used at a gathering in somebody's home.

With Tupper's bowls, the demonstration could be spectacular. Close a bowl full of liquid and throw it across a living room. A shopper who had seen only an unfamiliar piece of plastic in a store could suddenly see a reason to buy one.

In 1951 Tupper hired Wise and withdrew the containers from retail stores. The entire business would now depend on people arranging demonstrations in their homes. If a dealer stopped holding parties, the company could no longer make up those sales at the local department store.

Wise had already organised a Tupperware business called Patio Parties. Now she had to make the method work across the country. One of the people who later described how it worked was Lavon Weber. She wanted a coffee table. When she heard about a dealer who had made ten dollars in one evening, she started calculating how many parties it would take to buy the table if she earned five dollars each time.

There were costs before she could begin selling. A dealer had to acquire materials, prepare a demonstration and persuade a hostess to invite guests. Irene Ellis remembered turning up hours early to prepare for her first evening party. The lively demonstration was the visible part of the work. The invitations and preparation happened before the guests ever saw a bowl.

The system gave the hostess a reward in merchandise and the dealer a margin on each sale. Some of the guests became sellers themselves. The next party might take place in a home where Wise had never been and where nobody had ever met her. That was how a local selling method could grow into a national operation.

By 1954 the annual sales figure had reached twenty-five million dollars. In April that year, Brownie Wise appeared on the cover of BusinessWeek. She was the first woman to do so.

That same year she inaugurated a national gathering known as the Jubilee at the company's Florida headquarters. The dealers could meet their colleagues, learn how to sell, and be rewarded for their work. The company made these occasions unusually elaborate. A film from the 1956 Jubilee shows a dealer being presented with a motorboat as part of a wish-granting spectacle. The rewards were something other sellers could see for themselves.

But the bowls still had to come from Earl Tupper's factory. By 1957 it was having difficulty supplying the sales organisation. Wise was trying to keep dealers selling; Tupper was responsible for manufacturing and wanted control over the operation in Florida. Their relationship increasingly played out in letters.

One of those letters concerned a collection of Wise's motivational talks called Best Wishes. She had ordered six thousand copies for the sales division without first obtaining Tupper's approval. In her reply she pointed out that she had made other large decisions without getting his permission first, including projects at headquarters.

An arrangement that had allowed her to build the sales organisation was now a point of dispute. The factory owner wanted to know where his company's money was going. The executive in Florida had been accustomed to running her part of the business with considerable independence.

By the end of 1957 Tupper was questioning expenditure on the annual gatherings, prizes and other expenses. He demanded that she bring the accounts to New England. Wise said she was ill and could not travel. On January 28, 1958, Tupper flew to Florida himself.

Wise was dismissed. She had become the most publicly recognisable figure in the sales business, but she had no shares in the company. She received compensation after the dismissal. There was no piece of the factory or of the distribution system that she owned and could simply transfer to another business.

She could, however, try to sell something else. She started Cinderella Cosmetics, a home-party company. She had trained people to demonstrate products, recruit dealers and organise sales meetings. If they followed her, she would have a network again.

The first Jubilee without Wise took place that summer. There was a pirate theme, and the company was demonstrating new products. Behind the festivities lay an unusual uncertainty. Wise had advertised Cinderella in the local newspaper, explicitly inviting Tupperware dealers to find out about the new venture. Former staff member Tony Ponticelli later described the concern that she might attract their most successful sellers.

Hamer Wilson, one of the executives now in charge, decided to confront the offer publicly. According to Pat Tahaney, who worked for the company, Wilson told her to obtain around two thousand copies of the newspaper for distribution. He then gave the dealers assembled at the Jubilee a chance to leave and go with Wise.

Tahaney remembered that nobody got up and left.

That recollection cannot tell us what every dealer privately considered. But two of the sellers later explained their own perspective. Elsie Mortland had actually discussed the new venture with Wise. She did not think she could sell the cosmetics with the conviction she felt for Tupperware. And Lavon Weber, the woman who had once calculated how to afford a coffee table, recalled that the existing business was doing well and the dealers depended on its income.

The women had learned a method from Wise. They had also spent years learning to sell this particular product. They had customers, supplies and an established organisation. Their skills could travel with them, but a working business would have to be built again around the new products.

Cinderella lasted about a year. Meanwhile the company removed photographs and other traces of Wise from its offices, according to former staff. The PBS documentary reports that employees dug a hole behind headquarters and buried six hundred remaining copies of Best Wishes. The book that had been a point of contention while she was in office was now being disposed of.

Tupper sold the company later in 1958 for millions of dollars. Because Wise owned no shares, she received none of the money from the sale.

The parties went on. In the following years, sellers took the model to homes in other parts of the world. Wise's own papers and photographs eventually reached an archive, and decades later a park in Florida was named after her. The history the company had tried to leave behind survived.

The first demonstration had required a person to pick up a bowl and show what its lid could do. By the time Wise lost her job, many other people knew how to do that without her. Even when the company removed her books and pictures, the way she had taught its dealers to sell remained in their hands.
