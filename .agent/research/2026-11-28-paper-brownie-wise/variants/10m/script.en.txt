What made an ordinary-looking plastic bowl worth gathering your friends together to buy? How did the woman who created Tupperware's sales network turn that little demonstration into a national business? And what happened when she tried to start over with a different product?

In the years after the Second World War, a manufacturer named Earl Tupper was making household containers out of plastic. At the time, plastic had not yet become the familiar material that fills our kitchen cupboards.

His bowl had a flexible lid that fitted around the rim. There was a technique to using it. You pressed the lid into position, lifted a little part of the edge so some air could escape, then pressed it shut again. When you wanted to open the container, you peeled the lid away.

The mechanism was useful. It was also a problem for somebody trying to sell the bowl. A shopper who saw it alongside ordinary kitchenware might never know how the lid was supposed to work. The thing that made it different was something you had to do with your hands.

The woman who found a way around that problem was Brownie Wise. She was a divorced mother working as a secretary when she began selling household goods through home demonstrations. The home party wasn't her invention. She had learned it while working with Stanley Home Products. What she did was make the method fit a product that was difficult to explain in a store.

Imagine the difference between looking at a bowl through a shop window and watching somebody toss a sealed bowl full of liquid across a living room. That was the sort of demonstration used at Tupperware parties. You could handle the lid, see how it fitted, and watch whether it held. Everybody in the room could understand the result without knowing anything about polymers.

Once you had seen the demonstration, you could arrange another party for people who hadn't. And this was the important part: Wise wasn't merely going to sell a lot of bowls herself. The method allowed her to teach other people how to sell them.

For the hostess, there was a reward in products. For the dealer, there was a margin between the wholesale cost and the retail price. For the company, there was another room full of potential customers, some of whom might decide to sell Tupperware themselves. Each part of the arrangement gave somebody a reason to organise the next party.

A former dealer named Lavon Weber remembered a very specific thing she wanted to buy: a coffee table. She heard about a woman who had made ten dollars at a party, and worked out how many parties she would need to hold if she earned five dollars each time. She had to pay for the materials to get started. Selling was work, not a gift from the company. But the amount she earned could be linked to something she wanted in her own home.

Another dealer, Irene Ellis, later described arriving to set up for her first evening party hours before it began. She was preparing to sell a product, in front of people whose attention she had to win. The demonstration might look like a spontaneous game when everybody was watching, but the seller had to get ready for it.

Wise had been building her own business selling Tupperware under the name Patio Parties. Her operation began attracting the manufacturer's attention. The question for Tupper was whether he should continue distributing his products in the usual way while a separate group of dealers sold them at parties.

In 1951 he changed the business. He hired Wise to run the direct-selling operation and pulled Tupperware out of retail stores.

This was more than a new advertisement. If a customer wanted a bowl, the dealer network was now the route to the customer. If that network stopped putting on parties, the company's ordinary retail shelves would not be there to pick up the slack. Growing the company meant finding and training people, not just making more plastic bowls.

A dealer couldn't run every demonstration in the country. Wise helped build a structure of sellers, managers and distributors. Distributors got the products into the network; managers and experienced dealers helped bring new people into the business. The company published material that people in different locations could use to learn how to sell.

The job was particularly appealing to some women who wanted to make money but couldn't easily take a regular job outside the home. A party still required preparation, travel, invitations and an audience willing to buy. Results depended on the seller and her circumstances. But the format offered a different way to organise work.

Wise also gave the sales force an occasion to come together. At the company's Florida headquarters, annual gatherings known as Jubilees combined training, awards and elaborate celebrations. Successful dealers were given a public place in the life of the company. They could meet people doing the same work in other parts of the country and return home with practical ideas and recognition for their sales.

By 1954, the company was doing twenty-five million dollars in annual sales. In 1955, BusinessWeek put Wise on its cover, the first woman to appear there. She had come a long way from selling goods in somebody else's living room.

There were now two different kinds of power inside the business. Wise had a prominent position and a relationship with the people selling the product. Tupper owned the company and controlled its manufacturing. When they worked together, the bowls and the party network strengthened each other.

By 1957, manufacturing was having trouble keeping up with demand. Disagreements over management and expenses were becoming sharper.

One surviving piece of correspondence concerns a book Wise had written to encourage sellers. She had arranged for six thousand copies to be printed without first obtaining Tupper's permission. From the sales side, such a book belonged to the system of training and motivating the dealers. From the owner's side, it was an expenditure he had not approved.

Their correspondence also shows that communication between them had deteriorated. Tupper wanted financial records brought to him in New England. Wise replied that she was ill and could not make the trip. He flew to Florida himself in January 1958.

Wise lost her job. The break was abrupt, and it exposed a difference between being the most visible person in a company and actually owning part of it. She had no shares. She pursued a settlement, but there was no ownership stake to take with her.

She did have experience that nobody could take away. She knew how to demonstrate products, recruit sellers, organise a business around parties and speak to the people doing the work. She started a new company called Cinderella, selling cosmetics.

The new venture presented a revealing test. The Tupperware dealers had built careers around a method she had helped develop. Would they follow her to a new company when there were no bowls involved?

The next Tupperware Jubilee was organised without her. Her successor, Hamer Wilson, distributed an advertisement for Cinderella to the people who had come to the gathering. He openly gave them the opportunity to leave and join her.

We don't have to invent what Wise was thinking, or pretend to know the private feelings of every person in that audience. A former staff member, Pat Tahaney, later remembered that nobody got up. A dealer named Elsie Mortland explained her own decision in terms of her belief in the Tupperware products.

There were good practical reasons a person might stay with the existing business. A dealer who knew how to sell a bowl already understood the lid, the customer and the supply network. The cosmetics company offered a different product and a different business. The skill of giving a demonstration was transferable; the results of the previous demonstration were not.

Cinderella lasted about a year. Tupper sold Tupperware in 1958 for millions of dollars. Wise received compensation over her dismissal, but nothing from the sale of the company itself.

In later years she worked on other ventures and pursued art. Her role in Tupperware's rise did not disappear from history. Her papers, photographs and the records of the business were eventually preserved, and a Florida park was named in her honour decades after her dismissal.

The inventor had solved a problem in the kitchen. Wise had solved a problem in the shop: how to make somebody understand a product by letting them see it work. Her success had helped create a business that could keep running even after she left. The little party had become an institution, and the institution was no longer hers.
