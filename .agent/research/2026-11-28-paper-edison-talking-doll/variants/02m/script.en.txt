One customer sent back an Edison talking doll after its voice faded during an hour of play.

In 1890, Thomas Edison and his business partners put a very unusual toy on the market. A porcelain-headed doll had a miniature record player hidden in its metal body. There were holes in its chest for sound to escape and a crank in its back.

Turn the crank and a human voice would recite a nursery rhyme. Each wax record had to be made separately. Women at the factory repeated the same verses into recording machines, over and over.

The toy cost ten dollars without clothes. With a costume, it could cost twice as much.

But the little mechanism had trouble surviving ordinary use. Needles came loose, wax broke, and the speed of the crank changed the sound. In April, a Boston toy dealer complained that several dolls had already been returned. Some had stopped speaking altogether.

Production ended in early May. Edison worked on a replacement, but the toy company could not raise enough money to manufacture it.

More than a century later, scientists had a different problem. A metal recording from an earlier experiment, made in 1888, was bent out of shape. They couldn't safely play it on a phonograph.

Instead, they scanned its surface in three dimensions and turned the tiny variations in its groove into digital sound.

In 2011, a woman's voice reciting Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star emerged from the damaged ring. Almost the entire recording had survived.

That wasn't a recording from one of the returned toys. It was an early experiment, preserved long after the business had failed. The contrast is remarkable: a voice survived a hundred and twenty-three years, yet a toy sold to a family could barely last an afternoon. What had happened to that customer's doll?
