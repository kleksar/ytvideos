Why did Thomas Edison put a record player inside a doll? Why were toy stores sending those dolls back to the factory? And how did one of the experimental voices survive long after the toy business collapsed?

In the spring of 1890, the Edison works in West Orange, New Jersey, was turning out dolls nearly two feet tall. Their porcelain heads and jointed limbs looked like other expensive dolls of the period. The difference was inside the metal torso. A miniature phonograph sat behind a group of holes in the chest.

There was no battery and no switch. A small handle at the back turned a wax cylinder. A needle followed the groove cut into its surface, causing a thin diaphragm to vibrate. Those vibrations became the sound of someone reciting a nursery rhyme.

Each doll could repeat a verse lasting roughly twenty seconds. And because there wasn't yet a practical way to mass-produce identical recordings, people had to perform the verses again for each new record. The women making the recordings were part of the production line, just like the people assembling the mechanisms.

The dolls had been a long time coming. Edison had noted the possibility of talking toys back in 1877. Ten years later, an inventor named William Jacques demonstrated a small device intended for toys. Edison licensed the project, and his assistant Charles Batchelor worked on the mechanism. They tried different materials and designs before the commercial version arrived.

The company was thinking big. By early 1889, its president reported that it had ordered a hundred thousand doll bodies from Germany. That was an order, not a count of dolls made or sold. The company still had to turn those parts and the phonographs into a toy that could survive the journey to the store.

Even before the launch, there was a warning. In March 1890, one of the investors wrote that she admired the dolls on display but found the words difficult to understand unless she knew the verse in advance.

Then the dolls went on sale. A newspaper listed the price at ten dollars for one without clothes, rising to twenty for a dressed doll. This was not a throwaway novelty. Someone was paying for a toy that was supposed to speak whenever they turned the handle.

On April twenty-fifth, a Boston dealer sent the company a letter. Five or six dolls had recently come back. The works inside some of them were loose. Others simply wouldn't talk. A customer from Salem reported that a doll's voice had become almost inaudible after an hour of use. Another return had cost the shop money just to get the toy shipped back.

There were several ways for these machines to fail. A needle could become detached from the diaphragm. A wax record could crack. Repeated playing could wear the groove. Even a doll in perfect mechanical condition didn't have a particularly clear voice, and a child had to keep the crank moving at a reasonably steady speed.

The factory stopped producing the dolls in early May. A later company memorandum recorded over ten thousand dolls accepted from the works. Around seventy-five hundred were still packed in storage a year after the launch. The difference doesn't establish how many families actually bought one.

Edison tried to improve the phonograph. By July, the toy company's directors had examined his new models and asked him to make fifty machines by hand. It looked like there might be a second attempt. But by the end of October the company owed more than fifty thousand dollars and couldn't obtain a loan to put the revised toy into production.

The voice project, though, wasn't entirely lost.

In 2011, scientists at a laboratory in California examined a metal recording that had been made for an experimental doll in 1888, before the wax records went on sale. The ring was badly bent. Playing it with a needle could not solve the problem.

So they measured the shape of the groove with a three-dimensional optical scanner. A computer reconstructed the sound from the measurements. A woman reciting Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star had left her voice in the metal. Only the first syllable was missing from the recovered recording.

The laboratory hadn't repaired a broken toy or brought a commercial doll back to life. It had recovered an earlier human performance from a piece of damaged metal.

The people buying those dolls in 1890 couldn't know that one of the experiments would be heard a century later. They wanted something far simpler: a toy that would still talk tomorrow.
