How did a nursery rhyme become a ten-dollar toy in the nineteenth century? Why did the machine that spoke it fail in the hands of customers? And what would it take to hear a voice from the experiments again, a hundred and twenty-three years later?

Start with an ordinary doll. The expensive models of the late nineteenth century could have porcelain heads, jointed limbs and carefully made clothes. A maker could change a face or give the doll a different costume. Giving it an actual human voice was a much more difficult problem.

In November 1877, while Thomas Edison was developing his phonograph, he noted that the principle might be used to make toys produce speech and other sounds. His early machine recorded sound by making impressions in a sheet of foil. It could reproduce a voice, but turning that process into something people could buy and use at home took further work.

In 1887, an inventor named William White Jacques showed one of Edison's associates an unfinished device for talking toys. Jacques needed permission to make commercial use of the patented phonograph principle. Edison agreed to license him, and a company was formed to turn the idea into a business.

At the laboratory, Edison's assistant Charles Batchelor worked on the toy phonograph. A full-sized machine would not fit inside the body of a doll. It needed to be small, inexpensive and easy to operate. Batchelor experimented with diaphragms made of different materials and designed ways to return the reproducing needle to its starting position.

They also tried different recording surfaces. In 1888, some of the experimental recordings were made on metal rings. In 1889, work continued with wax. By the time the toy reached stores, wax was the material inside the commercial dolls.

The device that customers could buy in 1890 stood about twenty-two inches tall. Behind the porcelain head and clothes was a metal body containing a miniature phonograph. Its little horn pointed toward openings in the chest. A crank stuck out from the back.

The doll didn't need to move its mouth to talk. Rotating the handle moved the cylinder beneath a needle. The needle moved in response to the shape of the groove and set the diaphragm vibrating. The horn directed that sound out of the body.

The process that made the groove worked the other way around. A human voice set a recording diaphragm in motion. Its movements were transferred to a cutting point, leaving a physical trace of the sound on the cylinder. A second needle could follow that trace later.

There was a peculiar consequence for the factory. It couldn't simply make one master recording and press thousands of cheap copies of it. A commercially practical method of duplication was not yet available. Women were employed to make recordings of familiar verses, and individual records were cut as they performed them.

Two dolls carrying the same nursery rhyme might therefore contain different performances. An ordinary machine part could be replaced with another piece of the same specification. But the exact performance on a damaged cylinder could be lost.

Meanwhile, the people selling the dolls were making very large plans. In February 1889, Jacques reported that a hundred thousand doll bodies had been ordered from Germany. That didn't mean a hundred thousand finished toys existed. The parts still had to arrive and be fitted with working mechanisms in New Jersey.

The intended customers were adults buying gifts, often for children. The problem was finding a price they would actually pay. The price advertised in a New York newspaper in April 1890 was ten dollars for a doll without a costume. Dressed versions ranged from twelve to twenty.

At the factory, there was an even more basic question: could the expensive toy reliably perform its one new trick?

One of the company's investors wrote in March that she thought the dolls themselves were beautiful. But unless she already knew the words they were meant to say, she had trouble understanding them. That wasn't a complaint made after a hundred years of deterioration. It was made before the dolls were released for sale.

Still, the company launched the product. Newspapers had been reporting on the idea for over a year. In April 1890, Scientific American put illustrations of the dolls and their manufacture on its cover. It was a remarkable new thing to see a familiar nursery toy fitted with a machine that could replay someone's speech.

And then the shops began reporting what had happened after the dolls left their counters.

On April twenty-fifth, Horace Partridge and Company, a toy dealer in Boston, wrote to the company about five or six recent returns. The mechanisms in some dolls had worked loose. Other dolls wouldn't speak at all. One customer in Salem reported that the voice had grown faint during about an hour of play until the words were impossible to understand.

The dealer had also shipped a doll to St. Joseph, Missouri. The buyer sent it back, and the shop reported spending roughly a dollar and sixty cents to retrieve it. The dealer planned to charge the expense to the toy company. Selling another doll was no longer just a matter of putting a box on a train. Each new shipment could bring another return.

These were not all the same fault. During development the engineers had already encountered a detached reproducing needle. Wax could crack, and the team had considered changing the way the wax ring fitted around its support. A worn groove could also produce a weaker, noisier sound, consistent with the Salem complaint, although we cannot establish the precise defect in that particular doll.

Even before a part broke, the sound had limitations. A crank turned more slowly would slow down the voice; a faster crank would speed it up. A toy phonograph lacked the carefully controlled playback of later machines. The fact that it could reproduce speech at all did not guarantee that every word would be clear.

Production ceased in early May, only about three months after it began. The number of dolls actually bought by customers is unknown. A later memorandum listed ten thousand and sixty dolls accepted by the toy company. In May of the following year, around seventy-five hundred were still in boxes. The balance is not a reliable retail sales total: shipped, returned and sold are different things.

For Edison, stopping production was not the end of the engineering work. He kept experimenting with the toy phonograph. In July, the directors inspected new models and asked him to build fifty machines by hand. They had not placed an order for a second mass-production run. First they needed a revised machine.

But improving the design required money. By the end of October 1890, the company was more than fifty thousand dollars in debt. It could not borrow against its stock of goods. There was no money to finance a new production run, even if the models now looked promising.

A different opportunity to work on the dolls appeared much later, in a museum collection.

The National Park Service preserved a metal ring believed to be the earliest surviving recording for an experimental talking doll, dated to late 1888. It was not one of the commercial wax cylinders from the Boston returns. The metal had lost its original shape, making conventional playback impractical.

At Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, scientists Carl Haber and Earl Cornell had helped develop a way to read damaged recording media without having to drag a needle through their grooves. Their equipment measured the surface in three dimensions. The computer could use those measurements to reconstruct the signal encoded by the groove, even though the original object was bent.

The result, in May 2011, was a digital recording of an unidentified woman reciting Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star. All but the first syllable of the verse could be recovered. Other rare doll records were later digitized, and by 2015 eight examples were available through the historical park.

The restored sound is not a guarantee of what an undamaged toy sounded like in 1890. The experimental recording is from a different material and year, and the sound comes from a digital reconstruction rather than a child's crank.

But it does preserve a real performance that would otherwise have been inaccessible. What a factory once needed a person to repeat for each individual recording, a computer could finally reproduce from a damaged survivor.

The two problems are separated by more than a century. Edison was trying to make recorded speech work every day in a child's hands. The later scientists were trying to recover a single voice without damaging the object that held it. The first business failed. The second attempt recovered almost a whole nursery rhyme.

And that is why a small metal ring can be one of the most precious survivors of a toy that was meant to be bought, taken home and played with.
