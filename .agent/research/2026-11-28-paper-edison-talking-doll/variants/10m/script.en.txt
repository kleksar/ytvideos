How did a nursery rhyme become a ten-dollar toy in the nineteenth century? Why did the machine that spoke it fail in the hands of customers? And what would it take to hear a voice from the experiments again, a hundred and twenty-three years later?

In the spring of 1890, a customer could buy a doll nearly two feet tall, with a porcelain head and jointed limbs. Beneath the clothes was a metal torso with holes in the chest. Inside it sat a miniature phonograph, and out of the back protruded a crank.

The doll didn't need to move its mouth to talk. Rotating the handle moved the cylinder beneath a needle. The needle moved in response to the shape of the groove and set the diaphragm vibrating. The horn directed that sound out of the body.

The doll cost ten dollars without clothes, and up to twenty in a costume. For that price, the buyer got a human voice that could recite a nursery rhyme whenever someone turned the handle. Yet in March, before the launch, an investor had written to the company that she could hardly make out the words unless she knew the rhyme already.

On April twenty-fifth, Horace Partridge and Company, a toy dealer in Boston, wrote to the company about five or six recent returns. The mechanisms in some dolls had worked loose. Other dolls wouldn't speak at all. One customer in Salem reported that the voice had grown faint during about an hour of play until the words were impossible to understand.

The same shop had sent another doll to a buyer in Missouri. Getting it back cost about a dollar and sixty cents, and the shop wanted the toy company to cover that expense. The dealer was reluctant to ship any more dolls while so many were coming back. How could a machine that had taken years to develop be so vulnerable?

Edison had considered talking toys as early as 1877, when his phonograph still recorded impressions in foil. A decade later, William White Jacques demonstrated a small unfinished device for toys. He obtained permission to use Edison's phonograph patents, and a company was set up to sell the product. Charles Batchelor, Edison's assistant, worked on shrinking the instrument and getting the needle back to the start of the recording.

In May 1889, Albert Blake Dick wrote from Paris that the needles had come loose from the glass diaphragms in most of the dolls he had taken there. By the next letter, the same part had failed in all of them. He had bought glue and managed to repair two. The weakness was already visible well before the toys appeared in American stores.

The recording itself was another vulnerable part. The laboratory had tried metal rings in 1888 and later switched to wax for the commercial toy. In January 1890, Edison employee Walter Mallory took a doll home to Chicago by train. When he tried it after the journey, it made a crackling noise. Inspecting the wax, he found damage in one spot. He suspected that the movement of the train had made the needle rub against that spot repeatedly.

Replacing a broken recording was a more complicated matter than replacing a needle. To make a record, someone spoke into a recording device. The vibrations of the voice drove a cutting point, leaving a groove that a playback needle could later follow. The factory did not have a practical method for making thousands of identical copies from one master.

Batchelor had another problem to solve: who would say the nursery rhymes? In August 1889 he asked the toy company to send its list of verses early, because the people being trained to record them needed practice. A theatrical agent approached about supplying young performers reported in September that children in training had school commitments, while those already performing were away on tour.

An illustration published by Scientific American the following spring shows a young woman at a recording machine in a small cubicle. The accompanying account describes a room with many performers repeating children's verses at the same time. Every separate performance mattered: two dolls reciting the same rhyme might carry different recordings. A damaged cylinder could mean the loss of one particular performance.

Meanwhile, the people selling the dolls were making very large plans. In February 1889, Jacques reported that a hundred thousand doll bodies had been ordered from Germany. That didn't mean a hundred thousand finished toys existed. The parts still had to arrive and be fitted with working mechanisms in New Jersey.

When the dolls reached the market in April 1890, the company still had reason to hope. A San Francisco businessman reported receiving samples on April twenty-fourth. Two were damaged and four were unable to speak properly. He nevertheless considered the working examples excellent and thought they could sell. A good doll could impress a dealer; getting a good doll into every customer's hands was another matter.

The Boston dealer wrote the following day. The dolls' defects were not all the same: needles could detach, a jolt could damage the wax, and the groove could wear down with repeated use. The Salem customer's complaint about declining volume fits the last possibility, but the precise fault in that doll is unknown. Even an intact machine needed a fairly steady hand on its crank to make the speech intelligible.

Production ceased in early May, only about three months after it began. The number of dolls actually bought by customers is unknown. A later memorandum listed ten thousand and sixty dolls accepted by the toy company. In May of the following year, around seventy-five hundred were still in boxes. The balance is not a reliable retail sales total: shipped, returned and sold are different things.

For Edison, stopping production was not the end of the engineering work. He kept experimenting with the toy phonograph. In July, the directors inspected new models and asked him to build fifty machines by hand. They had not placed an order for a second mass-production run. First they needed a revised machine.

There were still individual dolls to deal with. On July twenty-eighth, Edison forwarded a repair request to the toy company's manager, noting that the doll had reached the works and had been passed to an employee there. The surviving letter does not tell us whether the owner ever received a working toy back. It does show that the factory was handling at least one repair even after mass production had stopped.

But improving the design required money. By the end of October 1890, the company was more than fifty thousand dollars in debt. It could not borrow against its stock of goods. There was no money to finance a new production run, even if the models now looked promising.

Decades later, the problem was no longer how to keep a toy working in a nursery. It was how to retrieve the voice without destroying what remained of the recording. In 1967, museum curators cataloged a metal ring among objects from the desk of Edison's secretary, William Meadowcroft. A paper label identified it as a tin phonograph cylinder. The ring was badly bent.

At Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, scientists Carl Haber and Earl Cornell had helped develop a way to read damaged recording media without having to drag a needle through their grooves. Their equipment measured the surface in three dimensions. The computer could use those measurements to reconstruct the signal encoded by the groove, even though the original object was bent.

On May eleventh, 2011, a woman's voice emerged from the reconstructed signal: she was reciting Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star. Only the first syllable was missing. Historian Patrick Feaster searched archival documents to establish the recording's identity and date. The ring came from an experiment in late 1888, before the wax cylinders sold in 1890. It was never established as the record from any returned doll.

There is a useful comparison with another survivor. In 1984, a visitor brought a well-preserved commercial doll to the Edison museum, still in its box with its instructions. Curator Edward Pershey fetched a cassette recorder. The visitor operated the doll, and the recorder captured its recital of Little Jack Horner along with the sound of the mechanism. That recording documents an actual playback, whereas the voice from the bent ring was recovered without operating a doll.

By 2015, eight different doll recordings had been digitized. They can preserve human performances, but the recovered sound from the metal ring cannot tell us exactly how a customer's wax-cylinder doll sounded in 1890. The woman who spoke into the early recording machine remains unidentified. Her voice is still there.

The customer in Salem had returned a machine whose voice had faded after only an hour of use. More than a century later, a museum and a laboratory could preserve a different performance that had been made before that toy went on sale. Edison had wanted to give a doll a voice that could be heard every day. The technology was not ready for that ordinary demand. Yet a voice left in one of the experiments was still waiting to be heard.
