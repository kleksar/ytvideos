The dough is ready. I have six cream puffs to bake and a cold oven in front of me. I could wait for it to heat, or put the tray straight inside. Would skipping ten minutes of waiting actually save electricity? And would the puffs still be big enough to fill?

I'm going to compare A, a preheated oven, with B, a cold start. Pick a side. I'll give both the same dough, six portions of twenty-five grams each, the same electric fan oven and the same temperature setting. I want the lowest electricity use, but there's a catch. A cold-start puff has to retain at least ninety percent of the average volume of my preheated reference, and its top has to be close in color. That is my decision rule for this model, not a rule for food safety or a guarantee of taste.

The numbers come from published oven trials. I haven't baked these trays myself. Each method also includes ten minutes in the oven after switching the power off. That matters because the puffs don't come out as soon as I turn the dial.

I start A's clock while the oven is still cold. Ten minutes pass before the tray goes inside. Then I give it eighteen minutes of powered baking. The oven goes off, and the tray stays for ten more minutes. That's about thirty-eight minutes from the start, leaving aside handling time.

For B I put the tray into the cold oven and turn it on. I'm not going to take it out after eighteen minutes just because that's what I used for A. The dough is spending part of its baking time in an oven that's still warming up. I give it twenty-one minutes of powered baking and the same ten minutes of resting inside. The clock reads thirty-one minutes.

So although B bakes for three minutes longer with the dough inside and the power on, I get my tray about seven minutes earlier overall.

Time isn't my only problem. A cream puff needs room inside. The published trials measured the volume, and A's mean is 134.7 milliliters per large puff. B's is 124.8. They're not quite the same size. But B retains about ninety-three percent, and the measured difference in top color is less than one unit on the color scale used in the trials. It passes my two quality requirements.

Now I can compare the electricity. If A's consumption is represented by a hundred units, B's is seventy-seven. That's twenty-three percent less energy, not a promise of twenty-three percent off my whole electricity bill.

What happens if I let B bake longer? Two other cold-start timings in the color-matched comparison are twenty-two and a half minutes and twenty-four minutes. They use eighty-four and eighty-nine energy units respectively. The first gives me a mean volume of 126.7 milliliters; the second gives me 124.3. More time isn't a reliable way to keep getting bigger puffs in these data.

Under my original ninety-percent rule, all three qualify. The twenty-one-minute option uses the least electricity. But say I need at least ninety-five percent of the reference volume for a dessert. That puts my cutoff just under 128 milliliters. None of these three cold-start options reaches it. The preheated reference wins under that stricter rule.

I'd choose B if I could accept the slightly smaller puffs. I'd choose A if preserving that extra volume mattered more. This isn't permission to skip preheating for every recipe. It's a comparison of one particular dough and oven, with two different definitions of success. The best result isn't necessarily the puff that spends the shortest time baking, or the one that uses the least electricity at any cost. It's the one that meets the requirement I set before I switch the oven on.
