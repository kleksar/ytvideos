Twenty degrees or forty? I want my laundry clean, but I don't want to pay for heating water I didn't need. Which setting would you choose?

I'll compare two cotton washes using published laboratory results, not pretend I've run the experiment at home. Both use the same washing machine, the same good detergent, a half load and the same stain remover before washing.

My rule: the winner must beat a cleaning score of 1.03. If both do, I choose the one using less electricity. That's my strict decision line, based on the study's reference level.

At forty degrees, the cycle takes 178 minutes. At twenty, it finishes 34 minutes earlier. It uses 70 watt-hours per kilogram instead of 178. That's about 61 percent less electricity.

But is the laundry clean enough?

The lab measured how much light the washed stain strips reflected, compared with a reference wash. The warm cycle scored 1.112. The cold cycle scored 1.032. Look how close that is to my line! Both qualify, so cold wins on electricity.

Now I remove the stain pretreatment from both sides. Everything else in the setup stays the same. The forty-degree score barely moves: 1.109. The twenty-degree score falls to 0.994.

Under my strict rule, warm now wins. The study also discusses a verification tolerance under which that cold score could qualify; I'm not declaring it a regulatory failure.

So cold can be a good energy-saving choice, but I can't assume it cleans equally well. These are particular programs from a 2014 laboratory test, not a promise about my machine. Hygiene wasn't tested.

For ordinary laundry I check the care label and choose a suitable program and detergent. If I need to change something, I check the stains before blaming the temperature. Next time both settings are suitable, I still have a choice to make.
