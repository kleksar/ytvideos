I have a load of stained cotton to wash and two buttons that look almost identical. One says twenty degrees Celsius, the other says forty. Can I save electricity without sacrificing the result I need? And could changing the way I deal with stains change which button I press?

I don't have two identical washing machines in my kitchen. Instead, I'm going to reconstruct a published laboratory comparison from 2014. The testers used cotton towels, sheets and pillowcases, and attached fabric strips carrying standard stains. That gives me a much fairer starting point than picking two shirts out of a laundry basket and guessing which was dirtier.

I'll put the twenty-degree program on the left and the forty-degree cotton program on the right. Both have the same machine, a half load, the same good detergent and the same stain pretreatment. The winner has to score above 1.03 on the study's cleaning index. If both qualify, I take the one that uses less electricity. It's a strict comparison rule I've chosen, not a claim that every household must meet that number.

Which side would you choose before I turn over the results?

First, the clock. The forty-degree cycle takes two hours and 58 minutes. The twenty-degree cycle finishes in two hours and 24 minutes. Already the cold program has saved 34 minutes. But those are the actual durations of these two programs, not a rule that cold water always makes a machine faster.

Now the electricity meters. On the right I have 178 watt-hours per kilogram of laundry. On the left, 70. The cold wash uses only about two fifths as much electricity. I haven't won yet, though. If I have to wash everything again, the cheap cycle might not be a good decision.

This is where the little stained strips matter. After washing, the testers dried them and measured their reflectance. A higher combined result compared with a reference wash gives a higher cleaning index. It isn't a percentage of dirt removed.

Forty degrees gets 1.112. The twenty-degree result is 1.032. Both are above my target of 1.03, but one is almost touching the line. I can choose cold under the rule I announced, even though the warmer program removed stains better by this measure.

Now I'm going to change one part of the setup: the stain pretreatment. I remove it on both sides, keeping the good machine, detergent and half load. The right-hand cleaning score moves from 1.112 to 1.109. Hardly any change. On the left it drops from 1.032 to 0.994.

Now my strict target sends me to the warm cycle instead. That's not a legal verdict on the cold program. The paper discusses a four-percent verification tolerance, and the authors consider the cold result without pretreatment capable of meeting the benchmark with that tolerance. My deliberately strict line shows how a decision can change when I demand a particular result.

What is the saving worth in money? I'll use an example of four kilograms of laundry and an electricity price of thirty cents per kilowatt-hour. I'm assuming the tested energy per kilogram applies to this example. With pretreatment, the modeled bill for the cold cycle is 8.4 cents; the warm cycle is about 21 cents. The difference is around thirteen cents. At three such washes a week for a year, that is about twenty dollars in washing electricity. It doesn't include the price of products, water or drying, and it isn't a forecast for every family.

For my own laundry, the useful lesson is not to choose twenty degrees blindly. The care label, compatible detergent, stain treatment and actual program all matter. And this study didn't test hygiene, so I can't turn its stain scores into advice for laundry requiring disinfection.

I began by looking for the cheapest wash. I ended up with a better rule: choose the cheapest suitable cycle that meets the cleaning result I need. In this comparison, a small change before the door even closes determines whether cold meets my target.
