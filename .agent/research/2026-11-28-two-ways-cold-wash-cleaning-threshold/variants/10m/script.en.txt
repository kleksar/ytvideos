If I could turn my washing machine down from forty degrees Celsius to twenty, would I actually save money? Would I get the same cleaning result? And if a stain doesn't come out, how many second attempts can I afford before the saving disappears?

I've got an ordinary household problem. I need to wash cotton laundry, and I don't want to heat more water than necessary. But I also don't want to pull the laundry out and discover that I have to start over. Rather than pretending to test two machines myself, I'll use measurements from a laboratory study carried out in December 2014 and published the following year.

On my left is the twenty-degree program, on my right the forty-degree standard cotton program. Both sides start with the same kind of cotton laundry, the same machine and a half load. They use the same detergent and the same pretreatment for the stains. The researchers chose products and machines they described as good performers for this comparison. I'm not going to turn those descriptions into recommendations for brands I don't know.

There are five standard kinds of dirt on the lab's little test strips, including oily soil, blood, chocolate and milk, and red wine. The researchers wash the strips along with cotton towels, sheets and pillowcases. Afterwards the dried strips are measured for how much light they reflect. Combining those readings and comparing them with a reference wash produces the cleaning index. A higher number means better cleaning by this measure, not a literal percentage of dirt removed.

Before I show either result, I'll draw a line at 1.03, the nominal reference level discussed in the paper. My rule is strict: I want to exceed that line. Among the cycles that do, I choose the lower electricity use per kilogram. I also record the time. If you're making this choice with me, pick twenty or forty now.

The cold program finishes in 144 minutes. The warm program is still running for another 34. When both finish, the energy meter says 70 watt-hours per kilogram on the left and 178 on the right. That's a difference of 108 watt-hours for every kilogram of laundry, or just over sixty percent of the warm program's consumption.

If electricity were the only criterion, I'd be done. But the cleaned strips give me a much closer contest. The warm program scores 1.112. The cold program scores 1.032. Both exceed the line. The cold result is above it by only two thousandths of a point. That is enough for my decision rule, so my first round goes to cold. It does not mean the two cleaning scores are equal.

Now I want to know whether the step before washing matters. In another matched pair from the same research, I leave the pretreatment out of both sides. I keep the same good machine, good detergent and half load. Neither side gets special treatment.

The forty-degree cycle now scores 1.109, only slightly lower than before. The cold one scores 0.994. That falls below my strict target. The cold cycle still uses less electricity, 81 watt-hours per kilogram compared with 173. But my rule doesn't award a win just for consuming less. Warm is the only qualifying choice in this round.

There is an important detail here. The study itself discusses a verification tolerance of four percent, which is why its authors say the cold result without stain remover could meet the minimum benchmark. My strict line isn't that verification procedure. If I were satisfied with a lower cleaning score, the result of my decision would change. If I demanded more than the first cold result, it wouldn't qualify even with pretreatment. A winner only makes sense in relation to the goal I've chosen.

What about the detergent? Perhaps I'm paying for heat when changing another part of the wash would have a bigger effect.

I can compare the same good machine at half load without stain remover, using different detergents in the published tests. With the lower-rated detergent, the cold score is 0.918 and the warm score is 1.012. With the better detergent, the scores are 0.994 and 1.109. Both temperatures improve.

Here's the comparison that makes me reconsider my assumption about the dial. The cold program with the better detergent and stain pretreatment scored 1.032. The warm program with the lower-rated detergent and no pretreatment scored 1.012. The first score is higher. That isn't a fair way to declare the temperature alone responsible; I've changed two other things. But it is a good reason not to assume that every forty-degree wash beats every twenty-degree wash.

The basket matters too. The researchers tried a medium-rated detergent with no stain remover in the same good machine. With a half load, the cold cleaning score was 0.970 and the warm one was 1.085. With a full load, those scores were 0.921 and 1.009. In the full-load test, the electricity per kilogram fell at both temperatures. That offers a trade-off: the energy number per kilogram can improve while the stain score drops. The numbers don't tell me to always run a half-empty machine; they tell me that load size belongs in the comparison.

The two temperatures also came with different programs and running times. The research explains cleaning as an interaction of temperature, chemistry, mechanical action and time. That's why I cannot take these figures and predict what happens if I simply shorten the program on another machine. I'd have changed the experiment.

Now I'll put the first two energy numbers into a household example. Suppose each load is four kilograms and the measured energy per kilogram applies to it. At twenty degrees, that makes 0.280 kilowatt-hours. At forty, 0.712. At a hypothetical price of thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, I'd pay 8.4 cents for the cold cycle and 21.36 cents for the warm one. That's nearly thirteen cents saved for each successful cold wash. If I do three such loads every week, 156 loads in a year, I save about 67.4 kilowatt-hours, or twenty dollars and twenty-two cents.

That is a calculation, not a year's measurements. It covers washing electricity only. If I buy a different detergent or use a different amount of stain remover, their costs matter. If I dry the laundry in a machine afterwards, that adds a different energy bill. These test results do not tell me the total cost of washing and drying at home.

And now the second attempt. I'll use the same modeled energy per wash for a hypothetical rewash. Two cold cycles would total 0.560 kilowatt-hours, still below one warm cycle. But three cold cycles would reach 0.840, more than one warm wash. If I try cold once and then run the warm cycle, the total becomes 0.992 kilowatt-hours. These are arithmetic examples, not measured cleaning outcomes. The paper didn't show that repeatedly running the same stained load would remove the remaining dirt, and I don't know whether the energy figures would stay identical on a second wash.

What do I actually do with all of this? I start with the laundry's care instructions and choose a suitable program and detergent. If there are stains, I follow the product and fabric instructions rather than treating every stain in the same way. I can use the lower-temperature setting where it is suitable and check the result. I can't use this study to settle a question of disinfection: hygiene was outside its tests. My machine and today's detergents can differ from those tested.

I haven't discovered one magic temperature for every load. I've discovered why the cheapest button is not necessarily the cheapest way to get my laundry clean. In the first comparison, cold met my target at far lower energy use. Remove the pretreatment, keep the strict target, and the decision changes. What I do before closing the washing machine can be just as relevant to that choice as the number on the dial.
