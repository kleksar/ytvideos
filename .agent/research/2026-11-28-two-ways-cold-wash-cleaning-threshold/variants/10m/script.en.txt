Could a cheaper wash end up costing me more than the expensive one? I've got a load of stained cotton, and my machine offers twenty and forty degrees Celsius. I want to know which setting saves electricity, which gets the laundry clean enough, and what happens to the saving if I have to wash it again.

I'll put the two programs side by side. On each side I have a half load of cotton, the same washing machine, the same detergent and stain pretreatment. The numbers come from a laboratory study carried out in 2014, not from a test I've performed at home. Before I see the numbers, I'm going to make a choice: I'll take the program with lower electricity use, provided it exceeds a cleaning index of 1.03. That's a deliberately strict line based on the reference level in the paper, not a new household standard.

The twenty-degree program finishes after two hours and twenty-four minutes. I wait another thirty-four minutes for the forty-degree one. For each kilogram of laundry, the electricity use is seventy watt-hours on the cold side and one hundred and seventy-eight on the warm side. So if I only look at the meter, cold wins by a wide margin. But I'm not buying electricity for its own sake. I want clean laundry.

The testers had a way of comparing the two loads without having to guess whether one shirt looked dirtier than another. They used cotton bedding and towels with small strips of fabric carrying five standard stains. After washing and drying the strips, they measured how much light the stains reflected. Combining the results against a reference wash gives a cleaning index. The higher the index, the better the cleaning by that measure. It isn't a percentage of dirt removed, and it doesn't tell me whether every individual stain disappeared.

Here are the two results. Forty degrees scores 1.112. Twenty scores 1.032. The cold bar just clears my target of 1.03. Both qualify, so I'll take the cold program for this load. I'm using about sixty-one percent less electricity, but I'm also accepting a lower cleaning score than the warm program. That narrow margin above my target is going to matter.

Imagine I get another similar load ready and skip the stain remover. The paper contains another matched pair in which neither side received that treatment. Same machine, detergent and half load. This time the cold score drops to 0.994. The warm score barely changes, to 1.109. Cold still uses much less electricity: eighty-one instead of one hundred and seventy-three watt-hours per kilogram. But under my strict rule it's the warm program that qualifies. The cheapest cycle hasn't changed; my decision has.

Now I'll move the line rather than change the laundry. The authors discuss a four-percent verification tolerance and consider that cold score without pretreatment capable of meeting the benchmark. I'm not testing regulatory compliance here. In my own model, if I require a score above 0.99, both programs in that second pair qualify and cold wins again. If I require above 1.12, neither of the four results qualifies. There is no reason to force a winner. The test gives me the scores; the decision rule is mine.

I want to see what the first saving actually buys. Suppose I have four kilograms of laundry and the measured energy per kilogram applies to this load. The cold wash uses 0.280 kilowatt-hours and the warm wash 0.712. At thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, I'm saving just under thirteen cents on electricity. With three such successful washes every week, that's about twenty dollars and twenty-two cents a year. The tariff, load and frequency are assumptions. I haven't measured a year's bills, and I'm not including water, detergent, stain remover or drying.

What if I get the laundry out and decide I need another wash? I'll model the repeat using the first pair's energy and time figures, assuming each program takes the same time and energy on a repeat. Two cold cycles use 0.560 kilowatt-hours, which is still below one warm cycle. But the two cycles occupy the machine for four hours and forty-eight minutes instead of two hours and fifty-eight. I've saved electricity and lost nearly two hours. A third cold cycle would also erase the energy saving. None of these calculations establishes that a second or third wash actually cleans the remaining stains.

If I try cold and then switch to warm, I use 0.992 kilowatt-hours. That particular load costs more in electricity than going straight to warm. But suppose I do this over a series of loads, and only some of them need the second wash. At one warm repeat for every ten cold starts, the modeled average energy is about 0.351 kilowatt-hours per load. That's still well below 0.712. If just over six out of ten loads need that warm repeat, the average saving disappears. The study didn't measure that frequency. I'm drawing a break-even line, not predicting how often your laundry will need a rewash.

The time calculation is less forgiving. Even one warm repeat in five cold starts raises the average machine time to about 180 minutes per load, slightly longer than going straight to the warm program every time. That's because I saved only thirty-four minutes on the first wash. So I could have a good electricity result and a bad result if I urgently need the machine free. My choice depends on what I value and whether my first wash succeeds.

Could I improve the first wash instead of planning to repeat it? In another pair from the study, the machine and half load stay the same and neither side gets stain remover. I change the detergent category. With the lower-rated powder, the warm result is 1.012, below the 1.032 achieved in my original cold trial with better powder and stain treatment. I can't credit that difference to the temperature; I changed two other conditions. But I'd be wrong to assume that every forty-degree program outperforms every twenty-degree program.

There's another useful comparison. The researchers also tested a lower-performing machine with a lower-rated powder. On that setup, adding stain remover raised the twenty-degree score from 0.811 to 0.896 and the forty-degree score from 0.928 to 0.999. Both improved; neither reached my strict line. In the earlier good-machine comparison, stain treatment was enough for cold to pass. Here it isn't. I can't turn that first result into a guarantee that buying a stain remover makes any cold wash adequate.

My basket is another choice. In the same good machine, using the medium-rated powder without pretreatment, going from half to full load lowered electricity per kilogram at both temperatures. It also lowered the cleaning score at both temperatures. That's a trade-off, not a recommendation to run a half-empty machine all the time. These are specific programs with different running times and mechanical conditions. I can't isolate temperature from everything else or transfer the result to a different machine by changing its timer.

So the next time I have an ordinary load of cotton, I look at the care label, choose a suitable program and detergent, and deal with stains according to the fabric and product instructions. If cold is suitable, I can try it and judge whether the result is adequate. I won't use a stain score to make a disinfection decision; the researchers did not test hygiene. If the clothes need special care, that requirement comes first.

I started with two temperature buttons and an electricity bill. Now I have two questions to answer before pressing either one: what will count as clean enough for this load, and what would it cost me to start again? In the first laboratory comparison, twenty degrees cleared my strict line and saved electricity. Without pretreatment, forty was the only qualifying option under that same line. The extra calculation tells me why it's worth getting the first wash right. The cheapest wash is the one that meets the result I need without unnecessary work.
