Would I save more water if my shower head could tell me when to stop? And how much of that saving would actually show up on my water meter?

I've got two ways to take the same shower. In A, I have a shower head that records water use but doesn't tell me anything while I wash. In B, I have the exact same kind of shower head, with a colour warning turned on and a sign explaining the colours. There's no automatic shutoff. I can keep washing if I want to.

Before I show the result, pick a side. My target is the smallest total volume of water used by the household per day. I don't get extra points for a green light if I use the water somewhere else.

I'm modelling this comparison from a real experiment in Singapore. In 2020, researchers recruited households in four housing estates. The people who joined had the same type of recording shower device installed in both bathrooms. For the first month, the researchers could find out how much water was normally being used. After that, the households were assigned to the two groups.

One group continued with its lights off. The other got colour feedback and the explanation in the shower cubicle. I like this comparison because both groups had the recording hardware from the start. The treatment wasn't simply a swap from a powerful shower head to a low-flow one.

The first interesting result came from a question the researchers asked before the lights went on. How much water do you think you use when you shower? The average answer was around thirteen litres. The recorded average was about twenty-five.

Now imagine I've stepped into side B. The shower head is green at first, then blue, then purple. At eighteen litres it turns red; beyond twenty it flashes red. I can still rinse the shampoo out, but I no longer have to guess how much water I've used.

The group that received these signals initially saved around seven litres per shower. After two weeks the saving settled at about five litres. Across the treatment period, the estimated average saving was 5.54 litres per shower. So the signal didn't need to cut the water supply to change the outcome.

At this point I'd be tempted to declare B the winner and stop the comparison. But I chose a different finish line: the meter for the whole home.

I'll use a common starting budget based on the study's household averages. Imagine two copies of the same home, each using 496 litres per day. Shower use accounts for 163 litres. The other 333 cover all the rest.

Now I apply the reported household-level effects to the B copy, leaving A as the reference. The shower saving is 37 litres per day. That brings the shower budget down to 126. If the other category stays at 333, I'd expect the daily total to be 459.

The total reduction reported in the study was only 23.5 litres. The implied difference outside the shower category is an increase of 13.5 litres a day. My model therefore has 126 litres for showers plus 346.5 for everything else, giving 472.5 litres in total.

This doesn't tell me which tap used those extra litres. A shower head can also be used for cleaning. People might change where they get water, or change some other habit. The study doesn't establish the precise explanation. I can't honestly draw a particular person wasting water and call that what happened.

What I can show is the difference between the two finish lines. The estimated daily shower saving was 37 litres, but the whole-home saving was 23.5. Roughly 36 percent of the former didn't reach the household meter.

For this study, I'd choose B. It's a useful reduction, not a promise that every home will save the same amount. I'd also keep an eye on the meter after the novelty wears off: the households were observed for months, not years.

A little light helped people notice their water use. The bigger lesson for me is to measure the thing I actually want to improve. If my goal is to save household water, the final score belongs to the household meter.
