If I put a water warning light in my shower, will I save water, or will I just get better at making one little number look good? And if I wanted to find out whether the gadget was worth using at home, which numbers should I measure?

I'm comparing two copies of the same daily routine. On side A I have a recording shower head, but its lights are turned off. On side B I have the same shower head, with the warning switched on and a sign explaining the colours. Neither automatically shuts off the water when I reach a target.

Pick a side. My main score is litres of water used by the whole household per day. I'll keep a separate score for each shower to see what changes in the bathroom, but only the first decides the winner.

This is a model based on a real experiment in Singapore, not two showers I've personally taken.

Here is the surprise in the experiment. The warning saved water in the bathroom, but for every roughly three litres saved there, about one was offset elsewhere in the home. I want to see how two meters can tell such different stories without either one being broken.

In the Singapore study, people were asked to estimate their own shower use. Before the warnings were activated, the average answer was about thirteen litres. The recorded average was about twenty-five. That's why a warning might be useful even when the shower head stays the same: it shows me something I can't otherwise see.

For the field experiment, 407 households were recruited from four high-rise housing estates. The researchers installed recording shower heads in both bathrooms and observed households for a month before assigning them to the comparison groups. The random assignment was balanced on characteristics including household size and initial water use.

If I simply compare today's water bill with last month's, I can't tell whether a shower warning caused the change. Maybe the number of people at home changed. In this trial there was a second group living through the same months, with the same equipment but without the information. That gives me another number to check before I credit the lights.

Now I'll walk through the shower on each side. In A, I turn on the tap and there is no colour signal. In B, the colour begins at green. After ten litres it moves to blue, then purple; at eighteen it turns red, and beyond twenty it flashes red. The sign explains the colours. I can finish washing.

The clock isn't deciding the colour. In a simple example, ten litres can pass through a tap in two minutes at five litres a minute, or in one minute at ten litres a minute. Both use the same amount. Those are illustrative flow rates, not measurements from Singapore. A volume warning tracks the amount I've actually used.

When the intervention began, the estimated saving was around seven litres per shower. Two weeks later it had settled at about five. The average saving over the intervention period was 5.54 litres per shower, compared with an initial average of about twenty-five. That's approximately twenty-two percent.

The size of that change also depended on how much water people had used before. In the baseline data, the higher-use group averaged nearly 34 litres per shower, and the lower-use group around 15. With feedback, the higher-use group cut about five litres more than the lower-use group. I wouldn't set the same personal saving target for someone who starts at fifteen and someone who starts at thirty-four.

Now I'll look at what was actually recorded across the two groups. On the household meter, the group with the warning went from about 495 litres a day to 476. That's a drop of nearly nineteen. If I only had those two readings, that might be my answer.

But the group without the warning moved from about 497 to 502 litres a day. Its consumption went up by nearly five, although that rise was not statistically significant. The groups started at slightly different levels, so I compare their changes, not just the final numbers. A drop of about nineteen on one side and a rise of about five on the other gives a difference of about twenty-four litres.

The study's adjusted estimate is 23.5 litres a day saved across the whole household. For the shower category alone the estimate is 37 litres. Those are estimated effects relative to the comparison group, not the exact before-and-after readings of a particular home. That's why the shower figure can't simply be copied onto the water bill.

To see where the difference comes from, I'll reset my two model homes to the same starting point: 496 litres a day. I divide that into 163 for the shower category and 333 for the rest, based on the combined baseline. A will stay at this reference level. It's a simplified comparison, not a replay of the control group's actual meter.

On B, I take 37 litres away from the shower budget. If nothing else changes, that leaves 459 litres across the home. But the adjusted total effect gives me 472.5 litres instead. The difference is 13.5 litres. I can draw that as water added back to the other category: it offsets part of the original gain.

Could I find the extra water by walking into the kitchen and pointing at a tap? No. The study has a household meter and shower-device records. Subtracting one category from the total gives the remainder; it doesn't identify which household activity changed. Even the shower head itself might be used to fill a cleaning bucket.

Imagine that I used to fill that bucket in the shower and now fill it at the sink. The shower meter would record a saving, but the household meter would not. That's one possible explanation, not a reconstruction of what any participant did. Some shower records also had to be recovered after the devices' limited memory overwrote old events. The authors checked their shower-use trend without the estimated records and reported similar results.

The study examined another clue. Among households that reduced shower-head use after receiving feedback, each litre saved was associated with about 0.15 litres more outside that category. But that relationship explained only about one percent of the variation in other use. It is weak evidence for a particular mechanism. I can't use it to say someone deliberately rewarded themselves for saving water, or apply fifteen percent as the offset for every household.

What I can do is explore the consequences in a simple model. I'll keep the shower reduction of 37 litres fixed and change only the increase elsewhere. If there is no increase, the whole-home saving is 37. With the study's implied 13.5-litre increase, it is 23.5. If other water use rose by exactly 37 litres, the household saving would be zero. Beyond that point, my home would use more water overall despite using less in the shower. The last two cases are hypothetical boundaries, not observed results.

I can also put the estimated net reduction into a thirty-day illustration. If 23.5 litres persisted every day, I'd save 705 litres. It would be 1,110 litres if the entire shower saving translated into household savings. The difference is 405 litres. Those figures are a multiplication, not a measured monthly bill or a prediction for every household.

If I wanted to try this at home, I would give both comparisons a fair chance. I'd record the whole-home meter at the same time every day for a period with the warning off, then repeat with it on, using the same shower head. I would note unusual laundry days, visitors or leaks. One busy weekend could swamp the saving I'm looking for. I'd compare daily averages across similar periods and keep washing properly. If I wanted to check persistence, I'd keep reading the meter after the first few weeks.

My home test would still have a limitation: it has no second group living through the same period. It can help me decide whether the warning is useful in my home, but it can't prove that every change was caused by the light. The Singapore experiment followed volunteers for a month without the intervention and two months with it. I can't turn that into a promise of permanent savings.

So which side wins? In this experiment, B. The estimated net reduction is about 4.7 percent of baseline household use. I would try the feedback, but I wouldn't judge success by how often the shower stays green. I'll look at the meter that counts the water everywhere in my home.
