If I put a water warning light in my shower, will I save water, or will I just get better at making one little number look good? And if I wanted to find out whether the gadget was worth using at home, which numbers should I measure?

I'm comparing two copies of the same daily routine. On side A I have a recording shower head, but its lights are turned off. On side B I have the same shower head, with the warning switched on and a sign explaining the colours. Neither automatically shuts off the water when I reach a target.

Pick a side. My main score is litres of water used by the whole household per day. I'll keep a separate score for each shower to see what changes in the bathroom, but only the first decides the winner.

This is a model based on a real experiment in Singapore, not two showers I've personally taken.

I want to start with a question. If I finish washing and turn the tap off, how much water has gone down the drain? Unless I measure it, I can only guess.

In the Singapore study, people were asked to estimate their own shower use. Before the warnings were activated, the average answer was about thirteen litres. The recorded average was about twenty-five. That's why a warning might be useful even when the shower head stays the same: it shows me something I can't otherwise see.

For the field experiment, 407 households were recruited from four high-rise housing estates. The researchers installed recording shower heads in both bathrooms and observed households for a month before assigning them to the comparison groups. The random assignment was balanced on characteristics including household size and initial water use.

If I instead compared a family that had already volunteered to save water with a different family that wasn't interested, I'd be mixing the gadget's effect with differences that existed before it arrived. Starting with the same hardware and assigning the intervention after the baseline helps make a fairer comparison.

Now I'll walk through the shower on each side. In A, I turn on the tap and there is no colour signal. In B, the colour begins at green. After ten litres it moves to blue, then purple; at eighteen it turns red, and beyond twenty it flashes red. The sign explains the colours. I can finish washing.

The clock isn't deciding the colour. In a simple example, ten litres can pass through a tap in two minutes at five litres a minute, or in one minute at ten litres a minute. Both use the same amount. Those are illustrative flow rates, not measurements from Singapore. A volume warning tracks the amount I've actually used.

When the intervention began, the estimated saving was around seven litres per shower. Two weeks later it had settled at about five. The average saving over the intervention period was 5.54 litres per shower, compared with an initial average of about twenty-five. That's approximately twenty-two percent.

It wasn't the same for everyone. The researchers divided baseline observations into higher-use and lower-use groups. With warnings, the higher-use group reduced its shower use by around five litres more than the lower-use group. If I'm already using very little water, I may have less room to cut back.

The study also asked people again how much water they thought they used. With feedback, their average estimate became much closer to the recorded average. This is an outcome I would want: a better sense of how much water I'm using.

But here's where the comparison gets interesting. I could take the shower result and claim I've found the answer. Instead I'll follow the water meter.

Both copies of my model start with 496 litres per household per day, the study's average baseline. Of that, 163 litres belong to the shower category and 333 to everything outside it. These aren't the needs of one person or numbers from my house.

The researchers estimated a reduction of 37 litres per household per day in shower use. I subtract 37 from 163. Now B has 126 litres in that category.

Suppose everything outside it stayed exactly the same. I'd add 126 and 333 and get 459 litres. Side A is still at the starting 496. That would be a saving of 37 litres across the whole home.

The reported estimate of the whole-home reduction was only 23.5 litres per day. To reproduce both estimates in my simplified budget, I have to add 13.5 litres to the other category. I now have 126 for showers and 346.5 for the rest, which adds up to 472.5.

I didn't lose those thirteen and a half litres through a rounding error. That's the difference between the estimated shower saving and the estimated whole-home saving. If I only looked at the former, I'd miss roughly thirty-six percent of the original gain.

Now I want to know why. The shower devices could be used for things besides bathing, and the researchers had to process their records. They excluded very small water events because they could be cleaning rather than showers. The devices also had limited memory, so some old records were overwritten and had to be estimated. The authors checked the main shower-use trends against analysis without those filled-in records and reported similar results.

For the home as a whole, they had meter readings. Water assigned to the non-shower category was calculated from the difference between the whole-home amount and the shower estimate. It wasn't a camera observing what everyone did with every tap.

Could the warning have encouraged people to use another tap for cleaning? Perhaps. Could some people have changed another habit after using less in the shower? Perhaps. The authors discuss possible explanations, but the data don't establish that a person consciously decided to waste water as a reward. I won't act out such a decision and present it as fact.

What I can do is explore the consequences in a simple model. I'll keep the shower reduction of 37 litres fixed and change only the increase elsewhere. If there is no increase, the whole-home saving is 37. With the study's implied 13.5-litre increase, it is 23.5. If other water use rose by exactly 37 litres, the household saving would be zero. Beyond that point, my home would use more water overall despite using less in the shower. The last two cases are hypothetical boundaries, not observed results.

I can also put the estimated net reduction into a thirty-day illustration. If 23.5 litres persisted every day, I'd save 705 litres. It would be 1,110 litres if the entire shower saving translated into household savings. The difference is 405 litres. Those figures are a multiplication, not a measured monthly bill or a prediction for every household.

Would I use the warning? Yes, in this comparison. The whole-home estimate remains lower by about 4.7 percent. But if I were testing this at home, I'd start with a period of normal use, keep the shower hardware unchanged, and record the meter over comparable periods. I'd note changes in household occupancy, laundry or other water use rather than trying to force my result to match the study. I'd keep washing properly and stop the test if the warning made that difficult. I'd also keep checking after the first few weeks.

There is no promise that my household would reproduce the Singapore average. The experiment covered volunteers and lasted three months, one before the intervention and two with it. That's more informative than my impression after one shower, but isn't evidence of a permanent habit.

My answer is to switch on the warning if I want to try this approach, then check the whole-house result. I don't need to turn a shower into a contest against a red light. The useful contest is between my total water use with the information and my total water use without it. That's how I'd find out whether I'm actually saving water.
