THE DOCTOR WHO FOUGHT AMERICA'S POISONED ALCOHOL

On Christmas Eve, 1926, people began arriving at New York hospitals desperately ill.

Some were confused. Some were blind. Others slipped into comas. They had consumed illegal liquor — and something in it was killing them.

For the city's chief medical examiner, Charles Norris, the deaths were not just a medical emergency. They exposed a terrible conflict at the heart of an American experiment.

For nearly seven years, the United States had tried to stop the sale of alcoholic drinks. But people still wanted to drink. Criminal suppliers still wanted to sell. And some of the alcohol reaching customers had come from a source never intended for a glass: industry.

That industrial alcohol had been deliberately made unfit to drink under federal rules.

Norris argued that the government bore moral responsibility for continuing a system whose dangers were becoming impossible to ignore.

Was the government trying to kill its citizens? The evidence does not establish that. But officials knew that poisoned alcohol was reaching people, and the policies designed to separate industrial alcohol from beverages became part of a deadly controversy.

To understand why, we have to go back to the promise that started the experiment.

At the beginning of the twentieth century, alcohol was a source of deep concern in the United States. Excessive drinking could devastate a person's health, drain a family's income and contribute to violence. Advocates of temperance and prohibition believed that restricting alcohol could protect homes and communities.

Many women organized around the issue at a time when they had limited political power. Religious groups, reformers and other supporters built a powerful movement. They did not all have the same motives, but they shared a belief that a country with less drinking could be a better country.

In January 1920, the Eighteenth Amendment took effect. National Prohibition restricted the manufacture, sale and transportation of intoxicating liquor. The amendment did not simply make the act of drinking itself a federal crime. There were also legal exceptions, including certain medical and industrial uses of alcohol.

And the law did change the country. Historical accounts indicate that alcohol consumption fell, especially early in Prohibition. To say that the ban had no effect would be wrong.

But a drop in consumption was not the same thing as the disappearance of demand. Some people still wanted a drink. And an illegal market emerged to supply them.

Bottles were smuggled, made in secret or diverted from legal uses. Their contents could be difficult to verify. A customer buying from an illegal supplier might not know whether the label described what was actually inside.

That distinction could be fatal.

The alcohol in an ordinary drink is ethanol. Another chemical, methanol, is sometimes called wood alcohol. They have similar names, but they do not have the same effect on the body. Methanol is highly toxic. Swallowing it can cause blindness and death.

Methanol had industrial uses of its own. It could be mixed into illicit liquor separately. Another danger came from industrial ethanol that had been diverted into the beverage market despite having chemicals added to make it unsuitable for drinking.

This practice had a name: denaturing.

Years before national Prohibition, the government had allowed certain industrial alcohol to be distributed without the beverage tax, provided it was made unfit for consumption. Factories needed alcohol for products and manufacturing. The additives helped distinguish untaxed industrial alcohol from alcohol sold for drinking and discouraged diversion.

That purpose did not disappear under Prohibition. Industry still needed alcohol. The government still wanted industrial supplies kept out of the illegal drinks trade.

But bootleggers found ways to acquire diverted industrial alcohol. Some tried to remove the additives before selling the liquid. Others mixed substances into what they sold. A drinker's glass could be the last stop in a chain of decisions they had never seen.

The federal government responded in part by changing some denaturing formulas to make industrial alcohol harder to divert. In October 1926, officials approved a revision to one industrial formula that would contain more wood alcohol. It was due to take effect in January 1927, after the Christmas poisonings.

The intention described by officials was to prevent misuse of industrial supplies. But the risk was no longer hypothetical: people were becoming seriously ill and dying after consuming poisonous liquor.

By the end of 1926, New York's hospitals and morgues were dealing with the consequences. News of the Christmas poisonings spread. Here was a public health crisis unfolding during a policy that had promised a healthier nation.

Norris's job brought him face to face with the dead. As the city's first chief medical examiner, he had helped build a system intended to investigate suspicious deaths using medicine and science. His chief toxicologist, Alexander Gettler, used chemical analysis to investigate poisons that could otherwise be hard to see.

The work could reveal that a substance was dangerous. It could not automatically reconstruct every step by which every bottle had reached every person.

That is an important limit. Some dangerous liquor contained industrial alcohol that had been diverted. Some was adulterated through other channels. The records do not let us assign every death to one federal formula. Nor can we assume that a formula announced after the Christmas outbreak caused those earlier poisonings.

Norris nevertheless made a public moral argument. He condemned a system in which officials knew people were dying from poisonous liquor while government rules continued to require certain industrial alcohols to be denatured with toxic chemicals.

The Treasury defended the need to keep industrial alcohol unfit for drinking and difficult to divert. Officials and critics disagreed about which products could be redirected into the illegal market, how effectively the additives could be removed, and who should be held responsible.

This was not one secret switch labelled poison. There were different formulas for different industrial uses, and they changed over time. In late 1930, one prominent formula was revised to remove wood alcohol, taking effect at the start of 1931. Other formulas had different histories.

The argument was larger than one recipe. If a rule is designed for an industrial product, what should officials do when they know that product is being diverted and sold to people as a drink?

And what responsibility belongs to the sellers who pass a dangerous liquid to customers?

Both questions matter. Focusing only on the government's formula would hide the role of bootleggers. Focusing only on the criminal seller would avoid the difficult question Norris raised about a known public danger.

In December 1933, the Twenty-first Amendment repealed the national constitutional prohibition. That did not mean every place in America instantly adopted the same rules. States retained important powers over alcohol, and local restrictions continued in some places.

Nor did repeal turn alcohol into a harmless substance. Legal products could still cause dependence, illness, injury and death. But people no longer had to depend on the same nationwide system of illegal beverage sales to obtain a drink.

Norris's story leaves us with a question that did not end in 1933. Why do some harmful substances remain legal, while others are prohibited? And who benefits from each arrangement?

There is no single answer that fits every country or every drug. Laws are shaped by history, public pressure, medical knowledge, commercial interests and politics. And a legal status is not a simple ranking of danger.

Consider alcohol today. Governments regulate it and collect taxes from its sale. Those taxes can raise substantial sums. But revenue coming into a treasury is not the same as a clean profit for society.

Excessive alcohol use also creates costs: medical care, injuries, lost productivity and suffering borne by individuals and families. A government may receive money from sales while other public institutions pay for some of the consequences.

A tax can have two effects at once. It can raise revenue and, by increasing the price, discourage some purchases. Whether it does both, and by how much, depends on the policy and the response of the market.

Tobacco creates another conflict. Nicotine can cause strong dependence, and tobacco use causes serious disease. Governments tax tobacco; some states also have ownership interests in the industry. An authority connected to a business and responsible for limiting its harms can face competing incentives.

That does not prove that every government wants people to become addicted. It does mean the public has a reason to examine whose interests are represented when rules are made.

What about cannabis?

Alcohol and cannabis can both impair driving and judgment. Both can cause harm, and some people develop disorders involving their use. But the risks are not identical. They vary with the substance, the dose, the frequency of use, a person's age and other circumstances.

Canada legalized and regulated nonmedical cannabis in 2018. In a national survey, 37 percent of people who had used cannabis in the previous year said they usually bought it from a legal source in 2019. In the 2024 survey, the figure was 72 percent.

Those were survey results from different years, not the same individuals tracked through time, and not a count of every sale. They suggest that legal sellers became an important source. They do not prove that the illegal market disappeared or that legalization eliminated health risks.

The question is not whether one rule magically solves everything. It is how a policy affects access, product safety, health, enforcement, treatment and the people living with its consequences.

The distinction matters even more with opioids.

Some opioid medicines are used in medical care, including the treatment of severe pain. They also carry risks, including dependence and overdose. Legal medicine and illegal drugs are not two sealed worlds with no connection between them.

In 2007, a company connected to the maker of OxyContin pleaded guilty to misbranding the medicine after it had been promoted as less addictive and less vulnerable to abuse than other pain medications.

That case involved specific conduct by a specific company. It does not mean every doctor acted dishonestly, every prescription was inappropriate or every patient developed an addiction. It does show why claims about safety need to be tested, especially when selling more of a product is profitable.

Later waves of overdose deaths involved heroin and then synthetic opioids, especially illegally manufactured fentanyl. These changes had multiple causes. No single story describes the path of every person affected.

There is another fact that can get lost when the debate becomes a fight about punishment and profit: substance use disorders can be treated. For opioid use disorder, medications including methadone and buprenorphine are among the treatments that can help people recover.

A person with an addiction is not a failed policy or a statistic. They may be a parent, a colleague, a neighbor, or someone trying to get through a difficult day. People need accurate information, access to care and support as well as laws that take their safety seriously.

Charles Norris saw what happened when the consequences of a policy arrived at his workplace as human bodies. He believed that keeping a dangerous industrial product away from drinkers was not enough if the actual system was still putting poisoned liquor within reach.

His warning was about accountability. Officials had to look at what was happening, not only at what a policy was intended to achieve.

We should ask the same questions today. Who makes money? Who carries the costs? Who has influence over the rules? And when the evidence shows that people are being harmed, who has the power — and the willingness — to change course?

The law can draw a line between legal and illegal. It cannot, by itself, make the people on either side of that line disappear.
