THE EMPIRE THAT PROFITED FROM OPIUM

In 1839, officials in southern China faced a question that sounds painfully modern.

What happens when a government tries to stop a drug trade that powerful people have learned to profit from?

At the port of Canton, a Chinese commissioner ordered a vast stock of opium surrendered and destroyed. The drug had entered China illegally. The people selling it knew that. So did the authorities trying to stop them.

Within months, Britain and China were on the path to war.

When the fighting ended, China would have to pay, among other things, for the opium it had destroyed. But the drug itself would still be illegal in China.

To understand how that happened, follow the money backward across the sea, from the Chinese coast to India. There, under British rule, a powerful company had helped turn opium into revenue.

This is not a story in which one government secretly controls every addiction. It is something more unsettling: a story of ordinary institutions, each protecting its own interests, while the costs fall on people somewhere else.

Britain wanted goods from China. Tea was becoming an important part of British life, along with silk and porcelain. Chinese traders, however, did not want enough British goods to balance that appetite. Silver flowed toward China to pay for the difference.

British merchants and the East India Company looked for ways to make this trade more profitable. One answer came from a crop grown in India: the opium poppy.

Opium was not new to the region, and it had medical uses. But as smoking became more widespread in China, the trade expanded dramatically. Some people developed dependence. Families, officials and communities confronted the consequences, while buyers and sellers kept the market alive.

The company was not simply a private shop. It governed territory in India and exercised powers backed by Britain. In parts of the territory it controlled, it oversaw opium production, processed the crop and sold the finished product at auctions in Calcutta, now Kolkata.

Private traders bought the opium. They carried it toward China and sold it through networks that included Chinese intermediaries. The company did not need to stand at every point of sale to benefit from the system. Money came in when the drug was auctioned in India.

The distinction matters. The company controlled a large part of production and sales in India. Private traders handled much of the onward trade. Chinese merchants and officials also participated in the networks. The system crossed borders, and responsibility was distributed among the people who made it work.

China had prohibited the import of opium. Yet a prohibition written on paper could not by itself remove demand, police an extensive coast or eliminate corruption. The trade continued to grow.

Chinese leaders saw more than a broken law. They feared the effects of opium use and the outflow of silver used to pay for it. In 1839, the Qing emperor sent a senior official, Lin Zexu, to Canton with orders to suppress the trade.

Lin pressured foreign merchants to surrender their stocks. Thousands of chests were confiscated and destroyed. He also appealed to British authorities, questioning why a harmful trade should be carried on against China.

The destruction was an assertion of Chinese authority. To British merchants, it was the loss of valuable property. And to British officials, the confrontation formed part of a broader dispute over how Britain would trade and conduct diplomacy with the Qing empire.

There were arguments in Britain, too. Not everyone accepted the opium trade as moral or the war as justified. But opposition did not stop the fighting.

British naval power proved devastating. The First Opium War lasted from 1839 to 1842. Chinese forces were defeated, and the Qing government was forced to negotiate.

The resulting Treaty of Nanking opened ports to British trade and ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain. It also required China to pay compensation, including six million dollars for the surrendered opium.

Here is the detail that changes the picture: the treaty did not make opium legal in China.

The country had been made to pay for a drug that remained contraband under its own law. Trade continued, and the conflict between prohibition, foreign commerce and revenue did not disappear.

Years later, during a second war involving Britain, France and China, opium was included in an import tariff in 1858. The fighting continued until 1860. The change took place in the shadow of military coercion, but it is too simple to claim that every clause was the result of one single demand or one motive.

By then, the importance of opium revenue could be discussed openly in the British Parliament. A debate in 1859 described the money collected through the Indian opium system and the financial consequences of losing it.

Those figures concerned the revenue of British-controlled India. They are not a calculation of Britain's net profit after every cost. But they make one point unmistakable: this was not just a hidden exchange between a few smugglers. It was connected to the finances of a colonial government.

A farmer cultivating the crop, a merchant carrying it, an official counting revenue and a person becoming dependent in China occupied different places in the same chain. They did not share the same power. They did not receive the same benefit. And many of the people bearing the costs had little influence over the decisions that sustained the trade.

It would be easy to end the story there: a foreign empire profited from a dangerous drug, and everyone else suffered.

But history becomes more useful when it makes us ask a harder question. What happens to policy when a product can cause harm and generate money at the same time?

The answer is not always prohibition. And prohibition is not the absence of regulation. It is one form of regulation, with consequences of its own.

In 1920, the United States banned the manufacture, sale and transportation of intoxicating liquor nationwide. Supporters had real reasons to fear alcohol's effects on families, health and public life. Many believed a ban would help.

The law changed behavior. Historical accounts indicate that alcohol consumption fell, particularly early on. But it did not make the desire to drink disappear.

Alcohol was still produced and sold illegally. Criminal organizations made money in the underground market, and enforcing the law became a bitter political struggle. In 1933, the United States repealed national Prohibition.

That experience proves neither that bans can never reduce use nor that bans always succeed. Both things can be true at once: consumption can fall, and an illegal market can survive.

Alcohol then returned to a legal, regulated economy. Today governments collect taxes on alcoholic drinks. In Britain, the sums run into billions of pounds. That is revenue for a budget, not a receipt proving the country has made a profit from drinking.

Heavy alcohol use can contribute to injuries, disease and harm to families. Public services and society bear costs as well. Different parts of government may face different incentives: a treasury receives money, while hospitals and communities deal with some of the damage.

Higher taxes can also have more than one purpose. They may raise money while making a product more expensive and discouraging some consumption. To understand a policy, we have to look at what it actually does, not just at the fact that money changes hands.

Tobacco presents another conflict. Nicotine is highly addictive, and tobacco use can cause serious disease. Some governments collect tobacco taxes. In some countries, the state also owns tobacco businesses. When an institution is connected to an industry and responsible for regulating it, those interests can pull in different directions.

None of that proves that every government wants its citizens to become dependent. It does show why public decisions deserve scrutiny, especially when the people protecting health and the people counting revenue are part of the same system.

The legal line can look even stranger when we compare alcohol with cannabis.

Both can impair judgment and driving. Both can cause harm, and some people develop a disorder involving their use. Their risks are not identical. Dose, frequency, age, potency and the way a substance is used all matter. A legal label is not a medical safety certificate; an illegal label is not a precise scientific ranking of danger.

In 2018, Canada legalized and regulated nonmedical cannabis. Licensed sellers became an alternative to illegal suppliers, but legalization did not make every risk vanish or end every unlicensed sale.

In one national survey, the share of people who had used cannabis in the previous year and said they usually bought it from a legal source rose from 37 percent in 2019 to 72 percent in 2024. That is a survey of people, not a direct measurement of every sale. It tells us that many consumers reported changing where they obtained cannabis. It cannot tell us, on its own, whether the entire policy succeeded or failed.

The point is not that every drug should be treated like cannabis, or that one country's rules can simply be copied everywhere. The question is which combination of prevention, regulation, enforcement and treatment reduces harm in a particular setting — and what evidence would show us when it is not working.

A final example takes us somewhere neither the story of a street dealer nor the story of a tax collector fully explains: a doctor's office.

Opioid medicines can be important for treating pain. They also carry risks, including dependence and overdose. In 2007, a company connected with the maker of OxyContin pleaded guilty to misbranding the drug after falsely promoting it as less addictive and less likely to be abused than other pain medicines.

This was a commercial failure with consequences for real people. It does not mean every prescription was inappropriate, every patient became addicted or every physician acted in bad faith. And the later rise of heroin and illegally manufactured fentanyl involved many factors; it cannot be reduced to a single path that every person followed.

But the case asks the same question as the opium auctions: who benefits when a dangerous risk is made easier to ignore?

There is a temptation to imagine two separate worlds. On one side, legal products with respectable labels. On the other, illegal substances and criminal markets. History keeps revealing the connections between them: pain and profit, public revenue and public health, a useful medicine and a risk that must be honestly managed.

A person with a substance use disorder is not a budget line or a warning poster. Dependence is treatable, and recovery is possible. For opioid use disorder, medicines such as methadone and buprenorphine can be part of effective treatment. Access to care, accurate information and support matter alongside laws and enforcement.

Back in 1839, Chinese officials destroyed the opium they could seize. Thousands of miles away, a system that profited from producing and selling it had not been dismantled. The confrontation became a war; the financial incentives remained.

Today's choices are different, and so are the substances and societies involved. But the questions still matter. Who earns money? Who pays the costs? Who has the power to change the rules? And are those rules reducing harm for the people they are supposed to protect?

The answers will not fit on a simple label. They begin when we look beyond the label — and follow the chain all the way to the people living with its consequences.
