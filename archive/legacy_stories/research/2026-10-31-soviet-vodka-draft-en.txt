THE GOVERNMENT WITH A DRINKING PROBLEM

In April 1985, the leaders of the Soviet Union faced a problem they could no longer ignore.

Alcohol was damaging health, disrupting families and costing the economy dearly. The government wanted people to drink less.

There was just one complication.

The government also made money when they drank.

At a meeting of the Politburo, the country's top leadership discussed a plan to cut the production of vodka and other alcoholic drinks. A deputy finance minister warned that the proposed reductions would leave a hole in the state budget. He warned about something else, too: if less vodka reached the shops, people might make more alcohol themselves.

Mikhail Gorbachev had recently become the Soviet leader. He heard the warning, and according to the meeting notes, he answered: “We cannot tolerate our drunk budget any longer.”

The phrase captured a dilemma hiding in plain sight. The state was trying to fight the consequences of a product it also sold. Cutting those sales could help people. It would also cost the government money.

Gorbachev decided the country had to try.

Over the next few years, the same policy would produce real benefits and serious consequences at the same time.

The Soviet government did not invent drinking in 1985. By then, alcohol had a long place in daily life. It appeared at celebrations, around tables, after work and in the rituals through which people marked good news and bad.

It could also become a source of terrible harm. Families lived with the consequences of heavy drinking. Accidents, illness, violence and lost work were among the problems that officials and researchers connected to alcohol.

For the people gathered in the Politburo, this was not a secret. In the same meeting, the official presenting the plan described letters from citizens worried about drinking and demanding action. Whether every figure offered in that room was accurate is another question. But the leadership was responding to a problem many people recognized.

The state had a particular relationship with alcohol. It controlled the legal industry and the sale of alcoholic drinks. A bottle purchased through the official system did more than put vodka on a table. It sent money into a state-run economy.

Some of that money helped fund the government itself.

So the bottle had two different meanings, depending on where you stood. For a family living with heavy drinking, fewer bottles might mean a safer home. For an official responsible for balancing public finances, fewer sales also meant less revenue to pay for the state's commitments.

The people facing those consequences did not have equal power.

In the Politburo discussion, the deputy finance minister did not say the government should abandon the fight against drinking. He supported reducing production. His concern was the scale and the speed.

He warned that the proposed cuts could reduce state budget revenue by billions of rubles. These were projections made before the policy was carried out, not a final bill for what happened later.

The planning authorities raised another concern. If there was less vodka for sale, what would the state offer people instead? Other desirable goods could not simply be assumed to appear.

Gorbachev was impatient with arguments that, in his view, left the government dependent on people continuing to drink. He insisted that reducing vodka production was central to the campaign.

The leadership moved ahead. In May 1985, the Communist Party and the government adopted measures against drunkenness and alcoholism.

This was not a complete ban on alcohol. It was a campaign to make it harder and more expensive to buy, while pushing society toward sobriety.

The state cut production. Prices rose. The number and hours of sales outlets were restricted. Penalties for drinking in public and at work grew tougher, while official campaigns promoted a sober way of life.

On paper, there was a clear chain of cause and effect: fewer places selling alcohol, fewer bottles produced, less drinking, less harm.

But the Politburo had already heard that another chain might be set in motion.

The first results were dramatic. Official alcohol sales fell sharply. By 1988, state sales were less than half their 1984 level, according to a later study of the campaign.

That is the moment when a tidy version of the story would end. The state decided to sell less. The state sold less. Problem solved.

Except that a government sales ledger records what went through the government's hands. It cannot count everything people drink.

As legal supplies became harder to get, people increased production of homemade alcohol. Some turned to other sources. The deputy minister's warning had not been idle.

That created a new problem for anyone trying to understand whether the campaign worked. If a bottle disappeared from a state shop, had a person stopped drinking? Or had the sale simply moved somewhere the state could not count it?

Researchers looking back at the campaign had to estimate the alcohol missing from official records. Those estimates are imperfect. Homemade production is difficult to measure precisely, particularly in a system where much of it was illegal.

But the distinction matters. The sharp drop in recorded sales was larger than the estimated drop in total consumption. People did find other sources. The best available estimates also suggest that those sources did not replace everything that disappeared from the shops.

The country was not simply drinking exactly as before in a different place. Nor had drinking disappeared.

The effects differed across regions and among people: a long queue for one person, a different source or no change at all for another.

The campaign was not experienced only as a message about health. Its rules reached into workplaces and public life, with restrictions and penalties.

Meanwhile, the state had another set of numbers to confront.

Fewer official sales meant less money entering the budget through the alcohol system. The finance officials had warned about that before the campaign began. Now the loss of revenue became part of the political pressure surrounding it.

That did not mean alcohol had secretly been good for the economy. Income from sales was one part of the accounts. Illness, accidents, lost work and the suffering of families were costs, even when they did not appear beside that income as a single neat subtraction.

The problem was that the benefits and costs did not arrive at the same desk, on the same day, in the same form.

A reduction in sales could leave a budget manager searching for money immediately. A reduction in harm might appear across hospitals, workplaces and households, and only become clear in statistics collected over time.

And then, in the health statistics, something striking did appear.

In Russia, then one republic within the Soviet Union, life expectancy for men rose from about sixty-two years in 1984 to about sixty-five in 1987. Death rates also fell during the campaign.

That does not mean every individual man suddenly gained three years of life. Life expectancy at birth is a population measure calculated from death rates in a given period. It tells us what those rates imply, not what happened to every person.

Researchers have linked a substantial part of the improvement to lower alcohol consumption during the campaign. They have studied changes across regions and causes of death, not merely the government's sales figures.

No single statistic can isolate the effect of every measure or rule out every other influence. But the improvement was not just a bookkeeping trick.

This is the point where the original question becomes harder.

The campaign was unpopular. It reduced a source of state income. It also coincided with a remarkable improvement in a measure of how long people in Russia could expect to live.

If you judged it by the government's revenue, you saw a loss. If you judged it by access to alcohol, you might have seen an unwelcome restriction. If you judged it by death rates, you saw evidence of lives that might not have been cut short so early.

The same campaign produced all of these arguments at once.

And the government was not a single person with a single interest. Some officials pushed to restrict alcohol. Finance and planning officials worried about the missing revenue and consumer goods. People outside the government bore the costs and experienced the consequences in very different ways.

By 1988, the pressures were mounting. The campaign had become unpopular, and the loss of alcohol revenue had become difficult for the state to ignore.

In October, the Communist Party's Central Committee officially ended the anti-alcohol campaign. But a policy is easier to announce than to unwind. Production took time to recover. Some restrictions remained. Higher prices and reduced availability did not all disappear overnight.

Years later, researchers continued to debate how much the campaign, its retreat and the enormous political and economic changes that followed each contributed to Russia's changing mortality. The collapse of the Soviet Union and the upheaval of the 1990s cannot be explained by a single bottle of vodka.

Still, the meeting notes preserve a revealing moment.

Before the campaign began, a finance official had warned that cutting vodka production could cost the budget money and send some demand into homemade alcohol. Both problems mattered later. Gorbachev had insisted that the country could not keep relying on what he called a drunk budget. The health figures suggest there was another price to the old arrangement, one that families and communities had been paying all along.

The answer to our opening question is not that governments always want people to drink. In 1985, this government itself chose to reduce alcohol sales. The answer is that a state can be divided: one part receives the revenue, another confronts the harm, and the public lives with both the rules and their consequences.

The more a budget depends on a harmful product, the harder it can be to change the system around it — even when there are good reasons to try.

Gorbachev's phrase was about a budget. The story behind it was about whose money the state counted, whose losses it could see, and whose lives were affected when the numbers changed.
