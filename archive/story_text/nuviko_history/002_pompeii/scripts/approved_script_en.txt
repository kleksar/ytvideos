At first, lightweight white stones began falling on Pompeii. People sheltered beneath their roofs and shut their doors, hoping to wait out the strange storm. But the stones kept coming. Roofs began to collapse under their weight. And by the following morning, the city would face a danger that neither doors nor walls could keep out.

It happened in the year 79 AD. To understand how one of the most famous cities of ancient Rome was destroyed, we need to reconstruct not only the eruption of Mount Vesuvius, but also the decisions its residents had to make. Should they leave or stay? Run through streets where stones were falling from the sky, or take shelter in a building that might collapse?

We don't know what happened to every family, and we won't invent anyone's final conversations. But we do have letters written by an eyewitness, marks left on walls, objects found inside homes, and layers of volcanic rock. Together, they let us piece together the disaster, almost hour by hour.

On an ordinary day, Pompeii was a bustling trading city on the Bay of Naples. Carts rolled through its streets. Workshops made goods, bakeries ground grain and baked bread, and stalls sold ready-to-eat food. People covered walls with announcements, campaign slogans, and personal messages.

Some residents lived in spacious houses with painted walls and interior gardens. Others worked in shops, rented modest rooms, or lived in slavery. Pompeii wasn't just a city of wealthy Romans. Its people had very different rights, resources, and opportunities.

Rising above the rooftops was Mount Vesuvius. Fields and vineyards covered its slopes. Today we know it was a volcano, but to most people living nearby, it did not look like a warning of imminent destruction. Vesuvius had gone a very long time without a major eruption, and memories of earlier disasters offered little clear understanding of what was coming.

The ground in this region, however, was far from peaceful. Seventeen years before Pompeii's destruction, a powerful earthquake had damaged buildings and streets. The city was still being rebuilt, and archaeologists can see traces of those repairs today. More earthquakes followed, but an earthquake alone did not tell residents that an eruption of this magnitude was about to happen.

Sometime in late summer or fall of 79 AD, an ordinary day changed. Scholars still debate the exact date. August twenty-fourth was long considered the traditional date, but archaeological evidence also points toward the fall. For this story, the sequence of events matters more than the season.

Sometime after noon, an enormous cloud rose from Mount Vesuvius. It shot upward, then spread into wide branches near the top. One of the main eyewitnesses, Pliny the Younger, compared its shape to a pine tree. He was watching from Misenum, on the other side of the bay. Not from Pompeii itself.

What Pliny described was not ordinary smoke from a fire. Magma was rising inside the volcano, and gases were forcing huge quantities of scorching fragments and ash into the atmosphere. A towering eruption column rose tens of kilometers into the sky. The wind carried its contents toward the towns south of Vesuvius.

That wind determined which danger would reach Pompeii first. The city began to be covered in pumice: a light, porous rock formed from frothy volcanic material. One piece can look almost harmless, especially compared with glowing lava.

But imagine not one little stone, but a continuous rain of rock that goes on for hours. Stones cover roads and courtyards, piling up on roofs and at doorways. Each new layer makes the streets harder to cross. What you might brush off your shoulder at first gradually becomes a serious obstruction.

For someone inside a house, the temptation is obvious: wait it out. A roof offers some protection from falling debris, and outside it's difficult to see where you're going. But pumice keeps accumulating overhead. Eventually, its weight becomes dangerous for the roof structure. In Pompeii, archaeologists have found the remains of people killed when roofs and walls collapsed.

Some residents did leave while an escape route was still open. That's an important part of the story, and one that movies often overlook. Pompeii was not a city where everyone stayed and died at the same moment. We don't know exactly how many people escaped, but evidence survives of people who lived through the eruption and of later attempts to return for possessions.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the bay, another dramatic event was unfolding. Pliny the Elder, the eyewitness's uncle, commanded the Roman fleet at Misenum. At first, the unusual cloud interested him as a subject of study. He planned to sail closer and investigate it. Then a woman living near the volcano sent a request for help. His mission changed.

Pliny ordered ships to put to sea and headed toward a place others were trying to escape. The closer the vessels came to shore, the thicker the falling ash became, and the more debris struck the water. They couldn't reach their intended destination. Eventually, Pliny arrived at Stabiae, where his acquaintance Pomponianus was staying.

This was not a rescue directly from the city gates of Pompeii. The letter describes another part of the coastline. But it reveals the larger situation: people were trying to escape by land and by sea, while the eruption was steadily cutting off possible routes.

By evening, Pompeii was far darker than usual. Almost no sunlight could penetrate the volcanic cloud. The streets were buried beneath mountains of pumice. Familiar routes through the city had become difficult to navigate. With every step, it grew harder to avoid stumbling over stones.

And there was another danger: earthquakes. As the eruption continued, the shaking intensified. Buildings already weakened by earlier damage and the weight of fallen material could collapse. A study published in 2024 suggests that some residents may have died because of earthquake-related building collapses during the eruption, rather than directly from volcanic ash or extreme heat.

It was a terrifying choice. Outside, stones were falling from the sky. Inside, the ceiling could come down. And no one could know how long it would last: another hour, or another week. There were no modern alerts, no communication networks, and no maps showing safe zones. People had to make decisions based on what they could see and hear around them.

Pliny the Younger recorded that, across the bay, people called out to locate their relatives because darkness had swallowed everything around them. He himself left town with his mother. According to his account, the ground shook so violently that carts would not stay in place, even with stones wedged beneath their wheels.

While pumice continued to rain down on Pompeii, the neighboring city of Herculaneum faced a different sequence of events. At first, the wind carried most of the falling stones away from it. But that did not mean it was safe. During the night, pyroclastic flows rushed down the slopes of Vesuvius: mixtures of hot gas, ash, and rock fragments moving at tremendous speed close to the ground.

These flows are nothing like a slow river of lava that you might have time to walk around. They spread rapidly, pour into low-lying areas, and destroy what lies in their path. Herculaneum was struck by this danger before Pompeii. Its buildings and streets were buried beneath a thick layer of volcanic deposits.

Pompeii, meanwhile, was still enduring the rain of stones. It continued for many hours. By the following morning, around three meters of pumice had accumulated in some places. Picture the height of the ceiling in an ordinary room, and imagine almost that much loose rock piled up in the streets. Opening a door, climbing out of a ground-floor room, or crossing a mound of debris had become extraordinarily difficult.

Then the intensity of the falling stones changed. For a short time, it may have seemed that the worst was over. But the layers of volcanic deposits tell scientists this was not the end of the eruption. Conditions above Vesuvius were changing. Parts of the enormous eruption column collapsed, sending new pyroclastic flows racing across the ground.

One of them reached Pompeii the following morning. A powerful mixture of volcanic gases, fine ash, and hot rock fragments swept into places where people had tried to hide. It moved far faster than a human could run, and it didn't need an open door to turn a house into a deadly trap.

This is where the familiar movie image of Pompeii's destruction falls apart. The city was not simply swallowed by a flood of glowing red lava. The main destructive forces were falling pumice, collapsing buildings, earthquakes, and pyroclastic flows. Different people died in different circumstances, and scientists are still working to understand the precise mechanisms.

One study estimated that a deadly flow reaching Pompeii may have affected the city for around ten to twenty minutes. For people trapped in a cloud filled with ash they could inhale, that was far too long. Other studies and museum accounts emphasize the effects of intense heat. We cannot determine the exact cause of death of every person whose remains have been found.

What is clear is that once these flows arrived, those still inside the city had almost no chance of escaping. Places where homes, streets, workshops, and gardens had stood only hours earlier were covered by volcanic deposits. The ancient city we know today was buried.

Pliny the Elder never returned from his voyage. He died at Stabiae. Years later, his nephew described both that journey and his own escape in letters to the historian Tacitus. We know an extraordinary amount about this disaster because that human eyewitness account survived. But the letters were written after the eruption, so their details have to be compared with the archaeological evidence.

Life in the region did not end forever after the disaster. Some residents survived. Others tried to return and recover whatever belongings were left. Officials under Emperor Titus dealt with the aftermath. People continued searching the site of Pompeii for building materials and possessions. But the city as it had once functioned was never restored.

Over time, soil and new layers of human activity covered the ruins. The memory of the city gradually faded. Systematic excavations began here in the eighteenth century. Researchers went on to discover not only walls and household objects, but also traces of the people caught in the catastrophe.

One of Pompeii's most famous features is the plaster casts of the victims. These are not people who turned to stone, as photographs sometimes make them appear. Their bodies decomposed over time, leaving hollow spaces in the hardened volcanic material. In the nineteenth century, archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli developed a method of pouring plaster into those spaces, recreating the shapes of bodies and even the folds of clothing.

The remains of more than a thousand victims have been found. But that is not a complete death toll, and certainly not a count of Pompeii's entire population. Archaeologists have not investigated every location, and many people managed to leave the city. What survives tells us about individual human lives, but it cannot give us a complete record of every resident and every final decision they made.

And that is what makes Pompeii so extraordinary. Almost two thousand years separate us from its inhabitants, yet we can still see a street, a bakery, a wall covered in writing, and dishes used for meals. We can glimpse a world where people made plans for tomorrow, repaired their homes, worked, and argued. They had no idea that their ordinary day would become a chapter in history.

Today, almost everyone knows Pompeii as the city destroyed by Mount Vesuvius. But to the people living there in 79 AD, it was, above all, home. And when that enormous cloud rose above the mountain, they were not facing an abstract historical question about how volcanoes work. They faced one immediate, practical question: would they make it out alive?

Some did. Others remained beneath roofs that could no longer bear the weight of the stones. Still others survived the long night, only to be trapped when the scorching cloud arrived.

The ash buried the city, but it preserved traces of the lives lived there. And today, whenever archaeologists uncover another street or room, we learn not only about Pompeii's final day. We learn about the thousands of ordinary days that came before it.
