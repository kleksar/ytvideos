THE GOVERNMENT WITH A DRINKING PROBLEM

On April fourth, nineteen eighty-five, the leaders of the Soviet Union were about to make an expensive decision.

They intended to sell less vodka.

In a country where the state controlled the legal alcohol industry, the money from those sales flowed back into the state economy. The officials considering the plan understood that cutting production would also cut government income. One finance official put a figure on the loss he expected the following year: four billion rubles.

The plan went forward anyway.

Why would a government deliberately give up so much money? And when the cost became real, would it keep its promise?

The answer begins with a document that was not written for the public: working notes from a meeting of the Politburo, the Soviet Union's top leadership.

By April nineteen eighty-five, Mikhail Gorbachev had been Soviet leader for less than a month. The meeting before him had an agenda item about drinking. A commission had spent years preparing proposals, and the official presenting them, Mikhail Solomentsev, described a problem reaching far beyond the balance sheet.

He reported that the authorities had received thousands of letters expressing concern about drinking. He said one letter from Moscow carried more than a thousand signatures and another came from Novosibirsk. Among the passages he quoted was a call to remove bottles from the shelves.

Those letters reached a room in which the money from the bottles was already being counted.

Heavy drinking could devastate families and damage health. Officials also connected it to accidents, violence and lost work. Solomentsev presented the campaign as an urgent response. His proposals included cutting production, raising prices and making alcohol harder to buy.

Then the finance ministry had its turn.

First Deputy Finance Minister Dementsev agreed that excessive drinking was a serious problem. He supported reducing production. But he challenged how quickly and by how much the state intended to do it.

He warned that the cuts under discussion could reduce the Soviet state budget's revenue by four billion rubles in nineteen eighty-six, and by more in later years. Those numbers were forecasts for the proposed plan, not a count of money already lost.

He raised another danger. If the state sold less, people might produce more alcohol at home or divert industrial alcohol. The loss of a legal sale would not necessarily mean the end of a drink.

The planning authorities had a related concern: people would still have money in their pockets, but the state might not have enough other goods they wanted to buy.

Then Gorbachev answered. According to the translated working notes, he insisted that cutting vodka production was central to the fight against alcoholism. “We cannot tolerate our drunk budget any longer.”

It was an extraordinary admission from the head of a government that sold the drink itself. The state wanted to protect people from alcohol's consequences. It had also come to rely on income from alcohol sales.

A month later, the leadership acted.

In May, the Communist Party and the Soviet government adopted measures against drunkenness and alcoholism. The campaign covered the Soviet Union. It did not ban every drink. Instead, it cut production, raised prices, and restricted where and when alcohol could be sold.

For a shopper, the policy could mean fewer outlets and shorter hours. For someone drinking at work or in public, it could mean tougher penalties. The government promoted sobriety in public campaigns.

There was coercion, too. The measures expanded compulsory treatment. A worker penalized for an alcohol-related violation could face workplace sanctions or be moved back in the housing queue. The policy was presented as a way to improve health, but some of the tools used to enforce it reached deeply into people's lives.

Then came the numbers the government could count most easily: sales.

In Russia, then a republic within the Soviet Union, official alcohol sales per person fell dramatically between nineteen eighty-four and nineteen eighty-seven. Measured as pure alcohol, the yearly figure dropped from roughly ten and a half litres per person to fewer than four.

On the state's books, the change was unmistakable. But the books had a blind spot.

Dementsev had warned that some drinking would move away from the shops. And as the legal supply became harder to obtain, homemade alcohol became more common.

Imagine looking only at the state ledger. A bottle is gone from the shop. It is also gone from the sales figure. But the ledger cannot tell you whether the person who might have bought it stopped drinking, found another source, or made alcohol at home.

If enough of the market had simply moved out of sight, a collapse in official sales might say very little about what had happened to consumption.

So how could anyone find out?

Researchers later tried to estimate the alcohol that did not pass through state shops. Their methods could not count every hidden bottle; the figures for homemade alcohol are estimates, not a complete inventory.

What they found changed the story.

The unofficial supply appears to have grown substantially. But the estimated increase did not replace everything that had disappeared from the legal market. In Russia, estimated total consumption — official and unofficial combined — also fell between nineteen eighty-four and nineteen eighty-seven, although by much less than official sales alone suggest.

The first answer had been too simple: empty shelves did not mean everyone had stopped drinking. The opposite answer was too simple as well: the drinking had not all just moved somewhere else.

A second set of numbers pointed in the same direction.

During the campaign, death rates fell in Russia. Estimated life expectancy at birth for Russian men rose from about sixty-two years in nineteen eighty-four to about sixty-five in nineteen eighty-seven.

That does not mean that every man gained three years of life. Life expectancy is a measure calculated from death rates in a given period. The change reflected an improvement in those rates across the population.

Researchers have linked a substantial part of the improvement to lower alcohol consumption. They have compared changes across regions and studied causes of death connected to drinking. The campaign had many parts, and the data cannot tell us exactly which rule produced each result. But the evidence points to more than a change in where people bought a bottle.

The same campaign was now generating two very different kinds of news for the government.

On one side were signs of improved health. Fewer deaths meant more than a number in a report: families could have more time with people they might otherwise have lost.

On the other side was the government's own income.

The finance ministry's warning had been about future revenue. Once the campaign was underway, reduced official sales became a real financial pressure. Money that would have entered the state system through legal alcohol sales was no longer coming in at the same rate.

The lost revenue was visible to the budget. Many of the benefits appeared elsewhere: in death rates, in workplaces, and in the lives of families. They were harder to place on the same line of a government account.

That difference did not make the revenue imaginary. Governments need money to meet their commitments. But it also did not make every ruble from alcohol a gain for society. The state's income was only one part of a much larger picture that included illness, accidents, lost work and harm at home.

And there was another cost to the leadership: the campaign was unpopular. Restrictions, queues, higher prices and coercive enforcement shaped how people experienced it. Even where reducing heavy drinking could bring benefits, the state's methods created resistance.

The pressures did not wait for one neat final decision.

By late nineteen eighty-seven, the leadership was moving to expand alcohol production. In October nineteen eighty-eight, the Communist Party acknowledged serious problems with the campaign and called for sales to be normalized.

The reversal was gradual. Some restrictions stayed in place, and production took time to recover. The policy did not vanish from daily life on the day officials changed direction.

The Soviet Union was also facing problems far beyond alcohol. Later researchers have debated how the retreat from the campaign, economic upheaval and other changes contributed to Russia's mortality in the years that followed. This one policy cannot explain the collapse of the Soviet state or every change in public health.

But it does answer the question that opened our story.

Why did the government give up a source of money? Because its own leaders believed that the harm connected to drinking had become too great to ignore. They knew the budget would feel the loss. They chose to act anyway.

Why did they begin to reverse course? Because the missing revenue and the campaign's unpopularity became powerful pressures, even while the health figures showed reasons to take reduced drinking seriously.

Back at the Politburo meeting, the dispute had looked like a clash between an ambitious leader and officials worried about money. The years that followed exposed something larger.

The people collecting revenue, the officials responsible for health and the families living with the consequences did not stand in the same place. A policy could help some of them, burden others, and do both to the same household.

Gorbachev called it a drunk budget. The phrase was memorable because it named a dependence inside the system itself: the state was earning money from a product whose harms it also had to confront.

That is the uncomfortable lesson of this story. When an institution earns from a harmful product, reducing the harm can also threaten an income people have learned to depend on. Whether the product is legal or illegal, the question is to follow the money, count the harm, and ask who has the power to change the rules.
