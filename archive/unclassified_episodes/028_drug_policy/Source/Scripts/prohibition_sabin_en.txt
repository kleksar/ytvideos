THE WOMAN WHO CHANGED HER MIND ABOUT PROHIBITION

In July 1932, a group of women gathered in Roslyn, New York, to make a decision that could split their own movement.

They had spent years fighting to end America's ban on alcohol. Now they faced a presidential election, and the candidate promising repeal was a Democrat: Franklin Roosevelt.

Many of the women were Republicans. Some feared that taking sides in the election would damage an organization built across party lines.

Among them was Pauline Morton Sabin, a mother of two and a prominent Republican who had campaigned for the sitting president, Herbert Hoover.

Four years earlier, Sabin had publicly called for the alcohol ban to be overturned. But before that, she had supported it. She later said she had favored Prohibition for the sake of her sons.

Now the movement she had helped build had to decide how far it would go to undo the law.

Why had she changed her mind? And what would happen if the women fighting beside her could not agree on what to do next?

To understand that room in Roslyn, we have to go back to the danger the law was supposed to solve.

Before Prohibition, alcohol could tear families apart. Wages vanished at the saloon. Children went hungry. Some women lived with the fear of a husband coming home drunk.

In an era when many women had few legal and economic protections, a man's drinking could decide whether there was food on the table, or whether home was safe.

For decades, campaigners pushed to limit alcohol. Many were women. They organized, gave speeches, and pressed politicians for change.

Sabin, too, was sympathetic to Prohibition. She hoped that her sons might grow up spared a danger that had brought suffering to so many homes.

In 1920, the United States put that hope into law. A constitutional amendment banned the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquor nationwide. There were exceptions, including some medical and religious uses. Drinking itself was not generally outlawed by the amendment.

In many places, the familiar corner saloon closed. But people did not all stop wanting alcohol.

Some made it themselves. Some bought it from hidden bars. Smugglers brought supplies across borders and along the coast. Illegal networks found customers, and some officials took bribes to look away.

That did not mean everyone drank, or that the illegal market looked the same in every town. It meant that a business could survive outside the law, and that its customers included people who publicly appeared to respect that law.

Sabin was watching. Gradually, her confidence began to change.

In 1928, she wrote that respectable citizens were buying alcohol despite the ban. She worried about what children learned when adults defended a law in public and broke it in private.

In the same article, she passed on a story told to her by a schoolboy she knew. At a meeting about the transition to college, he said, students had discussed drinking for more than an hour. They considered its effects on sport, schoolwork, and reputation.

According to his account, no one mentioned Prohibition.

It was one secondhand story, not evidence of what every young American thought. But Sabin saw in it a warning: a law intended to shape the next generation could be treated by young people as though it did not exist.

She admitted that she had favored Prohibition when she heard it discussed as an idea. After years of watching it in practice, she had reached a different conclusion.

Her aim was still temperance. She no longer believed this law was the way to achieve it.

Other women strongly disagreed. They feared that ending Prohibition would bring back the saloons and the suffering they had spent years trying to prevent.

The conflict was not simply between people who cared about families and people who did not. Some women wanted to protect them by keeping the ban. Sabin wanted to protect them by replacing it.

Changing her mind was only the beginning. The law was written into the Constitution. To remove it, she would need far more than an article.

Sabin knew how politics worked. She had helped build a Republican women's organization and become a member of the party's national committee. In 1928, despite her doubts about Prohibition, she campaigned for Herbert Hoover.

Hoover won. But he did not offer the repeal Sabin wanted. Shortly after his inauguration in 1929, she resigned from the Republican National Committee.

That year she helped found the Women's Organization for National Prohibition Reform. Its task was to bring together women who opposed the existing law and make their voices impossible for politicians to overlook.

Sabin had advantages many mothers did not: wealth, connections, and political experience. They helped her attract attention and build an organization. She did not speak for every woman, and the supporters of Prohibition had powerful organizations of their own.

Over the next three years, the movement grew. But success brought a difficult question closer.

To end national Prohibition, supporters would need political power. And political power meant choosing candidates.

In 1932, Roosevelt and the Democrats offered the clearest presidential route toward repeal. For Republicans who wanted the ban gone, supporting him meant crossing a party line. For a movement that included women from both parties, a formal appeal to vote for him risked driving some members away.

That was the choice before the executive committee at Roslyn.

The press was excluded from the meeting, so we do not know everything said inside. We do know what was reported afterward.

The committee voted fifty-one to nineteen to urge the organization's members, Republicans and Democrats alike, to support Roosevelt.

Sabin backed him. Four years earlier, she had worked to elect his opponent.

The decision had consequences. A prominent member resigned. Sixty-four Republican members announced that they would not support the Democratic nominee.

The women had united to change the law. Now their movement was divided over what that change was worth at the ballot box.

Outside the meeting, other pressures were changing the country. The Great Depression had brought economic distress and strained public finances.

For some, legal alcohol offered the prospect of jobs, business, and tax revenue. For Sabin and many other campaigners, repeal promised a different way to deal with drinking and its consequences. Business interests, voters, activists, and politicians could support the same change for different reasons.

Under the ban, illegal sellers could profit from people who still bought alcohol. Repeal opened the way for licensed businesses to sell it legally and for governments to collect alcohol taxes again. Illegal sales did not simply vanish, and tax revenue did not erase the costs of illness and other harm.

Roosevelt won the election. In 1933, federal law first permitted the sale of low-alcohol beer. Later that year, the states ratified the Twenty-first Amendment, ending national Prohibition.

Sabin's organization had played an important part. It had not done it alone. Repeal came through a wider movement, economic pressure, elections, and decisions by states across the country.

The law Sabin had come to oppose was gone. The danger that first drew her to support it was not.

There is another part of this story that matters. Prohibition did not simply leave drinking unchanged.

Researchers studying the period estimate that alcohol consumption fell sharply at the beginning of the ban, before rising again in the years that followed. The estimates are uncertain, but they challenge the claim that the law accomplished nothing.

And repeal did not make alcohol safe. Legal sales can be taxed and regulated, yet alcohol can still cause dependence, illness, violence, and harm to families. After the national ban ended, different places continued to set different rules.

A ban could reduce consumption and still leave an illegal market. Repeal could bring sales back under legal rules and still leave people exposed to harm.

Today, societies still make different choices about alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, and other drugs. Their risks and legal markets differ, so Sabin's experience cannot settle those debates for every substance or country.

What it can do is remind us to judge a policy by what happens after the promise: whether it reduces harm, and at what cost.

Pauline Sabin began with a hope for her sons. Years later, she stood with a movement willing to split over how to undo a law she had once supported.

Her goal had not disappeared. Her certainty about the way to reach it had.
