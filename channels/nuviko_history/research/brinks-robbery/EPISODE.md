## Замысел и сюжет

**Viewer question.** How did an armed gang get through the locked doors of a Boston cash depot, and what finally gave investigators enough to bring the men to trial?

**Story answer.** The men had secretly obtained keys that fit the depot’s locks. A fight over the money and a shooting fractured the group. From jail, one of the gunmen admitted his role and gave the investigators an account they could test against evidence they had already collected and leads they could now pursue.

### 1. A way through the locked doors

January 17, 1950. Five employees are finishing the day’s count at Brink’s, a company that moves and guards money. Armed men in Halloween masks reach the second-floor counting area through a series of locked doors. They tie up the employees and leave with nearly $2.8 million, including roughly $1.2 million in cash. Once the truck is gone, the employees free themselves and call police.

### 2. They had been inside before

The story goes back to the preparation as Joseph O’Keefe, one of the gunmen, would later describe it. The gang watches the depot from nearby rooftops until they can tell from the lights what kind of work is going on inside. They take lock cylinders away one at a time, get keys made to fit, then put the cylinders back. The staff can keep using the same doors and the same keys. On several nights the gang approaches and calls the job off. On January 17, a lookout watches from a roof and gives the signal to go ahead. The men unlock the doors and reach the counting room.

### 3. A truck and a suitcase

A green Ford seen near the depot gives investigators a lead. A stolen truck matching the description turns up at a dump in Stoughton, cut apart with a torch and stuffed into sacks. O’Keefe and another suspect live in that town. Police search their homes and find nothing that ties either man to the truck or the robbery. Three years after the heist, there is still too little evidence to charge anyone.

Years later, O’Keefe tells the FBI he had received some of the money before the truck turned up. Two gang members had handed him a suitcase containing $98,000. He kept $5,000 and trusted another member, Adolph Maffie, to hold the rest. O’Keefe says he never saw that money again. Maffie says some was stolen and some went toward O’Keefe’s defense in a separate case. O’Keefe keeps pressing his associates for money. In June 1954, he is shot in the wrist and chest and survives. He ends up in jail for violating probation on a gun charge.

### 4. A witness who has to incriminate himself

From jail O’Keefe keeps writing to the others, asking for money. The men he is asking for help are the same men he can name in the robbery. On January 6, 1956, he starts telling FBI agents how the job was done. To explain who walked through the doors, he has to put himself there too.

The investigators can now go back over details that had been sitting in the case file for years. The chopped-up Ford fits the getaway account. The story about the locks leads to a locksmith who remembers making keys for Anthony Pino, the man O’Keefe identifies as a planner, on several evenings in 1949. Six men are arrested on January 12. The next day a Massachusetts grand jury indicts the gang. O’Keefe pleads guilty to armed robbery and later testifies against his accomplices.

### 5. The building in the courtroom

Before the trial, a worn $10 bill draws the attention of police in Baltimore. They find that much of the cash carried by the man who spent it came from the robbery. The trail leads to a picnic cooler hidden behind an office partition in Boston. Some of the notes are so water-damaged they come apart when handled. The investigators have recovered part of the loot. The center of the case is still what happened on Prince Street.

For the trial, the FBI builds scale models of the depot and its surroundings from photographs, sketches and information from the staff. The building the gang once watched from the rooftops is laid out for the jury. O’Keefe takes the stand and identifies what the others did. The jury convicts all eight men on trial. The film ends on the model, with the route through the depot visible again and the part O’Keefe played now part of the public record.

### N001

The doors were locked. The men who came through them had keys of their own.

By the time they reached the counting room, they were wearing Halloween masks. It was January 1950 in Boston, and five employees at Brink’s, a company that guarded and transported money, were finishing the day’s count. The gunmen tied them up and carried off nearly $2.8 million. About $1.2 million was cash. The rest was in checks, money orders and other securities.

Almost six years later, one of the gunmen began naming his accomplices from jail. His name was Joseph O’Keefe. By then he’d been shot, and he’d spent months writing to the very men he was about to name, asking them for money.

### N002

In the months before the robbery, the gang watched Brink’s from nearby rooftops. They learned to tell what was going on inside by which lights were on. They broke in after hours and set about making it easier to come back. On separate visits, they took out the center of each lock, had a key made to fit it, then put everything back. The employees could still open the doors with the keys they’d always used.

The gang came close to going through with the robbery several times. They turned back as late as the night before. On January 17, a lookout on a roof gave them the signal to go in.

They had the employees tied up and were filling bags when a buzzer went off. A garage attendant wanted to come in. Two gunmen headed toward the door. By the time they saw him, he was walking away. The gang loaded the bags onto a truck. The employees worked themselves free and called the police.

### N003

A green Ford truck had been seen outside Brink’s. In March, a stolen Ford matching that description turned up at a dump outside Boston. Someone had cut it apart with a torch and stuffed the pieces into sacks.

The dump was in the town where O’Keefe and another suspect lived. Police searched their homes and found nothing that tied either man to the truck or the robbery. By the third anniversary, there still wasn’t enough evidence to charge anyone.

Years later, O’Keefe told the FBI he’d already received some of the money by the time the truck turned up.

### N004

Two of his accomplices had handed him a suitcase. Inside was $98,000 from the job. He kept $5,000 and asked another man in the gang, Adolph Maffie, to hold the rest.

O’Keefe said he never saw that money again. Maffie said some had been stolen and the rest had gone toward O’Keefe’s legal bills in another case. O’Keefe kept demanding money from the gang.

In June 1954, someone opened fire on him in Boston. He was hit in the wrist and chest and survived. Later that summer, he was jailed for violating probation on a gun charge. He kept writing to his old partners, still asking them for money.

### N005

On January 6, 1956, FBI agents visited O’Keefe in jail. He began telling them who’d planned the robbery, how the gunmen had gotten in and where he’d been that night. He put himself in the counting room with the others.

The agents checked what he told them. The truck they’d found in pieces fit his account of the getaway. And the story about the keys led them to a locksmith. He remembered keeping his shop open late to make keys for Anthony Pino, one of the men O’Keefe named as a planner. When agents showed him locks from Brink’s, he said they looked like the ones Pino had brought him.

Six days after O’Keefe began talking, the FBI started making arrests. O’Keefe pleaded guilty to armed robbery.

### N006

The case was headed to court, and investigators were still looking for the money. That June, an arcade operator in Baltimore suspected a customer had paid him with a fake ten-dollar bill. Police found cash on the man and more in his hotel room. The FBI checked the serial numbers and traced much of the money to Brink’s.

The man said he’d gotten the cash at an office in Boston. Behind a wall in that office, agents found a picnic cooler full of bills. Some fell apart when they handled them.

### N007

For the trial, the FBI built scale models of Brink’s and the streets around it. They used photographs, investigators’ sketches and details from the people who’d worked there.

During O’Keefe’s cross-examination, a model was brought into the middle of the courtroom so he could point out features of the building. The gang had spent months watching Brink’s from the rooftops. Now the jurors could see it in miniature and hear from one of the men who’d walked through those locked doors. O’Keefe told the court what each of his former partners had done.

In October, the jury found all eight men on trial guilty. The judge sentenced them to life in prison.
