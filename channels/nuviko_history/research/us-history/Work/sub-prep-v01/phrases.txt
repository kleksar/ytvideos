In 1803, two American
negotiators were in France
hoping to buy a city.
They wanted New Orleans,
a port near the mouth
of the Mississippi River.
Farmers could float
their goods down that river
and ship them out to sea.
If another country could shut
the port, it could cut
them off from their markets.
Napoleon's government
offered the Americans
something much bigger:
all of Louisiana.
The purchase roughly
doubled the size
of the United States
on paper.
It also opened a fight
over what would happen
as the country grew.
Would slavery spread
into those lands?
Every new state could change
who held power in Congress.
And the people already living
on the land
had never been asked
whether
it was France's to sell.
The United States had begun
as thirteen British colonies
along the Atlantic.
After years of disputes
over taxes
and who had the right
to impose them,
the colonies went to war
with Britain and declared
independence in 1776.
They claimed that people
had equal rights,
even as slavery remained
legal and millions of people
had no political voice.
In the peace treaty of 1783,
Britain recognized the new
country, with the Mississippi
as its western boundary.
Indigenous nations lived
on both sides of that line.
For the American
government,
the immediate problem
was that the river's outlet
at New Orleans lay
outside its borders.
Napoleon had wanted
Louisiana as part
of a revived French
empire in the Americas.
But a revolution
in France's Caribbean colony
of Saint-Domingue, now
Haiti, was wrecking his bid
to restore control there.
With the colonial plan falling
apart and war with Britain
looming, he agreed to sell.
France transferred its claims
to Louisiana.
The people who lived
there still had their own
governments, communities
and lands.
As settlers moved west,
new states could apply
to join the Union.
Each state, large or small,
would send two members
to the Senate, one of the two
chambers of Congress.
As long as the number
of states that allowed
slavery matched
the number that banned it,
neither group had a majority
there on its own.
Then Missouri applied
to join as a state
where slavery would be legal.
Congress agreed to bring
in Maine as a free state
and Missouri as a slave state.
It also drew a line
through the rest
of the Louisiana Purchase,
banning slavery north
of that line, with Missouri
as the exception.
The compromise kept
the balance for a time.
Meanwhile, the government
was opening land
to settlers in places
already inside the nation's
claimed borders.
The Cherokee fought
removal through petitions
and the courts.
In 1838, soldiers forced
thousands from their homes
and sent them west.
Many died
during or as a result
of the removal remembered
as the Trail of Tears.
The map of the country
barely changed.
The people who could remain
on that land did.
Then the external borders
began to move again.
Texas broke away
from Mexico in 1836
and joined
the United States
nine years later
as a slave state.
Mexico disputed
the annexation, and the two
governments disagreed
over where Texas ended.
American troops entered
the disputed land
between the two
claimed borders.
After they clashed
with Mexican forces,
Congress declared war.
To the northwest,
the United States and Britain
settled their competing
claims in Oregon by treaty.
The war with Mexico
ended differently.
After American troops
took Mexico City,
Mexico ceded a vast
area that included California
and much of what is now
the American Southwest.
The United States
stretched from the Atlantic
to the Pacific.
These diplomatic lines
ran through lands
where Indigenous nations
still lived.
The new territory made
the old fight urgent again.
Congress admitted
California as a free state
in 1850 and passed a tougher
federal law requiring
the return of people
who had escaped slavery.
Four years later
it organized the territories
of Kansas and Nebraska
under a new rule.
Settlers would determine
whether slavery
was allowed there,
even though the old Missouri
line had barred it.
People on both sides
rushed into Kansas.
Rival groups fought
over elections, and then
they fought with guns.
The line
on the map could no longer
hold the peace.
The fight reached
the Supreme Court.
Dred and Harriet Scott,
an enslaved
couple who had lived
in places where slavery
was prohibited,
had sued for their freedom.
Dred's case went
to the Supreme Court.
He lost.
The Court's majority also said
Congress had no power
to ban slavery
in federal territories.
The compromise
that had once kept slavery
out of part of the West
was now under attack
from both lawmakers
and judges.
In 1860, Abraham Lincoln
won the presidency
on a platform that opposed
the spread of slavery.
Leaders in the slaveholding
South feared that as more
free states joined,
they would lose the power
to protect slavery.
South Carolina declared
that it was leaving the Union.
Other slave states followed
and formed the Confederacy.
Federal troops still held
Fort Sumter
in South Carolina.
In April 1861, Confederate
guns opened fire on the fort.
A country that had spent
decades pushing its borders
outward was now fighting
over whether it would remain
one country at all.
The Union went to war
to keep the country together.
Enslaved people forced
the war to confront
a second question.
At Fort Monroe in Virginia,
three men escaped
slavery and asked the Union
garrison for refuge.
When a Confederate
officer demanded them
back, the Union
commander refused.
More people came.
They were taking action
for their own freedom,
and the army
had to decide
what it would do
when they arrived.
In 1863 Lincoln
declared enslaved people
free in areas still in rebellion.
Where the Confederacy
held the ground, enforcing
that declaration depended
on what happened in the war.
The Mississippi
mattered again.
The Confederacy still held
Vicksburg, a fortified
city on the river.
Union troops surrounded it,
and people inside sheltered
from shells in caves
dug into the hills.
Vicksburg surrendered.
Days later the Union
captured the last Confederate
stronghold on the river,
separating Confederate
territory west
of the Mississippi
from its main
forces in the east.
The river that had helped
drive
the Louisiana Purchase
now cut
through the rebellion.
The war continued for almost
two more years.
In April 1865, Confederate
general Robert E. Lee
surrendered his army
to Ulysses S. Grant
at Appomattox.
Other Confederate
armies surrendered later.
By the end of the year,
the Thirteenth Amendment
had abolished slavery
throughout the country,
with an exception
for punishment
after a criminal conviction.
The Union had survived.
The map kept changing.
The United States bought
Alaska from Russia
and later annexed Hawaii
after the overthrow
of its monarchy.
Alaska and Hawaii
became states in 1959,
bringing the number to fifty.
Other places
under American sovereignty,
including Puerto Rico,
remained territories.
Back in 1803, the negotiators
had gone to France for a way
to get goods out to sea.
They came home
with a deal that changed
the country's scale.
As the United States
pushed west,
it took land
from Indigenous peoples,
and every new state
reopened the question
of where slavery
would be allowed
and who would have
the power to protect it.
The war kept the country
together and led
to the abolition of slavery.
The people who had fought
for freedom still had to fight
to exercise their rights
inside those borders.
