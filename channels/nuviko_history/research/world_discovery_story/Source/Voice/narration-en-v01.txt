In sixteen forty-two, two Dutch ships approached a mountainous coast in the southern Pacific.

Their commander, Abel Tasman, had never seen these distant islands before.

But as the ships sailed along the coast, smoke rose from the land.

Someone was already living there. For the Māori, these islands were home.

Their ancestors had crossed the Pacific from East Polynesia centuries before Tasman's arrival.

The Dutch would come to know the islands as New Zealand.

So when was this place discovered? The answer depends on who is asking.

And the same question changes the story of how people discovered the world.

Our story begins much earlier, in Africa, where our species, Homo sapiens, emerged.

Over many generations, groups of people moved beyond Africa into other regions.

This was not a single expedition, with a shared destination or a map.

Landscapes changed, coastlines shifted, and people moved through a world we cannot fully reconstruct.

In the Middle East, remains of our species are roughly ninety thousand years old.

That is evidence people were there, not a record of every step they took.

Farther east, people reached the great landmass that included present-day Australia and New Guinea.

People were living in Australia at least fifty thousand years ago.

Sea levels were lower then, joining Australia and New Guinea into one landmass.

But even at lower sea levels, open water separated it from Southeast Asia.

Someone had to cross the sea, long before the charts we know today.

We do not know the exact route, the first landing, or the first arrival date.

In Europe, too, evidence places Homo sapiens there more than forty-five thousand years ago.

Neanderthals, another kind of human, were already living across parts of Eurasia.

Across the planet, falling seas also exposed land between northeastern Asia and Alaska.

This broad region, called Beringia, connected places that are separated by water today.

But the precise routes and timing of people's journeys into the Americas remain uncertain.

In what is now New Mexico, human footprints survive beside an ancient lake.

Those footprints date to roughly twenty-three to twenty-one thousand years ago.

The footprints do not tell us who arrived first, or which way they came.

They tell us that people were already living there by that time.

Far south, people lived in present-day Chile around fourteen thousand five hundred years ago.

Over time, many different peoples developed across the Americas, with their own histories and connections.

By now, people lived in many parts of the world. Was the world discovered?

Not in the same way for everyone. Different communities knew different parts of it.

A sailor knew coasts and currents; a trader carried news from farther away.

Stories, journeys, and observations gradually built larger pictures of the world.

One surviving Babylonian map shows how its makers imagined the world around them.

It is a view from one place, not a record of everything humanity knew.

Meanwhile, knowledge passed between people across Africa, Asia, and Europe through travel and trade.

Across the North Atlantic, another journey reached a shore already known to others.

Norse voyagers crossed from Greenland to Newfoundland, on the edge of North America.

At a settlement there, wood was cut in the year ten twenty-one.

That date proves a Norse presence, not the year the first Norse ship arrived.

Indigenous peoples had already lived in the region long before the Norse arrived.

Other maps were drawing together knowledge from communities around a different sea.

In twelfth-century Sicily, the mapmaker al-Idrisi assembled accounts of lands and routes.

His work drew on information from many places, including lands he had not necessarily visited.

But maps on paper were only one way to carry knowledge across great distances.

In the Pacific, navigators read the stars, ocean swells, and signs from birds.

They carried knowledge of distant islands and ocean routes across generations.

From East Polynesia, settlers eventually reached the islands we call New Zealand.

Permanent settlement dates to the late thirteenth century; first arrival may have been earlier.

Those settlers became ancestors of Māori communities, long before Tasman's ships appeared.

On the other side of Asia, ships were connecting already busy ports.

For centuries, trade linked communities around the Indian Ocean, from Asia to East Africa.

In the early fifteenth century, Chinese fleets led by Zheng He sailed through these waters.

Some voyages reached East Africa, visiting ports that were already part of those networks.

The world was connecting in many directions, not waiting for one group to open it.

Then an Atlantic voyage brought two worlds into a connection with immense consequences.

In fourteen ninety-two, Christopher Columbus reached Caribbean islands while sailing west from Europe.

He had not reached an empty world. Taíno people already lived on those islands.

The encounter began a period of conquest, forced labor, and devastating disease.

These changes unfolded over time and brought catastrophe to Indigenous communities.

For Europeans, reports from the Atlantic forced a new question: what lay across the ocean?

In fifteen oh seven, a world map printed the name America on South America.

The map also showed land separated from Asia by an ocean to the west.

Its unusual outline was not yet an accurate picture of the whole Pacific.

But a voyage was about to make the scale of that ocean impossible to ignore.

In fifteen nineteen, an expedition led by Ferdinand Magellan sailed west from Spain.

The ships passed the southern tip of South America and entered the Pacific Ocean.

The crossing was far longer and more punishing than the crew had expected.

Magellan was killed in the Philippines, before the expedition could complete its journey.

In fifteen twenty-two, the ship Victoria returned to Spain under Juan Sebastián Elcano.

The expedition had circled the globe, but Magellan himself had not completed the voyage.

Educated people already knew Earth was round; the voyage revealed its vast, connected oceans.

Yet a journey around the world did not mean every coast was known to everyone.

That brings us back to the coast Tasman approached in sixteen forty-two.

Māori communities had been living in New Zealand for generations before the Dutch arrived.

After a violent encounter, in which four of his men were killed, Tasman sailed away.

He left without landing. For the Dutch, these islands were newly encountered land.

For people already living there, this was a new arrival, not a discovery of home.

One great continent remained beyond these stories of settlement and contact: Antarctica.

In the seventeen seventies, James Cook sailed around the southern seas without seeing the continent.

Ice and immense distances kept the land itself out of sight.

In eighteen twenty, several expeditions sighted parts of the Antarctic coast.

Even then, its complete coastline and interior were still far from understood.

Today, a world map can look like a single, finished answer.

But it brings together many stories: migration, navigation, local knowledge, trade, and mapping.

A place can have different dates for settlement, contact, and appearance on a map.

To understand any date of discovery, ask one question: who discovered this place, and for whom?
