Pompeii wasn't destroyed by lava. The real danger was far more deadly. In AD 79, Vesuvius erupted. But instead of rivers of molten rock, pumice, light volcanic stones, began raining down on the city. At first, they may not have seemed like much of a threat. But the stone rain continued for hours. Streets slowly disappeared beneath the debris. In some places, the layer of pumice reached around three metres deep. Under its weight, roofs collapsed. The next morning brought a new danger. Pyroclastic flows swept into the city: fast-moving clouds of searing gas, ash and volcanic particles. Pompeii was buried beneath the eruption.
