At a plague cemetery in London, archaeologists found 181 coins. They lay in the grave of a woman who died during the epidemic. We do not know why the money remained with her. Perhaps people feared infection if they removed the dead woman's clothing. But the same cemetery holds another important detail. Even in the communal graves, bodies were carefully laid out in rows. Fear did not bring all essential work to a stop. Someone continued to do it. The cemetery was established near the Tower of London when plague reached the city in late 1348. Ordinary burial grounds were running out of space. People did not know what caused the disease. They had to decide whether to open the door to a neighbor, care for a sick relative, or go to market. Lives depended on those decisions. But people often had no reliable way to tell which choice was right. How do you protect yourself from a danger you cannot recognize? And how do you preserve a city when everyone is afraid to come close to another person? Medieval Europe was not a collection of isolated villages. Cities needed grain, cloth, and metal. Ships sailed between ports, merchants carried goods across borders, and people traveled for work and returned home. This network made life possible even in places that could not produce everything they needed. It also created routes for infection. Animals and parasites moved alongside people and cargo. The disease could cross distances that no single sick person traveled from beginning to end. By the end of 1347, the epidemic was spreading across the Mediterranean. The following year, it swept through Florence, reached England, and made its way to London. The disaster also affected other parts of Eurasia and North Africa. Looking at a map, it is tempting to draw a single line and trace the disease's movement. In reality, there were many routes and separate outbreaks. Neighboring regions could experience them very differently. News of the disaster could arrive before the disease itself. People in England knew of the approaching danger before the first local outbreaks. But the warning did not answer the central question. What should they do now? Behind every point on the map was a place that still needed food. An order could close the gates. An order alone could not feed the people inside. So, authorities faced a problem with no simple solution. How to limit dangerous connections without depriving a city of the things it needed to survive? For someone who fell ill, that vast geography meant nothing. A fever began followed by severe weakness. In the bubonic form of plague, the lymph nodes became painfully swollen, often in the groin or under the arms. Sometimes the infection affected the blood or lungs. Today, a doctor knows what infection such symptoms may indicate. In the 14th century, people looked for different explanations. An imbalance within the body, corrupted air, or the influence of heavenly bodies. Religious beliefs linked the disaster to punishment for sin. If someone believes the air is dangerous, fragrant herbs and incense seem like reasonable protection. They act according to the knowledge available to them. The problem is that this knowledge does not explain what is happening. The cause was a bacterium, Yersinia pestis. It persists in natural reservoirs among small mammals and their fleas. A person can become infected through the bite of an infected flea or through contact with infected animal tissue. In the pneumonic form, transmission through respiratory droplets is possible during close contact with a sick person. Scientists are still investigating which routes predominated in different medieval outbreaks. They also debate the role of parasites that live on humans. A single diagram featuring a rat cannot confidently explain the entire pandemic. But people around someone who was ill saw a simpler sequence. One person became sick, and then someone nearby became sick, too. Even without knowledge of bacteria, closeness began to inspire fear. And so, a question about disease became a question about another person. When the sick person was a stranger, you could step away. But what could you do when the person who fell ill lived with you? In Florence, Giovanni Boccaccio described this change. In the introduction to the Decameron, he recounts how people tried to survive the epidemic. Authorities removed refuse, barred sick people from entering the city, and issued advice on staying healthy. Measures were taken, but the disaster could not be stopped. Some limited their contact with others, shut themselves away with a small circle of people, and tried to live moderately. Others drank, visited taverns, and sought entertainment. Some left the city. This is a work of literature, not a census of how every Florentine behaved. But it reveals the fear of people who no longer knew which of their habits were safe. Particularly painful are the passages about relatives who left the sick without help. Boccaccio describes the breaking of bonds people usually rely on in a crisis. A home, where a person should have been cared for, could become a place of isolation. Not everyone had the means to leave. Waiting out the disaster in the countryside required money, shelter, and people to provide for everyday life. In the Decameron itself, the group that leaves Florence has those resources. But if everyone leaves, who will bring water to the sick? Who will bake bread? Who will take away the dead? This was one of the epidemic's hardest contradictions. Staying away from the sick could reduce the danger to an individual, yet someone still had to care for those who could no longer help themselves. And when we return to the London burials, it becomes clear that a story in which everyone fled would be incomplete. At the East Smithfield Cemetery, there were individual graves as well as communal burial trenches. It is easy to imagine the latter as chaotic pits, but archaeologists found carefully arranged bodies. The cemetery itself required organization. Land had to be set aside, graves dug, the dead brought in, and burials carried out. The living did this work in a city where they too could fall ill. Excavations cannot tell us what each of them felt, but they show the result of their work. Even amid enormous losses, people were not always buried hastily and without order. At the same time, fear could lead to very different actions. Across Europe, false accusations spread against Jews that they were poisoning wells and causing the disease. Long-standing prejudices found a new justification. Persecution and mass killings followed. These accusations had nothing to do with the true cause of the plague. People became victims of violence that infection did not make inevitable. Other people committed it. So, it would be just as wrong to see the entire era only as a time of mutual help as it would be to see nothing but cruelty. Evidence of both care and brutality has survived. That evidence matters no less than what we know about the disease itself. Plague placed people in terrible circumstances, but their actions did not all amount to the same response. By the early 1350s, the first wave had weakened in many places. Plague would return again and again, but even now survivors faced consequences that the end of mass deaths could not undo. Historical accounts often cite estimates that roughly a third or as much as half of Europe's population died. These are not the results of an exact count. Medieval records are incomplete and newer research emphasizes how uneven the losses were. A percentage calculated for one city cannot simply be applied to an entire continent. On the ground, however, the consequences were tangible. Where many people had died, fields remained with too few hands to work them and workshops were short of workers. Land alone did not produce a harvest. A workshop without a craftsperson could not fill an order. Those who hired labor had to compete for the people who remained. Some workers gained the opportunity to demand higher wages or choose another employer. For land owners and workshop owners, this meant higher costs and a weakening of their customary control over other people's labor. In England, the response included an ordinance in 1349 and the Statute of Laborers passed 2 years later. They attempted to limit wages, workers' movement, and their ability to take better terms elsewhere. Able-bodied people were required to accept work under the prescribed rules. Surviving did not yet mean freedom to direct one's own life. New opportunities had to be fought for while those in power tried to restore the old relationships. The plague did not abolish dependence and poverty in a single blow. Its consequences differed from place to place, but the enormous losses forced people to confront a question that had once seemed settled. What is human labor worth, and who has the right to set its price? Ways of protecting a city from another outbreak also changed. For a trading port, the challenge remained the same. Keep disease out without stopping a way of life that depended on arriving people and cargo. In 1377, the authorities of Ragusa, today's Dubrovnik, adopted a special rule. People arriving from places where the disease was raging were forbidden to enter the city immediately. First, they had to spend 30 days apart in designated places outside it. The rule also applied to local residents returning from infected areas. Even someone with a home in the city could be required to wait before going back inside. The wait itself was a severe ordeal. Conditions in the first places of isolation were harsh. Food was brought in from outside. The decision to separate arrivals immediately created another obligation, to supply them with what they needed. Visits to people in isolation were restricted, too. The delivery of necessities had to be arranged separately. Otherwise, the people coming to help would themselves defeat the purpose of isolation. Later, a 40-day period became widespread. The word quarantine comes from the Italian word for 40. Doctors still could not see the pathogen, but the authorities were already changing the way people came into contact with one another in an effort to reduce risk. They had found a measure they could carry out even without a complete explanation of the disease. It was not a final victory. Plague returned for centuries, nor did quarantine turn a port into an impregnable fortress. But between helpless waiting and bringing trade to a complete halt, another course of action had emerged, to separate a person's arrival from their meeting with the city by a period of time. Now, let us return to London, to the cemetery near the tower. The people buried there did not know what had caused their illness, but something survived in their remains that allowed researchers to investigate it centuries later, fragments of bacterial DNA. In 2011, researchers published a reconstruction of the genome of the plague bacterium from the remains of victims of the London epidemic. By then, Yersinia pestis had long been known to science. Now, researchers could connect it to particular people who had died during the Black Death and study an ancient form of the pathogen. Later, another important trace was found far from Europe in what is now Kyrgyzstan. In the Chüy Valley, gravestones from 1338 and 1339 survived with references to pestilence. Scientists studied remains from these burials and found the plague bacterium there as well. The findings, published in 2022, placed the identified strain at the base of a genetic branching associated with the Black Death. In other words, an important clue to the early history of the pandemic had emerged from about 10 years before the disaster in Florence and London. This is not a map of every infected person's journey, nor does it establish who the first patient was, but later theories can now be compared with physical traces of the disease itself. Genetic analysis can identify the pathogen. To understand how people lived through the disaster, we need other evidence, too. Letters, books, documents, and the burials themselves. The arrangement of the graves preserves part of that story as well. The money remained with the dead woman, and beside her lay other people buried in an order that someone continued to maintain. We do not know the names of those who dug the graves and brought the dead here. We do not know what drove them. Duty, wages, faith, or care for their loved ones. But the city held together in part because of their work. They carried it out without yet knowing what was killing their neighbors.
