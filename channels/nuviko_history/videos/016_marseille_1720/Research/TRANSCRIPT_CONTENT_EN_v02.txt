In May 1720, a French merchant ship approached the coast of Italy.
Her captain asked to enter the port of Livorno.
The authorities refused.
People had been dying aboard, one after another.
Even the ship's surgeon was dead.
The captain had good reason to fear that something terrible was travelling with him.
He continued west, towards Marseille.
There, he reported the deaths and handed health officials his papers.
Yet people and valuable cargo were allowed into a quarantine station on the mainland,
close to the city.
Within months, the captain would be imprisoned, the ship would be burned,
and Marseille would become a city of the dead.
The most unsettling part is that the danger had not been entirely invisible.
Marseille already had rules designed to keep the plague out.
The warnings were there.
What happened next is the story of a city that failed to act on them in time.
To understand why, we need to go back almost a year.
In July 1719, a three-masted French merchant ship, the Grand Saint-Antoine,
left Marseille for the eastern Mediterranean.
Her captain was Jean-Baptiste Chateau.
Marseille was one of France's great trading ports.
Its merchants connected Europe with the markets of the Levant.
Ships returned with spices, cotton, silk, and other valuable goods.
A successful voyage could bring a considerable profit.
Over the following months, the Grand Saint-Antoine visited several ports in the eastern Mediterranean.
Cargo accumulated in her hold.
Among the most valuable items were textiles, bales of cotton and silk intended for European buyers.
The cargo was worth roughly 100,000 ecu, an enormous sum.
Some of it was intended for the Beaucer fair, where merchants gathered in the summer.
Delays threatened serious losses.
One of the men with an interest in the cargo was Jean-Baptiste Estelle,
Marseille's first Echevin, or senior city official.
The people who stood to lose money had connections to those responsible for the city's affairs.
That connection would become part of the controversy over what happened next.
In the spring of 1720, the voyage began to go wrong.
On April 3rd, a passenger who had boarded at Tripoli died.
Then, more people fell ill.
Some died with frightening speed.
The ship's surgeon was among the dead.
For the crew, his death was terrifying.
The man who should have cared for the sick could no longer help them.
There was still a long journey ahead, and the cause of the deaths had not been conclusively established.
According to the later account of Marseille physician Jean-Baptiste Bertrand,
Chateau was so alarmed that he separated himself from the crew,
staying at the stern and giving orders from there.
The captain went to Livorno.
Local officials did not allow the ship to dock.
A medical certificate described the deaths in terms of a dangerous, contagious fever.
Whatever its exact wording meant to the authorities at the time,
the deaths were a warning that could not be ignored.
The ship continued towards France.
On May 25th, the Grand Saint-Antoine reached the waters off Marseille.
18th century Marseille was not defenceless against plague.
The city had spent decades building a sanitary system.
Arriving captains declared where they had travelled, what they carried,
and whether there had been illness aboard.
Ships from suspect ports, their passengers and cargo, could be placed under quarantine.
For the most dangerous cases, officials could use more remote islands, including Jarre.
Isolation was meant to keep people and goods away from the city until the danger had passed.
Chateau had clean bills of health from the eastern Mediterranean ports he had visited.
These were documents saying that, when issued, those ports were not officially considered infected.
But the deaths during the voyage raised a different question.
The Marseille health authorities had the captain's declaration and information about the alarming deaths.
Instead of keeping everything in the most remote isolation,
the authorities allowed passengers and valuable cargo to be taken to the mainland infirmaries,
known as the Lazaret.
It was a quarantine facility, but it lay much closer to the city.
Historians have connected this decision to the commercial interests surrounding the cargo.
The degree of personal responsibility of each official remains debated.
It would be too simple to pretend that we know every conversation,
or can name one person who caused the catastrophe.
While the city continued its daily life, people connected with the ship kept dying.
A sailor died after its arrival.
On June 12th, a guard placed aboard the ship died as well.
Porters who handled the cargo fell ill.
The danger had not disappeared.
Yet, the response remained slow.
Passengers were eventually allowed to leave quarantine.
Goods were still being handled at the infirmaries.
How exactly the infection crossed from the Lazaret into Marseille is not fully established.
A common explanation is that contaminated goods, including textiles, left the quarantine area.
People and their belongings may also have helped carry infection beyond it.
Three centuries later, no complete chain of transmission can be reconstructed with certainty.
By late June, suspicious deaths were appearing in the city itself.
A woman on Rue Belle Table became ill and died.
Soon afterwards, a tailor and members of his family died.
In early July, other patients developed swollen glands, buboes associated with plague.
In a crowded trading city, people died from many illnesses.
At first, a handful of deaths in poorer districts did not force the authorities to shut down the port.
But the warning signs were multiplying.
Deaths at sea, deaths in quarantine, sick porters, and now deaths in the streets.
On July 7th, the health surgeon Gerard recognized plague in sick porters.
On July 10th, a young physician, Jean-André Pessonel, diagnosed plague in a patient in the city and warned the authorities.
His warning was not treated with the urgency the situation demanded.
Officials may have hoped the disease could be contained.
They also knew that acknowledging an epidemic would have consequences.
Closing a port could devastate trade.
Quarantine could isolate the city.
Whatever the balance of motives, the delays cost precious time.
By the end of July, more districts were affected.
In August, the crisis overwhelmed the city.
Hospitals filled.
There were too few people to care for the sick.
Those with the means tried to leave.
Others stayed behind, trapped by fear, illness, or the restrictions imposed around Marseille.
Escape carried its own danger.
As people moved through Provence, the epidemic could spread with them.
Marseille was increasingly cut off.
Trade stopped.
The economic disaster that officials had feared had arrived, alongside a far greater human tragedy.
At the height of the outbreak, historical estimates describe hundreds of deaths each day,
sometimes approaching a thousand.
Bodies could not be buried quickly enough.
Ordinary services collapsed under the scale of the emergency.
One of the worst scenes was at La Tourette, where many bodies lay in the open.
A local official, the Chevalier Nicolas Rose, organized their removal and burial.
Workers and prisoners were brought in for the dangerous task,
using the old fortifications as part of the burial site.
Doctors, clergy, officials, and ordinary residents continued to help.
Many exposed themselves to the disease.
Some died.
In September, Charles Pessonel, father of the young doctor who had warned the authorities in July,
died during the epidemic.
The disaster was no longer confined to one ship or one quarantine station.
It had engulfed a city and spread into the surrounding region.
The Grand Saint-Antoine had already been isolated near the island of Jars.
On September 26th, she was moved into a cove there to be destroyed.
Acting on orders from the Regent of France, the authorities burned the ship.
The vessel that had returned with a fortune in cargo ended its last voyage in flames.
But burning it could not undo what had already happened.
Passengers had left quarantine, goods had been brought ashore,
and plague was spreading through Provence.
The epidemic would eventually recede, but its toll remained immense.
Estimates vary.
Tens of thousands died in Marseille, and the wider outbreak is often estimated to have killed around 100,000 people across Provence.
The exact totals remain uncertain.
What happened to the captain?
Chateau was arrested and imprisoned at the Chateau d'If on an island off Marseille.
He spent nearly three years in custody.
In 1723, a court dismissed the case against him and ordered his release.
The man who brought the ship home was released.
The decision did not erase the deaths, and it did not settle every historical question about the actions of the city's authorities.
More than two and a half centuries later, the wreck was identified near Jarre in 1978.
Archaeologists investigated the site, and an anchor recovered from the seabed is now displayed at the Marseille History Museum.
The physical remains bring us back to the ship, but the story was larger than one vessel.
Marseille had a quarantine system.
Its officials had information about suspicious deaths.
The decisions they made did not prevent the disease from reaching the city.
There was no single moment when the outcome became certain,
and we cannot know exactly how many lives different decisions would have saved.
What we can see is a series of warnings, followed by a series of choices,
in a city caught between commerce and the threat of disease.
The ship was burned.
The case against its captain was dismissed.
For Marseille, the cost of those months would remain long after the flames had gone out.
