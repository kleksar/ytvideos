Why We Can’t Stop Scrolling Bad News

One more update. Then you’ll put the phone down.

You opened the news to answer one question. Is the storm getting closer? Has the fighting stopped? Is that frightening claim actually true?

Now twenty minutes have passed. Each answer has opened another question. You may be waiting for the piece of information that will make it feel safe to leave.

But what if that update never arrives?

Long before the first smartphone, someone recognized this restless search. In eighteen fifty-four, Henry David Thoreau published Walden. In one passage, he pokes fun at a familiar type: the man who wakes from a short daytime nap and immediately asks, “What’s the news?”

Thoreau was drawing a satirical portrait of a habit his readers might recognize. He compared the demand for morning news to the need for breakfast. The desire to check had already become something a writer could joke about.

Yet his world was hardly an age of peaceful, perfectly measured information.

By the eighteen-nineties, New York newspaper publishers Joseph Pulitzer and William Randolph Hearst were fighting for circulation. Headlines grew larger and more sensational. Papers could issue several editions in a single day. There was always another edition to sell.

News could help readers understand danger, politics, and the decisions affecting their lives. That value existed alongside a commercial problem: every paper needed people to pick it up. The next headline could offer useful knowledge. It could also offer another reason to keep looking.

Then television made news available on a different schedule. On June first, nineteen eighty, CNN began broadcasting around the clock. Viewers with access to the cable channel could follow a developing story without waiting for the evening bulletin.

That was useful. It also meant the broadcast could continue while the answers remained uncertain. A channel could fill twenty-four hours. An unfolding event might take days, months, or years to understand.

Later, another change appeared in how we moved through information. Engineer Hugh Williams recalled that, in two thousand five, his team developed an automatically continuing display for MSN’s image search. People looking through pictures would no longer need to keep clicking through separate pages.

This was an image search interface, with a practical navigation problem to solve. And its early “infinite” scroll had a surprisingly definite ending: one thousand images.

Other teams were exploring related ideas. In two thousand six, a public discussion of Humanized Reader described a news reader that added material as someone approached the bottom of the page. Supporters liked the flow. Critics worried about losing their place. Developer Aza Raskin acknowledged a similar approach at Microsoft.

There was no single moment when one inventor switched the world into endless scrolling. But a consequential design choice was taking shape. A page could keep supplying material without asking you to press “next.” Continuing required less interruption. The moment for deciding whether to continue became easier to pass.

Now put that interface beside a worried person.

A headline reports a disaster. Below it comes a prediction. Then a reaction to the prediction. Then someone insisting the danger is being hidden.

You may have learned the confirmed facts several posts ago. But the stream keeps producing reasons to feel that the story is unfinished. And of course it is unfinished. The world keeps happening.

The difficulty comes when we start waiting for that stream to deliver a feeling of completion.

The wording of a headline can help draw us in. In a study published in twenty twenty-three, researchers analyzed thousands of tests from the website Upworthy. Readers had been randomly shown different headlines for the same stories. The analysis found that headlines with more negative words attracted more clicks.

That finding comes from one website and one period. It measured what people opened, not whether they became dependent on the news. Still, it shows how a few words can influence where attention goes.

Coming back can involve something deeper than curiosity. A three-year study followed more than four thousand Americans across the aftermath of the Boston Marathon bombings and a later attack in Orlando. Greater media exposure after the first tragedy predicted later distress. Greater worry predicted more news consumption when the next tragedy happened.

This was observational research. It could not prove that the coverage alone caused the distress. But the pattern fits a troubling possibility: worry can bring someone to the news, and what they encounter can leave them wanting to check again.

“What if the next update is the one I need?”

During the pandemic, researchers tested a more immediate question. In two randomized experiments, people were assigned to general COVID-related content, stories of kindness during the pandemic, or no such content. Those who saw the general COVID news reported less positive emotion afterward than the no-content group. The kindness stories did not show the same reduction.

These were short-term effects in a particular emergency. They tell us something about a moment after reading, not the permanent condition of someone’s mind. Even within one crisis, different content could leave people feeling different.

Doomscrolling gives a name to repeatedly seeking out distressing information, sometimes continuing even as we feel worse. Time spent reading alone cannot tell us whether someone has a mental health disorder.

Our reasons for checking can be very ordinary. We care about people. We dislike being caught unprepared. We want to understand what happens next. Those are understandable wishes. But understanding an event takes more than watching it accumulate updates.

Consider a storm. An official warning may tell you where it is moving and what precautions to take. That information can change your decision. A tenth replay of the same frightening footage may leave the decision exactly where it was.

Both appear on a screen. You can ask what each one is helping you do.

That question offers a place to begin.

For the next seven days, try a small personal experiment: give one daily news check a beginning and an ending.

This is a way to explore what works for you, not a treatment or a guarantee.

Before opening the feed, finish this sentence: “I want to find out…” Choose something specific. What has actually changed? What is confirmed? Does this require me to act?

Then choose where to look. Start with a reliable report. When the question concerns your immediate safety, look for the relevant official guidance. You do not have to settle every argument attached to the story.

Give that check a modest time window. Ten minutes could be a starting point, adjusted to your work, responsibilities, and circumstances. Keep emergency alerts, public safety warnings, and essential contacts available. During a developing emergency, your need for information may be very different.

For routine news, consider checking at chosen times instead of responding to every notification.

When the session ends, ask three questions. What did I learn? Is there something useful I can do? When would it make sense to check again?

Sometimes the answer will be an action: change a journey, contact someone, follow a safety instruction, or read a fuller explanation. Sometimes the answer will be that the facts are still uncertain. You can leave a question open. You can write it down and return when there is a reason to expect new information.

Then make the next activity concrete. Put the phone down and wash the dishes. Step outside. Open the project you meant to work on. Give a conversation your full attention.

At the end of the week, notice what happened. Did you miss information you actually needed? Were you better able to concentrate? Did your worry change? Adjust the experiment around the answers. Some days will demand more news. Some habits will take longer to change. You can begin with one pause, in one ordinary part of the day.

Thoreau’s impatient reader wanted to know what had happened while he slept. We still recognize that urge. Across newspapers, television, and scrolling screens, the promise of the next update has kept changing its form. Our ability to receive information has grown enormously. Choosing what deserves our attention remains a human task.

You may never receive the headline that announces everything is settled, everyone is safe, and you can finally stop. You can still learn enough to make your next decision.

And then there is the rest of your day. A meal to make. A person to call. Something to learn slowly, without refreshing.

The next update will arrive. You can choose when to meet it.
