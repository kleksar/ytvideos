# Episode 035 — When Wages Rose, Why Did the Basics Get Harder to Afford?

**Draft status:** candidate narration for review; not yet accepted by the user. The claim IDs in brackets are production notes and are not spoken.

## HOOK

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Establish the central contradiction between rising silver wages and a declining modeled purchasing-power margin.

Imagine two moments in London, a century apart. In Allen's series for London building laborers, the later daily silver wage is more than twice as high. [C1] Yet Allen's modeled margin above the basic basket has narrowed. [C3]

How can someone earn more silver and still have less room above the cost of the basics?

The answer is not hidden in the wage alone. It lies in what that silver had to buy.

We will follow the comparison across several centuries, then stop at the moment the numbers seem to contradict each other.

## INTRO

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Explain the welfare-ratio method and its limits before presenting the historical comparisons.

To make that comparison, Robert Allen used a measure called the welfare ratio. [C6] It sets assumed annual laborer earnings against the reconstructed cost of a poverty-line basket for a notional family of four.

A ratio of one means the assumed earnings match the basket's cost. Above one means they exceed it; below one means they fall short. [C6]

But this is a model, not a household account discovered in an archive. The annual-income calculation assumes 250 workdays in a year. The model adds only a 5% minimum rent allowance to the basket; it is not an observed rent bill. [C6]

The reconstructed basket itself is broader than a loaf of bread: it includes categories such as beans or peas, meat, dairy, eggs and beer, alongside soap, linen, candles, lamp oil and fuel. [C7] That is a historian's standardized benchmark, not a receipt from a known family's kitchen.

It does not tell us what a particular family actually bought, paid, or worked. These are not actual family budgets or recorded work schedules. [C6] The model gives us a consistent benchmark for comparing estimated earnings with the cost of basic necessities.

That distinction matters. We are not going to invent a worker, fill a kitchen with imagined food, or turn a ratio into a story about one family's private life.

We are asking a narrower question: how did the estimated annual earnings of London laborers compare with a reconstructed minimum basket, and what happens when wages and prices move at different speeds?

## INVESTIGATION / DEVELOPMENT

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Establish the historical baseline, introduce the post-plague contrast, then build the sixteenth-to-seventeenth-century wage-price reversal. Invite a prediction before revealing the price index.

Start before the Black Death.

In Allen's reconstruction, London laborers stood at about three-quarters of the modeled poverty line. [C5] In other words, the earnings estimate was below the amount required to match the basket.

That is the first position on our scale: less than one.

The fifteenth century presents a different picture. Allen finds that earnings were generally above the modeled poverty line. [C5]

The comparison had moved to the other side of the threshold.

That does not tell us that every household was secure, or that every worker had the same experience. It tells us that, in this reconstruction, laborers' earnings generally exceeded the cost of the specified basket.

Now narrow the lens to London building laborers in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

For 1500 to 1549, the daily silver wage in Allen's tables is 3.2 grams. [C1]

The modeled welfare ratio is 1.42. [C3]

On this measure, assumed annual earnings were 1.42 times the cost of the poverty-line basket: a margin of 42 percent above the benchmark. [C3][C6]

That sounds like a useful margin.

And if we looked only at the daily wage, the next period might seem even better.

Between 1500–1549 and 1600–1649, the daily silver wage rises from 3.2 grams to 7.1 grams. [C1]

More than twice as much silver for a day's work.

So what should happen to the ability to afford the basics?

Pause here and make a prediction.

If the daily wage more than doubles, would you expect the modeled margin above the basket to grow, shrink, or stay about the same?

Hold on to your answer.

We have not looked at prices yet.

For 1600 to 1649, the welfare ratio is not higher.

It is 1.16. [C3]

The modeled margin above the poverty line has narrowed from 42 percent to 16 percent. It remains above one, but the distance above the benchmark is much smaller. [C3]

So where did the apparent improvement go?

Look at the price index.

London's consumer-price index rises from 0.45 in 1500–1549 to 1.25 in 1600–1649. [C2]

These are relative index values, not prices in currency; Allen sets Strasbourg in 1745–1754 at 1.00. [C2]

That increase is larger, proportionally, than the rise in the daily silver wage from 3.2 grams to 7.1 grams. [C1][C2][C4]

Prices rose faster than daily wages in this comparison. [C4]

That is the answer to the opening puzzle.

The daily wage was higher in silver terms, but the basket's measured price in Allen's index rose faster.

The model therefore shows a smaller margin between assumed annual earnings and the reconstructed basket. The result is not that the ratio fell below the poverty line. It did not. At 1.16, it remained above one. What changed was the size of the modeled cushion. [C3][C6]

## CLIMAX

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Deliver the numerical revelation and resolve the audience prediction. Make the distinction between nominal wages and purchasing power unmistakable.

Put the three measures side by side.

Daily silver wages move from 3.2 grams to 7.1. [C1]

The consumer-price index moves from 0.45 to 1.25. [C2]

And the welfare ratio moves in the opposite direction, from 1.42 down to 1.16. [C3]

Now return to your prediction.

If you expected the higher wage to mean a larger margin, the price index changes the answer.

The daily wage rose, but prices rose faster. That is why the modeled relationship between annual earnings and the basic basket became less favorable. [C1][C2][C4]

This is the central distinction: a wage stated in silver is not the same thing as purchasing power.

The number of grams can rise while the amount those wages can cover, measured against a basket, becomes smaller. [C1][C2][C3]

In this case, the margin narrowed; it did not disappear. [C3]

## RESOLUTION

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Extend the comparison into the nineteenth century, deliver a final numerical contrast, and leave its cause open.

There is one more point in this story.

For London laborers in 1850–1899, Allen reports a welfare ratio of 2.15. [C3]

That means the modeled annual earnings were more than twice the reconstructed poverty-line basket's cost. [C3]

Compare that with 1.16 in the seventeenth-century comparison.

The difference is striking.

But we should resist the temptation to turn this final number into an easy explanation.

The ratio tells us where the comparison ended up. It does not establish why it became so much higher. The causes of this later change remain unresolved. [C3]

And remember what the numbers represent. The calculation still relies on assumed annual earnings, a reconstructed basket and a notional family. It does not give us the actual accounts of a household living in nineteenth-century London. [C6]

We have found a changing relationship between earnings and the cost of basic necessities. We have not recovered the complete experience of the people behind those numbers.

## FINAL

**Editorial purpose (not spoken):** Return to the reconstructed basket and close on the difference between modeled economic position and individual experience.

Return to that reconstructed basket.

Imagine it beside four figures, each representing a different point in London's economic history.

About 0.75 before the Black Death. [C5]

Then 1.42 in 1500–1549. [C3]

Down to 1.16 in 1600–1649. [C3]

And finally, 2.15 in 1850–1899. [C3]

The figures do not tell us what one family ate, what rent it actually paid, or how many days its breadwinner worked.

They tell us something narrower, but still important: how estimated laborer earnings compared with a reconstructed measure of the basic necessities.

And perhaps the most revealing part of this history is the reversal in the middle.

More silver did not automatically mean more purchasing power.

Because earning more and being able to afford more are not always the same thing.

The basket stayed our benchmark. The numbers around it changed.

And that is how the cost of the basics can tell a very different story from the wage itself.
