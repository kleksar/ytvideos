## Result

All seven claims have documentary support with qualifications. The safe causal conclusion is that World War I accelerated national adoption of an already discussed idea; there is no demonstrated continuous causal line from Germany’s 1916 action to modern rules worldwide.

1. Germany introduced the first nationwide DST regime in 1916, with transition beginning 30 April at 23:00. Earlier proposals existed; local Port Arthur/Fort William practice is reported for 1908. Germany should not be called the inventor or first place to change clocks at any scale. Austrian-Hungarian use also followed in 1916.
2. British Summer Time received Royal Assent on 17 May 1916 and first applied on 21 May at 02:00 GMT. Distinguish enactment from first application.
3. The 1918 U.S. Standard Time Act established seasonal federal DST. The 1919 act repealed §3 specifically, not federal time zones. Wilson vetoed it on 15 August; the House overrode the veto on 19 August and the Senate on 20 August. Farm objections were important, but not the sole reason.
4. Congressional testimony records claims about dew delaying field work, fixed work hours and neighboring shops closing early. This establishes that complaints were made, not that every farm experienced the problem or that national impact was measured.
5. Year-round U.S. War Time ran from 9 February 1942 through 30 September 1945. A separate September 1945 act set the actual end.
6. The 1966 Uniform Time Act set initial common dates and let states opt out. Current dates (second Sunday in March / first Sunday in November) began in 2007; NIST describes the rules while DOT is the regulator.
7. War made time-shifting a state conservation measure and accelerated spread. It is not established that Germany's 1916 measure was necessary or sufficient for modern worldwide observance.
