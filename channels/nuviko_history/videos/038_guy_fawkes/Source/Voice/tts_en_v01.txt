On the night of November 5, 1605, a man and barrels of gunpowder were discovered beneath the House of Lords in London. The man gave his name as John Johnson. His real name was Guy Fawkes.

That same day, King James the First of England was due to attend the opening of Parliament. Members of both Houses would be gathered there with him. The people who had hidden the gunpowder planned to kill them all in a single explosion.

But Fawkes was not the leader of the plot. And yet, centuries later, it is his name that millions of people still remember.

Two years earlier, England had a new monarch. Many English Catholics hoped that James would ease the religious restrictions imposed under his predecessor, Elizabeth the First. Catholics faced fines and persecution, and Catholic priests were being hunted down.

Those hopes for change were disappointed. So a small group of Catholics decided to take action. They did not represent all English Catholics. The plot was a decision made by specific individuals.

The group was led by a nobleman named Robert Catesby. His plan was to blow up Parliament during its opening ceremony and kill the king.

Guy Fawkes also joined the conspiracy. He was a soldier who had served in the Spanish army. His job was to guard the gunpowder and, when the time came, ignite it.

But the explosion was only the beginning of their plan. The conspirators hoped to launch an uprising and seize the king's young daughter, Princess Elizabeth. They believed that once James was dead, they could bring about changes in the country.

In 1605, the conspirators rented a space beneath the House of Lords and brought the gunpowder inside. The opening of Parliament had been postponed several times, but it was finally scheduled for November 5. Everything seemed ready.

Then a letter appeared.

On October 26, Lord Monteagle received an anonymous warning advising him not to attend Parliament. The letter reached the royal authorities.

Who sent it remains unknown. Suspicion fell on one of the conspirators, a relative of Monteagle, but his authorship was never proven.

The conspirators learned about the letter. Now there was a danger that the authorities would search the building and discover the gunpowder. Even so, they decided to go ahead.

On the night before Parliament was due to open, the authorities conducted a search. They found Fawkes near the room containing the gunpowder and arrested him. The explosion never happened.

While Fawkes was being questioned in London, Catesby and other conspirators tried to gather supporters outside the capital. But the large uprising they had hoped for never materialized. A few days later, the authorities caught up with the fugitives. Catesby was killed in a shootout.

Fawkes was imprisoned in the Tower of London and interrogated. A royal order has survived authorizing the use of torture against him. In January 1606, Fawkes and the other surviving conspirators were executed.

The conspirators had wanted to change the position of English Catholics. But after the plot failed, suspicion toward Catholics intensified, and the authorities introduced new requirements for their oath of allegiance.

November 5 itself became a day of thanksgiving for the survival of the king and Parliament. Over time, a tradition developed in Britain of marking the day with bonfires and fireworks. People began burning effigies of Guy Fawkes on those bonfires.

And so a man who had never led the conspiracy gave his name to a celebration that commemorates its failure.
