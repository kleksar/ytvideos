## A · German commander’s report

**Title:** What a German Commander Acknowledged About Brest Fortress

**Thumbnail:** [A · A COSTLY ASSAULT](../Thumbnails/v06/thumbnail_A_v01.png)

**Description:**

On July 8, 1941, German commander Fritz Schlieper signed a military report that acknowledged the heavy cost of assaulting Brest Fortress.

The fighting had begun on June 22, when German artillery struck the fortress. Its separated sections became isolated battlefields, while Soviet defenders struggled with disrupted communications, dwindling supplies, scarce water, and wounded comrades.

By the end of June, the main organized defense had ended. Yet individual soldiers and small groups continued resisting into July. Major Pyotr Gavrilov was captured on July 23.

Schlieper's report recorded 453 killed or missing and 668 wounded in his division during the fighting for Brest-Litovsk as a whole, not exclusively within the fortress.

This film follows the battle and what the German commander's account reveals about the resistance his division encountered.

## B · How long the fighting lasted

**Title:** Did Brest Fortress Really Hold Out for More Than a Month?

**Thumbnail:** [B · WHO HELD OUT?](../Thumbnails/v06/thumbnail_B_v01.png)

**Description:**

The familiar claim that Brest Fortress held out for more than a month folds two different stages into one.

German artillery attacked on June 22, 1941. Soviet defenders fought across separated fortified sections, facing broken communications, shortages of supplies, and dangerous attempts to reach water.

By the end of June, the main organized defense had ended. But the fighting did not simply stop there: individual soldiers and small groups continued to resist during July, under very different conditions.

Major Pyotr Gavrilov was captured on July 23, severely wounded and exhausted. His survival inside the fortress does not establish that a unified garrison held the entire complex until that date.

The film also examines German commander Fritz Schlieper's July 8 report, the 453 killed or missing and 668 wounded reported for the fighting around Brest-Litovsk overall, and an inscription dated July 20 whose author remains unknown.

## C · The unidentified inscription

**Title:** Who Wrote the Famous Message at Brest Fortress?

**Thumbnail:** [C · WHO WROTE THIS?](../Thumbnails/v06/thumbnail_C_v06.png)

**Description:**

“I am dying, but I will not surrender! Farewell, Motherland.”

These words were discovered on a wall inside Brest Fortress after the war. The inscription was dated July 20, 1941, but the identity of the person who wrote it has never been reliably established.

To understand the circumstances around those words, the film returns to June 22, when German artillery opened fire on the fortress. Soviet soldiers fought from separated positions as supplies ran low and access to water became increasingly dangerous.

The main organized defense ended by late June, but isolated resistance continued into July. Major Pyotr Gavrilov was captured on July 23, though he cannot be identified as the last defender.

The film also examines a July 8 report by German commander Fritz Schlieper, who acknowledged the heavy cost of the assault. One account came from the attacking commander; the other was left by someone whose name remains unknown.
