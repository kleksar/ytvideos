The Soviet Union was developing an aircraft for a nuclear war with the United States. An enormous machine capable of carrying twelve cruise missiles with nuclear warheads and reaching speeds of more than two thousand kilometers per hour.

But the most unusual thing about this aircraft wasn't its size. Or even its speed.

It didn't have to fly over American cities to strike them. It was designed to carry its missiles to a launch area thousands of kilometers from their targets, and fire them without approaching the main air defense zones.

To do that, the Soviet Union needed an aircraft capable of covering enormous distances while carrying heavy weapons. And, when necessary, accelerating to more than twice the speed of sound.

The problem was that these requirements worked against each other.

A large aircraft needed enormous fuel reserves. But the more fuel and weapons it carried, the heavier it became. Accelerating that much weight to supersonic speed required exceptionally powerful engines. And those engines could burn through the fuel needed for a long-distance flight very quickly.

Soviet engineers had to combine the capabilities of a long-range missile carrier and a supersonic aircraft in a single machine.

The result was the Tu-160 — the heaviest combat aircraft in aviation history.

Its story began at the height of the Cold War.

In the 1970s, the Soviet Union and the United States possessed enormous nuclear arsenals. Intercontinental ballistic missiles, capable of delivering warheads across the ocean in a matter of tens of minutes, had become the backbone of their strategic confrontation.

These missiles were deployed in land-based silos and aboard submarines. But both superpowers continued developing strategic aircraft.

The reason was that a bomber offered something a ballistic missile, once launched, could not.

During a crisis, an aircraft could take off, head toward a designated patrol area, and then return to base if the order to use its weapons never came.

Once a ballistic missile had been launched, that option no longer existed.

But by the 1970s, American detection systems, fighter aircraft, and air defenses were becoming increasingly sophisticated. It was growing more dangerous for large bombers to approach heavily defended targets.

The Soviet Tu-95 could cover intercontinental distances, but it remained a subsonic aircraft. The faster Tu-22M was designed for different missions and could not replace a heavy strategic missile carrier with the required range.

Meanwhile, the United States was developing a new supersonic bomber, the B-1.

The Soviet Union needed its own next-generation aircraft. Not simply a fast bomber, but a carrier for long-range cruise missiles.

In the early 1970s, development of a new strategic aircraft began in the Soviet Union. Eventually, the Tupolev design bureau became the lead organization responsible for the project.

The engineers faced a challenge that couldn't be solved simply by making an existing bomber bigger.

They needed an entirely new design.

And the first major contradiction involved the wings.

For long flights at subsonic speeds, an aircraft benefits from large wings with a relatively low sweep angle. This helps provide the necessary aerodynamic characteristics and improves fuel efficiency.

But when an aircraft transitions to supersonic flight, the requirements change.

Air resistance increases sharply. At high speeds, wings with a greater sweep angle become more advantageous.

If the aircraft had wings optimized only for supersonic flight, it would face difficulties during takeoff, landing, and extended subsonic cruising.

But if it retained large wings designed for efficient cruising, reaching the required maximum speed would be difficult.

The designers chose a solution that allowed the aircraft to change its geometry in flight.

The Tu-160 received variable-sweep wings.

During takeoff, the movable sections of the wings are positioned forward, giving the aircraft a wingspan of almost fifty-six meters.

As the aircraft accelerates, the wings can gradually sweep backward. At their maximum sweep angle, the wingspan decreases to approximately thirty-six meters.

But on an aircraft of this size, moving wings presented an exceptionally difficult engineering challenge.

The Tu-160's maximum takeoff weight was intended to reach two hundred and seventy-five metric tons.

The enormous movable wing sections had to be connected to the aircraft's central structure in a way that could withstand substantial aerodynamic forces.

To achieve this, the engineers developed a massive central load-bearing structure incorporating titanium.

Manufacturing it required specialized techniques for welding and machining large metal components.

The variable-sweep mechanism allowed the aircraft to operate in different aerodynamic configurations. But it also increased the aircraft's weight and complexity.

Now the designers needed engines powerful enough to accelerate this enormous machine to its intended speed.

Four NK-32 engines were developed for the Tu-160.

With their afterburners engaged, each engine produced roughly twenty-five metric tons of thrust. Together, they provided approximately one hundred metric tons of maximum thrust.

The engines were installed in pairs beneath the central section of the wings, in separate engine nacelles.

This powerplant allowed the aircraft to reach speeds exceeding two thousand kilometers per hour at high altitude.

For a machine more than fifty-four meters long, with a maximum takeoff weight greater than that of the American B-1B, this was an exceptional achievement.

But supersonic flight came at a price.

With the afterburners operating, fuel was consumed at an extraordinary rate. If the aircraft attempted to fly its entire route at maximum speed, it would exhaust its available fuel supply far too quickly.

That was why subsonic flight remained the primary mode for long-distance operations.

Supersonic speed was an additional capability the crew could use when necessary, not a way to complete an entire intercontinental journey.

But even an aircraft this powerful was not yet a fully capable strategic missile carrier.

It needed weapons.

The Soviet Union was developing long-range cruise missiles capable of traveling thousands of kilometers under their own power after launch.

One of these was the Kh-55.

It had its own engine and navigation system, and could carry a nuclear warhead.

Unlike a conventional bomb, which an aircraft drops over or near its target, a cruise missile could continue flying along a predetermined route.

The Tu-160 was designed with two large internal weapons bays, positioned one behind the other along the fuselage.

Each bay could accommodate a rotary launcher carrying six Kh-55 missiles.

In total, a single aircraft could carry twelve of these missiles.

Keeping the weapons inside the fuselage offered an important advantage.

Missiles mounted externally would create additional aerodynamic drag and reduce the aircraft's flight performance. Internal weapons bays allowed the aircraft to preserve its aerodynamic shape. The rotary launchers enabled the missiles to be fired sequentially.

Operating such a machine required a crew of four.

The cockpit accommodated the commander, the co-pilot, and two navigators, one of whom was responsible for weapons employment.

Now the sheer scale of the aircraft began to make sense.

A single fuselage had to accommodate enormous fuel reserves, four powerful engines, a complex variable-sweep wing structure, equipment for long-distance flight, and two weapons bays carrying heavy missile armament.

And it was these missiles that defined the Tu-160's role in the confrontation with the United States.

The aircraft was not designed to destroy American military infrastructure single-handedly. Its purpose was to complement land-based and submarine-launched ballistic missiles by providing another means of delivering nuclear weapons.

However, the Tu-160 was not invulnerable. American fighter aircraft, radar installations, and air defense systems remained serious threats. The aircraft also depended on airfields, logistical support, and communications.

Nevertheless, the introduction of such a missile carrier expanded the capabilities of Soviet strategic forces. The United States now had to account for another type of nuclear weapons delivery system.

By the early 1980s, the main elements of the Tu-160's design had been brought together in a prototype aircraft.

On the ground, engineers could test the engines, wing mechanisms, control systems, and other components separately. But only flight could demonstrate how this entire complex structure would work as a single machine.

On December eighteenth, nineteen eighty-one, the prototype Tu-160 took to the air for the first time. Test pilot Boris Veremey was at the controls.

That flight opened a new phase of the program. Engineers now had to study the aircraft's behavior in the air, gradually testing its performance and onboard equipment.

Meanwhile, preparations for serial production were underway in Kazan.

Building the Tu-160 required mastering the manufacture of large and complex structural components, organizing the assembly of an enormous airframe, and coordinating the different factories responsible for producing the aircraft's individual systems.

In 1984, testing began on the first production Tu-160.

And in April 1987, the new missile carriers began entering service with an operational long-range aviation unit in Pryluky, in what was then the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic.

More than ten years had passed between the beginning of the aircraft's development and its arrival in military service.

The Tu-160 acquired an unofficial nickname: the White Swan.

Its enormous white fuselage, long pointed nose, four engines beneath the central wing section, and movable wings gave it an unusual silhouette. On the ground, it looked like a gigantic machine with its wings spread wide. During high-speed flight, the wings swept farther backward, transforming the aircraft's entire appearance.

NATO gave it the reporting name Blackjack.

But shortly after the Tu-160 entered operational service, the country it had been built for ceased to exist.

In December 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed.

Some of the missile carriers that had been built remained in independent Ukraine, while others were located in Russia. In the following years, some of the Ukrainian aircraft were destroyed as part of disarmament programs, while eight were transferred to Russia.

Production of the Tu-160 almost completely stopped.

But the surviving aircraft remained in service. Later, Russia began modernizing them, and eventually resumed the production of new aircraft. In January 2022, a newly built Tu-160M took to the air for the first time. This was not an older aircraft that had undergone modernization, but a newly manufactured missile carrier.

By that point, forty years had passed since the prototype Tu-160's first flight.

The Cold War had ended. The Soviet Union no longer existed.

But the aircraft built for a nuclear confrontation with the United States had outlived the country that created it.
