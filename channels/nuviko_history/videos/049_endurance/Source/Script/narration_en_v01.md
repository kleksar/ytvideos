# Endurance — English production narration v01

## HOOK

On November 21, 1915, a ship called Endurance sank in the Weddell Sea, near Antarctica.

All twenty-eight men of her expedition were still alive. But they had no ship, no radio, and no way to tell the outside world where they were.

They were stranded on drifting sea ice, with three small lifeboats and whatever supplies they had managed to save.

To get everyone home, six men would eventually have to cross more than eight hundred miles of open ocean in a boat barely seven metres long.

The other twenty-two would have to wait, without knowing whether those six had survived.

And even if the boat reached help, someone would still have to find a way back through the ice.

## INTRO

In 1914, Ernest Shackleton set out to lead the first expedition across Antarctica, travelling from one coast to the other by way of the South Pole.

His ship, Endurance, was supposed to carry the expedition into the Weddell Sea, where the crossing would begin.

But in January 1915, moving pack ice closed around the vessel.

For months, Captain Frank Worsley tracked their position while the crew maintained the ship and waited for a chance to escape.

The sea ice moved with the winds and currents. Instead of carrying them toward their destination, it took the trapped ship wherever it drifted.

Then, in October, the pressure became too great.

The wooden hull began to give way. Water entered, and on October 27, Shackleton ordered the men to abandon ship.

They carried three lifeboats, provisions and equipment onto the surrounding ice.

On November 21, Endurance sank.

The expedition had never reached its starting point on the Antarctic continent. Now the men had to find another way home.

## DEVELOPMENT

Shackleton first tried moving toward land on foot.

But hauling heavy lifeboats across broken ice proved almost impossible. Ridges blocked their path, cracks forced detours, and the men made little progress.

They abandoned the march and established a camp on a drifting floe.

For months, they waited as the current carried them north. They rationed their food, hunted seals and penguins, and kept the lifeboats ready.

In April 1916, the ice beneath them began breaking apart.

On April 9, they launched their boats into the water.

For nearly a week, the crews rowed and sailed between moving floes, struggling to reach Elephant Island.

On April 15, all twenty-eight men finally landed.

They had reached solid ground, but Elephant Island was uninhabited and far from normal shipping routes. No rescue ship knew they were there.

Shackleton decided that a small crew must leave to find help.

Their destination was South Georgia, roughly 1,300 kilometres — over 800 miles — to the northeast, across the Scotia Sea.

There were whaling stations on the island. If the men could reach one, they could obtain help and organise a rescue.

Their only suitable vessel was the largest lifeboat, the James Caird.

The carpenter, Harry McNish, raised its sides, fitted a partial covering of wood and canvas, and worked to make the hull more watertight.

Shackleton initially intended to leave Thomas Crean on Elephant Island to assist his second-in-command, Frank Wild.

But after discussing the plan, he decided to take Crean on the ocean crossing instead.

On April 24, Shackleton departed with Worsley, McNish, Crean, Timothy McCarthy and John Vincent.

Twenty-two men remained under Wild's command.

Wild's first challenge was finding shelter.

An ice cave offered protection from the wind, but it was wet and unsuitable for living in. The men abandoned it.

Instead, they built low stone walls and used two overturned lifeboats, canvas and other salvaged materials to form a roof.

The shelter was cramped, but it gave the men somewhere to live as winter approached.

While Wild organised life inside it, the James Caird was already far out at sea.

## CLIMAX

Finding South Georgia required more than sailing northeast.

It was a small island in an enormous ocean. If the James Caird passed too far to either side, the men might miss it entirely.

Worsley had to navigate with a sextant, a compass and calculations of their estimated progress.

He needed the sun to determine their position. But cloud cover, heavy seas and the constant movement of the boat made observations difficult.

During the crossing, he obtained only four usable observations of the sun.

Between them, he estimated their position from the course steered and distance travelled, adjusting whenever new evidence allowed.

The others kept the boat moving and afloat.

They worked in watches, handling the sails, steering and bailing water. Ice accumulated on the vessel, adding weight that had to be removed.

After nearly two weeks at sea, they were approaching where Worsley calculated South Georgia should be.

On May 8, land appeared through a break in the clouds.

They had found the island.

But heavy seas and dangerous surf prevented an immediate landing. For two more days, they remained offshore, searching for a safe approach.

On May 10, the six men finally landed in King Haakon Bay, on South Georgia's western coast.

The whaling stations were on the other side of the island.

With the James Caird and its crew in their exhausted condition, attempting to reach those stations by sailing around the coast was too dangerous.

Shackleton decided to cross the mountains instead.

McNish, Vincent and McCarthy remained behind. Shackleton, Worsley and Crean set out on foot on May 19.

There was no established route through the mountains and glaciers.

At one steep icy descent, Worsley and Crean held the rope while Shackleton used a carpenter's adze to cut steps into the ice.

Descending roughly five hundred feet took them about two hours.

Then the three continued across snowfields and ridges, searching for a passage toward the inhabited coast.

On May 20, after approximately thirty-six hours of travelling, they reached the whaling station at Stromness.

The three men left on South Georgia's western shore were subsequently brought to safety.

But no news of any of this had reached Elephant Island.

Wild and the other twenty-one men were still waiting.

Inside their shelter, they maintained a daily routine, gathered food when they could and endured the Antarctic winter without knowing whether the James Caird had reached its destination.

Wild kept the group organised.

Each day, he rolled up his own sleeping bag, repeating that Shackleton might arrive that day.

The others continued the work of maintaining the camp and finding food.

Days passed. Then weeks.

## RESOLUTION

Shackleton's first rescue attempt, aboard the whaling ship Southern Sky, was stopped by pack ice. The next attempt, using the Uruguayan vessel Instituto de Pesca No. 1, and a later voyage aboard the schooner Emma also failed to reach Elephant Island.

Three attempts had failed, and the men on the island still had no news.

As winter continued, food became increasingly scarce. By late August, the men were using what remained and gathering limpets and seaweed along the shore.

Shackleton secured a fourth opportunity.

The Chilean government provided Yelcho, a small steam tug commanded by naval officer Luis Pardo.

It was not an icebreaker. The ship could not force its way through a solid barrier of pack ice.

On August 25, Yelcho departed with Shackleton aboard.

As the pack ice shifted toward the northeast, Shackleton chose to approach Elephant Island from the northwest.

Pardo then had to bring the vessel safely through the waters near the island.

In fog, he navigated among numerous grounded icebergs, guiding the small tug toward the camp.

On August 30, Yelcho reached Elephant Island.

The men ashore were preparing food when a cry announced the approaching ship.

At first, the words “Ship O!” were mistaken for “Lunch O!”

Then the men saw the vessel.

After more than four months of isolation, a ship had reached their shore.

Pardo brought Yelcho close enough for the evacuation to begin.

All twenty-two men who had remained on Elephant Island were taken aboard.

Every one of them was alive.

## FINAL

The wider Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition had a different outcome. On the opposite side of Antarctica, the separate Ross Sea Party suffered its own ordeal, and three men died.

The planned crossing of Antarctica was never completed.

But the twenty-two men rescued from Elephant Island, together with the six who had sailed for help, accounted for every member of the Endurance party.

The expedition lost its ship. All twenty-eight men came home alive.
