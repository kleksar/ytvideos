In 1960, a radio telescope turned toward a distant star.

Within minutes, its receiver picked up a powerful, pulsing signal. The needles on the recording equipment jumped off the scale.

For a moment, a small group of scientists faced a question they had not expected to confront so quickly.

Had they heard someone?

The signal disappeared. The scientists needed a way to tell whether it had come from the direction of the star, or from somewhere closer to home.

The astronomer leading the experiment was Frank Drake. His project was called Ozma, and it was one of the first modern attempts to search for radio signals from another civilization.

Drake chose two stars that were relatively close to Earth and, in some ways, similar to the Sun. If another civilization existed around one of them, perhaps it used radio. And perhaps a signal could reach us.

His team pointed a telescope in West Virginia toward the stars and listened. They weren't waiting for an answer to a message they had sent. They were searching for a signal that might already be there.

Drake later remembered how startled the team was. They had prepared to search, but hadn't fully planned what to do if they actually heard something remarkable.

The team returned to the same frequency on later days. They also set up a second receiver and a small antenna to help check for interference. About ten days later, the pulses returned, and both receivers detected them.

It was radio interference from Earth. Drake suspected equipment on an aircraft, but its exact source was never established.

They had set out to listen for another world. Instead, they had heard their own.

That could sound like the end of the story. But it leaves a much bigger question.

If the signal came from Earth, what did the rest of the search tell us about the universe?

Ozma listened to two stars, over a limited range of frequencies, using the equipment available at the time. It was a beginning, not a survey of everything that might be out there.

Imagine standing in a vast city with a radio. You choose one station and listen for a while. All you hear is static.

Is the city empty?

Someone could be broadcasting on another frequency. The signal could be too weak. They might transmit at a different time. Or they might communicate in a way your radio can't receive.

And of course, the city really could be empty.

That's the difficult part. The limits of a search tell us why something might have been missed. They don't prove that something is waiting to be found.

Since Ozma, researchers have examined many more stars with much more capable instruments. And finding nothing in a search doesn't mean learning nothing.

If a search is sensitive enough to find a particular kind of powerful signal from a particular place, and finds none, that can put limits on how common such signals are in the places examined.

But the conclusion belongs to that search: its targets, its timing, and the kinds of signals it could detect. Other possibilities remain.

There's another reason the question is harder than it first sounds.

What do we mean when we ask, “Are we alone?”

Are we asking whether any life exists elsewhere? Whether another world has produced complex organisms? Or whether someone has built technology we could detect?

And even if we find evidence of another civilization, could we actually have a conversation?

Those are different questions.

Finding microbes on another world would change our understanding of life. But microbes wouldn't be waiting beside a radio, hoping someone calls.

And finding a signal wouldn't necessarily mean finding a neighbor we could speak to.

Imagine a civilization a thousand light-years away. This is a thought experiment; we don't know of one there.

If they sent a signal toward us, it would take a thousand years to arrive. If we replied immediately, our answer would take another thousand years to reach them.

Two thousand years between the first message leaving their world and our reply arriving back.

The people who sent it might still be there. Or they might not. A signal alone would not settle that question.

And there's another possibility. Perhaps the silence is difficult to explain because life itself is exceptionally rare.

We've discovered thousands of planets around other stars. That gives us many places to look. It doesn't tell us how often chemistry becomes life, or how often life develops technology.

For now, we have one confirmed example of a living world.

Ours.

Which brings us to a rather unusual experiment.

In 1990, a spacecraft called Galileo passed close to Earth on its way to Jupiter. Scientists led by Carl Sagan used the opportunity to ask a question that sounds almost ridiculous.

Could its instruments find signs of life on the planet it was flying past?

Of course, the researchers already knew Earth was inhabited. They were testing what clues the instruments could detect, and how those clues could be interpreted.

There was oxygen in the atmosphere, alongside methane in a combination that demanded an explanation. Light reflected from the surface contained a feature associated with vegetation. And there were radio transmissions with features suggesting technology.

Taken together, these clues pointed toward a living, technologically active world.

Galileo was close to Earth. Searching for faint signs around a distant star is a much harder problem. And no single clue should be treated as a universal guarantee of life.

Still, the experiment lets us turn the question around.

For most of this story, we've been looking outward, wondering whether anyone else has left a trace.

Now, for a moment, look back.

On that planet, in 1960, a small team aimed a telescope at the stars. They found a signal from Earth. And their search became part of a question we are still asking.

We still don't know whether life exists beyond Earth. No extraterrestrial life has been confirmed.

There may be other living worlds. Technology may be rare. There may be traces we haven't yet learned how to recognize.

We can't choose an answer just because we like it.

But we can keep looking. We can build better instruments, test what we find, and let the evidence change our minds.

So far, we know of only one place where life exists.

And somewhere on that planet, someone is still listening.
