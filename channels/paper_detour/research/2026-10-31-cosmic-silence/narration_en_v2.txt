The first possible answer came with a problem.

It was showing up on the wrong antenna.

In 1960, Frank Drake and his team pointed a radio telescope toward a star. They were searching for a signal that might have been made by another civilization.

And then the receiver started pulsing.

The needles jumped off the scale. For a moment, the question wasn't whether anyone would ever hear something. It was what on Earth they were supposed to do next.

The signal disappeared.

The team kept returning to the same frequency. They also set up a second receiver, connected to a small antenna that could pick up local interference.

About ten days later, the pulses came back.

And there they were on the little antenna, too.

The signal was radio interference from Earth. Drake suspected equipment aboard an aircraft, but its precise source was never established.

They had pointed at a star and found a piece of Earth.

It was an answer to one question: what had caused that particular excitement in the control room? But it left the much bigger question untouched.

If something from our own planet could appear while a telescope was pointed at a distant star, how would we recognize a signal from another world?

And what might be out there that a radio telescope can't hear at all?

Drake called his project Ozma. The team wasn't waiting for a reply to a message they had sent. They were listening for something that might already be on its way.

They had two stars to search, a limited range of frequencies, and an awkward problem: no one knew what an alien transmission would look like. Or whether there was a transmitter in the first place.

Imagine trying to find a radio station without knowing its frequency, when it broadcasts, or whether anyone has built it.

You listen to one patch of the dial. Nothing. You try another. Still nothing.

Each search tells you something about the signals it could have detected. But it can't tell you what's happening across the entire dial.

The station might be somewhere else. Or it might not exist at all.

Since Ozma, astronomers have searched many more stars with far more powerful instruments. When a search finds no signal of the kind it was designed to detect, that result can rule out some possibilities in the places it examined.

A different kind of signal might still escape that particular search. So might an entire living world.

Because a planet can be alive without broadcasting a single word.

Imagine finding a world covered in microbes. No towers. No telescopes. No one staring back at ours.

It would be one of the greatest discoveries in history. And it would leave one question unanswered: is there anyone we could talk to?

Now imagine that there is. A civilization one thousand light-years away.

Suppose they send a signal toward Earth. It travels for a thousand years before it reaches us. We send a reply immediately. That answer needs another thousand years to reach them.

Two thousand years between their message leaving home and our answer arriving.

What would be left of us after two thousand years? What would be left of them?

We don't know. The senders might still be there, or they might not. A signal alone wouldn't tell us.

And this is only a thought experiment. We haven't found that civilization. We don't know whether there is anyone to hear, nearby or far away.

We have found thousands of planets around other stars. That's thousands of real places to investigate. But a planet is not a promise.

We don't know how often lifeless chemistry becomes living matter. Or how often life develops into anything capable of building a radio.

Perhaps other living worlds are common. Perhaps life is extraordinarily rare. For now, our sample contains one confirmed living world.

Ours.

Which brings us to a different experiment, thirty years after Ozma began.

In 1990, a spacecraft called Galileo was on its way to Jupiter. Its route brought it close to Earth.

Scientists led by Carl Sagan used the opportunity to ask a peculiar question: if you examined our planet with instruments, what evidence of life would you find?

This wasn't a search for an unknown world. The researchers knew exactly where Galileo was and that Earth was inhabited. They wanted to test what the data could reveal.

The first clues came from the atmosphere. There was oxygen, along with methane in a combination that called for an explanation.

Then the surface: reflected light carried a feature associated with vegetation.

And finally, the radio. Transmissions with features suggesting technology.

The clues, taken together, pointed to a living planet with a technological civilization.

Here, though, is the difference: Galileo was close to Earth. Its observations didn't show that the same signs would be easy to detect from another star. And a single clue on another planet would need careful testing before anyone could call it life.

Still, Galileo gives us a way to see the question from the other side.

Picture Earth as an unfamiliar world. Its atmosphere, its light, its radio waves: all clues for someone trying to work out what is happening there.

Down on the surface, in 1960, a small team was doing the same kind of detective work in reverse. They pointed a telescope at the stars. When the first startling signal appeared, a little antenna helped them discover that it came from home.

In 1960, a second antenna helped rule out one possible answer. In the Galileo study, researchers looked for the clues a living Earth could leave behind. The two experiments asked different questions, but both had to turn a measurement into an explanation.

We still have no confirmed discovery of life beyond Earth. That doesn't mean the search has been pointless. It means we are still learning what to look for, and how to recognize what we find.

Maybe one day we'll discover a living world. Maybe we'll find a signal from a civilization that existed long before our own. Maybe the answer will be stranger than either of those.

For now, the only living world we know is the one doing the looking.

And somewhere on that planet, someone is still listening.
