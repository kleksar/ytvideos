HE COULD FIND IT. WHY COULDN’T HE COMPARE IT?

Two patients. A flash on the left or the right. Can they tell where it appeared?

In separate trials, both could answer by speaking, by pointing with the left hand, or by pointing with the right.

Now change the task. Put two shapes on opposite sides. Same or different?

For one of them, DDC, accuracy was about 90 percent when both shapes appeared on the same side. Across the two sides, it fell to about 55 percent—no better than chance in the researchers’ analysis.

He could report where a flash appeared. But comparing two shapes across the screen was suddenly difficult.

How can finding information work when comparing it does not?

The answer begins decades earlier, with a chicken, a shovel, and a patient known in research as P.S.

P.S. had undergone surgery to treat severe epilepsy. The operation cut the corpus callosum, a major bundle connecting the brain’s hemispheres. It disrupted a key route for sharing information, though it did not remove every possible pathway.

In a classic test, P.S. kept his gaze on a central point as researchers briefly showed a chicken claw to its right and snow to its left.

Remember: those are halves of the shared visual field, not one picture for each eye. Both eyes contribute to the central part of each side.

P.S. chose from a spread of pictures. His right hand selected a chicken. His left chose a shovel.

So far, each choice fits a picture. But when asked to explain the choices, his spoken answer linked the shovel to cleaning out a chicken shed.

The shovel fit the snow. The explanation fit the chicken.

One action, two different clues. Researchers interpreted the answer as the speaking system explaining a choice using the information available to it. The experiment revealed a mismatch in the information guiding the hand and the explanation. It did not reveal everything P.S. experienced.

Then came a more personal test.

Researchers presented the word “job” to P.S.’s right hemisphere. He spelled out “automobile race” with letter cards, mostly using his left hand. Shortly afterward, when asked about a future job, he answered aloud that he wanted to be a draftsman.

Different answers in different conditions. That may sound like two people sharing one head. But the tests did not prove two personalities or two permanent plans. They showed that what a person can answer depends on the information and the task.

For years, the famous split-brain demonstrations encouraged a simple picture: information shown on one side might be available to one hand, while speech could report what appeared on the other.

The two patients from 2017 complicated that rule.

They could report the location of a brief stimulus in either visual half, using different response methods in separate trials. That does not mean every piece of information had been combined into one complete picture. It means the task of locating a single stimulus allowed answers that a simple hand-and-side rule could not predict.

Then came the comparison test. Only DDC took part in that series. When the two shapes appeared within the same visual half, his comparisons were usually right. Put one shape on each side, and his accuracy dropped to chance.

Finding one thing and comparing two things are not the same mental job.

To report a location, the task asks whether a stimulus appeared and where. To compare shapes across visual halves, the task asks whether information from two locations can be related. In the experiment, those abilities did not rise and fall together.

That resolves the apparent contradiction in the opening. Reporting a stimulus from either side does not guarantee that two stimuli can be compared across sides. The researchers measured different abilities with different tasks.

What the results mean for conscious experience is a further question. The 2017 researchers argued that their findings supported one conscious agent. Other researchers challenged that interpretation. Neither the pointing test nor the comparison test is a direct counter of subjective experience.

Back to the screen: one shape on each side. DDC could often compare them when they were on the same side. Across sides, he performed at chance.

We can say what the experiments found: some answers remained available across the visual field, while comparing information across the two sides was impaired in one participant.

We can’t read a person’s entire inner experience from a hand, a spoken answer, or a score on one test.

One circle. One report: find.

Two shapes. A relation: compare.

Same screen. Different job.

The experiments can show where comparison broke down. They cannot tell us exactly what that experience felt like.
