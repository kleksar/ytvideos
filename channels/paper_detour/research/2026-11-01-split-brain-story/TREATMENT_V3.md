# Paper Detour — Version 3 treatment

## The Man Who Had to Ask His Own Hand

**Logline:** A split-brain patient tries to identify a number through a spoken guessing game. His left hand returns “up” or “down” clues, allowing his voice to reach the answer. What does that success show about information sharing—and what does it leave unanswered about consciousness?

**Working English title:** *The Man Who Had to Ask His Own Hand*  
**Alternative:** *Can Your Hand Help You Guess a Number?*  
**Estimated runtime:** about 5–6 minutes. Keep it short if the documented sequence cannot sustain the game; do not add made-up turns just to reach a target duration.

## Why this engine has more dramatic traction

Earlier versions presented an anomaly, then explained it. This version gives the viewer a simple objective and a visible process: guess the hidden number, read the hand’s clue, narrow the options, and see whether the spoken answer can reach the target. Each turn changes what the viewer thinks the patient can do. The study itself supplies a completed action and a result, rather than relying on a list of disconnected experiments.

The human question is concrete: can someone give a spoken answer with help from a response channel that carries information the speaking system does not simply name? It does not require invented emotions, dialogue, or a claim that two independent people live in one head.

## Story beats

**0:00–0:25 — The game.** Show a simple paper range of numbers and the cards `GUESS`, `GO UP`, `GO DOWN`, `CORRECT`. Invite the viewer to follow a clearly labeled demonstration of the rule. A number is flashed to one side of the visual field; the spoken guesses and hand signals make the game possible. Promise the result: the participant was able to say the right answer. Ask how the hand could help.

**0:25–1:05 — The constraint.** Introduce the participant as J.W., identified in the 2020 review of the study. He had undergone split-brain surgery for severe epilepsy. Keep anatomy to one sentence: the operation cut the major bridge between the hemispheres. The visual-field detail matters: a brief target in the left half of the field initially reaches the right hemisphere; speech normally relies mainly on the left. Say “initially” and “mainly.” Do not describe the hemispheres as two complete personalities.

**1:05–1:50 — Teach the rules.** Establish the “20 Questions” form in simple terms: one side provides a target, the speaking output makes guesses, and the left hand indicates whether the guess should go up or down. The feedback gives a direction, not a spoken name for the hidden digit. The audience understands how a very small clue can narrow a range. Show one short numerical example only as an explicitly illustrative demonstration—not as the patient’s recorded sequence.

**1:50–2:35 — The relay.** The core visual is one body, one voice, one hand, and the paper cards physically passing instructions across the frame. Avoid a second head, puppet show, or two characters. In the published experiment, the patient used the feedback arrangement to vocalize the correct answer. State only that documented outcome; do not invent the exact number, the individual guesses, timing, or a triumphant reaction.

**2:35–3:15 — First payoff, then the harder question.** Confirm that the game reached the right answer. The opening puzzle is solved early enough that the film does not hold a known result hostage. Now ask what this proves. The MacKays described explicit exchange of information and instructions. But a hand-guided correct answer is not a direct reading of private experience.

**3:15–4:05 — Two interpretations.** One interpretation is that distinct processing systems exchanged useful cues. A different interpretation is that the patient had access to the relevant information but needed another route to produce the spoken answer. The review itself notes such an alternative. Present both as interpretations; do not choose one as established fact.

**4:05–4:45 — A boundary, not a universal rule.** Briefly bring in the 2017 DDC study only to prevent overgeneralization. The tests showed that localization, response method, and comparison across visual fields can come apart; comparison figures were tested only with DDC. Use one clear contrast—about 90.4% within a visual field versus 55.4% across fields—and label it as a different experiment with a different participant. Do not make it a new subplot.

**4:45–5:30 — Resolve what the game means.** Return to the number cards. The experiment shows a route by which a person could use hand responses to help produce a correct spoken answer. It demonstrates a behavioral exchange under a particular task. It does not establish two conscious agents, nor does it settle that the entire subjective experience was unified.

**5:30–end — Final image.** The hand places the `CORRECT` card beside the number; then the number is covered. Keep the answer visible, while the interpretation remains carefully bounded. Final idea: the answer is measurable; the experience behind the answer is not read directly from this game.

## Testable opening lines

**A — Action-first, preferred:**

> “A number flashes. He guesses aloud. His left hand points: ‘Go up.’ He guesses again. ‘Go down.’ In a real split-brain experiment, those clues helped a patient’s voice reach the right answer. How?”

Use an on-screen note that the number of turns and any digits shown are an illustrative reconstruction of the game mechanic, not the participant’s historical trial record.

**B — Audience puzzle:**

> “Keep a number between zero and nine in mind. If I guess too low, give me one clue: ‘up.’ If I guess too high, say ‘down.’ A split-brain patient used a similar exchange—between his spoken guesses and his left hand. And he reached the right answer.”

Do not depict the audience interaction as a replication of the study. Keep the actual historical result distinct from the paper demonstration.

## Fact boundary and sources

- **Primary report:** D. M. MacKay & V. MacKay, “Explicit dialogue between left and right half-systems of split brains,” *Nature* 295, 690–691 (1982), [DOI 10.1038/295690a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/295690a0). The accessible abstract reports a split-brain patient showing considerable explicit interhemispheric exchange of information and instructions. The full short report is behind a subscription wall in this session; exact hidden digit, individual spoken guesses, and complete trial sequence are not verified here.
- **Accessible review giving the game details:** de Haan et al., *Neuropsychology Review* (2020), [DOI 10.1007/s11065-020-09439-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11065-020-09439-3), especially the discussion of MacKay & MacKay (1982). It describes the “20 Questions” number game: a digit was flashed in the left visual field; the speaking left hemisphere made oral guesses; the right hemisphere signaled “up” or “down” with the left hand; the patient was able to vocalize the correct answer. The review also notes an alternative reading: the patient may have had the information but difficulty vocalizing it without the game. Attribute this summary to the review unless the original report is obtained.
- **Boundary-check study:** Pinto et al. (2017), [DOI 10.1093/brain/aww358](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/aww358), and the 2020 review. Two other participants completed the localization tasks; cross-field comparison testing was conducted with DDC. Keep the participants and studies visibly separate.
- **Not facts:** the example number sequence, exact utterances in the game, expressions, emotions, room, order and length of individual attempts, and whether the experience belonged to “one” or “two” conscious subjects. Do not fabricate them.

## Retention argument and what would falsify it

The hook asks the viewer to solve a small rule-based game. The key goal is explicit early: can the patient reach the correct number? The feedback card changes the situation one move at a time. The answer arrives before the consciousness discussion, so the ending adds interpretation rather than withholding a known result. The DDC finding acts as one concise boundary check instead of another full experiment.

This is a stronger editorial hypothesis than the two earlier drafts because it centers a documented action, a clear objective, escalating feedback, and a completed result. It still does **not** prove the film will achieve above-average retention. The test is a controlled comparison of openings A and B, followed by the same cut: randomly assign viewers, compare 15-second and 30-second continuation and comprehension, and then validate with actual channel retention after publication. A concept test cannot predict YouTube performance by itself.

## Paper Detour treatment

Build one tactile game board from compatible foreground, middle, and background layers. The numeral, spoken guess cards, directional arrows, hand, and final answer must move relative to each other; letters emerge from behind cards or the hand with real occlusion. Use the house paper typography, foreground exits for every accent word, and soft moving film grain for the full runtime. Keep each primary shot at least three seconds and plan a visible, readable action in each shot. This is a story treatment, not yet the required phrase-by-phrase directing, camera, layer, motion, text, edit, sound, and asset score.
