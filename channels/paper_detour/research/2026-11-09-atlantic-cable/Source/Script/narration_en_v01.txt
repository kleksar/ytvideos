In August 1858, Queen Victoria sent a message to the president of the United States. It took about sixteen hours to get through. That was worth celebrating. Until then, news had crossed the Atlantic at the speed of a ship. This message had traveled through a wire lying on the ocean floor.

Within weeks, the wire went silent.

It still stretched from Ireland to Newfoundland. Crews had risked their ships getting it there. Now an operator could send a signal into the cable and no one on the other shore could read it. To make the connection work again, they'd have to solve two very different problems. One was how to lay a cable across an ocean. The other was how to make a whisper of electricity come out of it as a message.

Cyrus Field, an American businessman, had spent years persuading investors and governments to back the link. The idea was easy to picture. Stretch the telegraph wire that already carried messages across land all the way across the Atlantic. In practice, the wire needed a waterproof coat around the copper and a jacket of iron to protect it. The finished cable was too heavy for any one of the ships available to carry it.

So a British warship, the Agamemnon, took one half. An American ship, the Niagara, took the other. On the first expedition, in 1857, the cable snapped after hundreds of miles had already gone over the side. There wasn't enough left to start again.

The following year they tried a different maneuver. Meet in the middle of the ocean. Join the two ends. Then sail away from each other, with one ship headed for Ireland and the other for Newfoundland. The cable would sink behind them while the operators on the ships used it to stay in touch.

A storm battered the fleet. Then another break forced them to turn back. On the next voyage the two ships finally reached opposite shores. For the first time, a telegraph message could cross the Atlantic.

The operators could get a message through, but they had to work painfully slowly. A telegraph sends a message as a series of electrical pulses. If the pulses reach the other end distinctly, an operator can read them. Over the length of this cable they arrived weak and spread out. Send the pulses too close together and they start to run into each other.

Wildman Whitehouse, the company's chief electrician, drove the cable with high voltages to make its weak signals easier to detect. But pushing the cable harder put more stress on the very thing that kept it working underwater: the insulation around the copper. The signals deteriorated. The line that had carried Victoria's telegram stopped working within a month.

There was another way to read those signals. William Thomson, a Scottish physicist on the project, had built a receiver around a tiny mirror. A weak electric current could turn the mirror by a fraction. The mirror threw a spot of light across a scale, making the movement large enough for the operator to see. With a more sensitive receiver, a faint pulse could carry information without the sender having to force such a strong one into the wire.

The mirror addressed the problem of hearing the signal. The next expedition still needed a cable that could survive the sea.

Field secured backing for another attempt. This time the cable had better insulation and stronger protection. And by 1865 there was finally a ship that could take the whole length. The Great Eastern had been built to carry passengers. Its cavernous hull now held immense coils of cable.

The crew could watch the signal as they paid out the line. If they detected a fault, they could stop and haul part of the cable back up to fix it. That mattered when defects appeared along the way. The men found the damaged sections, repaired them and carried on.

Then, with Newfoundland still about six hundred miles away, they stopped for another repair. As they were bringing cable back up, it snapped. The free end dropped to the seabed. The crew lowered hooks to try to catch it. They couldn't get it back aboard, and the Great Eastern sailed home without finishing the line.

In July 1866, the Great Eastern set out from Ireland again with another improved cable. This time it reached Heart's Content, Newfoundland, with the line intact. Operators on either shore could pass messages back and forth over a connection that stayed in service.

Then the ship went back out. There was still a nearly complete cable lying on the bottom, the one they'd lost the year before. If they could lift the end, they could join a new length to it and finish the job.

The crew finally caught it and raised it to the deck. The test came in the ship's electrical room. With the old line hauled up from the seabed, they waited to see whether Ireland could still reach them. On Thomson's instrument, the spot of light moved. The cable was still alive. They joined it to fresh cable and brought that line ashore too.

Eight years earlier, one cable had lasted only a few weeks. Now the Atlantic had two working telegraph lines. A ship still took days to reach the other shore. Its news could already be there.
