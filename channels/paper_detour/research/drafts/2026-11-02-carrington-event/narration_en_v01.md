# Paper Detour 038 — narration EN v01

The telegraph operator disconnects the battery.

The machine keeps sending.

In Washington, in September 1859, a line carries messages on a current no one in the office has switched on. Outside, the night sky glows red. The wire is drawing its power from a storm that began on the Sun.

But the people at the telegraph desk do not know that yet.

The telegraph is still new. It turns electrical pulses into messages that travel through long wires between cities. A battery pushes the current. At the far end, an instrument clicks out the signal.

During the last days of August, strange auroras appear far from the polar regions. In the hours before dawn, red light spreads across skies where people have never seen the northern lights. Some mistake the glow for a distant fire.

Then, on the morning of September first, the British astronomer Richard Carrington is observing a large group of sunspots. He projects the Sun's image through a telescope onto a screen, protecting his eyes from direct sunlight. As he watches, two brilliant patches flare into view near the dark spots.

Another observer, Richard Hodgson, sees the same flash independently.

The flash lasts only minutes. The change it announces is already travelling toward Earth.

The Sun has sent out an immense cloud of charged material and magnetic force. Space between the planets is almost empty, but it is not nowhere: the cloud crosses it and reaches Earth in about seventeen hours.

Earth's magnetic shield bends and shifts under the impact. High above the ground, the disturbance drives powerful currents through the upper atmosphere. Those currents change the magnetic field around the planet.

And a changing magnetic field can push electricity through a long wire.

At the telegraph stations, the hidden storm arrives as a problem at the desk. Needles swing. Signals blur. Operators are shocked; in some offices, sparks jump from the equipment. Lines that normally carry a controlled current now have another current running through them.

The aurora spreads far south. It is bright enough in places to cast shadows. People step outside to watch the sky. In the same night, the wires beneath them are part of the storm.

At one Washington station, operators test a strange possibility. They disconnect the batteries. The telegraph still works. A newspaper later reports that the line carried messages for about two hours on the current in the wire itself.

For a moment, the network's own power source has become unnecessary. The sky is energizing the line.

Across other stations, the result is less useful. Instruments jam. Connections fail. Sparks and damage stop communication. The same invisible force that makes the aurora beautiful can make a working wire unreliable.

The storm passes. The aurora fades. Telegraphs return to ordinary batteries, and the stations return to ordinary messages.

But the night has changed the meaning of the wire. It is not only a path connecting one city to another. It is a long conductor reaching through a planet whose magnetic field can be disturbed by the Sun.

The people of 1859 could see the flash, the lights and the strange current. They could not yet see how all three belonged to one event.

Today we call it the Carrington Event. For a few hours, a storm from the Sun reached down into human machines—and a telegraph answered without its battery.
