## Finding

The strongest distinct carousel angle is the separate 10 July 1988 FM stereo broadcast for São Paulo’s Japanese community. One stereo channel carried the presenter’s Portuguese speech; the other carried the same text encoded as sound and translated into Japanese. A radio connected to a computer reportedly decoded it onto the screen. This is a self-contained miniature story with a clear question and payoff. It differs from the parent film’s MSX Avenger episode, its two published Shorts, and the exploratory `Packaging/Posts/carousel_radio_v01` draft about radio → cassette → computer.

Editorial hook for development: **Could one radio broadcast speak Portuguese while sending Japanese text to a computer at the same time?**

Four-beat arc:

1. Set the São Paulo broadcast on 10 July 1988 and the programme’s Japanese-community audience.
2. Reveal the split stereo channels: spoken Portuguese in one; sound-coded Japanese translation of the same text in the other.
3. Show the reported connection from radio to computer and the text appearing on screen.
4. Resolve the question: one FM broadcast was reported to serve a human listener and a computer-readable display in parallel.

The final card/post may invite a comment about what listeners would have preferred to receive by radio; the answer to the hook stays within the carousel.

## Source-check and limits

The primary contemporaneous source for this angle is [Superinteressante, “Computador FM,” 31 August 1988](https://super.abril.com.br/comportamento/computador-fm/). Its visible text explicitly names the 15:00 broadcast on 10 July, Rádio USP FM in São Paulo, Clip Informátka Especial, its dedication to the Japanese community, two FM stereo channels, Portuguese narration on one channel, and “o mesmo texto” encoded in sound and translated into Japanese on the other. It reports that a radio-to-computer connection displayed the decoded message.

The previous research summary was incomplete on one point: the article **does** directly call the Japanese channel a translation of the same text, so no caveat is needed about whether it was a translation. The article remains a contemporary journalistic report rather than a technical record. It supplies no broadcast audio, source/transcript pair, encoding specification, computer/interface details, or independent reproduction. Narration/cards can state what the article reports; they must not imply that this project reproduced or independently verified the decoding.

The same article separately reports that, from June, Fejes sent a computer version of Clip Informátka for people with hearing impairments. Keep this as a separate initiative: the source does not establish that the 10 July Japanese-community transmission was the same programme or used the same implementation. Do not describe the Japanese translation as the hearing-access version.

Do not attach this 10 July transmission to the film’s distinct recording identified as episode 102/1987 or to the reported MSX Avenger data match. Do not claim that the radio pioneer was first in the world, or name an unverified computer model.

## Supporting Short candidate: computer text for a hearing-access initiative

A separate, viable micro-story is the attempt to make a radio programme’s content available as computer text to people with hearing impairments. It has a complete payoff: the 2001 Folha retrospective describes text appearing on a computer screen, like subtitles. This angle differs from the two released Shorts (software sent as radio sound; the reported Avenger data match) and the proposed carousel (Portuguese speech alongside a Japanese translation on stereo).

Editorial promise for development: **How could a radio programme reach people who couldn’t hear it?**

Cause-and-effect outline: ordinary radio delivered the programme by voice; Alexandre Fejes Neto and the Rádio USP project made a computer version of Clip Informátka; a later first-person retrospective describes the programme text appearing on a computer screen like subtitles; resolve with that concrete text display as the way information was presented.

The 1988 Superinteressante article reports that, from June, Fejes sent a computer version of Clip Informátka for people with hearing impairments in São Paulo. Magaly Prado’s [Folha Online retrospective, 10 November 2001](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/folha/pensata/ult514u40.shtml) recalls a late-1980s Rádio USP programme for deaf people that showed text through a computer, like subtitles. Folha is retrospective testimony, not technical documentation. These sources support describing an initiative and a reported text-display method; they do not establish universal access, audience reach, measured success, exact technical architecture, or that the June version and the later-described implementation were identical. Keep this initiative distinct from the 10 July Japanese-community stereo broadcast.

Do not use the source’s audience estimate as evidence of actual users or reach. Do not claim every deaf or hard-of-hearing person could receive the service, that the service succeeded at scale, or that the project reproduced the system.
