# The Radio Broadcast That Carried a Computer Game

Rarovista · Episode 001 · English original · narration draft

Approximate duration: 5–7 minutes at a conversational pace, plus pauses. Timing is an estimate until a voice recording exists. Section headings are production markers, not spoken. Visual directions are in `001_visual_plan.md` and are not part of the narration.

## 1. The sound

Imagine turning on the radio and hearing a stream of electronic squeals.

Not a song. Not a voice. Just a stream of electronic squeals.

Today, that might sound like a broken connection. But in the nineteen-eighties, a broadcast like this could carry something very different: a computer program.

One Brazilian radio show sent software over the air. Decades later, a developer took an old recording of that show and tried to read the strange signal again. The result, the developer reported, matched data from a game.

So how can a radio station broadcast a game without showing a single image?

The answer starts with something you could hold in your hand: an ordinary cassette tape.

## 2. The computer that listened

Before downloads became a familiar part of everyday life, many home computers loaded software from tapes. The tape stored changing patterns along a strip of magnetic material.

Play a song, and those patterns become music. Play a computer tape, and they become a sequence of tones. To human ears, those tones might sound like noise. To a compatible computer, they can represent instructions and data.

Here's the trick. A computer works with binary values: zeros and ones. A recording system can turn those values into sound patterns. A compatible computer works backwards, interpreting the patterns as bits and assembling the data again.

Think of it like a message written in whistles. You don't need to understand the whistles yourself. You need a machine that knows the code.

Different computers and formats used different ways to do this. A random recording was not automatically a program for every machine. The sounds had to match what that particular computer expected.

## 3. From tape to air

Now picture the route that signal could take.

A tape player can play the tones through a cable. A radio station can broadcast sounds. And a listener can record sounds from the radio onto another tape.

Put those steps together, and a program can travel through the air as audio. A listener with suitable equipment could record the broadcast, then play the resulting tape to a compatible computer.

The radio isn't somehow displaying a game. It carries the sound. The computer does the work of reading it.

And this wasn't an invisible miracle. Reception, recording quality, volume, speed and compatibility mattered. If the signal was badly distorted, or the wrong machine tried to read it, the program might fail to load.

## 4. The Brazilian broadcast

One example comes from São Paulo. Rádio USP carried a technology programme called Clip Informática. An archived recording identified as episode one hundred and two is catalogued as being from nineteen eighty-seven.

According to the archive, the end of this recording contains software audio intended for the MSX family of computers. The archive credits Edson Kadoya with digitising it.

That doesn't tell us exactly who was listening on the day or what happened in their homes. The recording's catalogue gives a year, not an exact broadcast date. But the premise is clear: a radio programme could carry data that a computer was meant to read.

To someone hearing the broadcast without that context, the sound might have seemed like a strange interruption. For a machine prepared to interpret it, that same sound could carry useful information.

## 5. A second life for the signal

Years later, the story acquired another layer. A developer named Weber Kai set out to decode data from an online recording that Kai identified as episode one hundred and two. We haven't independently established whether that video and the archive's copy are the same recording.

Kai wrote that the available audio was quiet and running fast. The relevant section was extracted and read with a software tool, using settings adjusted for that recording. Kai's instructions describe six output files, each corresponding to a block of tape data.

Then came the comparison. Kai reported that the recovered data matched an MSX game called Avenger.

There are limits to what that proves. The public notes describe a match, but don't provide a complete comparison record or demonstrate the game running. We haven't independently repeated the decoding.

What we can say is still remarkable: a recording identified with an old radio broadcast contained a signal that a later investigator reported matching to game data.

## 6. More than games

The same broad idea could carry more than software. A contemporary magazine described a separate Rádio USP broadcast in nineteen eighty-eight: one stereo channel carried Portuguese speech, while the other carried Japanese text encoded as sound. A connected computer could display the text.

That was a different broadcast, so we shouldn't assume the game recording used the same stereo arrangement. It reveals the larger idea: a machine could transform the sounds from a radio into information a person could use.

## 7. The answer

So how do you hide a computer game in a radio broadcast?

You don't hide a picture inside a song. You turn data into an audio pattern, transmit the pattern, and let a compatible computer translate it back.

What sounds like an annoying interruption to one listener can be a message to a machine.

And in one old Brazilian recording, a signal that survived for decades may still carry the trace of a game — waiting for someone who knows how to listen.
