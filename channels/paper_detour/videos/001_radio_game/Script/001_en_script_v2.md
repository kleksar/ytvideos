# How a Radio Broadcast Carried a Computer Game

Rarovista · Episode 001 · English narration draft v2

Approximate target: 5–7 minutes. Section headings are not spoken. The opening sound effect must be an original, illustrative reconstruction, visibly labeled as such. It must not be represented as the actual archive recording.

## 1. A sound worth recording

Imagine hearing a computer game on the radio.

Not the music from the game. The game itself.

To your ears, it might sound like a string of electronic squeals. But to the right computer, those sounds could be instructions.

In the nineteen-eighties, a Brazilian radio show sent computer programs through the air. Years later, someone found an old recording of that show and asked: could the signal still be read?

What they reported finding takes us back to a time when the download button was a cassette recorder.

## 2. The signal

The show was called Clip Informática. It aired on Rádio USP in São Paulo and covered the world of computing.

An archive preserves a recording identified as episode one hundred and two. The archive dates its copy to nineteen eighty-seven and says that near the end is software audio for the MSX family of home computers.

Now, when we say software audio, we don't mean someone reading out a program. We mean the program was represented by sound.

Which raises an obvious question: how do you turn a whistle into a game?

To answer that, we need to look inside a cassette tape.

## 3. What the tape knew

A cassette stores changing patterns along a strip of magnetic tape. Those patterns can be played back as sound. Usually that sound is a song or a voice. But a tape can also hold a sequence of tones that a computer can interpret.

Computers work with bits: zeros and ones. A cassette system can represent those values using patterns in a sound signal. A compatible computer listens to the patterns, turns them back into bits, and assembles them as data.

Imagine a message written in whistles. You might hear only a squeal. A machine that knows the code can read the message.

Different machines used different formats, so a recording wasn't automatically readable by every computer. The signal had to be in a form the receiving machine understood.

## 4. A cassette without a shop

Now follow the signal.

A radio station can broadcast sound. A listener with suitable equipment can record that sound onto a cassette. And a compatible computer can read the cassette.

Link those steps together, and software can travel through the air — not as a picture of a game, but as the audio pattern a computer needs to read.

The radio carries the sound. The computer translates it.

And just as a cassette could sound fuzzy or play at the wrong speed, a signal could be distorted along the way. If the computer couldn't reliably tell the patterns apart, loading could fail.

For a listener who wasn't expecting it, a burst of tones might have seemed like an interruption. For someone equipped to record it, it could carry software.

## 5. Listening again

Fast-forward to a much later listener: developer Weber Kai.

Kai set out to decode an online recording identified as episode one hundred and two of Clip Informática. We haven't independently established whether this video and the archive's nineteen eighty-seven copy are identical.

The available recording, Kai wrote, was quiet and running fast. That matters: when the speed of a recording changes, its tones change too. The sound may still be there, but a decoder needs settings that fit the recording it receives.

Kai extracted the relevant section and used a software tool to interpret the signal. The published instructions describe six output files, each corresponding to a block of tape data.

Six blocks — but blocks of what?

## 6. The comparison

Kai compared the recovered data with an MSX game called Avenger. The developer reported that the data matched.

That's a striking result, with a boundary. The public notes don't include a complete comparison record or demonstrate the game running. We haven't independently repeated the decoding. So this is Kai's reported match, not a launch we've witnessed.

The archive, meanwhile, identifies its own copy as software for MSX; it doesn't name the game.

Still, think about the journey. A program could become sound, leave a radio transmitter, be captured as a recording, and later be examined by someone trying to turn that sound back into data.

The message survived in a form that, to most ears, didn't sound like a message at all.

## 7. What the noise was saying

The trick wasn't a secret picture hidden inside music. It was a way of representing data as sound.

A cassette could carry that sound. A radio station could broadcast it. And a compatible computer could try to read it.

What sounds like noise to us can be a message to a machine. And in an old recording of a Brazilian radio show, that message may have survived long after the broadcast ended — waiting for someone who knew how to listen.
