# Could This Radio Signal Contain a Computer Game?

## 1. The clue

This is what a computer program might sound like on the radio.

Not a song about a game. Not an advertisement. A pattern of sound that a computer could read.

In the nineteen eighties, a Brazilian radio show broadcast software through the air. Years later, a developer tried to read an online recording identified as one of its episodes.

His instructions describe six files. And he reported that the data matched a computer game.

So what was hiding in the noise? And how can a radio signal carry software in the first place?

## 2. The two recordings

The show was called Clip Informática. It aired on Rádio USP in São Paulo and explored the world of computing.

One archive, called Datassette, holds a recording it identifies as episode one hundred and two. It dates its copy to nineteen eighty-seven and describes software audio for the MSX family of computers near the end.

The later investigation used an online recording that developer Weber Kai also identified as episode one hundred and two. We haven't independently established whether that recording and the archive's copy are identical.

Two recordings, one question: how could a stretch of sound contain something a computer might use?

## 3. A message written in whistles

To understand the trick, forget the radio for a moment. Look at the cassette.

Long before most people downloaded software online, many home computers could load programs from magnetic tape. A cassette player would turn patterns on the tape into an audio signal, and the computer would interpret that signal as data.

Computers use bits: zeros and ones. A recording system can represent those values with patterns in sound. A compatible machine works in the other direction, translating the patterns back into data.

Think of it as a message written in whistles. You don't need to understand the whistles if the machine knows the code.

But there is a catch. Different machines and formats used different ways of representing data. The signal had to be something the receiving computer was designed to understand.

## 4. A program takes the air

Now put the radio back into the picture.

A station can broadcast audio. A listener with suitable equipment can record that audio onto a cassette. A compatible computer can then try to read the cassette.

The station isn't broadcasting a picture of the game. The sound is carrying patterns that can represent the program.

Imagine that journey as one long chain: a signal leaves the station, travels through the air, reaches a radio, is captured on tape, and is played to a computer.

Every link matters. Distortion, recording level, playback speed, and compatibility can all affect whether the signal is readable. The sound might still be audible to a person while being difficult for a decoder to interpret.

And that brings us back to the old recording.

## 5. Listening again

Weber Kai set out to decode data from the online recording he identified as Clip Informática episode one hundred and two.

He wrote that the available audio was quiet and running fast. That's not just a matter of the sound being unpleasant. Change the speed of a recording and you change the timing of its patterns too.

Kai's published instructions describe extracting the relevant section and using a software tool with settings for that recording. They say the process should produce six files, each corresponding to a block of tape data.

Six files. But files of what?

## 6. The comparison

Kai wrote that he compared the results with an MSX game called Avenger, and that the data matched.

That is the reported discovery. It is also where we need to be precise.

The public notes don't provide a complete comparison record or demonstrate the game running. We haven't independently repeated the decoding. And the Datassette archive describes its own copy as MSX software; it doesn't name Avenger.

So we can tell you what Kai reported. We can't show you a verified launch or claim that the two preserved copies have been proven identical.

## 7. The sound and the message

Even with those limits, the idea is remarkable.

A program can be represented by sound. That sound can travel through a radio broadcast and survive in a recording. Years later, someone can try to read its patterns again.

To our ears, the result may be a squeal. To a compatible machine, it can be a message.

Sometimes the most interesting thing on the radio wasn't meant for human ears at all.
