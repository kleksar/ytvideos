## Narration

Kiran Maya Sheikh graduated with a computer science degree in 2025. She wanted stability, financial independence, and a first full-time job as a software engineer. Finding it, she later explained, was harder than she expected.

Kiran described a frustrating paradox: some internships and entry-level jobs asked for experience before offering a chance to gain it. She kept building her skills, volunteered on a project, and looked for a professional community.

She believed AI was one factor making an already difficult market harder for beginners. Her experience alone cannot tell us how much AI changed her chances, or whether it caused any particular rejection.

But AI can now help with some of the tasks beginners once learned on. Not every task. Not every job. Still, it raises a frightening question: what happens if the first rung of the career ladder starts to disappear?

A warning sign appeared in an unexpected place: a company building the technology itself. In October 2026, Anthropic reported that the share of junior roles among its technical hires had fallen from 27 percent to 9 percent since early 2025.

That doesn't mean nine out of ten junior jobs vanished. It doesn't tell us how many people were fired. Anthropic was growing, and one company is not the whole economy. But the change raises a question bigger than one office: if fewer beginners get the chance to practice, where will tomorrow's experts come from?

For generations, a career often worked like an apprenticeship. First you did the smaller tasks. You made mistakes someone else could catch. Over time, those tasks taught you how the whole job worked.

Now imagine an experienced worker with an AI assistant. The assistant drafts a document, searches for information, or produces a first attempt at code. The human checks it and makes the final decisions. If that arrangement lets a team take on more work, it could create opportunities. But if a company needs fewer people for those early tasks, it may also hire fewer beginners.

And that creates a paradox. Businesses still want experienced people. But experience takes somewhere to begin. If enough companies stop providing those first opportunities, the shortage may not appear immediately. It could appear years later, when the people who should have learned those skills were never given the chance.

Researchers have also started testing what AI assistance might mean for expertise itself. In a three-month experiment involving 133 patent lawyers, those given an AI assistant produced better drafts on average. Later, on a separate task they were instructed to complete without AI, experienced lawyers who had previously had access scored higher than their peers in the control group. Among junior lawyers, researchers found no clear average difference.

This was a working paper, not a verdict on every profession: the study was small, focused on one occupation, and only 91 participants completed the final task. Some findings became less certain in a sensitivity check. It does not prove that AI stops beginners from learning or makes them lose skills.

The career-ladder scenario is a possible chain of events, not a proven forecast. And the evidence so far is more complicated than the loudest headlines suggest. In a study published in March 2026, Anthropic researchers found no systematic increase in unemployment among workers in occupations more exposed to AI. They did find a tentative sign that hiring of younger workers had slowed in some exposed occupations. The researchers could not establish from that signal alone that AI caused it.

The International Labour Organization estimates that roughly one in four jobs worldwide has some exposure to generative AI. That means the technology could affect tasks within those jobs. It does not mean one in four workers will be fired.

But a change in the work doesn't have to erase a profession to disrupt someone's life. Machine translation and post-editing existed long before today's generative AI boom.

Take Julian Pintat, a freelance technical translator. In a personal account written in 2025, he described going entire months without work. By May, he said, most requests were for reviewing text produced by machine translation, work he regarded as much less well paid than translating from scratch.

His story didn't end there. By that autumn, he wrote that clients and conventional translation assignments had returned, although his business had not yet recovered to its former level. This is one person's account, not proof of what happened across an entire industry or why each client made its decisions. But it shows how uncertain and uneven a transition can feel to the person living through it.

Because a job is more than a list of tasks. It is an income, a way to build skills, and often a way to feel useful. Change enough of the tasks, quickly enough, and you may change the whole bargain between people and employers.

One possible future is a good one. Workers use AI to do more, businesses expand, new roles emerge, and some of the gains return to the people doing the work. But another future is possible too. The technology becomes more productive while the people whose work has changed struggle to share in the benefits. A new job may exist, but in another city, requiring experience or training the person losing work does not have.

And if many households earn less while the gains flow to a small group, there is another question. Who buys the goods and services all this new productivity creates? That isn't a prediction of collapse. It is a reason to ask how the benefits and costs of a transition are distributed.

The effects could reach beyond a paycheck. If entry-level work becomes harder to find, some people may postpone moving out, starting a family, or retraining. Others might gain time, better tools, or access to work they could never have done before. The same technology can open a door for one person and narrow it for another.

And AI may keep improving. We do not know how quickly, which tasks will become reliable, or how companies and governments will respond. A machine that can produce an impressive answer is not automatically ready to take responsibility for the consequences. Who checks it, who owns it, and who benefits from it are human decisions.

That is why the most important question may not be whether AI can do more work. The question is what kind of world we build around that ability.

Will companies invest in training people alongside their machines? Will workers have time and support to move into new roles? Will the gains make life more secure, or only make the race to keep up more exhausting?

There is another part to Kiran's story. In October 2026, Business Insider reported that she had landed her first software engineering job. Her experience is one person's story, not a measure of the whole market. It doesn't prove why her search was difficult, and it doesn't settle what happens next.

But it brings us back to the question she faced at the beginning: how does someone get the experience a career demands if the opportunities to gain it become harder to find?

The career ladder is not a law of nature. People built it, and people can rebuild it. But first we have to notice what happens if its lowest rung starts to disappear.
