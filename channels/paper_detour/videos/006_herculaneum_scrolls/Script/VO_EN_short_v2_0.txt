In 2023, a college student was at a birthday party when his phone gave him a chance to look inside a book no one had opened in centuries.

The book was a blackened scroll from Herculaneum, buried by Vesuvius in the year 79. It was so fragile that opening it could destroy the very words people wanted to read.

The student started a computer program remotely. Later, after driving friends home, he checked the result near a parking garage. On his screen were shapes that looked like Greek letters.

But the breakthrough began long before that night. And the scroll on his screen was not the only one with a story to tell.

Nearly two thousand years earlier, a villa near Herculaneum held a library of papyrus rolls. When Vesuvius erupted, the heat carbonized the scrolls. The disaster destroyed a town, but left these fragile objects behind.

Excavators found them in the eighteenth century. They had recovered a library, but reading it came at a cost. Some scrolls were cut open; writing was copied as layers were exposed, and material was scraped away to reach what lay underneath.

Other methods were more controlled. Antonio Piaggio developed a machine that slowly lifted delicate layers. Scholars recovered ancient texts, but many rolls remained dangerously difficult to open.

Over the following centuries, one of them, catalogued as Papyrus sixteen sixty-seven, was subjected to several attempts. Pieces were removed and preserved separately. What remained was a small, tightly rolled inner core. The original book was no longer whole.

That left a question: could you read what was still inside, without pulling the surviving layers apart?

Computer scientist Brent Seales and his collaborators pursued another approach. X-rays could produce a three-dimensional scan of a scroll. But the scan alone was a tangled maze: the papyrus was crushed, folded, and packed into layers that almost touched.

Researchers had to trace a single writing surface through that maze and flatten its shape on a computer. The physical scroll could stay where it was. Its digital surface could be unrolled.

Then came another problem. Much of the ink was carbon-based, and so was the charred papyrus beneath it. The difference was incredibly subtle. A beautiful image was not enough; researchers needed evidence that the marks were really ink.

In 2023, the Vesuvius Challenge opened the problem to a wider community. In scans of a scroll called Paris Four, Casey Handmer noticed a cracked pattern that looked like possible ink. That clue helped Luke Farritor train a model to search for more.

Farritor was the student at the birthday party. The program he started on his phone was running on a computer elsewhere. The letters appeared only later, after the drive home. Over further work, specialists read a sequence as a Greek word associated with purple.

Others pushed the discovery further. Youssef Nader used his own approach on the same region, and Julian Schilliger worked on tools for mapping more of the scroll's surface. Together with Farritor, they entered the challenge's grand prize.

In February 2024, the three won. Experts had examined the submissions, and the technical team had checked the methods. The result revealed parts of several columns of writing inside Paris Four in digital images. Most of that scroll still remained unread.

The text discussed pleasure: did something have to be rare to be enjoyable? After so many centuries, a damaged scroll was revealing part of an ancient argument about ordinary life. But the author's name and the title remained unknown.

Now return to the small core we met earlier. Papyrus sixteen sixty-seven was a different scroll. Paris Four had helped demonstrate the method; it was not the same book, and its words could not be transferred to this one.

In 2026, researchers used new high-resolution X-ray scans to trace and virtually unroll the surviving core of sixteen sixty-seven. The work combined painstaking mapping, computer models, and specialists who examined the Greek writing. A machine did not simply translate a complete book.

The researchers reported a philosophical text, probably from a Stoic context. Its author and title are still unknown. And there is a crucial limit: the digital reconstruction covers what survived in that core, not the whole original book. Some damaged areas and uncertain letters remain unread.

The team's research was released as a preprint in 2026; publication in a peer-reviewed journal had not been confirmed at the time of our research. Its methods and readings remain open to scrutiny.

Look again at that small black core. The missing pieces have not returned. But without physically opening the remaining roll again, researchers have found a way to study words that were hidden inside it.

A book damaged by a volcano, and then by centuries of attempts to read it, can now be examined in a different way. Not every lost word will come back. But the fragile pages that remain no longer have to stay silent.
