## Narration

Imagine a quiet evening. The curtains are closed, the lights are low, and you've picked a movie on DVD.

You put the disc into the player beside your TV, settle onto the couch and press play. No TV app. Just a movie on a disc.

So why did a DVD player come up in a case about a television collecting viewing data?

The case was about Vizio. According to the U.S. regulator, the company had remotely installed tracking software on some TVs that were already in people's homes.

The setting was called Smart Interactivity. It talked about offers and recommendations. But the regulator said it didn't clearly tell people that their viewing data was being collected.

Back on the couch, the movie is playing. The disc is still in the DVD player. But the picture is on the TV's screen.

And that's where the system looked.

The TV collected information about selected pixels on its screen and sent it to Vizio's servers. There, it was compared with a library of shows, movies and ads to identify what was playing.

No camera had to watch the room. Even a movie from an external DVD player could be recognized from the picture on the screen.

That viewing history could travel further. According to the regulator, Vizio shared viewing histories and IP addresses with other companies. They could use the data to measure audiences and target ads on other devices. Their agreements prohibited re-identifying consumers and households by name.

In 2017, Vizio agreed to pay two point two million dollars to settle the case. The agreement also required clearer disclosures and consent.

If you have a smart TV, you can look in its privacy settings for viewing data, content recognition or personalized ads. Options vary, and switching off one setting doesn't guarantee that all data collection stops.

Now you're back on the couch, the remote in your hand. The disc never left the player.

The TV could recognize the movie on its screen, not the disc itself.
