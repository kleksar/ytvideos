We found their tools. Where are their clothes?

Picture a riverbank. More than a million years ago, someone picks up a stone and chips away at it until one edge becomes sharp. Water, wind and time will wear away almost everything around it. But that stone will survive.

Thousands of generations pass. New people come to the river, bringing new tools with them: bone needles, shaped by careful human hands. Stone and bone outlast the people who made them. Almost all their clothing is gone.

But not quite everything.

Here’s a tiny sandal, woven from esparto grass. A living person once wore it. Imagine them pulling the straps tight, stepping outside, and heading off to whatever the day had in store. Thousands of years later, the sandal is still here.

Suddenly, the past has more than tools. It has feet.

Follow its owner's footsteps, and we reach the other bank: Roman Britain. Tunics, cloaks, belts, shoes. Now we're not looking at isolated traces. We're looking at people, dressed and ready to go about their lives.

One look gives way to another, and the river carries us on.

By around 1400, the silhouettes have changed again. Long garments, belts, head coverings. Straight lines give way to folds and layers. Even from a distance, you can see how different a passerby looks.

Now it's Amsterdam, 1559. A family portrait. Look closely: these people belong to the same family, but each has a style of their own. Different hats, fabrics, little details. One painting, and a whole family of strangers looking straight at us across the centuries.

Which would you choose?

The river carries us into the eighteenth century. A French dress spreads wide. Beside it, a British court outfit: fine decoration, a waistcoat, a long coat. You wouldn't exactly blend into the background wearing either of these. Walk into a room, and your outfit arrives before you do.

But wide is not the limit.

In the nineteenth century, the crinoline appears beneath the skirt. Suddenly, a person is surrounded by a whole dome of fabric. Imagine trying to get through a doorway, or sitting next to someone in a carriage.

Now let's jump ahead. The 1920s. In some dresses, that enormous dome gives way to a completely different shape: long, straight and loose. Like a broad wave on the river suddenly smoothing out.

One more step, and we're on a modern street. Jeans and dresses, sneakers and tailored suits, all side by side. We wear these things every day, hardly thinking that one day they'll be history too.

Picture a museum in the future. Behind the glass: our sneakers, a jacket, someone's favorite T-shirt. Visitors study them as closely as we study that ancient sandal. Maybe they wonder who wore them, and where they were going.

The river keeps flowing. Things survive, disappear, and surprise people centuries later.

We found their tools. What will they find after us?
