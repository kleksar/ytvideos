We found their tools. Where are their clothes?

Imagine a riverbank. The water moves on, while we look back more than a million years. Early Homo lived in Western Europe. Their stone tools survived. A stone has shape, weight, a sharp edge. It seems that with only a little more, we could picture the person who held it.

But what were they wearing? What did they wear? Did they wear anything at all?

The stone remains. Clothing seems to have disappeared from history. Somewhere, surely, there must be a trace.

Time moves forward. On another bank, we find Magdalenian tools. They tell us about the work of human hands, about what people did. But we still cannot see what they wore. The further we travel, the more we want one thing: not a guess, not an invented silhouette, but something real.

Then, at last, a small sandal made of esparto grass.

It survived. For the first time, we can look not at a guess, but at something a living person once wore. Imagine them putting it on and setting out for the day, just as we do. Suddenly, the distance between us feels smaller.

If one small object can bring a person closer, what else can clothing tell us?

Another crossing: Roman Britain. Clothing now becomes part of the human figure. One step further, to silhouettes from around 1400. The shape changes: the shoulder line, the folds, the outline of the body. We see how clothing changes a figure.

In Amsterdam, in 1559, a merchant family remains in a portrait. We look beyond their faces. Their clothes speak too, through colour, shape and detail. Which would you choose?

We are not seeing everyone who lived in that age. We are seeing one particular family in one particular portrait: several figures together, with silhouettes and details we can still examine centuries later.

Now the eighteenth century. A French dress from the 1750s to the 1770s. Beside it, British court dress from around 1750. Two outfits, two different impressions.

Then a mid-nineteenth-century crinoline. Its volume seems to push the space around its wearer outward.

Next, the 1920s. Some dresses have a much straighter silhouette, as if a great wave has passed and the water has gone smooth.

In the 2020s, different shapes share the same street. To us, it's ordinary. To someone from the past, it might be a mystery.

The river keeps moving. One day, someone will study us: our belongings, the traces we leave behind. Will they find our clothes, or only what survives when the fabric is gone?

We found their tools. What will they find after us?
