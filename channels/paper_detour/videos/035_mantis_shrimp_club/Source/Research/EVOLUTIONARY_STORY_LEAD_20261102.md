## Primary-source basis reviewed

1. Haug et al. (2010), [“Evolution of mantis shrimps (Stomatopoda, Malacostraca) in the light of new Mesozoic fossils”](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2148-10-290). Their fossil morphology and phylogenetic reconstruction propose differentiation in the raptorial apparatus: relatively similar limb pairs in early forms, followed by increasing differentiation of the front pair and reduction/specialization of other pairs. This is a reconstructed evolutionary scenario, not a sequence of directly observed ancestors. The authors leave the exact function of some fossil morphologies uncertain; possible smashing in one form is explicitly speculative.
2. Van der Wal et al. (2017), [“The evolutionary history of Stomatopoda (Crustacea: Malacostraca) inferred from molecular data”](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3844). The molecular analysis recovers specialized smashing as monophyletic, but reports low to moderate support on parts of the deeper tree and uncertainty about the timing of specialized smashing and spearing.
3. Van der Wal et al. (2025), [“Total-evidence phylogenetic analysis resolves the evolutionary timescale of mantis shrimps…”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2025.108346). The study combines morphological and molecular evidence, samples representatives of all seven superfamilies, and estimates the crown-group age with a broad credible interval. Its phylogenetic estimates support a single origin of specialized smashing within Gonodactyloidea, while morphological-only and molecular-only analyses differ in some relationships.
4. Yaraghi et al. (2018), [“Ecologically Driven Ultrastructural and Hydrodynamic Designs in Stomatopod Cuticles”](https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.201705295). Comparative study of a smasher and spearer presents evidence for substructural exaptation in the club region and for redeployment of a raptorial appendage as a biological hammer. This is relevant support for a broad redeployment idea, not a license to invent the exact ancestral event or selective pressure.

## Editorial read

The subject can support an evolutionary Short if it is widened from “how one modern club works” to “how a set of hunting limbs became specialized.” The surprising central question is why one pair of limbs stands apart when earlier fossil forms show several more similar raptorial pairs. The main reveal is not a lab method; it is that modern striking and spearing forms represent distinct hunting specializations, while fossils and phylogenetic reconstruction let us infer a longer transformation in the apparatus.

### Candidate causal spine

1. **Present-day mismatch:** modern mantis shrimp can carry a dramatically specialized front hunting limb—club in smashers, spiny spear in spearers—while other limb pairs are much less conspicuous.
2. **A different fossil arrangement:** early and stem forms in the fossil record preserve several raptorial limbs with less differentiation; the famous specialized weapon is not the starting shape.
3. **A gradual contrast emerges:** across fossil morphologies and the reconstructed tree, the front pair becomes more distinct while the other pairs change relative size and form. Each anatomical difference changes what can plausibly be done with the limb.
4. **The split:** specialization does not yield one universal weapon. Living lineages include striking and spearing strategies; the evolutionary picture contains different functional outcomes.
5. **Return:** the modern club is one endpoint of a broader history in which a hunting limb was increasingly differentiated. The history is reconstructed from comparative anatomy, fossils, and phylogeny—not witnessed as a linear ancestor-to-descendant march.

This is a story outline for editorial development only. The apparent stage ordering must be checked against the accepted phylogeny before precise wording; do not depict fossil taxa as direct ancestors of one another, assign unsupported prey or selective pressures, or say the club evolved specifically from the spearing form as a settled fact. Keep the present-day mechanical details from Weaver et al. (2012) as supporting context only if they serve a causal story beat.
