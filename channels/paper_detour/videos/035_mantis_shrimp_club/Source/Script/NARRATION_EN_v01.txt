At first, the mantis shrimp’s striking club looks like one solid piece. What keeps damage from tearing straight through it?

The researchers looked inside. A CT scan revealed three regions: the impact region, the periodic region, and the striated region. The club wasn’t uniform. Its structure changed from the outside inward.

Next came maps of hardness and mineral composition. The outer impact region was especially hard and mineral-rich; deeper in, the material had different properties.

Microscopy and X-rays revealed another clue: chitin fibres arranged in a twisting, spiral-like stack. In damaged sections, micrographs showed tiny cracks deflecting between fibres, sometimes stopping after crossing several layers.

Then came the dramatic-looking strike—but it happened on a computer. The researchers modelled the club hitting a steel target. This was a simulation, not a filmed experiment. It showed how stresses move through the layered structure.

Together, the layers and fibre arrangement help explain how the club resists damage spreading into a catastrophic break. Not invulnerability—resistance.

And there’s a limit to what this investigation tells us: it explains how the club works today, not how this structure first evolved.
