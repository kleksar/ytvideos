At first, the mantis shrimp’s striking club looks like one solid piece. What keeps damage from tearing straight through it?

The researchers looked inside. Micro-CT showed the club’s two segments. In cross-section, they identified three regions: impact, periodic, and striated. The club’s structure changed from the outside inward.

Maps of hardness and mineral composition exposed another clue. The outer impact surface was especially hard and mineral-rich; deeper in, calcium and phosphorus declined as material properties changed.

Microscopy and X-rays revealed chitin fibres arranged in a twisting, spiral-like stack. In damaged sections, micrographs showed tiny cracks deflecting between fibres, sometimes stopping after crossing several layers.

Then came the dramatic-looking strike—but it happened on a computer, not in the lab. The model showed stresses moving through the club’s overall shape, but left out the tiny fibre layers.

Together, the layers and fibre arrangement help explain how the club resists damage spreading into a catastrophic break. Not invulnerability—resistance.

And there’s a limit to what this study tells us: it helps explain how the club works today, not how this structure first evolved.
