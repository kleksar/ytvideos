Bread. Fuel. Something to wash with. Something to light the room.

Before the Black Death, a London labourer's yearly earnings cover only about three-quarters of the basic needs of a family of four. To pay for the full year's basket at that same daily rate would take roughly three hundred and thirty-three paid working days. But the model assumes only two hundred and fifty paid days in a year. That one annual wage falls short.

Later, the same benchmark becomes much easier to cover. Then the daily wage more than doubles — and the basics move further away again.

How much work did an ordinary year's necessities cost? Follow the price through the centuries, and the answer takes an unexpected turn.

We will stay in London and compare a labourer's daily pay with the cost of basic needs. For the later periods, we follow building labourers, whose daily wages are lower than those of skilled building craftsmen.

The yardstick is a reconstructed basic budget for a man, a woman and two children. It includes food, linen, soap, candles and fuel, with a small housing allowance. Think of an annual bill for a defined set of necessities.

Divide that bill by a day's wage, and its price becomes a number of wage-days. A lower number means each day's pay buys more of the basket.

These are price tags expressed in wages, rather than surviving records of anyone's working year. Families had different incomes and needs; employment could be interrupted. The calculation assumes two hundred and fifty paid days and adds only five percent for rent.

Keep that benchmark in mind. Now we can watch what happens when the value of a day's labour changes.

Before the Black Death, the model's annual wage covers about three-quarters of the basic family bill. Imagine that bill divided into four equal parts. A year's assumed earnings pay for three. One part remains unpaid.

That gap is the meaning of the three hundred and thirty-three wage-days. It measures how far the daily wage falls short of supporting the model family on the assumed working year.

Then the plague changes the labour market. Population loss helps explain why real earnings improved in the following period.

The struggle over wages survives in a very different kind of evidence: a law.

In thirteen fifty-one, England's Statute of Labourers complains that workers are refusing service unless they receive higher pay. It tries to impose customary rates from before the plague.

The text names ploughmen and shepherds, but also carpenters, masons and other building workers. It sets wage limits and provides penalties for disobedience. Workers who refuse the required oath or fail to keep it can face the stocks or prison.

Read that as a conflict over the price of labour. The complaint comes from people trying to hold that price down. It gives us their accusation, rather than a neutral account of every worker's earnings.

Labour has become scarcer. Employers still need it. The attempt to restrain wages reaches into law.

On this measure, fifteenth-century labourer earnings are generally enough to cross above the basic-needs line. The relationship between a day's work and a year's necessities has changed.

For the first half of the fifteen hundreds, the London building-labourer figures give us a more precise comparison: about one hundred and seventy-six days' pay for the annual basket.

On the model's two-hundred-and-fifty-day year, that leaves a margin above this particular bill.

Move forward a century. The average daily wage, measured in silver, rises from three point two grams to seven point one. More than double.

Before we look at prices, make a prediction. Would that year's basic basket now cost fewer days' pay, or more?

More.

For sixteen hundred to sixteen forty-nine, the annual basket represents about two hundred and sixteen days' pay. Roughly forty more than in the earlier comparison.

The wage has risen. But so has the bill.

Across those two periods, the silver wage grows to a little over twice its earlier level. The consumer-price index grows to nearly three times its earlier level. Prices have outrun pay.

Think through what a doubling would do on its own. If the basket's price stayed fixed, twice the daily pay would cover it in half as many wage-days. If pay and prices doubled together, the wage-day cost would stay the same.

Here, prices rise faster. So each day's larger payment buys a smaller share of the basket.

The consequence is the extra forty wage-days. The reconstructed basket still fits within the model's annual earnings, but uses up more of them.

That is how a larger daily payment can accompany a tighter basic budget. Count the silver alone, and the improvement looks enormous. Put the price of necessities beside it, and the picture changes.

Now move to the second half of the eighteen hundreds. The London building-labourer figures put the annual basket at approximately one hundred and sixteen days' pay.

That is about a hundred fewer wage-days than in the early sixteen hundreds. The basic bill has become smaller relative to a day's earnings.

This changes the room available in the calculation. A budget swallowed by the benchmark leaves no margin within that budget for anything beyond it. When the benchmark takes a smaller share, more earnings remain outside that particular bill.

That remainder could go towards more food, different housing or other needs. It cannot automatically be counted as leisure or savings. A higher wage rate only produces earnings when the worker can obtain paid work; an actual family's bills need not match our benchmark.

The nineteenth-century figure establishes a substantial improvement on this measure. Explaining its causes would require more than these wage-and-basket comparisons.

What they do establish is that time alone did not make the basics steadily easier to afford. Between the early fifteen hundreds and the early sixteen hundreds, the wage-day cost went up. In the later nineteenth-century comparison, it was much lower.

Return to the bread, the soap, the fuel and the light.

Beside them, put a day's pay.

Across the centuries, that payment grows. What changes a basic budget is how much of those necessities it can carry home.
