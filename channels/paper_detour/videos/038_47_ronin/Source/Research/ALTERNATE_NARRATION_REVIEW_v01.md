**Status:** editorial and factual review, 2 November 2026. At the time of review, the attached Russian text was a candidate. The user subsequently selected it as the working narration; it is now preserved in [narration_ru_v06.md](../Script/narration_ru_v06.md), with RU as this episode's language. ## Editorial read

The alternate draft has a stronger character arc than the short v05 draft: Ōishi first tries to preserve the Asano house, then leads men into an attack whose cost includes his son. It also gives the group more than one motive and builds a useful second act around the danger to their families. The clearest new story beat is Ōtaka Gengo's farewell letter to his mother: he says he received no special favors from Asano and warns that the authorities might punish relatives if they regarded the attack as conspiracy.

The draft is substantially longer than v05 and adds several parallel threads. Its most useful through-line is still compact: Asano attacks Kira and is sentenced; the retainers lose their lord and divide over what to do; Ōishi tries to restore the house while others press for revenge; the Daigaku route closes; the group attacks; the state both recognizes loyalty and punishes private violence; the story later becomes Chūshingura. Horibe is useful as an early counterpoint, but the sources do not support presenting him and Ōishi as permanent, equal rival leaders. Horibe's group first proposed an immediate attack, then changed course when they heard Kira could be protected by Uesugi forces; by the time they reached Akō, Ōishi had chosen surrender and Horibe's group joined the broader restoration effort.

| Asano attacked Kira during the imperial-envoy ceremony on 21 April 1701; Kira was speaking with Kajikawa, Asano struck twice, and Kajikawa restrained him. | Supported by the surviving eyewitness account as summarized by Bitō. Kajikawa's account reports Asano's words about a grudge from recent days, but does not reveal its cause. | This is a vivid, usable opening. Keep the grudge line clearly as Asano's reported words; do not turn it into a specific insult, bribe, or cause. Avoid extending the moment with unverified dialogue or timing. |
| “A witness heard of a recent insult” / later rumors about bribes and humiliation. | The source records a grudge, but not its cause. Bribery and etiquette stories appear in later accounts whose reliability is disputed. | Do not imply that the witness heard a specific insult. If mentioning the stories at all, put their provenance and limits in research notes; the narration does not need a catalogue of theories. |
| “Hundreds” lose service, income, and the future of their families. | Loss of the domain and retainer service is supported. The exact headcount and the claimed immediate effects on every family are not established in the sources checked for this review. | State the confiscation and loss of service. Avoid “hundreds,” “income,” or family consequences unless a specific count and evidence are added. The family-risk beat can instead use Gengo's letter, which directly addresses that danger. |
| Horibe presses for killing Kira while Ōishi chooses surrender and restoration. | The early contrast is supported, but the draft overstates it as an ongoing two-leader contest. Horibe and two companions initially advocated revenge, then went to Akō and joined the group after Ōishi's surrender decision. | Use Horibe briefly as an early voice for immediate revenge; then show the retainers converging around efforts to secure reconsideration/restoration. Do not stage a prolonged personal rivalry. |
| After Daigaku is placed with the main Asano house in Hiroshima, restoration becomes impossible and Ōishi privately switches to revenge. | The Daigaku decision is a concrete turning point and makes restoration through him moot. Bitō explicitly cautions that Ōishi's private expectations and exact moment of decision cannot be known. | Narrate the closing of that route and the group's subsequent preparation. Do not claim to know Ōishi's private realization or the exact day he decided. |
| Ōtaka Gengo writes his mother that he received no special favor and warns that family could be punished. | Supported by Gengo's farewell letter, dated 5 September 1702, as cited by Bitō. | Strong optional character beat. Attribute the statement to Gengo's letter as an in-story document; keep the account concise and do not assign him a full set of motives not stated in the letter. |
| Oishi's fifteen-year-old son Chikara joins; the father leads his son to death; Chikara argues for suicide at Sengakuji. | Chikara was a participant. One account says he urged immediate seppuku at Sengakuji; other sources differ over the group's choice at that moment. | The son is a strong stake if needed, but the “father led his son to death” line is interpretation. If using the Sengakuji exchange, preserve its single-source status in the ledger and do not present it as uncontested. |
| The attackers fight through the residence, repeatedly search rooms and hiding places, and Kira is nowhere to be found. | Terasaka's 1703 account gives a detailed attack narrative, but later versions expanded its action. The exact room-by-room sequence and staging in the submitted draft were not established in the current source check. | Keep the clear sequence of entering, searching, finding, and killing. Do not add guard counts, tactical movement, repeated failed searches, exact hiding places, or dialogue without a close check against the account. |
| 47 attack; Terasaka is apart from the other 46; the reason remains unknown/disputed. | The usual scholarly count is 47 attackers and 46 surrendered/sentenced. Terasaka's participation and separation remain disputed; his 1703 account says he went to Sengakuji, while the leaders' account says he did not enter Kira's residence. | Keep the 47/46 distinction, but phrase Terasaka's status as disputed rather than asserting he “separated” at a known moment. Do not declare his reason simply unknowable without noting the conflicting accounts in the ledger. |
| The shogunate's views were divided; after seven weeks it ordered seppuku on 20 March 1703. | Supported. The surviving council report records divided views; Smith dates the order to 20 March. | Keep this. It answers the opening without reconstructing an undocumented debate. |
| The vendetta becomes the famous play *Kanadehon Chūshingura* in 1748. | Supported. The 1748 play fictionalized the event and moved it to an earlier historical setting to avoid censorship. | Use this as the cultural afterlife, while distinguishing the historical event from the later play. Avoid claiming that the play simply “recorded” what happened. |

## Recommended next-draft direction

Keep the v05 opening contradiction and compact cause-and-effect spine. Add only the most valuable character evidence: the reported grudge line from Asano, a brief Horibe counterpoint before his group joins Ōishi's restoration effort, and—if the target runtime supports it—Ōtaka Gengo's letter as a concrete sign that the group understood the risk to relatives. Consider Chikara only after weighing the single-source Sengakuji exchange. Let the raid have an entry/search/find/kill sequence without battle choreography. End with the divided official judgment, the March 20 sentence, and the 1748 play as the later transformation of the story.

## Sources checked

- Bitō Masahide, [“The Akō Incident, 1701–1703”](https://www.columbia.edu/~hds2/pdf/2003b_Bito_Ako_Incident.pdf), especially pp. 151–162 and 165–167: eyewitness report, attack, grudge, Horibe/Ōishi chronology, Daigaku, Gengo's letter, divided judgment.
- Henry D. Smith II, [“The Trouble with Terasaka: The Forty-Seventh Rōnin and the Chūshingura Imagination”](https://nichibun.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/252): the 47/46 discrepancy, Terasaka's 1703 account, and conflicting accounts of his participation and departure.
- Henry D. Smith II, [“Rethinking the Story of the 47 Rōnin”](https://www.columbia.edu/~hds2/47ronin/47ronin_rev.htm): the dates, seven-week interval, uncertainty around Asano's grudge, and later reception.
- Columbia University ExEAS, [“Law and Society: The Story of the 47 Rōnin”](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/special/japan_1450_47ronin.htm): historical event and the 1748 play's fictionalized setting.
