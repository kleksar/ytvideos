# Paper Detour 038 — factual basis v06

**Status:** 2 November 2026. The user selected the latest supplied Russian narration as the working text for this episode and asked to retain it. This ledger preserves the earlier review and marks claims that still need closer evidence review. It is not an independent fact-check.

## Story promise

The former retainers of Asano seek to finish the confrontation their lord began, then face a state judgment that holds loyalty and public order in tension. Later retellings turn the event into the story of the Forty-Seven Rōnin.

## Claims, evidence, and boundaries

| ID | Claim in the selected text | Evidence / boundary | Storyboard handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| F01 | On 21 April 1701 Asano attacked Kira during the imperial-envoy ceremony, struck twice, and was restrained by Kajikawa. Asano said he had a grudge from recent days. | Bitō summarizes Kajikawa's eyewitness account. It records the grudge phrase but gives no cause. Later bribery/insult explanations are not established by the eyewitness account. | Show the reported confrontation without adding dialogue, a specific insult, or a cause. |
| F02 | Asano was sentenced to seppuku and Akō was confiscated; Kira was not punished. | Bitō and Columbia ExEAS. The confiscation ended the retainers' domain service. | Show the order, closed domain gate, and retainer status change. Do not invent a family-level income figure. |
| F03 | Retainers considered armed resistance, following Asano in death, restoration through Daigaku, and revenge. Horibe's group initially pressed for an attack; Ōishi surrendered the castle and pursued restoration. | Bitō, pp. 157–162. Horibe's group changed course and later joined the restoration effort; they were not permanent equal rivals to Ōishi. | Visualize changing positions over time, not a prolonged personal duel. |
| F04 | In July 1702 Daigaku was released from house arrest and placed in the custody of the main Asano house in Hiroshima, closing restoration through him. | Bitō, p. 160; Akō Castle Digital Museum. Bitō cautions that Ōishi's private expectations and decision moment cannot be known. | Show the route through Daigaku closing; do not depict his private thoughts. |
| F05 | Ōtaka Gengo's farewell letter to his mother says he received no special favors from Asano and warns that relatives could be punished if the attack were treated as conspiracy. | Bitō, pp. 162, 165, citing the 5 September 1702 letter. | The letter is a supported prop and family-risk beat. Do not assign Gengo an unstated complete motive. |
| F06 | The selected text describes a planned attack, a search for Kira, and his killing. | The 1703 account attributed to Terasaka gives a detailed attack account; later retellings add dramatic details. The current review has not verified every room-by-room, guard, repeated-search, and combat claim in the selected text. | Use whole-image reconstructions based on the selected narration; add no further tactics, room details, dialogue, injuries, or weather. Keep violence non-graphic. |
| F07 | Forty-seven are associated with the attack; forty-six surrender. Terasaka's exact role and departure are disputed. | Smith, “The Trouble with Terasaka”; Bitō also notes the 47/46 disagreement and that Terasaka was evidently dismissed immediately before or after the attack. | Keep 47/46 distinct. Depict Terasaka's separation as a visual uncertainty, not a known exact moment or motive. |
| F08 | The shogunate's decision followed seven weeks; the sentence was announced on 20 March 1703. Views were divided over loyalty and law/order. | Smith, “Rethinking the Story of the 47 Rōnin”; Bitō, pp. 165–167. Do not invent a transcript of the deliberations. | Show elapsed time and the opposing considerations as a visual metaphor, not a literal debate scene. |
| F09 | The forty-six were sentenced to seppuku and buried beside Asano. | Columbia ExEAS; Bitō; Smith. Exact ceremonial details are not required by the selected story. | No graphic or staged death scenes. Use the sentence, graves, and distance as symbols. |
| F10 | *Kanadehon Chūshingura* appears in 1748 and later popular retellings reshape the event. | Bitō and Columbia ExEAS. The play is a fictionalized adaptation and sets the story in an earlier historical period. | Distinguish the stage story from the historical event. |
| F11 | “Hundreds” lost income/family futures; the attack was a universally admired act; detailed inner motives and repeated house searches. | These assertions are present in the selected narration but were not established by the source check summarized above. Public response and individual motives were mixed/uncertain; exact raid details vary among accounts. | Preserve the user's narration as requested, while keeping visuals restrained and avoiding additional factual specificity. Review these claims before any factual metadata or educational captions are prepared. |
| F12 | Ōishi's son Chikara takes part; one account says he urged the group to commit seppuku at Sengakuji. | Smith, “The Trouble with Terasaka,” cites one account describing the fifteen-year-old Chikara's proposal; other accounts differ over the group's choice at that moment. | Show Chikara as part of the group as stated in the selected narration; treat the reported Sengakuji exchange as source-dependent if dramatized. |
