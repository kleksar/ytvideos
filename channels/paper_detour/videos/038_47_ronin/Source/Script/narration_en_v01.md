# Paper Detour 038 — narration EN v01

Status: DRAFT for review; no voice recorded. Estimated 4–5 minutes at a deliberate pace. The actual motive behind Asano's first attack is not established in the surviving record, so this narration does not invent one. Spoken narration ends before the two-scene, ten-second end screen; exact timing awaits an authorized recording.

In the shogun’s own castle, a samurai draws his sword.

By nightfall, his lord is dead, his family’s domain is gone, and every man who served him has lost his place.

They have two choices: accept the ruling—or try to answer it themselves.

The story begins in Edo, the city now called Tokyo, in the spring of 1701. Japan is in the Edo period, a long era of peace under the Tokugawa shogunate. The conflict unfolds not on a battlefield, but inside the shogun’s castle.

Asano Naganori, the lord of Akō, has been assigned to help receive imperial envoys at Edo Castle. Kira Yoshinaka, a senior official, is responsible for instructing him in court protocol.

Inside the castle’s long pine corridor, Asano attacks Kira with a short sword. Kira survives. The surviving record does not give a reliable explanation for Asano’s grudge. What follows, though, is immediate.

The shogunate orders Asano to perform seppuku. His domain is confiscated. The castle and its people pass out of his family’s hands. The men who had served him are now rōnin: samurai without a lord.

The news reaches Ōishi, one of Asano’s senior retainers, at Akō. Some of the men want to resist surrendering the castle. Others consider dying with their lord. Ōishi instead leads them through the handover, while the group appeals for the Asano house to be restored.

They do not yet have a single path. Their first aim is to change the ruling and preserve the house they served. But the shogunate does not reverse its decision. Their castle is gone. Their lord is dead. And Kira, the man Asano attacked, remains alive.

The former retainers begin to separate. Some leave. Others wait. Ōishi keeps the group from acting before they are ready. For nearly two years, the men live apart from their old life while the possibility of restoring the Asano house fades.

Then they make a different plan.

On a winter night in January 1703, Ōishi and the former retainers raid Kira’s residence in Edo. They kill him, then carry his head to Sengakuji, the temple where Asano is buried. They surrender to the authorities.

Their revenge has succeeded. Their lord’s domain has not been restored. And now the shogunate must decide what to do with the men who carried out the killing.

The retainers say they acted for their lord. But the shogunate’s decision against Asano had been the law of the state. If the retainers can answer that judgment with violence, the authority that holds the country together is at risk.

Some officials see loyalty in the men’s actions. Others see the danger of letting private revenge override the state’s judgment. The shogunate sentences them to die by seppuku.

Their deaths do not bring their lord back or reverse the shogunate’s judgment. Instead, they leave a question behind: when loyalty to a lord and obedience to the law collide, which one should a samurai follow?

They are remembered as the Forty-seven Rōnin. Later generations reshaped the events into Chūshingura, a famous stage story of loyal retainers. The plays made the men legends. But the real story is already powerful: the rōnin did not simply avenge their lord. They forced a government to judge the difference between loyalty and law—and paid for the answer with their lives.
