Ōishi leads forty-five men into Kira’s residence.

Seven weeks later, the shogunate orders forty-six men to die by seppuku.

Their revenge has succeeded. Why, then, are they condemned?

The answer begins in Edo, in the spring of 1701. Asano Naganori, lord of the Akō domain, is helping receive envoys from the imperial court. He draws his sword in the shogun’s castle and attacks Kira Yoshinaka, a senior government official.

Asano cuts Kira lightly before guards pull him away. No reliable account explains exactly what caused the attack. The shogunate’s response, however, is swift: Asano is ordered to perform seppuku, and his domain is confiscated that same day.

His retainers are suddenly without a lord. Ōishi, Asano’s chief retainer, must decide what to do with the men who have lost their place—and disagree over what should come next.

Some propose holding Akō Castle against the shogunate. Others consider following their lord in death. Ōishi chooses to surrender the castle, in part to avoid bringing further trouble on Asano’s younger brother. The group asks the government to restore the Asano house.

But some retainers have not given up on Kira. One small group considers an early attack, then backs away after word spreads that the powerful Uesugi family may protect him. They could die before reaching their target.

So Ōishi keeps the group from rushing. He continues to press for the Asano family’s restoration, while the men wait and argue over their next step. Some leave. Others stay with the vendetta. For many months, their actions move between a public petition and a private plan.

The group does not turn toward revenge in a single moment. The paths they are trying—saving the house, changing the shogunate’s ruling, and reaching Kira—overlap. One by one, the official options fail to change the outcome.

Then Ōishi and forty-five others act.

On the night of January 30, 1703, they raid Kira’s residence in Edo. They kill him, take his head to Sengakuji, where Asano is buried, and surrender themselves to the authorities.

The deed is done. The men do not flee. They place themselves in the hands of the same government whose ruling they had defied.

For the next seven weeks, officials debate what the shogunate should do. The men had carried out a private vendetta against someone the state had not punished. Yet some officials also saw loyalty in their action. Could loyalty excuse a private killing? Or would that let every retainer judge and punish on his own?

The shogunate sentences forty-six men to die by seppuku. The familiar name “Forty-Seven Rōnin” includes Terasaka, whose role in the raid and disappearance afterward are described differently in the records.

Ōishi spent nearly two years guiding a divided group through choices with no safe outcome. The raid had brought Kira’s head to Asano’s grave. It had not returned their lord or his family’s domain.

Later generations reshaped the vendetta into Chūshingura, a stage story of loyal retainers. It gave the group the name “Forty-Seven Rōnin.” The shogunate sentenced forty-six. What happened to the forty-seventh is still disputed.
