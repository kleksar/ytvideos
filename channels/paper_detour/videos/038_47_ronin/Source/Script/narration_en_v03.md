Ōishi leads forty-six men into Kira’s residence.

They kill Kira and carry his head to their former lord’s grave.

Seven weeks later, the shogunate sentences forty-six men to die by seppuku.

If this was loyalty, why did it end in a death sentence?

The story begins in Edo, in the spring of 1701. Asano Naganori, lord of the Akō domain, is helping receive envoys from the imperial court. Inside the shogun’s castle, he draws his sword and attacks Kira Yoshinaka, a senior government official. Asano wounds Kira lightly before guards pull him away.

The cause of the attack is unknown. The shogunate’s ruling is not: Asano must perform seppuku, and his domain is confiscated that same day. Kira is not punished.

For Asano’s retainers, the ruling leaves their lord dead, their home gone, and Kira alive. They disagree over what to do. Some call for holding Akō Castle against the shogunate. Others consider following Asano in death. Ōishi, the chief retainer, chooses to surrender the castle. A fight there could bring new trouble on Asano’s younger brother, Daigaku. Instead, Ōishi and the others petition to restore the Asano house.

In Edo, Horibe and a small group favor attacking Kira at once. They abandon that plan after hearing that the powerful Uesugi family may protect him, then go to Akō and urge the others to hold the castle. Ōishi refuses a siege and persuades them to surrender peacefully. He continues to pursue restoration while others still look toward revenge.

Then, in July 1702, the shogunate releases Daigaku from house arrest in Edo and places him under the custody of the main Asano family in Hiroshima. The path to restoring the Asano house and the Akō domain under him is closed.

Ōishi and other retainers meet in Kyoto. Months of petitions have not changed the ruling. The choices that might have forced the shogunate to reconsider are running out. Kira remains alive. The group now prepares to carry out the attack.

On the night of January 30, 1703, forty-seven former retainers attack Kira’s residence in Edo. They kill him and carry his head across the city to Sengakuji, where Asano is buried.

After the attack, Terasaka separates from the other forty-six. He lives until 1747; his role in the raid and the reason he left the group remain disputed. The other forty-six surrender to the authorities.

For seven weeks, officials debate the fate of the men who surrendered. They had carried out a private vendetta against a man the state had left unpunished. Some officials saw loyalty in their act. But if the shogunate excused the killing, could every retainer enforce his own judgment?

The shogunate sentences the forty-six men in custody to seppuku. Their vendetta later becomes Chūshingura, a famous play about loyal retainers.

Their sentence carries both judgments: seppuku honors their standing as samurai; their deaths uphold the shogunate’s law.
