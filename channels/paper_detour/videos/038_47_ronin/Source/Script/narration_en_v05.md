The story begins in Edo, in the spring of 1701. Asano Naganori, lord of the Akō domain, is helping receive envoys from the imperial court. Inside the shogun’s castle, he draws his sword and attacks Kira Yoshinaka, a senior government official. Asano wounds Kira lightly before guards pull him away.

The cause of the attack is unknown. The shogunate’s ruling is not: Asano must perform seppuku, and his domain is confiscated that same day. Kira is not punished. Asano had made the first attack; Kira had not killed him.

For the retainers pressing for revenge, Asano’s attack remained an unfinished conflict. While Kira lived, they believed their honor as his retainers was at stake.

The confiscation ends the retainers’ service and takes away their positions with the Asano house. They become rōnin: samurai without a lord.

The retainers split. Some call for holding Akō Castle; others consider following Asano in death. Ōishi, the chief retainer, chooses to surrender the castle, partly to avoid bringing further trouble on Asano’s younger brother, Daigaku. He petitions to restore the Asano house while some retainers continue to press for revenge.

In July 1702, the shogunate releases Daigaku from house arrest in Edo and places him under the custody of the main Asano family in Hiroshima. The path to restoring the Asano house and the Akō domain under him is closed.

With the route to restoration through Daigaku closed, the former retainers have two paths: leave Kira alive or mount a private attack the shogunate could punish. They meet in Kyoto. Months of efforts to secure reconsideration have changed nothing. They prepare to attack.

On the night of January 30, 1703, forty-seven former retainers enter Kira’s residence in Edo. Inside, they search for him. They find Kira and kill him. Then they carry his head to Sengakuji, where Asano is buried.

After the attack, Terasaka is apart from the other forty-six. He lives until 1747; his role in the raid and the reason for his separation remain disputed. The other forty-six surrender to the authorities.

Seven weeks pass before the shogunate reaches its decision. Some officials see loyalty in the raid; others fear a precedent in which any retainer could take revenge in his lord’s name.

The shogunate sentences the forty-six men in custody to seppuku. Their vendetta later becomes Chūshingura, a famous play about loyal retainers.

The order gives them a samurai’s form of death while punishing the private killing. It recognizes their standing and upholds the shogunate’s authority.
