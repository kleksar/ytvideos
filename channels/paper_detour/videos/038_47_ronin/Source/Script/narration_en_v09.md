# 47 Ronin — English production narration v09

Faithful translation of current RU v07 (user-selected story v06 with the documented attribution correction). Title/header are not spoken. Dates are written for speech. No new historical claims or research.

January thirtieth, seventeen oh three. Edo, the future Tokyo.

In the middle of the night, forty-seven armed men storm the home of a high-ranking official.

They break through the guards and search the rooms. But the owner is nowhere to be found.

He is the only man they want. And to kill him,

they are willing to pay with their own lives. But here is the strange part:

their enemy did not kill the lord they are avenging.

It was their lord who tried to kill him first.

So why did forty-seven samurai decide to finish someone else's fight?

And how did a killing that the state condemned as a crime

become a symbol of loyalty for three centuries? It all began with two sword strikes.

April twenty-first, seventeen oh one. At the shogun's castle, preparations are underway

for a reception for envoys from the imperial court. Among those taking part is the young lord of Ako,

Asano Naganori. In a corridor, he approaches an official named Kira Yoshinaka.

Kira is responsible for ceremonial protocol. He is talking to someone else.

Before he can even understand what is happening, Asano draws a short sword.

The first strike wounds Kira. He tries to escape.

Asano attacks again, but an official standing nearby restrains him.

It is over in moments. Kira is alive. Asano is under arrest.

Why did he attack? A witness heard Asano speak of a recent grievance.

Later, stories would emerge about bribes and humiliation.

But we do not know the true cause. To the government, something else matters more:

Asano drew a weapon in the ruler's castle during a state ceremony,

and struck a man who had not attacked him.

The sentence comes that same day. Asano must commit seppuku: ritual suicide.

His lands are confiscated. Kira goes unpunished. One failed strike has destroyed

an entire samurai house. And the consequences fall on people

who were not even in that palace corridor.

They did not attack Kira. They did not disobey orders.

Yet in a matter of hours, they lose the position

on which their families' futures depend. In Ako, hundreds of men

suddenly lose their lord, their service, and their income. They become ronin: masterless samurai.

What now? Some propose defending the castle by force.

Others consider following their lord into death.

But the samurai Horibe Yasubei demands something else: kill Kira.

Asano is dead. His house has been destroyed. Yet the man

he fought is still living freely.

To Horibe, leaving that fight unfinished means accepting dishonor.

But Oishi Kuranosuke remains the leading figure among the former retainers.

And he makes a decision that outrages those demanding revenge.

Oishi surrenders the castle to the authorities. He refuses to attack Kira immediately.

For those who wanted a battle, this is a bitter blow.

Their lord is dead, and his chief adviser is voluntarily handing over the family castle.

But armed resistance would mean defying the shogunate itself,

and endangering people who can still be saved.

He is not doing this because he has nothing left to lose.

He is doing it because he still has another way forward.

Asano has a younger brother, Daigaku. If the shogunate allows him

to lead a restored Asano house, the former retainers may be able to preserve

at least part of their old world. Without another killing.

The ronin now have two leaders, and two answers to the same question.

Horibe believes honor can only be restored with blood.

Oishi is looking for a way to save the family. Their dispute becomes a race against time.

While Oishi seeks a decision on Daigaku, those in favor of an attack discuss

how to reach Kira. But the official's home is well defended.

A rushed assault could leave the attackers dead while Kira survives.

Months pass. The former retainers scatter. Some look for a new lord to serve.

The longer the wait, the fewer men are willing to gamble with their future.

At last, an answer comes. July, seventeen oh two.

Daigaku is released from house arrest. But the domain of Ako is not returned to him.

He is placed under the supervision of relatives. The attempt to restore the house has failed.

We will never know whether Oishi believed that attempt would succeed.

But now he and his supporters begin preparing an attack on Kira.

That does not mean they have no other choice.

The ronin could go their separate ways, find new service, and start again.

But for some, that would mean abandoning everything

they understand as duty. And now Oishi is among them.

Making the decision is easier than finding men willing to carry it out.

They understand that even if they kill Kira, the authorities may execute them.

Their families could be punished too. One of the future attackers,

Otaka Gengo, writes a farewell letter to his mother. He warns her of the danger.

He also admits that Asano had never shown him any special personal favor.

He is not walking toward death because his lord once saved his life.

His motives are more complicated: duty, honor, and obligations to his comrades.

Not every former retainer is willing to pay that price. Some refuse.

In the end, forty-seven men remain. Among them is Horibe,

who has demanded revenge from the start. Oishi himself.

And his young son, Chikara. Now Oishi is risking more than his own life.

He once tried to preserve the Asano house so that his men

would not die in a pointless fight. Now he is leading them into an attack

from which they may never return.

That does not make his first decision a lie.

But it shows how much the situation has changed. Before they attack, they must discover

where Kira is, choose the right moment, and get past his guards.

The official himself knows that someone may seek revenge.

If he slips away, all their preparations will have been for nothing.

At last, the night of January thirtieth, seventeen oh three arrives. The ronin attack Kira's residence.

Fighting breaks out inside. The defenders resist, but the attackers advance through the house.

Then comes the worst discovery: Kira is nowhere to be found.

They have risked their lives to reach one man.

But if he escapes, all they will have left is a charge of armed assault.

The ronin search again and again. They go through the rooms and check hiding places.

Defeating the guards is not enough. They must be certain

that this is the very man they came to find.

With every moment spent searching, there is a chance that Kira has already escaped

while the fighting continued inside. At last, they find the owner of the house.

Twenty-two months earlier, Asano tried to kill him in the palace.

Kira survived. This time, there is no escape.

They kill Kira and cut off his head. Their revenge is complete.

But the victors do not flee Edo. Instead, they carry

Kira's head through the city to Sengakuji Temple, to Asano's grave.

There, they place it before their dead lord.

They have done what Asano could not.

And now they are ready to answer for it. But of the forty-seven

participants, only forty-six surrender to the authorities. One ronin, Terasaka, has left the others.

We do not know for certain why. He will outlive his comrades by decades.

The others must wait for a decision the authorities are in no hurry to make.

Until now, they have acted as one unit. They knew their target,

prepared the attack, and carried it out. Now they have neither a plan

nor a weapon that can change the outcome. All they can do is wait

for the state to judge their actions. On one hand,

forty-six men have killed an official whom the state had not found guilty.

Acquit them, and any band of samurai could overturn

the government's decisions by the sword. On the other hand, the ronin did not try to seize

power or Kira's property. They did not run once they had achieved their goal.

To many of their contemporaries, their actions looked like extraordinary devotion.

Can you admire their loyalty and still consider them killers?

A week passes. Then another. The ronin remain in custody,

not knowing whether they will live. On March twentieth, seventeen oh three, the shogunate announces its decision:

all forty-six must commit seppuku. It is a death sentence,

though they are allowed to die in a manner befitting their samurai rank.

That same day, Horibe, Oishi, and his son Chikara die.

Along with the others, they are buried beside Asano.

Horibe has achieved the revenge he demanded from the start.

Oishi tried to save the house without more bloodshed,

and ended up leading even his own son to his death.

But this is where the story begins its second life.

Over the years, the ronin become the heroes of plays, books, and eventually films.

In seventeen forty-eight comes the famous play, Kanadehon Chushingura.

The image of loyal retainers gradually overshadows the uncomfortable facts of the real case.

Revenge makes a compelling story: a long wait, a dangerous attack, a willingness to sacrifice everything.

And the easier the story becomes to tell, the easier it is to forget the question

that started it all. In the legend, it is simple:

noble warriors avenged their lord and sacrificed their lives.

The reality is more complicated. We do not know why Asano attacked Kira.

We do not know whether Kira had wronged him.

And the surviving letters do not allow us to assume that all the ronin

shared the same motive. But we do know what they did.

Forty-seven men decided to finish a fight their lord had started.

Forty-six paid for it with their lives. The state called their actions a crime.

Generations remembered them as a model of loyalty. And the story's central question remains unanswered:

if someone is willing to die for his honor, does that mean

he has the right to kill someone else for it?
