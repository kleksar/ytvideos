# The Great Emu War (1932)

November fourth, 1932.

In Western Australia, soldiers wait beside a dam with machine guns.

Ahead of them, around a thousand emus are approaching the water.

The birds have finally gathered in one place. The soldiers let them come closer, then open fire.

A few emus fall.

Then a machine gun jams.

The rest scatter across the open land.

It was one of their best chances to stop a whole flock. Almost every bird escaped.

How did Australia end up sending machine guns against emus?

To understand that, go back two days.

On November second, a small army unit arrives in the wheat-growing district near Campion. The men have two machine guns and orders to help local farmers get rid of the birds.

Those farmers have been fighting to keep their farms alive.

After the First World War, many former soldiers had settled on land in Western Australia. Some of that land was hard to farm even in good years. In parts of the district, settlers had already abandoned their farms.

Then the Great Depression struck. Wheat prices collapsed. Drought made everything worse.

For the families who stayed, the harvest was their livelihood. A lost field of wheat could mean a year of work with nothing to show for it.

And now the emus were arriving.

They were huge, flightless birds, nearly as tall as a person, with powerful legs built for running.

Thousands were moving through the farming districts looking for food and water. To the birds, the wheat fields were an easy meal.

To the farmers, they were crops that had taken months of work to grow.

The emus ate the grain and trampled what they didn't eat. They damaged fences as they moved through, leaving openings for rabbits to enter and do even more harm.

The farmers couldn't keep them out.

So they turned to the government for help.

The Defence Minister agreed to send soldiers with machine guns. A cameraman came along to record the operation.

On paper, the plan seemed simple. Find a large flock. Bring the guns within range. Open fire.

But the soldiers discovered the problem almost as soon as they arrived.

On their first day, they spotted a group of emus. Farmers tried to herd the birds toward the waiting guns.

Instead, the flock broke apart.

Some birds ran one way. Others ran another. By the time the soldiers could shoot, there were only small groups moving across the fields.

Only a few were killed.

The soldiers had weapons made to deliver sustained fire. But that meant little when the targets kept scattering.

And then came the opportunity at the dam.

Around a thousand emus were gathered near the water. This time the soldiers waited until they were close before firing.

But after roughly a dozen birds were killed, a gun jammed. The flock escaped.

The men had found the large target they needed.

And lost it.

So they tried something different.

They put a machine gun on the back of a truck.

If they couldn't get the birds to come to the guns, they could take the gun after the birds.

There was just one problem.

Emus can run fast. The truck couldn't keep up over the rough ground. It jolted so badly that the gunner struggled to aim.

A machine gun was now bouncing across the countryside, chasing birds it could barely reach.

The soldiers kept trying. But each encounter ended much the same way: a burst of gunfire, birds scattering, and more emus disappearing over the fields.

Their commander, Major Meredith, compared the birds' ability to withstand gunfire to that of tanks.

Back in Canberra, the operation was attracting a different kind of attention.

Politicians questioned the campaign. One joked that if anyone deserved medals, it was the emus.

A few days after the failed ambush, the government recalled the soldiers.

In Canberra, the shooting was over.

In the farming district, the birds were still eating wheat.

The soldiers had gone, but the damage hadn't stopped. Fences were still being broken. Crops were still at risk. The birds were still moving through the district.

The farmers asked for help again.

And the government agreed to another attempt.

So the soldiers returned with their guns.

This time, they killed more emus. But the work dragged on for weeks. The birds were spread across too much country, and the soldiers had to find them before they could fire.

Even when the guns were successful, another group of emus could turn up somewhere else.

By December, hundreds of birds had been killed. Even so, thousands remained in the district. The shooting hadn't stopped the damage.

The army ended its campaign.

For the farmers, there was no dramatic finish. They still needed ways to protect their fields. In the years that followed, people continued killing emus for bounty payments, while fences remained important for protecting crops.

For the birds, the campaign had been deadly. Many had been shot. But thousands remained across the region.

Australia had sent soldiers into farmland with machine guns to stop the birds from destroying the wheat.

The soldiers had fired, chased, withdrawn, and returned.

The farmers still needed protection.

The emus had suffered heavy losses.

Yet across the wheat-growing district, the birds continued searching for food and water.

The soldiers were gone.

And the emus kept coming.
