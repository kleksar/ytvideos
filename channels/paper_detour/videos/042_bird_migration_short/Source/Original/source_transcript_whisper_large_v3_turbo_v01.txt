In the 17th century, an English scholar seriously suggested that birds flew to the moon for winter.
And no, it wasn't a joke. For centuries, people had wondered where birds went when the cold arrived. Each autumn, swallows and storks vanished from parts of Europe. In spring, they returned. Where had they been? Some people thought swallows spent the winter asleep in the mud at the bottom of lakes. Others believed certain birds changed into different species. And Charles Morton proposed an even stranger answer. Birds spent the winter on the moon. Of course, people had known about seasonal bird movements since ancient times. But the distant winter homes of many European birds remained difficult to prove.
Then came a remarkable find. In May 1822, a white stork was shot near Klutz in northern Germany. When the bird was examined, something extraordinary was found in its neck. An arrow, almost 80 centimetres long. The arrow came from Africa. The stork had survived its wound, travelled a vast distance, and returned to Europe with the arrow still in its body. It became one of the earliest physical pieces of evidence for long-distance migration between Africa and Europe. The preserved stork is still part of the zoological collection of the University of Rostock.
