In November 1975, a massive cargo ship called the Edmund Fitzgerald was crossing Lake Superior. On board were 29 people and more than 26,000 tons of iron ore.

The next day, the ship disappeared during a storm. No one heard a distress call. And just minutes before it is believed to have gone down, the crew was still answering radio calls from the bridge.

What happened between that final conversation and the ship's disappearance remains unknown to this day.

The Edmund Fitzgerald left the port of Superior on November 9. Its destination was the Detroit area, where it was supposed to deliver its cargo after crossing Lake Superior. For a ship more than 200 meters long, this was another routine voyage across the Great Lakes.

Another freighter, the Arthur M. Anderson, was heading in the same direction. The two ships stayed in radio contact. As the weather began to deteriorate, both changed course toward the Canadian shore, hoping it would offer some protection from the waves.

But on November 10, the storm grew stronger. The wind shifted direction, and enormous waves built up on the eastern side of the lake. Snow reduced visibility. Both ships were now trying to reach the more sheltered waters of Whitefish Bay.

That afternoon, the Fitzgerald reported damage. Part of its deck railing had been damaged, there were problems with its ventilation equipment, and the ship had developed a list. Its pumps were running.

Later, its radars failed. The Fitzgerald was sailing ahead, while the Anderson followed behind, helping over the radio by reporting the ship's position relative to the shore and other vessels.

That evening, the Anderson contacted the crew again and asked how they were managing their problems.

The reply from the Fitzgerald was simple: “We are holding our own.”

That was the last known radio contact.

Soon afterward, the Fitzgerald's radar echo disappeared from the screen. When visibility improved, its lights were gone too. Repeated radio calls went unanswered.

The Anderson reported the disappearance to the Coast Guard. Then, after reaching sheltered waters, the freighter turned around and headed back into the storm to help with the search.

Lifeboats and other debris were found. There were no survivors. All 29 people aboard the Edmund Fitzgerald had died.

Later, search equipment located the wreck at a depth of about 160 meters. The ship lay in two main sections. The bow was upright, while the stern was upside down.

But even the discovery of the wreck did not provide a final answer to the central question: what exactly happened in those last few minutes?

The United States Coast Guard concluded that water had probably been entering the cargo hold gradually through hatch covers that were not sufficiently watertight. As more water entered, the ship settled lower and lower in the lake.

The National Transportation Safety Board offered a different explanation for the final stage of the disaster. According to its theory, water may also have been accumulating inside the ship, but then one or more hatch covers gave way under the pressure of the water, causing the cargo hold to flood rapidly.

These were the conclusions of investigations, not a record of the ship's final moments. No one from the Fitzgerald returned to explain what happened.

We know the route it took, its final radio messages, and where the wreck lies today. But between the words “We are holding our own” and the loss of the ship, there is still a gap that has never been fully reconstructed.
