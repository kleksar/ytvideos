The French army needed
radio communication between Paris

and the fortresses in eastern France.

At the fortresses, antennas were lifted
by tethered balloons,

but this depended on the weather.

In Paris, the army needed a permanent,
tall structure to support an antenna.

A tower built for an exhibition,
not for the military,

could serve that purpose.

The Eiffel Tower, about 300 metres tall,
opened in 1889 for the World's Fair.

In 1898, engineer Eugène Ducretet
sent a wireless Morse code signal

from the tower to the Panthéon in Paris.

This showed that the tower could be used
for radio experiments.

But one successful test didn't prove
that radio would be useful to the army.

In 1899, military engineer Gustave Ferrié
watched Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi

demonstrate wireless telegraphy.

Ferrié was then assigned to develop
radio for the French military.

Transmitters and receivers were tested
at military facilities and lighthouses.

Some within the military doubted
that the new technology would be useful.

In 1903, Gustave Eiffel,
the man behind the tower,

let Ferrié use it for experiments
and helped fund the research.

The military engineering department
gave permission to install antennas.

Ferrié's team worked in a wooden room
near the tower's southern leg.

Now the tall structure in Paris was
sending signals to fortresses

in eastern France, exactly where
the army needed communication.

Later, signals from the tower reached
a military base in Bizerte, Tunisia.

In 1909, a permanent military radio
station was installed

beneath the Champ de Mars.

The equipment was placed underground,

while the antennas were mounted
on the Eiffel Tower.

A tower built for an exhibition

had become part of
military communications.

Its antennas stood high above Paris,

while the radio station itself
was housed underground.
