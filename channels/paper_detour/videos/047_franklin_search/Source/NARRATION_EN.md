On September 3, 2016, a research vessel named Martin Bergmann was heading north through the Canadian Arctic. Its crew was going to join the search for HMS Terror, one of two ships that had disappeared during an expedition led by John Franklin. The main search was taking place much farther north. But Sammy Kogvik, a local man aboard the vessel, had told another crew member about something he had seen years earlier: a large wooden object rising out of the ice in a bay called Terror Bay. It was an observation, not proof that a ship lay below. The crew changed course anyway and went to examine the bay.

Erebus had been found two years earlier. Terror was still missing. Before Erebus was found, searchers knew where Franklin's crews had abandoned the ships, but not where either had finally sunk.

In May 1845, Erebus and Terror sailed from England to search for the Northwest Passage, a sea route through the Arctic connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. There were 129 people aboard the two ships, under Franklin's overall command. In September 1846, both vessels became trapped in ice near King William Island. Franklin died in June 1847. By April 1848, the 105 surviving members of the expedition had abandoned the ships and planned to travel south.

They recorded those events in a note left in a stone shelter. The note was written in 1848, but searchers did not find it until 1859. It established where the ships had been trapped and where the crews had left them. It did not establish where the hulls finally came to rest. A ship abandoned in ice might not remain at the place where its crew walked away. Anyone searching for Erebus and Terror had to reckon with that difference.

The crews' note was not the only evidence. Before it was found, Inuit people living and travelling in the region had already shared accounts of encounters connected to the expedition.

In 1854, the explorer John Rae received accounts from Inuit people about Europeans travelling south and about the bodies of men found later. An Inuit interpreter and guide, William Uligpak, also known as Mar-ko, played an important part in communicating this information. Rae also obtained objects that Inuit people had collected after the expedition disappeared. These reports did not locate a wreck. They did, however, describe events beyond the position recorded in Franklin's note.

Charles Hall later gathered further Inuit accounts, working with the interpreters and guides Takulittuk and Ipiivik. Some of the accounts described a ship seen among the ice farther south, away from the place where the crews had abandoned their ships. That ship was said to have sunk. The descriptions could not, by themselves, identify it as Erebus or Terror. But they pointed toward a different part of the coast.

In 1869, an Inuit man named Inukpudjijuk indicated an area to Hall on a map and made his own sketch. The area he indicated lay near the Adelaide Peninsula, in a southern region known in Inuit accounts as Ugyulik. Inukpudjijuk had not witnessed the sinking, and his indication was not exact coordinates. But it gave the accounts a specific geographical reference.

The 1848 note showed where the crews had left the ships. Inuit accounts suggested a vessel had later been seen farther south. Neither identified a wreck, but the southern coast had become a place worth searching.

For decades, searches found neither wreck. In the twentieth century, David Woodman revisited Inuit testimony recorded by nineteenth-century investigators. He compared descriptions of ships, coastal landmarks, and encounters with Franklin's men, using them to consider possible search areas. Woodman joined searches himself but did not find the ships.

In Gjoa Haven, Inuit historian Louie Kamookak collected accounts from elders, studied local place names, and related historical descriptions to the geography known by the region's residents. Names written down by nineteenth-century visitors did not always match modern charts. Understanding the places named in old accounts required knowledge of the actual coast, islands, and waters. Kamookak's work was rooted in the region's own history, not simply in the search for the British ships.

Woodman and Kamookak joined a search in 1997 with Parks Canada and other partners. No ship was found. Parks Canada continued working with Kamookak, whose research drew on accounts passed down by elders. Recorded testimony, local knowledge, and field searches contributed in different ways; none amounted to ready-made wreck coordinates.

In 2008, underwater archaeologists from Parks Canada began a multiyear search. They reviewed historical records and Inuit accounts and consulted local knowledge holders. They identified two principal areas: one near where the ships had been abandoned in 1848, and another farther south, where Inuit accounts had placed a vessel.

Parks Canada archaeologists scanned the seabed with sonar, using sound to image underwater objects. Government of Nunavut archaeologists examined sites on land, including former Inuit camps. Inuit knowledge holders contributed history and geography. Coast guard and other survey teams supported the work. Several seasons passed without a confirmed wreck.

Then, on September 1, 2014, a Government of Nunavut archaeology team landed on a small island in the southern search area. They were investigating traces of an Inuit camp, including a stone outline where a dwelling had once stood. During the survey, a pilot named Andrew Stirling noticed a rusted piece of metal on the shore. Archaeologists examined it and recognized part of a ship's mechanism for raising and lowering a boat. Its design corresponded to equipment shown in drawings of Erebus.

The team also found a wooden plug used to close a hole through which a ship's rope passed. Neither object pinpointed a wreck, but both connected that stretch of shore with shipboard equipment.

Archaeologist Douglas Stenton carried the finds to the icebreaker Sir Wilfrid Laurier, where members of the search team were based. The following morning, Parks Canada archaeologist Ryan Harris changed the area to be surveyed. Instead of continuing the sonar work as previously planned, the team moved its planned sonar survey closer to the shore where the objects had been found. The research boat Investigator began scanning the seabed.

Within minutes, a large ship's hull appeared on the sonar screen. After years of searches, a wreck had been located. Underwater archaeologists examined its construction and compared it with historical plans. They confirmed that the vessel was HMS Erebus. On September 2, 2014, one of Franklin's two missing ships had finally been found, in the southern search area near the Adelaide Peninsula.

The discovery did not come from exact coordinates preserved in an old account. The team had studied a place on land, recognized potentially relevant objects, and then shifted the search on the water. A previously broad search area had produced a confirmed location.

Terror, however, was still missing. In 2016, the main search focused farther north, closer to where the crews had abandoned the vessels. Martin Bergmann was heading there when Kogvik's report prompted a detour.

Kogvik had seen the object about six years earlier on a hunting trip. His observation did not identify a ship. Other stories in Gjoa Haven had also described an unknown vessel in Terror Bay. The Martin Bergmann crew, working with the Arctic Research Foundation, chose to investigate.

They surveyed the bay with sonar. On the seabed was the shape of a large, three-masted ship, sitting nearly upright on its keel. It lay roughly a hundred kilometres from the main search area. An underwater vehicle then provided views of well-preserved parts of the wreck, including interior spaces. The images showed that a ship was there. They did not yet establish its name.

The Arctic Research Foundation informed Canadian authorities of the discovery. Parks Canada specialists came to Terror Bay to examine it archaeologically. They studied underwater images and compared visible features with the historical plans, including details of the steering equipment and the bow. Those features distinguished the wreck from Erebus. On September 18, 2016, Parks Canada formally confirmed that the ship found in Terror Bay was HMS Terror.

The discoveries followed different leads. In 2014, finds on shore changed a sonar survey and led to Erebus. In 2016, Kogvik's report led a vessel to a different bay and the wreck of Terror. In each case, a location was tested and the evidence underwater was examined before a ship was identified.

Erebus lay south of King William Island, near the Adelaide Peninsula. Terror lay in Terror Bay, beside King William Island. What had been uncertain areas on a chart became two identified wreck sites, found in 2014 and 2016. More than a century and a half after the crews abandoned their ships, both locations were known. The search for Franklin's two missing vessels had reached its end: Erebus and Terror had been found.
