# English narration v01 — faithful production adaptation of accepted Russian v13

Pastry chef Nicolas Appert was looking for a way to preserve food for longer. In Brest, food prepared by his method remained in sealed vessels for three months at the roadstead, an anchorage near the port. When the Board of Health inspected the food, the broth, meat, beans, and green peas received a favorable assessment.

For the early nineteenth century, this was an unusual result. But preserving a few samples and teaching other people to get the same result were different challenges. How could one person turn years of experiments into a method others could use?

At the time, people preserved food mainly with salt, sugar, and drying. These methods extended shelf life, but changed the food’s taste and qualities. Appert, who worked as a pastry chef and distilled spirits, looked for another way.

He experimented with meat, broth, milk, vegetables, and fruit. He left prepared samples in storage, then checked their condition later to find out which methods had worked.

His method relied on glass bottles and wide-mouthed vessels. Appert filled them with food, sealed them with corks, fastened the corks with wire, and heated the vessels in a water bath.

Different foods required different preparation and heating times. The shape of the neck, the quality of the glass, and the seal all mattered.

A bottle could burst while heating. Appert put the vessels in cloth bags to contain the shards. Green peas created another problem: peas picked too young became too soft after processing.

In March 1809, a committee of the Society for the Encouragement of National Industry inspected samples prepared by Appert and gave their condition a favorable assessment.

Appert reported that some foods had been prepared more than eight months earlier, and one sample fifteen months before the inspection. But the society had kept the vessels for only about two months. The committee could assess the food’s condition, but had not observed the entire storage period Appert described.

The proposal to publish a detailed account of the method came from Appert himself.

On January 30, 1810, France’s minister of the interior sent him a letter. It set out a reward of twelve thousand francs on the condition that Appert prepare and publish a description of his method and provide the ministry with two hundred copies.

The description was to be submitted to the Board of Arts and Manufactures for consideration, and Appert had to pay for printing himself.

Appert described how to work with different foods: their preparation, the tools required, and the steps to follow. He explained the vessels, corks, heating times, and mistakes that could affect the result.

In 1810, Appert’s book appeared in France, turning years of individual work into a published guide.
