On April 4, 1865, an unusual machine began operating on the eastern edge of London. At Crossness pumping station, steam-powered pumps lifted the city's sewage into a covered reservoir. This was the southern end of London's new sewer system. Across the river, the northern branch led to Barking Creek. To understand why London built these two routes, yet still dumped its waste into the Thames, we need to go back to the problem they were supposed to solve.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, London's sewers emptied waste directly into the Thames. The sewage disappeared from sight, but it remained in the river flowing through the capital. And the Thames was a tidal river. Its current could change direction. During parliamentary debates in July 1858, no one could identify a discharge point where they could be certain the pollution would never flow back.

Simply carrying sewage beyond the city wasn't enough. London also had to decide where that sewage would enter the water again.

That summer, intense heat and low water levels made the smell from the polluted riverbanks unbearable. The stench reached the Houses of Parliament in Westminster, disrupting the work of lawmakers. Plans for intercepting sewers, large pipes designed to collect and redirect the city's waste, had been discussed before. The Great Stink made their construction urgent.

But the smell couldn't answer one crucial question: where should those new sewers end?

On July 22, Parliament debated two options. One was to discharge the sewage near Barking Creek. The other was to carry it farther downstream, to Sea Reach. The longer route meant building more infrastructure. The shorter route left an unresolved question about how the sewage would behave in a tidal river.

During the same debate, a representative from Woolwich said his district was already suffering from London's sewage. Others challenged that claim, pointing out that Woolwich discharged sewage of its own. The debate did not establish that London's sewage had caused the reported harm. The issue remained disputed.

On August 2, Parliament ordered the Metropolitan Board of Works, the authority responsible for major public works in London, to begin the necessary construction. The goal was to prevent the capital's sewage from entering the Thames within the metropolitan area, as far as reasonably practical. But the law did not specify a single discharge point. That decision was left to the board.

Soon, it settled on two routes. The northern sewers would lead to Barking Creek. The southern sewers would lead to Crossness Point. Sea Reach, the more distant option discussed in Parliament, was not chosen for the original system.

In 1859, construction began on the southern sewers and Crossness pumping station. There, steam-powered pumps would lift the sewage into a covered reservoir. The waste would be held until the tide was going out, then released into the river. Crossness opened in 1865.

Meanwhile, the northern network continued to develop. Its Abbey Mills pumping station was completed by 1868. And by around 1875, the northern and southern branches were operating together as one system.

So London finally had its answer. Sewage from the north went to Barking Creek. Sewage from the south went to Crossness Point. The new sewers carried intercepted waste away from the centre of London. At Crossness, the reservoir also made it possible to wait for the outgoing tide before releasing it.

But there was one major limitation. The original system did not clean the sewage before discharging it. The waste still entered the Thames. It was simply released farther downstream.

London had changed the route its sewage travelled and, at Crossness, the timing of its release. But it had not turned sewage into clean water.
