Accepted narration:

A Binance engineer interviews for a job at another company. First, he speaks with a recruiter. Then he speaks with someone claiming to be the company's manager. He gets a test assignment and downloads it to his work computer.

But the company doesn't exist.

A few weeks later, his real employer will lose more than six billion dollars.

The trap looks convincing: the invitation comes through a real job platform, the interviews are by video, and the recruiter even shows him her account, with personal photos and videos.

It all leads to one thing: running the code.

As he sets up the assignment, malware runs alongside it. Security systems miss it, giving the attackers access to a computer inside Binance.

According to Binance's preliminary findings, they use that computer to reach two more engineers' machines. Credentials found there give them access to the system that approves transfers from Binance wallets, allowing them to authorize transfers to addresses they control.

On October 29, crypto assets start moving to those addresses.

Security flags the large transfers. Binance halts ordinary withdrawals and, with its partners, tries to freeze the stolen assets.

But that isn't enough.

Through the compromised system, the attackers manage to give another program permission to move crypto assets directly from a wallet on Ethereum.

That program no longer needs Binance's ordinary withdrawal system. Binance has stopped that system, but the permission recorded on Ethereum remains active.

Revoking it requires a separate operation on the blockchain itself. Turning off withdrawals on Binance's website cannot cancel that permission.

From the first transfers to the finalization of the last transaction already sent, almost four hours pass. The permission is revoked later, after midnight.

The stolen crypto assets are valued at about $6.34 billion. About $2.85 billion worth of those assets is frozen or intercepted — but that does not mean they have been returned.

Binance says it will cover the loss with its own funds.

The company he interviewed with never existed. His real employer had to face the consequences.
