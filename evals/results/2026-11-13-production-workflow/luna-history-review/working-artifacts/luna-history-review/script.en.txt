On a winter day in 1919, a wave of molasses tore through Boston. But the real shock came after the flood.

A steel tank in the North End burst, sending molasses into the streets. At first, the company blamed sabotage. The cause remained unsettled.

Then the evidence pointed somewhere less dramatic—and more damning. The tank was not strong enough for its load. It had leaked for years. No engineer had been consulted before it was built. After years of hearings, the court-appointed auditor’s 1925 report rejected sabotage and assigned civil liability to the company.

Twenty-one people died. This was not simply a freak accident of sticky syrup. It was a preventable failure hidden inside an ordinary-looking tank.

So when you picture that Boston wave, remember what broke first: not just steel, but the assumption that somebody had checked it.
