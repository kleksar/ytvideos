“Please cut off your battery.” In 1859, Boston sent that request by telegraph.

At the other end of the wire was another operator in Portland, Maine.

The line was failing. Signals weakened, then surged until the machines couldn't work.

Normally, the batteries powered messages. During the storm, they made the interference worse.

Boston suggested an experiment: let the line run with both batteries disconnected.

Portland agreed. Boston had already switched its battery off. Then a reply arrived.

Portland said the message came through better than when the batteries were connected.

And real telegrams kept crossing between the two stations, with no batteries on the line.

Not for a moment. For more than two hours.

The power wasn't inside either station. It came from electricity induced in the wires.

The day before, astronomer Richard Carrington had seen a sudden flash on the Sun.

The solar storm that followed disturbed Earth's magnetic field, driving current through long wires.

Elsewhere, that current had disabled telegraphs. Here, it briefly became their power source.

When the storm faded, the batteries went back on. Their remarkable experiment began with one request.
